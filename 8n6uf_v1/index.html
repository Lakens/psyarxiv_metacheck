Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Numerical ratio reliably predicts performance in explicit non-symbolic quantity comparison, but whether it also predicts performance when the same objects are judged by their valence remains unclear. We examined this question in 156 participants assigned to one of four instructions: select the group containing fewer or more bacteria, or select the group depicted as good or bad. All groups viewed the same paired displays, making numerosity relevant to quantity judgments and irrelevant to valence judgments. Mixed-effects models assessed the relationship between numerical ratio, defined as the smaller numerosity divided by the larger numerosity, and response times and accuracy. As ratio approached 1, responses became slower and less accurate in the quantity task. In the valence task, neither response times nor accuracy showed a reliable relationship with ratio. The average ratio slopes differed significantly between quantity and valence judgments for both outcomes. These findings indicate that the behavioral expression of numerical relations depends on whether quantity is relevant to the required judgment.

Introduction

Numerical information provides a basis for evaluating and comparing groups of objects in the environment. Without counting or relying on symbolic number representations, humans can estimate numerosity and distinguish between sets containing different numbers of items (Feigenson et al., 2004). However, objects are rarely encountered solely as quantities: they also differ in their identity, physical properties, and affective significance. Depending on the observer's goals, a judgment may therefore concern either the relative quantity of objects or their qualitative properties. This raises the question of whether the numerical relation between two sets predicts performance not only when quantity is explicitly compared, but also when the required judgment concerns a nonnumerical property.

Numerical comparison is characterized by systematic effects of the relation between the quantities being compared. Early research on symbolic number comparison established the numerical distance effect, whereby performance improves as the absolute difference between two numbers increases (Moyer & Landauer, 1967). In non-symbolic numerical comparison, performance is also sensitive to numerical ratio, typically becoming slower and less accurate as the ratio of the smaller to the larger numerosity approaches 1 (Dietrich et al., 2015). Although numerical distance and ratio are closely related, they express numerical relations in terms of absolute difference and relative magnitude, respectively. For example, the pairs 2-4 and 8-10 have the same numerical distance, but their ratios differ (.50 and .80, respectively), indicating that the latter pair is relatively closer in magnitude. Numerical ratio therefore provides a measure of relative numerical similarity that can be used to examine how this similarity relates to response speed and accuracy.

The interpretation of performance in non-symbolic comparison also requires attention to set size and visual properties. Kaufman et al. (1949) introduced the term subitizing to describe the rapid and accurate apprehension of small numerosities, distinguishing it from the estimation of larger sets. Comparisons involving small numerosities may therefore differ in their processing demands from those involving larger numerosities. Moreover, numerosity covaries with continuous visual properties, including total surface area, item size, density, and spatial extent.

These properties must be considered when evaluating ratio-dependent performance, because sensitivity to numerical ratio does not, by itself, establish that the observed effects are independent of continuous visual information (Dietrich et al., 2015).

Evidence suggests that numerical magnitude can influence performance even when it is irrelevant to the required judgment. With symbolic stimuli, Dehaene and Akhavein (1995) observed numerical distance effects during physical matching judgments of Arabic numerals and number words, suggesting involuntary access to numerical magnitude. However, the presence of numerical effects can depend on the feature participants are instructed to judge. Fias et al. (2001) proposed that task-irrelevant numerical information is more likely to influence responses when processing the relevant feature recruits neural resources that overlap with those involved in numerical processing. Their evidence concerned the spatial-numerical association of response codes (SNARC), typically characterized by faster left-hand responses to smaller numbers and faster right-hand responses to larger numbers. Its occurrence in tasks that do not require magnitude judgments has been interpreted as evidence for involuntary magnitude processing (Dehaene et al., 1993). Using symbolic digits, Fias et al. (2001) observed SNARC effects during orientation judgments but not color or shape judgments. They attributed this pattern to the proposed overlap in parietal processing resources for orientation and numerical magnitude, with color and shape judgments relying less on these resources. Mitchell et al. (2012) reported a similar pattern with non-symbolic arrays, finding a SNARC effect during orientation judgments but not color judgments. These findings suggest that the behavioral expression of task-irrelevant numerical magnitude can vary depending on the feature participants are instructed to judge.

Evidence concerning numerical distance likewise suggests a role for attentional focus. Marlair et al. (2024) used frequency-tagging EEG with rapidly presented numerical stimuli in multiple formats, including digits, number words, canonical dot patterns, and finger configurations, while directing attention to magnitude, parity, color, or no specific feature. The neural distance effect was significant under magnitude and parity instructions, whereas evidence for the effect was only marginal under color and no-specific-feature instructions. These findings suggest that neural sensitivity to numerical distance can vary depending on the feature to which attention is directed.

Affective information offers another context for examining whether sensitivity to numerical relations extends beyond explicit quantity judgments. Young and Cordes (2013) found that numerical displays preceded by angry or happy faces were judged as less numerous than those preceded by neutral faces. Droit-Volet (2013) found that an aversive context led sequentially presented numerosities to be judged as larger, whereas no comparable difference from the control condition was observed for non-sequentially presented numerosities. When affective significance was a property of the objects being compared, Hamamouche et al. (2017) found lower numerical comparison accuracy for threatening than neutral objects; conversely, threatening images presented before neutral numerical displays improved comparison accuracy.

These findings show that affective information can influence numerical judgments, but they leave open the complementary question of whether numerical relations predict performance when affective information, rather than quantity, defines the required judgment.

Although previous studies have shown that numerical information can influence performance when it is task-irrelevant, and that affective information can influence numerical judgments, it remains unclear whether numerical ratio predicts performance when participants judge affective rather than numerical properties. Comparing ratio slopes across judgments of the same displays can therefore test whether the relationship between numerical ratio and performance differs between quantity comparisons and judgments of depicted valence.

To address this question, we compared ratio-dependent performance across relative quantity and valence judgments using identical stimulus displays. To support both judgments, we developed stimuli that varied in numerosity and depicted valence within a common category of objects. We selected bacteria because their associations with beneficial functions and harmful consequences provided a basis for constructing good and bad depictions. Each display contained two groups of bacteria differing in both numerosity and depicted valence. Depending on their instructions, participants selected either the group containing fewer or more bacteria or the group depicted as good or bad. Numerosity thus determined the correct response in the quantity task but was irrelevant to the required judgment in the valence task. Numerical ratio, defined as the smaller numerosity divided by the larger numerosity, served as the primary predictor of performance. We expected responses in the quantity task to become slower and less accurate as ratio approached 1. Specifically, we examined whether increasing ratio predicted longer response times and lower accuracy during good/bad judgments, and whether the average ratio slope across fewer/more instructions differed from that across good/bad instructions. Response times were the primary outcome, with accuracy providing a complementary measure.

Method Participants

We recruited 160 native Turkish speakers from Izmir University of Economics and randomly assigned 40 participants to each of four between-participant target-instruction conditions. Only self-reported right-handed individuals were recruited. Participants received monetary compensation for their participation and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision.

Handedness was subsequently assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (EHI; Oldfield, 1971), and participants with EHI scores below 40 were excluded from the analyses. A self-reported diagnosis of dyslexia, dyscalculia, or spatial neglect constituted an additional exclusion criterion.

Data from four participants were excluded: three because their EHI scores were below 40 and one because of a self-reported diagnosis of dyslexia. The final sample therefore comprised 156 participants: 39 in the fewer-target condition (26 women, 13 men; M = 21.38 years, SD = 2.49), 39 in the more-target condition (26 women, 13 men; M = 20.95 years, SD = 2.37), 40 in the good-target condition (26 women, 14 men; M = 21.13 years, SD = 3.17), and 38 in the badtarget condition (23 women, 15 men; M = 21.11 years, SD = 2.67).

All participants provided written informed consent before participation. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Izmir University of Economics (Approval No. E-97429853-050.04-115110).

Stimuli and Apparatus

The stimuli consisted of images of bacteria designed to convey positive or negative valence (hereafter referred to as good and bad bacteria). The images were generated using Google Gemini 2.5 Pro from prompts describing visual characteristics intended to convey positive or negative valence. The generated images were reviewed by the authors, and the final images used in the experiment were selected to provide clearly distinguishable good and bad depictions while retaining a common stimulus category. Numerosities ranged from 1 to 9. In each trial, we presented one array of good bacteria and one array of bad bacteria simultaneously on opposite sides of the screen, and the two arrays always differed in numerosity. The nine numerosities yielded 36 possible pairs of different numerosities, with each combination counted once regardless of left-right position. Each numerical pair was presented in four configurations that fully crossed the left-right position of the smaller and larger numerosities with the left-right position of the good and bad bacteria, yielding 144 unique stimulus displays (see Figure 1 for stimulus examples). Thus, relative numerosity and valence category were counterbalanced across screen position, such that neither the smaller/larger numerosity nor the good/bad category was systematically associated with the left or right side of the display.

To reduce covariation between numerosity and selected continuous visual properties, we followed the general stimulus-control logic described by Gebuis and Reynvoet (2011). Total surface area (TSA) and a study-specific measure of spatial extent were manipulated factorially across numerosity levels. Stimulus arrays were generated in Python 3.13. TSA was defined as the total number of pixels occupied by the bacteria within an array. Because the stimulus set included very small numerosities, including single-item arrays, we did not use convex hull as the measure of spatial extent. Instead, maximum radial extent was defined as the distance from the fixed center of the array canvas to the farthest occupied pixel. This measure could be applied consistently across all numerosities, including single-item arrays. In the final stimulus set, TSA ranged from 41,792 to 43,344 pixels in the low condition and from 66,808 to 68,112 pixels in the high condition. Maximum radial extent ranged from 240.63 to 417.97 pixels, with values below 330 classified as low and values at or above 330 classified as high. For each combination of numerosity (1-9) and valence (good, bad), the final stimulus set contained four arrays in each of the four TSA x maximum radial extent combinations. The resulting 288 individual arrays were paired to create 144 unique stimulus displays.

Across the 144 displays, signed left-minus-right differences in numerosity showed nearzero correlations with corresponding differences in TSA (r ≈ .02) and maximum radial extent (r ≈ -.04). These checks concerned the two manipulated visual properties and did not establish independence between numerosity and other properties, such as convex hull area or mean item area.

Stimuli were presented against a gray background (RGB: 128, 128, 128) on a 23.8-inch HP ProOne 440 monitor with a resolution of 1920 x 1080 pixels and a refresh rate of 60 Hz. The experiment was programmed and administered using PsychoPy 2025.2.4. Participants were seated approximately 60 cm from the screen, and responses were recorded using the "A" and "İ" keys on a Turkish QWERTY keyboard. Each display contained one good-bacteria array and one bad-bacteria array presented on opposite sides of the screen. Across the full stimulus set, numerosity, valence category, and left-right position were varied.

The same stimulus displays were presented in both the magnitude comparison and valence judgment tasks.

Procedure

The 144 unique stimulus displays were each presented twice, resulting in 288 experimental trials per participant. Experimental trials were presented in a randomized order for each participant. Each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 500 ms, followed by the stimulus display, which remained on the screen until a response was made or for a maximum of 2000 ms. The intertrial interval was 1000 ms. Before the experimental trials, participants completed eight practice trials with feedback for correct, incorrect, and slow responses (RT > 1500 ms). The practice phase included two trials from each of the four possible left-right configurations of relative numerosity and valence category. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible.

Participants placed their left index finger on the "A" key and their right index finger on the "İ" key and responded by pressing the key corresponding to the side of the screen that satisfied their assigned target instruction. In the magnitude comparison task, participants selected the side containing fewer or more bacteria, depending on their assigned instruction. In the valence judgment task, participants selected the side containing good or bad bacteria.

Design

The study comprised four between-participant target-instruction conditions organized within two task contexts. In the magnitude comparison task, participants were instructed to identify either the side containing fewer bacteria (fewer-target condition) or the side containing more bacteria (more-target condition). In the valence judgment task, participants were instructed to identify either the side containing good bacteria (good-target condition) or the side containing bad bacteria (bad-target condition). Each participant completed only one of the four targetinstruction conditions. Complementary target instructions were included within each task so that task-level performance could be evaluated across both possible target categories rather than being tied to a single response rule (fewer vs. more; good vs. bad). Accordingly, the primary task-level comparison averaged across the two target-instruction conditions within each task.

The same stimulus displays and response format were used across all four conditions.

Critically, the information required to determine the correct response differed between task contexts. In the magnitude comparison task, numerical magnitude was task-relevant, whereas valence category was task-irrelevant. In the valence judgment task, valence category was taskrelevant, whereas numerical magnitude was task-irrelevant. Reaction time (RT) was the primary outcome measure, with accuracy analyzed as a secondary outcome.

Data Preparation

RT analyses included correct responses only. Incorrect responses accounted for 4.03% of all experimental trials and were excluded. RTs outside the 150-1500 ms range were then removed, accounting for 0.50% of all trials. Finally, after this range restriction, trials with RTs more than 2.5 SDs from each participant's mean were removed, accounting for a further 2.60% of all trials. Following data preparation, 92.87% of the data were retained for the RT analyses.

The accuracy analysis used all experimental trials from the final sample and was not subjected to RT filtering. Condition-specific exclusion and retention rates are reported in Table S1 of the Online Supplementary Material (OSM).

Data Analysis

The primary analysis examined trial-level variation in RT as a function of numerical ratio. Numerical ratio was calculated as the smaller numerosity divided by the larger numerosity, such that values closer to 1 indicated more similar numerosities. Across these 36 numerical pairs, the mean ratio was .50; the ratio was therefore centered at .50 for the primary analysis. The numerical structure of all 36 pairs is provided in Table S2 of the OSM.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1. RTs were log-transformed to reduce positive skew and were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. Distributional information NUMERICAL RATIO EFFECTS ACROSS JUDGMENTS 13 before and after transformation is shown in Figure S1 of the OSM. Numerical ratio, centered at .50, target-instruction condition (fewer, more, good, bad), and their interaction were included as fixed effects. Because task context was fully determined by target-instruction condition, task context was not entered as a separate fixed effect. The model included participant-specific random intercepts and random slopes for numerical ratio, allowing both overall response speed and ratio sensitivity to vary across participants. It also included stimulus-specific random intercepts and random slopes for task context, allowing the effect of task context to vary across stimulus displays, as well as random intercepts for numerical pair. The model was fitted by maximum likelihood. Estimated ratio slopes and contrasts were obtained using emmeans and evaluated using asymptotic z tests and 95% confidence intervals. The main contrast compared the average ratio slope across the fewer-and more-target conditions with the average slope across the good-and bad-target conditions, directly testing whether the relationship between numerical ratio and RT differed when numerosity was task-relevant versus task-irrelevant.

Regression coefficients from the RT models are reported on the log-RT scale.

Accuracy was analyzed separately as a secondary outcome using all experimental trials from the final sample. Correct responses were coded as 1 and all other responses as 0. A binomial generalized linear mixed-effects model with a logit link included the same ratio-bytarget-instruction fixed-effects structure, with random intercepts for participant, stimulus display within task context, and numerical pair. Regression coefficients from this model are reported on the log-odds scale. Full fixed-and random-effects estimates for the primary RT and accuracy models are provided in Tables S3 and S4, respectively; diagnostic plots for the primary RT model are shown in Figure S2 of the OSM.

To assess whether the conclusions depended on using ratio as the numerical predictor, numerical distance (the absolute difference between the two numerosities; centered at 3.33) and pair size (the sum of the two numerosities; centered at 10) were analyzed in separate RT models.

Each model used the same fixed-and random-effects structure as the primary RT model, with ratio replaced by the corresponding numerical predictor. Ratio, distance, and size were modeled separately because they are mathematically related. Distance was treated as an alternative index of the numerical relation between the arrays. Pair size indexed total numerosity and was examined as a secondary predictor because the primary question concerned the relation between the two arrays.

Two additional robustness analyses examined whether conclusions from the ratio analysis depended on comparisons involving small numerosities. The first excluded pairs in which both numerosities were between 1 and 4, leaving 30 numerical pairs. The second applied a more stringent restriction, retaining only trials in which both numerosities were at least 5, leaving 10 pairs. The first analysis used the same model structure as the primary RT analysis. For the more restricted analysis, the ratio was centered at the mean of the restricted pair set (.754). Because the primary-model random-effects structure produced a singular fit, the final model was simplified to include random intercepts for participant and stimulus display within task context.

A simulation-based sensitivity analysis was conducted to characterize the sensitivity of the primary model to the main difference in numerical-ratio slopes between the magnitude comparison and valence judgment tasks. For each simulation condition, δ represented the hypothetical difference between the average numerical-ratio slope in the magnitude comparison task and the average numerical-ratio slope in the valence judgment task. Simulation details are provided in the OSM.

Results

Numerical Ratio and Response Time

Overall mean RTs were 601 ms (SD = 103) in the fewer-target condition, 541 ms (SD = 91) in the more-target condition, 561 ms (SD = 77) in the good-target condition, and 564 ms (SD = 88) in the bad-target condition.

The association between numerical ratio and RT differed between the magnitude comparison and valence judgment tasks. In the magnitude comparison task, RTs increased as numerical ratio approached 1, b = .572, SE = .024, 95% CI [.525, .620], z = 23.72, p < .001. In contrast, the estimated ratio slope in the valence judgment task was close to zero and did not differ significantly from zero, b = .001, SE = .016, 95% CI [-.029, .032], z = 0.09, p = .929.

Critically, the ratio slopes differed significantly between the magnitude and valence tasks, b = .571, SE = .026, z = 21.83, p < .001. Thus, the relationship between numerical similarity and response speed was significantly stronger when participants compared numerosity than when they judged valence category. This task-level pattern was also evident across the individual target-instruction conditions. Estimated ratio slopes were positive in both the fewer-target condition, b = .633, SE = .028, 95% CI [.579, .687], and the more-target condition, b = .512, SE = .028, 95% CI [.458, .566]. In contrast, the estimated ratio slope did not differ significantly from zero in either the good-target condition, b = .008, SE = .020, 95% CI [-.031, .048], or the bad-target condition, b = -.006, SE = .021, 95% CI [-.046, .035]. The ratio slope was larger for fewer than for more judgments, b = .121, SE = .027, z = 4.51, p < .001, whereas the good-and bad-target slopes did not differ, b = .014, SE = .027, z = 0.53, p = .598.

Accuracy

Overall accuracy was high in all four conditions: 94.28% in the fewer-target condition, 95.24% in the more-target condition, 97.40% in the good-target condition, and 96.95% in the bad-target condition.

Accuracy nevertheless showed the same task-dependent ratio pattern observed for RT. In the magnitude comparison task, increasing numerical ratio was associated with a lower probability of a correct response, b = -6.304, SE = .366, 95% CI [-7.021, -5.587], z = -17.24, p < .001. In the valence judgment task, the association between numerical ratio and accuracy was not statistically significant, b = .433, SE = .287, 95% CI [-.129, .995], z = 1.51, p = .131. The ratio slopes differed between the two tasks, b = -6.74, SE = .438, z = -15.39, p < .001.

The ratio-related slowing in the magnitude comparison task was accompanied by reduced accuracy, a pattern inconsistent with a speed-accuracy trade-off.

Robustness and Sensitivity Analyses

The same task-dependent pattern was obtained when the numerical relation between the arrays was indexed by numerical distance rather than ratio. In the magnitude comparison task, RTs decreased as the distance between the two numerosities increased, b = -.0514, SE = .0046, 95% CI [-.0603, -.0425], z = -11.29, p < .001. In the valence judgment task, the estimated distance slope did not differ significantly from zero, b < .001, SE = .0015, 95% CI [-.0029, .0028], z = -0.01, p = .990. The distance slopes differed between tasks, b = -.0514, SE = .0046, z = -11.21, p < .001.

Pair size showed a comparable task-dependent pattern. In the magnitude comparison task, RTs increased as the summed numerosity of the two arrays increased, b = .0255, SE = .0030, 95% CI [.0197, .0313], z = 8.63, p < .001. In the valence judgment task, the association between pair size and RT was not statistically significant, b = .0011, SE = .0009, 95% CI [-.0007, .0028], z = 1.20, p = .232. The size slopes differed between tasks, b = .0244, SE = .0030, z = 8.01, p < .001.

Finally, the task-dependent ratio pattern persisted under both restrictions on smallnumber comparisons. After excluding pairs in which both numerosities were between 1 and 4, and when restricting the analysis to pairs in which both numerosities were at least 5, the ratio slope remained positive in the magnitude comparison task (excluding both-small pairs: b = .577, SE = .025, 95% CI [.528, .625], p < .001; both numerosities ≥ 5: magnitude, b = .320, SE = .069, 95% CI [.185, .456], p < .001) but did not differ significantly from zero in the valence judgment task (excluding both-small pairs: b = .008, SE = .015, 95% CI [-.022, .039], p = .581; both numerosities ≥ 5: b = -.036, SE = .068, 95% CI [-.169, .098], p = .601). Together, these analyses indicate that the primary task-dependent ratio pattern was not driven solely by comparisons within the small-number range.

The observed task-level difference in numerical-ratio slopes was b = .571. The model provided approximately 80%, 90%, and 95% sensitivity for task-level slope differences of about δ = .073, .084, and .093, respectively. Thus, the model was sensitive to task-level slope differences substantially smaller than the observed difference. Simulation results are provided in Table S6 of the OSM.

Discussion

The present study examined whether the relationship between numerical ratio and behavioral performance differs when numerosity is relevant versus irrelevant to the required judgment. When participants compared numerical magnitude, responses became slower and less accurate as the two numerosities became more similar, showing a clear ratio-dependent pattern.

In contrast, when participants made valence judgments, the associations between numerical ratio and both RT and accuracy were not statistically significant. Critically, the ratio effects differed between the two tasks in both RT and accuracy. This task difference is consistent with evidence that task-irrelevant numerical information is expressed selectively rather than uniformly across judgments. Behavioral studies have shown that numerical effects can emerge in some non-magnitude tasks but not others, depending on the feature being judged (Fias et al., 2001; Mitchell et al., 2012), and recent EEG evidence similarly indicates that sensitivity to numerical distance varies with attentional focus (Marlair et al., 2024).

The present findings extend this pattern to ratio-dependent performance with non-symbolic numerical displays. With the same displays presented across tasks, numerical ratio strongly predicted both response time and accuracy when participants compared quantity, whereas the corresponding relationships were not statistically reliable when they judged valence. This dissociation suggests that sensitivity to the numerical relation between two sets is not expressed uniformly in behavior simply because the same numerical information is available; rather, its behavioral expression can vary with the judgment participants are required to make. In this sense, task context appears to constitute an important boundary condition on ratio-dependent performance.

The valence-task result also complements previous work linking affective and numerical processing. Emotional or threatening information has been shown to alter numerical judgments (Droit-Volet, 2013; Hamamouche et al., 2017; Young & Cordes, 2013). Here, when valence rather than quantity determined the required response, numerical ratio was not reliably related to Thus, the present findings are more narrowly interpreted as showing that numerical similarity was not reliably associated with behavioral performance when the required judgment concerned valence. They do not establish whether numerical information was processed at earlier stages without influencing the measured response, or whether such processing was absent.

Robustness analyses reinforced this task-dependent pattern. When numerical distance was used instead of ratio, greater distance was associated with faster responses in the magnitude comparison task, whereas the corresponding association in the valence judgment task was not statistically significant. Because small numerosities can engage processing characteristics that differ from those observed for larger sets under some conditions (Hyde, 2011), we also examined whether the ratio pattern depended on comparisons involving only small numerosities. The taskdependent ratio pattern persisted when such comparisons were excluded and remained evident when both numerosities were restricted to values of at least 5. Thus, the task-dependent RT pattern was not specific to numerical ratio as the index of the numerical relation and was not driven solely by comparisons within the small-number range. A comparable task difference was also observed for the association between pair size and RT.

A secondary finding was that the ratio-RT slope was steeper in the fewer-target condition than in the more-target condition. Previous work indicates that comparison instructions can influence performance in numerical comparison tasks. Using non-symbolic object arrays, Patro and Shaki (2016) found that spatial-numerical response patterns differed depending on whether participants selected the set containing fewer or more items, although their analysis concerned spatial response biases rather than sensitivity to numerical ratio. Complementing this evidence, Ogata et al. (2011) found an overall choice effect in a symbolic digit-comparison task, with slower responses when participants selected the smaller rather than the larger digit; importantly, however, numerical distance did not interact with choice. The difference between the fewer-and more-target slopes was a secondary finding for which no directional prediction had been specified. Future work could test directly whether selecting the smaller versus larger set systematically modulates ratio-dependent performance in non-symbolic comparison.

A further consideration concerns the contribution of continuous visual properties. Total surface area and maximum radial extent were systematically varied across numerosity levels, and their signed between-side differences showed near-zero correlations with numerical differences. These controls did not, however, remove covariation between numerosity and other visual properties, including convex hull area and mean item area. The ratio effect in the magnitude comparison task therefore cannot, by itself, establish that performance depended on numerosity independently of continuous visual information (Dietrich et al., 2015; Leibovich & Henik, 2013; Lourenco & Aulet, 2023). Because the same displays were used across tasks, the available visual properties were held constant at the stimulus-set level across task contexts. Thus, the observed task difference cannot be attributed to differences in the physical stimulus sets, although the contribution of particular visual cues to performance may have varied with task demands.

Several design features limit the scope of these conclusions. First, the tasks differed in their processing requirements as well as in the relevance of numerosity. Selecting the fewer or more numerous groups required comparing quantities across the two arrays, whereas selecting the good or bad group could be achieved by recognizing a distinctive bacterial appearance without comparing the arrays as quantities. The present design therefore does not isolate numerical task relevance from differences in comparison requirements. Second, the good and bad bacteria served as operational stimulus categories rather than independently normed affective stimuli, and the task could potentially be solved through visual category recognition without requiring affective evaluation. Future studies using independently normed stimuli and tasks more closely matched in their comparison requirements would help clarify the respective contributions of numerical relevance and other task demands. Finally, the sample was restricted to participants who reported being right-handed and met the EHI criterion, and it remains to be established whether the same pattern generalizes across handedness profiles.

Using identical displays, we found that the ratio-performance relationship differed between magnitude comparison and valence judgment in both RT and accuracy. The task difference in RT slopes was also observed when numerical distance replaced ratio and when the ratio analysis was restricted to pairs containing numerosities of at least 5. These findings indicate that the behavioral expression of numerical relations varies with task demands, although the present design does not identify the processing mechanisms underlying this difference.
