How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

Momentary Emotion Differentiation: A Theory-Consistent Index that is Disambiguated from

Momentary Affect

Emotion differentiation (ED), also called emotional granularity, refers to the extent to which an individual represents their emotional experience with specificity, and theorists have argued that those high in ED distinguish among discrete emotions rather than experience them as a global state of feeling generally "good" or "bad" (Barrett et al., 2001; Kashdan et al., 2015). Higher ED is thought to be functionally adaptive (Kashdan et al., 2014; Seah & Coifman, 2022), with high levels often being associated with indicators of well-being (Carolus & Starr, 2026) and low levels being associated with various forms of psychopathology (Demiralp et al., 2012; Kashdan & Farmer, 2014). Although ED has traditionally been conceptualized and measured as an individual difference, research suggests that it also fluctuates within individuals across daily life (Erbas et al., 2018). Translating ED from an individualdifference construct to a momentary one raises important questions about how ED should be conceptualized and operationalized at a single point in time. In the present study, we draw on existing emotion and ED theory to identify criteria that a momentary index of ED should capture. We then evaluate an existing momentary ED index in relation to these properties and introduce an alternative index designed to more closely align with the theoretical conceptualization of ED. Finally, we evaluate the properties of this alternative index in two ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies.

Clarifying how ED is calculated at the trait level, as an average across many emotional experiences, is important both to understand why this approach cannot be applied to a single moment and because existing momentary measures build on its logic. Thus, momentary indices of ED necessarily inherit some of the assumptions of the trait-level quantification of ED. At the trait level, ED is most commonly operationalized as the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of same-valence emotion ratings across EMA occasions (Thompson et al., 2021a). The ICC captures the degree to which a person's discrete emotions rise and fall together across occasions. To illustrate, a person low in negative ED reports anxiety, sadness, and anger that strongly covary, as if they were interchangeable indicators of a single negative state, whereas a person high in negative ED reports these emotions as only weakly covarying. Although sometimes absolute agreement ICCs are used, consistency ICCs, which are unaffected by stable differences in how intensely a person usually feels each emotion, are most frequently used (Thompson et al., 2021a). The consistency ICC's property of being agnostic to stable differences is a desirable one because stable differences are not themselves indicative of within-person ED. For example, a person who is generally more anxious than angry will rate anxiety above anger even when the two move in perfect tandem. This difference, which does not impact consistency ICC, reflects the individual's stable mean-level differences between emotions, rather than their ability to differentiate between emotions.

An important advancement in the study of ED was the development of indices to assess momentary ED. Because ICC summarizes covariation of discrete emotion item ratings across occasions, it cannot be computed for a single moment. Tomko et al. (2015) computed a separate ICC for each measurement occasion. At each prompt, participants rated 17 negative affect (NA) items belonging to three subscales (fear, hostility, and sadness). Tomko et al. (2015) then used an ANOVA to partition the variability in these 17 items into variance attributable to subscale membership, within-subscale item variance, and residual error. The ICC was the proportion of variance attributable to subscale differences relative to total variance, so the ICC was taken to indicate how much the ratings differed across the three subscales, which the authors called momentary ED. Then Grossmann et al. (2016) took a different approach, developing an entropy index to capture the diversity of emotions for a given occasion. Erbas et al. (2022) decomposed the ICC as a sum of individual occasions and treated each occasion's contribution as the momentary ED (hereafter referred to as sum-based ED). Of the momentary ED indices, the sumbased ED index is currently used most frequently.

Despite the growing use of momentary measures, research has shown that different ED operationalizations yielded substantially different empirical conclusions (Lane & Trull, 2022; Ottenstein & Lischetzke, 2020), highlighting the importance of testing conceptual and psychometric properties of emotional experiences captured by momentary ED indices. Additionally, momentary ED index development has outpaced theoretical clarity about what ED at a single moment actually is and how it relates to trait ED. As a result, there is little consensus on what properties a momentary index of ED should capture. In an attempt to address this gap, it is helpful to consider why ED is thought to be beneficial in the first place.

From a feelings-as-information perspective (Schwarz & Clore, 1983), more differentiated emotions are thought to facilitate effective emotion regulation by providing more specific information about emotion-eliciting situations, thereby helping individuals identify and implement context-appropriate regulatory responses (Barrett et al., 2001; Kashdan et al., 2015). Trait-level ED findings are consistent with this account (e.g., Seah & Coifman, 2022), but ED is not a static trait. It changes across the lifespan (Nook et al., 2018), increases after intervention (Van der Gucht et al., 2019), and fluctuates in daily life (Erbas et al., 2022). For example, people differentiate their emotions less on days when they experience more perceived stress (Erbas et al., 2018). Consistent with the view of traits as distributions of states (Fleeson, 2001), trait ED can be understood as a summary of how a person differentiates emotions across many moments. Because emotion regulation is a dynamic, moment-to-moment process that is facilitated by ED (Kalokerinos et al., 2019), understanding when people differentiate to a greater or lesser extent and the consequences of that ED requires measuring ED at the level of the moment.

Understanding trait ED as an aggregate of momentary states requires a definition of the state being aggregated. Drawing from principles of the feelings-as-information theory (Schwarz & Clore, 1983), we propose that, at a given moment, a person's emotions are differentiated to the extent that each emotion departs from that person's usual level of it by a different amount. The first principle upon which we base our definition for momentary ED is that if emotions are useful because they convey information about the current situation, then the informative part of an emotional experience is how it differs from what the individual usually feels. One's typical level of an emotion can be considered to be present across situations and therefore may not represent any unique information about the current one. The second principle is that if all of a person's emotions depart from their usual levels by the same amount, the experience amounts to a global change (e.g., feeling generally worse than normal), which signals the difference in degree of the situation (i.e., that something is "good" or "bad" in terms of emotional valence and intensity) but, in theory, conveys less specific information about a situation than non-global shifts would. When emotions depart from their usual levels by different amounts, the experience additionally relays information about the kind of situation (e.g., Smith & Ellsworth, 1985). For example, a larger increase in anger than in sadness might suggest a transgression rather than a loss, though both could be situations experienced as intensely unpleasant. The more the departures differ across emotions, the more specific the information the emotional experience provides. Momentary ED, then, captures the specificity of the information contained in a person's emotional experience at a given moment. So, if anger rises well above its usual level while sadness and anxiety stay near their usual levels, the moment is differentiated.

If all three rise by the same amount, the moment is undifferentiated, because the discrete emotions carry no information beyond a shared shift in negative affect. Two implications for the characterization of momentary ED follow. First, momentary ED is not the same as rating emotions differently at that moment. Instead, momentary ED concerns how emotions change relative to one another, with each judged against the person's usual level. Second, momentary ED is conceptually distinct from momentary affect intensity. In other words, how much emotions diverge from one another is different from how strongly the emotions are felt.

Informed by this theoretical conceptualization of ED, we derived several criteria for measuring momentary ED. First, the index should be higher when emotions depart from their usual levels by different amounts or in different directions and lower when they shift together such that two moments with the same overall affect but different patterns among emotions could receive different ED scores (conceptual alignment criterion). Second, when aggregating the index across occasions, the index should reproduce the person's ICC-based trait ED, reflecting that trait ED represents the distribution of a person's momentary ED states (converging-to-trait criterion). Third, the index should be computed on each discrete emotion rating's deviation from the individual's usual level of that emotion, so stable meanlevel differences between emotions do not count as differentiation (within-person-fluctuation criterion).

Fourth, the index should be separable from affect intensity (decoupled-from-affect criterion). A shift of all emotions by the same amount should ideally not change the index. When this kind of shift affects the momentary ED index's value, the index is confounding ED with affect intensity.

Earlier momentary indices (Tomko et al., 2015; Grossmann et al., 2016) are only weakly associated with the ICC and may capture different phenomena (Erbas et al., 2022). Therefore, they do not satisfy the converging-to-trait criterion. They also used raw emotion ratings, not deviation from the person's usual level, and so they also do not satisfy the within-person fluctuation criteria. Erbas et al. (2022) addressed both problems by expressing the ICC as a sum of contributions from individual occasions and treating each occasion's contribution as a momentary index. This sum-based index is computed on person-centered ratings and aggregates to an individual's ICC, satisfying the converging-totrait and within-person-fluctuation criteria. The methodological advancement has proved useful, and research utilizing the sum-based ED index has led to important advancements in clarifying how withinperson fluctuations are associated with momentary well-being (Erbas et al., 2022), social context (Springstein et al., 2024), and emotion regulation choice (Hohensee et al., 2025).

However, the sum-based index does not satisfy the conceptual alignment or the decoupled-fromaffect criterion. By construction, the sum-based index is a scaled square of how far the average of the discrete emotions at an occasion deviates from the person's mean (Erbas et al., 2022). It is a quadratic function of person-centered momentary affect. The sum-based index reflects only the shared component of the emotions at that moment, and the index is related to momentary affect in a U-shaped pattern by definition. As a result, two moments with the same momentary affect receive the same ED score regardless of whether the emotions moved together or separately. This issue also creates a dilemma when the sum-based index is an outcome variable. Controlling for momentary affect linearly misspecifies its Ushaped association with the index, whereas controlling for it quadratically removes most of the index's within-person variance and, within each person, all of it. A related problem arises when the index is a model predictor. When momentary affect and the sum-based index are specified as predictors together, the index's coefficients may reflect nonlinear effects of momentary affect rather than ED. The sum-based index is not well suited to studying the momentary associations between ED and other variables once momentary affect is taken into account, which is a modeling approach that is considered best practice (Dejonckheere et al., 2019).

If momentary ED reflects the specificity of the information contained in an individual's emotional experience, as we propose it does, then its validity should be evident in how that experience relates to the situation and the individual's own understanding of it. That is, in moments of higher ED, emotions should fit the eliciting situation more closely than in moments of lower ED. One way to define emotion-situation fit is to measure the degree to which an individual perceived their emotions to be appropriate for the features of a given situation (e.g., greater anger for a social slight versus greater sadness for a personal loss). This aligns with the feelings-as-information theory because differentiated departures from one's usual emotional levels, we argue, signal what kind of situation an individual is in, so emotions should be perceived as more closely related to that situation's features in moments of high ED. Moreover, in moments of higher ED, people should report greater emotional source clarity, defined as the extent to which people know the causes of their emotions (Boden & Berenbaum, 2011). If differentiated emotions provide more specific information about the eliciting situation, individuals should find it easier to identify what is producing their emotions when momentary ED is high compared to low.

Together, these predictions could test whether the momentary index captures the property that our definition specifies for momentary ED: emotional experience that is informative about the situation in which it arises.

In this study, we derived what we named divergence-based ED (Aim 1). When detailing the indices, we can only assess whether the indices meet three of the four criteria for measuring momentary ED based on their formulas: the conceptual alignment criterion (Aim 1a), the converging-to-trait criterion (Aim 1b), and the decoupled-from-affect criterion (Aim 1c). As the conceptual alignment criterion can only be inferred from their mathematical formula, we could only empirically evaluate both sum-based and divergence-based ED indices against the other three ED criteria (Aim 2) in two EMA samples. In Sample 1, college students reported their momentary emotions at the time of each EMA survey as well as their emotions in response to a recent event, allowing us to compare two concurrent reports with different referents. In Sample 2, community adults reported their emotions over the past hour, allowing us to test the generalizability of the new index in a different population. Specifically, we empirically verified whether both momentary ED indices aggregate to the ICC-based trait ED (Aim 2a; converging-to-trait criterion) and described their within-person variability (Aim 2b; within-personfluctuation criterion). We then examined how both ED indices relate to momentary affect and how much of their within-person variance remains after controlling for linear and quadratic affect (Aim 2c; decoupled-from-affect criterion). Based on their derivations, we expected quadratic control to remove most of the variance in the sum-based index but little of the variance in divergence-based ED.

In addition to examining the four criteria, we examined the concurrent validity of the sum-based ED and the divergence-based ED (Aim 3). We expected that, at the within-person level, higher ED for negative affect would be associated with a clearer perceived source of one's feelings and with greater fit between feelings and situations. This examination can only be performed in Sample 1 because in Sample 2, the time frame of the emotion items ("in the past hour") does not match that of perceived emotion sources ("in response to the event"). Finally, because both momentary ED and momentary affect are calculated from the same set of emotional ratings, we used simulation to assess artifactual ED-affect associations under varying patterns of emotional ratings to guide appropriate use of the index (Aim 4).

Measures Momentary Affect.

In each EMA survey, participants answered the item "At the time of the prompt, I felt [EMOTION] at the time of the beep" (Sample 1) and "I felt [EMOTION] during the past hour" (Sample 2). They were asked to indicate the extent to which they felt each of the following NA (Sample 1: bored, anxious, frustrated; Sample 2: sad, sluggish, worried, frustrated) and PA (Sample 1: content, happy, excited; Sample 2: content, calm, happy, enthusiastic) items. In both studies, items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (ranging from "1 = not at all,4 = moderately,7 = extremely"). Across both samples, each EMA survey presented emotion items in a random order. We averaged the NA and PA items, separately, at the survey level to reflect momentary NA and PA, respectively. In Sample 1, participants averaged M = 2.54 (SD = 0.68) for momentary NA and M = 3.15 (SD = 0.79) for momentary PA. In Sample 2, participants averaged M = 2.35 (SD = 0.79) for momentary NA and M = 4.32 (SD = 0.85) for momentary PA. Momentary NA showed less satisfactory within-person reliability in Sample 1 (ωwithin = 0.55) and less satisfactory within-person reliability in Sample 2 (ωwithin = 0.64). Momentary PA showed good within-person reliability in both Sample 1 (ωwithin = 0.77) and Sample 2 (ωwithin = 0.77). Both NA and PA showed great between-person reliability across both samples (ωbetween ranged from 0.83 to 0.91 across samples).

Event Recall. During each EMA survey, participants recalled an event that caused the largest emotional shift since the previous assessment (Sample 1: "over the last three hours"; Sample 2: "since the last survey"). Then they reported their event-related affect and beliefs about their event-related affect.

Event Affect (Sample 1).

Participants in Sample 1 reported their emotional experience in response to the recalled event by rating the same set of emotion items used for momentary affect (NA: bored, anxious, frustrated; PA: content, happy, excited). Items were rated on the same 7-point Likert scale Trait-level ED. Consistent with research (e.g., Erbas et al., 2014), we computed the averagemeasures ICC (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979) to assess consistency across EMA surveys among the NA and the PA, resulting in negative ED and positive ED, respectively, for each participant. Participants with negative ICC values were recoded as having a value of zero rather than excluded from analyses (Sample 1: eight for momentary NA and seven for momentary PA; 23 for event NA and six for event PA; Sample 2: eleven for momentary NA and one for momentary PA), because negative ICC values can be interpreted as representing low agreement between ratings (Giraudeau, 1996; Taylor, 2010). Like participants with low ICC values, these participants were thus considered to have high ED. ICC values were then transformed using Fisher's r-to-z transformation (Pond et al., 2012). Finally, because higher ICC values reflect greater similarity in ratings of different emotions across occasions (i.e., lower differentiation), we subtracted the transformed scores from one, so that higher scores reflect greater differentiation.

Momentary Sum-Based ED.

Following Erbas et al. (2022), we calculated negative and positive momentary ED separately using the NA and PA items, respectively. Within each valence, we first centered each emotion item around the participant's mean for that item. For each survey, we computed momentary non-differentiation as the squared sum of the centered emotion ratings divided by the participant's summed item variances across surveys; multiplying this value by -1 yielded the momentary ED score, such that larger (less negative) values indicated greater differentiation. Research shows that these theoretically derived momentary indices provide evidence of construct validity (Erbas et al., 2022).

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2019). Multilevel models were estimated using appropriate mixed-effects approaches to account for the nested structure of EMA data (momentary observations nested within individuals).

Variable centering. Before analyses, we person-mean-centered momentary NA and PA (Level 1) by subtracting each person's mean (across all prompts) from the value at that prompt. This allows us to focus on within-individual associations (Enders & Tofighi, 2007), which are the main aim of this investigation. We calculated person-mean affect intensity (Level 2) as each individual's mean NA and PA across the sampling period. Level 2 variables (mean affect and age) were grand-mean centered.

Data preparation.

To ensure reliable estimation of within-person variance and individual differences, data cleaning protocols were applied uniformly across both samples. Inclusion criteria required participants to have completed valid momentary assessments with complete data for both NA and PA simultaneously. Individuals with zero affective variance across the sampling period or those who completed fewer than two valid EMA surveys were excluded from the final analytic samples.

Data Simulation (Aim 4).

To test the effectiveness of the divergence-based ED index, we simulated EMA data in which true momentary ED was defined by a data-generating model. Since this momentary ED was not defined by any index, we could then examine how divergence-based ED performs when ratings are collected on bounded scales without assuming the index's own definition in the creation of the differentiated item ratings. At each occasion, each simulated person's latent emotion intensities combined a person-specific mean level, a fixed offset for each item (some emotions were rated as more intense than others), and within-person fluctuation. This within-person fluctuation consisted of a shared factor that moved all emotions together and item-specific factors that moved each emotion independently.

The item-specific share of that fluctuation served as our ground truth momentary differentiation, and it was generated independently of the shared factor such that true differentiation was independent of the direction of momentary affect. The item-specific share of fluctuation, then, is the item-specific share of within-person variance. In the null scenario, the divergence-based share of the fluctuation was fixed at 0.50 for every simulated person at every occasion. This way, any association between divergence-based ED and momentary affect had to be an artifact. In the alternative scenario, the divergence-based share of the fluctuation varied both between people and from moment to moment, which allowed us to examine how well the divergence-based ED index recovered the fluctuation. Latent values were rounded and censored to a 1-5, 1-7, or 1-9 scale to mimic the possible scales of participant EMA reports. All parameters were set relative to the given scale's range: within-person SD = 0.20 times the range (1.2 points on a 1-7 scale), between-person mean affect level SD = .05 times the range, and item-mean offsets spread over ±0.067 times the range ("close together") or ±0.20 times the range ("far apart").

We crossed scale range (1-5, 1-7, 1-9), number of items per affect index (2, 3, 4, 6, 10), person mean level (ranging from approximately 1% to 80% of rating at the floor or ceiling), item-mean spread (close, far), 14 different positions of the mean level affect, and scenario (null, alternative). This produced 840 conditions, each with 200 people with 60 occasions each. We replicated this eight times for the null scenario and five times for the alternative scenario for a total of 5,460 datasets. In each dataset, divergence-based ED was computed. The main outcome of interest was the within-person correlation between divergence-based ED and person-centered affect. Each person's correlation was computed and then averaged using Fisher's z. Under the null, this correlation is pure artifact. We used linear interpolation to find the percentage of ratings at the scale bound at which the artifact reached r = .10 and r = .30. Recovery was the average within-person correlation between divergence-based ED and the true item-specific share of fluctuation in the alternative scenario.

Aim 2b: Within-Person-Fluctuation Criterion.

To meet the within-person-fluctuation criterion, momentary ED indices should vary primarily across occasions within individuals rather than behaving as static between-person traits. Both the divergence-based ED and sum-based ED indices operated almost exclusively as within-person state variables (ICCs: 0.00 -0.08), indicating that over 99% of the variance occurred at the within-person level, confirming that both momentary ED capture dynamic state-level fluctuations rather than static individual differences.

Aim 2c: Decoupled-from-Affect Criterion.

To meet the decoupled-from-affect criterion, a momentary ED index should remain separable from overall affect intensity so that shifts in emotion levels or squared affect do not drive differentiation scores.

Across both samples, the divergence-based index captured distinct moment-to-moment variation across affective domains, showing moderate positive within-person correlations with linear PC NA (r = .34 to .36, p < .001) and weak or non-significant associations with linear PC PA (r = .01 to .15; Table 1, Tables S2-S4). In contrast, the sum-based index correlated negatively with linear PC affect across both NA sets and Sample 1 PA sets (r = -.19 to -.44, p < .001) but positively with past-hour PA in Sample 2 (r = .20, p < .001).

At the within-person level (Level-1; Table 1, Tables S2-S4), the sum-based index correlated strongly and negatively with (PC affect)² across all six item sets (r = -.82 to -.89, all ps < .001), reflecting an exact within-person relationship by definition (person-specific R² = 1.00). In pooled within-person regressions modeling ED as the outcome, linear and squared PC affect jointly accounted for 67% to 80% of the within-person variance in the sum-based index (R² = .67-.80, all ps < .001), compared with little to no variance explained in the divergence-based index (R² = .00-.13; (PC affect)² r = -.07 to .15). This confirms that divergence-based momentary ED captures distinct within-person variance rather than being driven by affect intensity. At the between-person level (Level-2), person-level averages of both ED indices were closely aligned (rs ≥ .99), indicating that this coupling with quadratic affect is specific to state-level within-person dynamics.

Aim 3: Concurrent Validity

Table 2 shows how using the divergence-based versus sum-based indices of ED led to diverging conclusions regarding concurrent validity. Specifically, when individuals reported a clearer perceived source of emotions and greater feelings-situation fit, their divergence-based ED was higher (e.g., perceived source β = 0.10, 95% CI [0.07, 0.12]; situational fit β = 0.04, 95% CI [0.02, 0.06] in Model 1). Conversely, the sum-based ED index was lower (e.g., perceived source β = -0.08, 95% CI [-0.10, -0.06]), reversing the substantive interpretation of what higher scores represent. For positive ED, associations with situational fit were largely uninformative or near zero (e.g., Model 1 PED fit to situation: β = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.00, 0.04], with CIs including zero).

To test the robustness of these findings, we examined parameter stability when controlling for PC affect (Model 2) and squared PC affect (Model 3). We do not inherently expect differentiation effects to be independent of affect intensity; rather, controlling for affect evaluates whether the predictive signal holds when accounting for affective levels. Parameter estimates for both indices remained stable from

Model 1 to Model 2 when controlling for linear affect (e.g., for NED perceived source, divergence-based ED was observed at Model 1 [β = 0.10, 95% CI [0.07, 0.12]] and Model 2 [β = 0.09, 95% CI [0.06, 0.12]], while the sum-based index was observed at Model 1 [β = -0.08, 95% CI [-0.10, -0.06]] and Model 2 [β = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.10, -0.05]]).

When introducing squared person-centered affect, findings showed that the sum-based index is strongly associated and largely shares variance with squared person-centered affect. Specifically, the sumbased ED index estimates in Model 2 (β = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.10, -0.05]) and the squared-affect estimate in Model 3 (β = 0.08, 95% CI [0.05, 0.10]), while similar in magnitude, were opposite in direction.

Furthermore, model comparisons demonstrated that adding squared affect yielded a negligible change in conditional R² between Model 2 and Model 3 for the sum-based index (∆R² ranging from -0.000 to -0.002), confirming that the sum-based index's predictive signal is mathematically redundant with squared person-centered affect.

Aim 4: Appropriate Use Guided by Simulation

When true differentiation is constant, divergence-based ED still becomes associated with momentary affect once a nontrivial share of ratings is at a scale bound. Our simulation (Table 3) indicates when artifactual ED-affect associations are negligible or not. With three to four items of a 1-7 scale, the artifact reaches r = .10 when about 9-11% of ratings are at the floor or ceiling and r = .30 at about 27-31%. Three factors move the artifact to lower thresholds. The first is that adding more items lowers the threshold for the bounding artifact, but it makes the index more precise. Within-person correlations between divergence-based ED and true momentary differentiation rose from 0.28 with three items to 0.53 with 10 items (1-7 scale, no floor effects). The second is that a larger range of response options (1-9 scale) means that a given percentage at the minimum reflects more compression of the underlying emotion (compared to 1-5 scale). The third is that when item means are close together, the threshold for a bounding artifact is lower. Moreover, this artifact is positive near the floor and negative near the ceiling. Therefore, divergence-based ED can be described as a state whose accumulation across occasions yields the ICC-based trait measure. Fourth, it retained most of its within-person variance after linear and quadratic control for momentary affect, and its associations with momentary outcomes were largely unchanged when we added a linear or quadratic affect term. Thus, it is decoupled from affect both mathematically and empirically (decoupled-from-affect criterion). Meeting the four criteria, divergence-based ED is shown to perform in accordance with the feelings-as-information perspective, supporting its construct validity. Specifically, the divergence-based ED showed positive associations with emotion source clarity and fit to situations, which held after accounting for momentary affect.

In another study using two clinical samples (Zhu et al., 2026), we found that individuals with major depressive disorder showed reduced divergence-based ED overall, especially when momentary affect was higher. In addition, the positive ED-affect associations were attenuated in people with major depressive disorder, consistent with theories. This additional evidence from clinical populations provided further evidence on the construct validity of divergence-based ED. Of note, however, is that the associations between emotion source and divergence-based ED in Sample 1 were small, and found only for NED. Our finding that PED scores were not associated with emotion source does not necessarily imply that the index performed in a theoretically inconsistent way. The feelings-as-information perspective is better suited to stress reactivity models, which heavily implicate NA. Further, PED has been less consistently linked with emotional experience and psychopathology (Thompson et al., 2021a).

Regardless, we provided preliminary evidence in favor of divergence-based ED's construct validity, and further research on the association between ED and the perceived source of emotion is an open question for later work.

We also evaluated the sum-based momentary ED index (Erbas et al., 2022) against the four criteria for ED. Our derivation of the index demonstrated that it is sensitive to common shifts among emotions but not how emotions depart from each other, thus not meeting the conceptual alignment criterion. The sum-based ED also aggregated exactly onto the ICC-based ED trait measure (converging-to-trait criterion) and showed substantial within-person fluctuations (within-person-fluctuation criterion).

However, once momentary squared affect is controlled, the sum-based ED shows little to no variance for analysis. This suggests that sum-based ED largely reflects the average intensity of different emotions and therefore does not meet the decoupled-from-affect criterion. Finally, inconsistent with how ED is theorized to facilitate regulation, when individuals reported a clearer perceived source of emotions and greater feelings-situation fit, their sum-based ED score was lower.

Beyond evaluating ED indices against four criteria and its concurrent validity, we performed simulations to test conditions where ED and affect became coupled in extreme cases. We caution that the bounds of rating scales can induce an association between divergence-based ED and momentary affect when ratings fall at the floor or ceiling. This means that researchers should be mindful of the distribution of emotional ratings in their data when using the divergence-based ED. We recommend that, when analyzing data, researchers report the proportion of emotional ratings falling at the scale endpoints (e.g., percentage of surveys at 1 or 7 on a 1-7 scale). Researchers are advised to calculate the proportions of scale endpoints in EMA emotion ratings within each individual and across the whole sample. Then, they could compare endpoint proportions against the thresholds presented in Table 3 before interpreting their results with divergence-based ED. If the spurious correlation between ED and affect (arising from the proportion of extreme emotional ratings) is below 0.10, the impact of this artifact can be considered negligible, allowing researchers to interpret ED effects with greater confidence. If the spurious correlation falls between 0.10 and 0.30, researchers should interpret their results with caution and be mindful about how their results may be influenced by the modest ED-affect spurious association. If the proportion of extreme emotional ratings results in a spurious correlation coefficient greater than 0.30, it becomes more difficult to disentangle the ED-affect associations from artifacts. In such cases, beyond controlling for momentary affect, researchers could also consider additional sensitivity analyses, for instance, by excluding observations where all items are rated at the scale floor, by excluding individuals whose emotional ratings were predominantly at scale endpoints, or by including the proportion of extreme ratings as an individual-level covariate. Finally, results from data simulation can inform study designs and power analysis when researchers pre-specify expected proportions of scale endpoints in EMA emotion ratings.

The momentary ED index and our studies have several limitations worth noting beyond what was described above. First, the current study compared divergence-based ED mainly with sum-based ED because we concentrated on how to derive a momentary ED index that mathematically links to ICC-based trait ED while retaining meaningful variability beyond momentary affect. Earlier momentary indices (Tomko et al., 2015; Grossmann et al., 2016) either cannot be computed from EMA emotion ratings or were shown to be weakly associated with the ICC (Erbas et al., 2022; failing to meet the converge-to-trait criterion) and were not evaluated here. Future research that comprehensively compares existing momentary ED indices is warranted. Second, each ED score (e.g., momentary negative ED) was assessed with three (Sample 1) or four (Sample 2) items, so each momentary value rests on few ratings and is sensitive to single items. Whether the index is more precise with larger item sets remains to be tested.

Third, the samples consisted of college students and community adults under 65, which limits the Finally, the level of mixedness in emotions at an EMA reflects both the nature of a situation (some situations trigger more complex emotions than others) and how much individuals differentiate emotions in response. The divergence-based ED index, like other momentary indices, does not separate these two sources (Ottenstein & Lischetzke, 2020). Future research should try to parse out the sources of pure ED from situational variances. In EMA, researchers could model features of events alongside divergence-based ED to compare divergence-based ED across recurring events of the same type. Other than EMA emotional ratings, researchers have developed ways to assess momentary ED with open-ended descriptions (e.g., Ottenstein & Lischetzke, 2020; Thompson et al., 2021b). Future research could consider using psycholinguistic methods or other tools to parse out the variance of ED ability from variances in situational factors. Nevertheless, divergence-based ED can be computed from the emotion items already included in most EMA studies and requires no additional measures. In addition, refining divergence-based ED with additional EMA items will likely make momentary ED no longer aggregate to ICC-based trait ED, warranting other types of person-level convergence evidence.

Conclusion

Despite growing interest in how ED unfolds in daily life, researchers have had few tools for studying momentary ED while accounting for how intensely people feel. In sum, this study derived a momentary ED (divergence-based ED) that matched the conceptualization of ED closely, converged to trait ED, and can be analyzed while controlling for momentary affect. These properties are evaluated in mathematical derivation and in two samples. Further, associations between emotion sources and momentary ED supported concurrent validity of divergence-based ED but not sum-based ED. Our simulation cautions against potential spurious correlation between divergence-based ED and affect due to proportions of emotion ratings at scale endpoints and offers recommendations for analysis and study design. By distinguishing how much emotions move apart from how much they move together, researchers can better identify when people differentiate their emotions in daily life and how momentary ED is implicated in emotion regulation.

Discussion Research has made important advances in bridging ED to the momentary level, but this work has raised questions about which properties of emotional experiences a momentary ED index should capture and how these properties relate to momentary affect. In this study, we drew on the ED conceptualization to derive four criteria for a momentary ED: sensitivity to how emotions depart from each other (conceptual alignment criterion), convergence with ICC-based trait ED (converging-to-trait criterion), computation on within-person fluctuations (within-person-fluctuation criterion), and separability from affect (decoupled-from-affect criterion). We introduced the divergence-based ED and evaluated it alongside the commonly used sum-based ED (Erbas et al., 2022) against these criteria from their formula and from empirical evidence. We also evaluated whether momentary ED indices performed in line with the feelings-as-information perspective (concurrent validity). Our derivation showed that divergencebased ED but not sum-based ED met the conceptual alignment criterion. Across the two EMA samples, the aggregation of both ED indices matched trait ED and showed substantial within-person fluctuation. Unlike sum-based ED, divergence-based ED retained most of its within-person variance after controlling for momentary affect. In Sample 1, higher divergence-based ED and lower sum-based ED were associated with greater perceived clarity regarding the source of emotions and with greater feelingssituation fit, consistent with the feelings-as-information perspective. Below, we discuss each index's performance, the conditions under which divergence-based ED can be used with confidence, and directions for future research. This research is motivated to evaluate whether existing and newly proposed ED indices meet the four identified ED criteria. First, an index qualifies as a measure of ED only if it can sensitively capture the extent to which different emotions deviate from one another. According to our derivation, at a given EMA survey, the total deviation of an individual's emotions (relative to an individual's typical level) can be decomposed into two components: one reflecting a common shift across all emotions (sum-based deviation) and another reflecting the degree to which each specific emotion deviates from this common shift (divergence-based deviation). Our indicator is based on this second component. Consequently, it is zero when all emotions shift by the same magnitude, and its value increases when emotions deviate from their baselines by varying magnitudes or in different directions, thus meeting the conceptual alignment criterion. Second, our divergence-based ED index aggregated exactly onto the ICC-based trait measure of ED (converging-to-trait criterion). Third, it varied almost entirely within persons (within-personfluctuation criterion).

generalizability of findings. Future research is needed to examine divergence-based ED in clinical samples as well as younger and older samples since ED changes after intervention (Van der Gucht et al., 2019) and across the lifespan (Nook et al., 2018). Person-level analyses of divergence-based ED show this feature as well. Future research needs to provide more validity evidence at the within-person level. Because aggregated divergence-based ED is perfectly rank-ordered with ICC-based trait ED, between-person associations are expected to reproduce trait-level findings in the literature and add little validity evidence to the momentary ED index herein proposed. This need for within-person validity evidence applies to both the divergence-based and the sum-based ED. More informative tests could investigate whether moments of higher divergence-based ED precede more effective or more situationally-appropriate emotion regulation or faster recovery of a person's affect toward usual levels at subsequent assessment moments.
