How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Who Agrees With Whom About What? A Tutorial on Multilevel Profile Analysis of Person

Who Agrees With Whom About What? A Tutorial on Multilevel Profile Analysis of Person

Judgments With the R Package profilemlm

Judgments of persons on many items at once are ubiquitous in psychological research and practice. Perceivers describe targets on trait adjectives, informants rate a patient's symptoms, teachers rate pupils on competencies. A recurring question about such judgments is how well they agree with some criterion, such as the target's self-ratings, the ratings of other informants, or behavioral measures. Take a simple study as an example. Several people who know Anna describe her on a list of trait adjectives, and Anna describes herself. Other groups do the same for Ben, Clara, and a dozen further targets. The descriptions of each target will resemble that target's self-description to some degree. The degree will vary with who is judging (some perceivers are better judges than others), with who is judged (some targets are easier to read than others), with the relationship between the two (some perceivers know some targets particularly well), and with what is being judged (some traits are easier to observe than others; Funder & Dobroth, 1987; John & Robins, 1993; for an overview, see Letzring et al., 2021).

Multilevel profile analysis (MPA) studies these sources of agreement together. For every perceiver-target pair, which we call a dyad, there is a vector of judgments across items, and for every target a corresponding vector of criterion values. Two such profiles about different targets are likely to correlate positively even for targets picked at random, simply because some items are endorsed more readily than others by everybody. Profile analysis therefore distinguishes agreement with this general pattern from agreement with what is specific to a target (Cronbach, 1955; Furr, 2008; for a review, see Borkenau & Leising, 2016). The Social Accuracy Model (SAM; Biesanz, 2010) casts this distinction as a multilevel regression. Each judgment profile is regressed on two components of the criterion profile, the average profile across targets and the target's deviation from it. The two agreement slopes are random coefficients that vary across perceivers, targets, and dyads, the sources of variance of Kenny's (1994) Social Relations Model (SRM). The SAM has become a standard tool of person perception research and has shown when, why, and for whom judgments agree more or less with a criterion (e.g., Human & Biesanz, 2011b; Rogers & Biesanz, 2015; Wessels et al., 2020).

The SAM as it is commonly applied treats items as fixed. This tutorial presents an MPA framework in which items are a random factor. Three things follow from this step. First, conclusions are meant to hold for person judgment in general, not for the set of items that happened to be used in a given study, and that inference requires treating items as a random factor (Judd et al., 2012). Second, the SAM assumes that distinctive agreement is the same for every item. With items as a random factor, this assumption becomes a variance that the model estimates.

Third, item properties can be tested as predictors of agreement within the same model.

Agreement depends on properties of the items such as their desirability and observability (John & Robins, 1993; Kenny & West, 2010; Wessels et al., 2025). Such effects have been tested in two ways. In the first, the item property moderates the agreement slopes of a SAM with fixed items (Human & Biesanz, 2011a). The test then rests on the number of judgments rather than on the number of items, and it assumes that items differ in agreement only through this property. In the second, agreement is estimated for each item, and the estimates are then related to item properties (Rau et al., 2026; Wessels et al., 2025). This second stage ignores the uncertainty of the first and no longer distinguishes perceivers, targets, and dyads. With items as a random factor, neither compromise is needed. The item property is tested in one step, the item is the unit of the test, and the model estimates how much items still differ in agreement beyond the property.

In sum, the framework keeps the SAM and adds items as a fourth source of variation next to perceivers, targets, and dyads. Researchers can then generalize beyond the items of their study, see how much agreement differs from item to item, and test which item properties account for these differences.

The framework is implemented in the R package profilemlm. Beyond items, the package brings together what applications of the SAM have so far set up one study at a time. These are predictors at the perceiver, target, and dyad level (e.g., Human & Biesanz, 2011b; Wessels et al., 2020), the decomposition of dyadic variables into perceiver, target, and relationship components (cf. Biesanz et al., 2011), a second profile next to the criterion, such as the perceiver's selfratings (Human & Biesanz, 2011b), and standardized effect sizes. We build the analysis in five steps on simulated data with known parameters, from the files a study produces to a full model with predictors at every level. We assume a reader who has fitted a mixed-effects model before but has no experience with profile analysis. The complete code and output of every step are in the package vignette, which is opened with vignette("mpa-tutorial", package = "profilemlm") and can be read alongside the text (see Data and Code Availability).

The Framework Terminology

This section fixes the terms used throughout. Judgments are made by perceivers about targets on items. A perceiver-target pair is a dyad, and the judgments of one dyad across the items form its judgment profile. For each target there is a criterion profile, for example the target's self-ratings on the same items. The judgments are the dependent variable. Each target has one criterion profile but several perceivers, so only the judgments vary between perceivers and dyads. The criterion profile is a predictor, and its slope is the quantity of interest, the agreement of the dyad with the criterion. The mean of a profile across items is its elevation (Cronbach, 1955).

We speak of a criterion and of agreement rather than of truth and accuracy. Whether a criterion can be taken to measure the truth about a target, and agreement with it to indicate accuracy, is a validity question that needs its own argument (Funder, 1995), and the argument differs for self-ratings, informant ratings, and behavioral measures. Where it can be made, the criterion becomes a truth variable in the sense of West and Kenny (2011) and agreement becomes accuracy. The analysis is unchanged.

Variables at the item, perceiver, target, or dyad level enter the model as predictors. A predictor can affect the judgments in two ways. A main effect shifts the judgments up or down the rating scale, whatever the criterion says. 1 For instance, informants recruited by the targets typically describe them more favorably than the targets describe themselves (Leising et al., 2010). Their judgments on desirable items are then higher, and their judgments on undesirable items lower, than the criterion predicts. This is a main effect of item desirability. A moderating effect changes how strongly the judgments follow the criterion profile. For instance, distinctive agreement may be higher in dyads in which the perceiver knows the target well, because such perceivers have seen more of what sets this target apart from others. In the model, desirability and acquaintance enter both as main effects on the judgments and as interactions with the two components of the criterion profile, its normative and its distinctive part (see The Models below). 1 In a model with interactions, a lower-order coefficient is the effect of its predictor when the variables it interacts with are zero, a conditional rather than a marginal effect. Because the two criterion components are centered, the main effect of a predictor is its effect for an item of average normativeness on which the target is not distinctive, which equals its average effect across the items of the study. We therefore keep the familiar term.

Only the interactions make them moderators of agreement. We say moderation when we speak of agreement and interaction when we speak of the model term.

We call variables measured for each perceiver-target pair, such as acquaintance or liking, dyadic variables. The relationship component of a dyadic variable is the part that is unique to the dyad once the perceiver's and the target's means of that variable have been removed (see Step 4 below).

The first subscript of γ, u, v, w, and z names the coefficient (0 for the intercept, 1 for normative, 2 for distinctive agreement), and the second subscript names the predictor k (0 for none, predictors are added below). γ₁₀ and γ₂₀ are the average normative and distinctive agreement, and the variances of the random deviations describe how agreement differs between units. Perceiver variance in b₂, Var(u₂₀), is individual differences in distinctive agreement. If the criterion is accepted as the truth, perceivers with high values are the more competent judges.

Target variance, Var(v₂₀), is differences in how judgable targets are, and dyad variance, Var(w₂₀), is differences in agreement between relationships. Items enter as a fourth, crossed random factor.

Every item is judged by every perceiver about every target, so item effects are crossed with (not nested in) the other three units. The item intercept z₀₀ is the extent to which an item is judged higher or lower, across all perceivers and targets, than the normative profile predicts. For instance, if perceivers describe targets in general as friendlier than the targets' average self-rating suggests, the item friendly has a positive intercept. The item slope z₂₀ is the extent to which distinctive agreement on an item is higher or lower than on the average item. Some items are simply judged with more agreement than others. Both are needed if conclusions are to generalize beyond the items used (see Judd et al., 2012, for an accessible introduction to stimuli as a random factor). The normative slope b₁ has no item deviation. Because Nᵢ takes one value per item, such a deviation could not be distinguished from the item intercept. Item properties can still moderate normative agreement as fixed effects (see Step 3 below). Equations 1 and 2 are Biesanz's (2010) SAM with items as a random factor. We call them the base model (Model 1 of the tutorial). Statistically, the base model is a cross-classified linear mixed-effects model (Baayen et al., 2008).

We call the approach multilevel profile analysis because the name places the model in the general framework of profile analysis (Borkenau & Leising, 2016; Furr, 2008 Furr, , 2009) ) and makes no claim about accuracy. It is unrelated to latent profile analysis, which sorts persons into classes. Profile analysis is often contrasted with trait-wise (variable-centered) analysis, in which agreement is a correlation across targets for each item (Back & Nestler, 2016; Borkenau & Leising, 2016). Given judgments on many items, the two rest on the same data and differ in the orientation of the analysis, that is, in whether judgments are compared across the items of one person or across the persons judged on one item (Kuper et al., 2025). For distinctive agreement, the two orientations converge. Given homogeneous item variances, the average distinctive agreement is the same in both orientations as the numbers of targets and items grow (Biesanz, 2010, pp. 870-871). MPA takes both orientations. The distinctive slope represents average agreement, its item random effects describe how agreement differs between items, and its perceiver, target, and dyad random effects describe how agreement differs between people and relationships. Borkenau and Leising (2016) recommend profile analysis for two aims that this tutorial shares, conclusions that generalize across items and predictors of agreement at the perceiver, target, and dyad level.

Substantive predictors such as item desirability or acquaintance extend the base model at the second level, and each extension is a new model. Predictors are numbered k = 1, 2, … in the order in which they appear in the model equations. In the simulated example, we use five: item desirability (DES, k = 1), its square (DES², k = 2, a measure of how evaluative an item is in either direction), the acquaintance of the dyad (KNOW, k = 3), perceiver training (TRAIN, k = 4), and target talkativeness (TALK, k = 5). Explaining Variance in Agreement. The random-effect variances describe how much the intercepts and the agreement slopes differ between perceivers, targets, dyads, and items. Predictors are added to explain these differences, and what a predictor explains is read by comparing the model with it to the model without it. A predictor can explain variance only at the levels at which it varies. A perceiver variable such as training can explain perceiver variance in normative and distinctive agreement, Var(u₁₀) and Var(u₂₀), but not target or dyad variance, and a target variable such as talkativeness can explain target variance, Var(v₁₀) and Var(v₂₀). A dyadic variable such as acquaintance can explain variance at all three person levels, because it has perceiver and target components of its own. Some perceivers know all their targets well, and some targets are well known to all their perceivers (see Step 4 below). Only its relationship component is confined to the dyad level. An item property can explain item variance in the intercepts, Var(z₀₀), as the normative profile does (see Step 1 below), and in distinctive agreement, Var(z₂₀). How much a predictor explains is expressed as the proportional reduction of a variance component when the predictor is added (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002), that is, the component in the model without the predictor minus the component in the model with it, divided by the former. mpa_compare() reports these reductions as prop_reduced for every component.

The reduction is a descriptive effect size and has no significance test of its own. Whether a predictor explains variance in agreement beyond chance is tested by the z test of its moderating effect, γ₁ₖ or γ₂ₖ, when the moderator was specified in advance, and for a set of moderators by a likelihood-ratio test of the two models. A comparison with the previous model shows what the newly added predictors explain, and a comparison with the base model in its final form (m_base in the code below) shows what all predictors explain together.

Designs, Data, and the Package

Three designs are common (Kenny, 1994; Table 2). In the one-with-many design, each perceiver judges one target and each target is judged by several perceivers. This is the typical informant study. In the half-block design, groups of perceivers judge groups of targets, and perceivers and targets are different people.

In the round-robin design, everyone in a group judges everyone else. The designs differ in what can be estimated. Dyad-level random effects can be separated from perceiver and target effects only when perceivers judge several targets and targets are judged by several perceivers, which excludes the one-with-many design, where each perceiver judges a single target. For the same reason, a dyadic variable such as acquaintance can be decomposed into perceiver, target, and relationship components (see Step 4 below) only in the two crossed designs. In a one-with-many design it is a perceiver variable. Separating dyad effects from residual error additionally requires several observations per dyad. In profile data these are the items.foot_0 The residual is therefore not measurement error in the psychometric sense. Besides error, it contains the item-specific part of a dyad's judgments. Round-robin data contain further dependencies that the models in this tutorial do not address (see Limitations and Outlook).

The package works from data as a study produces them. A perceiver survey exported from survey software typically has one row per perceiver with the questions about each target repeated in blocks ("loops"). A target survey has one row per target with the self-ratings. Item properties come from a separate norming study, in which the items are rated by human judges or by a large language model. mpa_data() reads these files as they are. It accepts three layouts of the judgments, and none of them has to be reshaped beforehand: one row per perceiver with target loops (format = "loop"), one row per dyad ("wide"), and one row per judgment ("long"). It then computes the normative and distinctive profiles, centers and, on request, rescales, detects at which level each variable varies, and detects the design. The criterion source is named by the user (in the example, the self-ratings are called self), and the output names follow (self.norm.c, self.dist). A criterion from another source, for instance the ratings of parents as the criterion for the judgments of teachers, is named accordingly and analyzed with the same models (for criteria formed from the perceivers' own judgments, see Limitations and Outlook).

Three decisions are made at this stage. First, the normative profile is computed leaveone-out (norm = "loo"). With the plain sample mean, each target's own criterion values enter the norm with weight 1/n, so that part of what is distinctive about the target would be counted as normative. With many targets the two options hardly differ. With few targets, or with norms computed within subgroups, leave-one-out is preferable. Alternatively, the normative profile can be taken from an external norm sample (norm = <data frame>), which fixes it independently of who was sampled. Second, the normative profile is centered at its mean across items (center_norm = "grand_mean"). The intercept γ₀₀ is then the expected judgment of an average item, and perceiver differences in intercepts, Var(u₀₀), reflect differences in elevation, a general tendency to use the upper or the lower end of the scale (with an agree-disagree format, acquiescence). What elevation means depends on how the items are keyed (see Extensions).

Third, predictors can be rescaled to -1…+1 with 0 at the theoretical scale midpoint (rescale).

All of them then share one unit, half the scale range, and 0 is a point with a meaning of its own rather than a sample mean. We rescale rather than standardize because the predictors are rating scales with a fixed range. 5 The common zero point matters once interactions enter the model, because each lower-order effect then refers to the point where the predictors it interacts with are zero. In Step 4 below, the distinctive-agreement slope γ₂₀ is the slope at the midpoints of the acquaintance and training scales. All agreement slopes are reported per criterion point.

mpa_formula() turns arguments into the model formula: which criterion source is used (criterion), which predictors have main effects (predictors), which moderating effects to include (interactions), which predictors receive random slopes in addition to their fixed effect (random_slopes), and which units get random effects (random_units). The last two arguments define the random part of the model. random_units names the units that get random effects, by default perceivers, targets, dyads, and items. The value "group" adds a random intercept for the groups of a half-block design. random_slopes names the predictors whose main effects vary across these units, in addition to the criterion components, which always do. Random slopes follow one rule. A predictor can have a random slope at a unit only if it varies within that unit.

Desirability, for example, varies within each perceiver (across items) and can have a perceiver slope, but it takes one value per item and cannot have an item slope. Likewise, a perceiver variable has no perceiver slope, and in a one-with-many design no variable has a dyad slope.

Printing the formula lists the slopes that were dropped for this reason.

mpa_fit() estimates the model and returns tables of fixed effects and variance components. By default it uses maximum likelihood with lme4 (Bates et al., 2015). With engine = "brms", the same formula is estimated by Bayesian sampling in Stan via brms (Bürkner, 2017), with the default priors of brms unless others are given (prior). 6 The tables then report posterior medians, posterior standard deviations, and credible intervals. mpa_compare() compares two models, mpa_effect_size() expresses main and moderating effects as standardized effect sizes, mpa_plots() draws the moderating effects, and mpa_simulate() generates data from the model.

The Example Data

Imagine a study of acquaintance in existing groups. Students from six seminar groups, each of size n = 8, describe the eight members of a partner group, with whom they have worked on a joint project for one term, on 30 trait adjectives. The members of the partner group are judged but do not judge in return. The rating scale runs from 1 to 101. We chose this fine-grained scale so that the simulated judgments are approximately continuous, as the linear mixed model assumes (for coarser scales, see Limitations and Outlook). The targets describe themselves on the same adjectives. For each target, a perceiver also rates how well they know this person (acquaintance, a dyadic variable). Perceivers report how much training in personality assessment they have had (training, a perceiver variable), and the targets' talkativeness is rated by the project supervisor (a target variable). The adjectives' desirability comes from a norming study.

We simulated such a study, a half-block design with 48 perceivers, 48 targets, and 30 items (Figure 1A). As the section Planning a Study shows, eight targets per perceiver is roughly the minimum at which perceiver differences in agreement can be estimated reliably, whereas dyad differences benefit from more items. We wrote the data to the three files such a study would produce. The file perceiver_survey.csv has one row per perceiver with the group ID (group) and training. Each of the eight target loops holds the target's ID, the acquaintance rating (know), and the 30 judgments. The file target_survey.csv holds the self-ratings and talkativeness, and items.csv holds the items' desirability (des). The four predictors are stored on a -1…+1 scale, so rescale is not needed here (the square of desirability is formed from these values).

The generating values were chosen to resemble published informant studies. Average normative agreement was set to .60 and distinctive agreement to .15 per criterion point, close to the .57 and .13 that Wessels et al. (2020) estimated for informants of varying acquaintance (for an overview of published values, see Letzring et al., 2021). The main effect of desirability was 6 judgment points per scale half, that is, judgments on a maximally desirable item were 6 points higher than on a neutral item with the same criterion value. The moderating effects on distinctive agreement were -.10 for desirability, .08 for acquaintance, and .08 for training. The first of these means that, per criterion point, judgments followed the distinctive profile by .15 points on a neutral item but by only .05 points on a maximally desirable item. Target talkativeness had a main effect of 3 points. Perceivers rated talkative targets higher on all adjectives, desirable and undesirable alike, as if, having more behavior to go on, they endorsed more of every description.

Talkativeness did not moderate agreement. Independently of the predictors, random effects were present for perceivers, targets, dyads, and items, among them item-level variation in distinctive agreement (SD = .04).

Step 0 asks how the judgment variance is distributed across perceivers, targets, dyads, and items before any predictor is entered. It is the reference for everything that follows, because what the criterion profile and the predictors explain is read as a reduction of these components.

Deciding on the Random-Effects Structure. The base model as fitted above, m1, is already the maximal model the design allows (Barr et al., 2013). A singular fit is not wrong, but it signals that the random structure is larger than the data support, and an oversized random structure costs power (Matuschek et al., 2017). We recommend a fixed sequence of decisions: 1.

Fit the maximal model.

2. If a variance is estimated as zero, remove that term (mpa_prune(m1) does this from the fit).

4. Carry the resulting structure through all later steps (random_structure), adding only the random slopes of newly introduced predictors and pruning again if a step becomes singular.

With Bayesian estimation (engine = "brms"), variances are not estimated as exactly zero, and one inspects the credible intervals of the random-effect SDs instead of pruning.

Summary, Extensions, and Limitations

Multilevel profile analysis lets researchers ask, in one model, how perceivers, targets, relationships, and items shape person judgments and their agreement with a criterion. The framework presented here keeps the logic of the Social Accuracy Model and treats items as a random factor. Because all judgments enter one analysis, item properties are tested as moderators within the model, with the uncertainty of the item-level estimates carried along, rather than in a second stage. Because items are a random factor, conclusions refer to typical items rather than to the items that happened to be used, provided the items were drawn from a broad item population (Leising et al., 2014; Leistner et al., 2025; Rau et al., 2025). The package makes the resulting models specifiable from a few arguments and brings together the analyses that SAM studies have set up one at a time, among them predictors at every level, the decomposition of dyadic variables, and standardized effect sizes. It constructs the normative and distinctive profiles, checks the identifiability of random slopes, and keeps the bookkeeping across steps, the parts of the analysis that are error-prone by hand.

The parameters have substantive interpretations that suggest which predictors to examine. In Funder's (1995) Realistic Accuracy Model, agreement requires that relevant information is available to the perceiver, detected, and correctly used. Acquaintance should raise distinctive agreement mainly through availability, because perceivers who know a target better have seen more of the target's behavior, and observable items because relevant behavior is more often available and detected. Liking should affect the main effect of desirability more than agreement, because it shifts the evaluative component of judgments rather than their substance (Leising et al., 2015).

Extensions. The package fits three kinds of models that go beyond the five steps. First, further item properties enter in the same way as desirability. Item keys are one such property, +1 for items that indicate a construct (for neuroticism, gets irritated easily), -1 for reversed indicators (remains calm in tense situations), and 0 for unrelated items (enjoys going to the opera). Their main effect is a construct-specific bias, and their moderating effect a constructspecific difference in agreement. The interaction of an item key with a perceiver variable would test, for instance, whether well-acquainted informants describe targets as more neurotic, as Leising et al. (2010) found with scale scores when liking was controlled. Keying also decides what the intercept means. When all items are keyed toward the desirable pole, the intercept mixes scale use with favorability, and the main effect of desirability is hard to separate from it.

When items are keyed in both directions, favorability cancels out of the intercept and is carried by the desirability term. The agreement slopes do not depend on keying, because reversing an item reverses judgment and criterion alike, so that b₁ and b₂ are unchanged. The spread of an item across targets is another property, which de Vries et al. (2016) found to predict self-other agreement across whole inventories. It is available as the item-level standard deviation of the criterion (self.sd).

Second, a model can contain more than one predictor profile (see the vignette predictor-profiles). Human and Biesanz (2011b) added the perceiver's own distinctive profile to the SAM. Its slope shows whether perceivers ascribe to targets what is distinctive about themselves. Because the normative profile and the target's distinctive profile are in the same model, this is assumed similarity beyond actual similarity (Kenny & Acitelli, 2001). It differs from the classical index, which compares the judgments with the perceiver's selfdescription without these controls (Cronbach, 1955). The same structure applies to profiles that are not judgments. Park and MacDonald (2019) regressed the self-reported profile of a person's current partner on the normative profile, the distinctive profile of the person's former partner, and the person's own distinctive profile. Current partners resembled former partners beyond the norm and beyond their resemblance to the person. In the package, a further profile is passed to mpa_data(), for instance with profiles = list(pself = "perceivers") for the perceivers' self-ratings, and named in mpa_formula() with profiles = "pself". Its distinctive component then receives a slope, random slopes, and moderators like the distinctive component of the criterion. Data such as Park and MacDonald's form unconnected pairs, for which the package keeps a single person-level unit. The vignette shows both cases on simulated data.

Third, perceivers and targets can have effects that are specific to an item. What the perceivers of a target share in their judgment of an item beyond the criterion profile is a target × item component. For instance, everyone who judges Anna may describe her as more punctual than she describes herself. Its counterpart is a perceiver × item component, what a perceiver's judgments of an item share across targets beyond the normative profile, the item-specific part of the perceiver effect (Rau et al., 2021; Wood et al., 2010). For instance, a perceiver may describe all targets as more ambitious than other perceivers do. The SAM has neither, because its perceiver and target effects are the same for all items. The package adds both as random effects for the cells (random_units = "target:item" or "perceiver:item"), which leaves Equation 1 unchanged. The target × item component is a random intercept and needs several perceivers per target. The perceiver × item component needs several targets per perceiver and also carries a slope for the distinctive profile, so that perceivers can differ in distinctive agreement item by item, Funder's (1995) judge × trait interaction. 12 The tutorial models omit both components, as the SAM does. Real data probably contain them, because informants share views of a target that the self-ratings do not capture (Wiedenroth et al., 2025) and perceiver effects are partly itemspecific. 13 Limitations and Outlook. The following points mark the limits of the models and of the package. Where an extension of the models would help, we name it. First, the choice of criterion remains the substantive limitation of every profile analysis. Self-ratings and informant ratings are themselves judgments, and their normative profiles are steeped in desirability, so that overall Second, the criterion profile can be decomposed further than into N and D. When the level of the criterion profile is itself of interest, as in research on self-enhancement, where the question is whether a target's self-view is more favorable overall than the criterion, D can be split into the target's mean deviation from the normative profile (the level) and the item-by-item deviations around that mean (the pattern), and each part then receives its own slope. The package does not yet make this split.

Third, the models are linear mixed models. Bayesian estimation with engine = "brms" would allow three further extensions that are not yet built into the package: ordinal or censored response distributions for bounded rating scales, distributional models in which the residual 12 Funder's (1995) trait × target interaction, palpability, is not a separate component. It is not separable from the target's distinctive profile, which already varies by item. 13 When the components are omitted, they are absorbed by the residual and by neighboring slope variances, the target × item component by the item-level variance of the distinctive slopes and the perceiver × item component by the perceiver-level variances of the slopes of item properties. Items or perceivers then appear to differ more than they do. The fixed effects and their standard errors are affected little (vignette, Section 9.2). variance depends on predictors or varies across units, 14 and nonlinear moderation estimated as smooth functions.

Fourth, round-robin designs contain dependencies the present models ignore. A person's perceiver and target effects may be correlated (generalized reciprocity, a limitation of the SAM noted by Biesanz, 2010), and the two directional judgments within a dyad share a relationship component (dyadic reciprocity). Both can be accommodated with multi-membership random effects and random effects for undirected dyads, an extension for brms rather than for lme4.

Fifth, the models require a criterion that is distinct from the judgments. Agreement with the ratings of another group of raters, such as parents as the criterion for teachers, is analyzed exactly as self-other agreement. Agreement among exchangeable informants, often called consensus (Kenny, 1994), is a different quantity. It is symmetric, and regressing one informant's judgments on the mean of the others would make the slope depend on how many others there are and would use every judgment twice, as criterion and as outcome. In the present framework, consensus appears as the variance of the target × item component described above. That component is a first step toward modeling consensus and its moderators, such as whether two perceivers share their liking of a target (Leising et al., 2013), which requires models of perceiver pairs beyond this tutorial.

Profile analyses of person judgments have so far been set up anew in every study, with different conventions for the criterion profile, the predictors, and the random structure. We hope that the framework and the package make such analyses comparable across studies and easy to use in empirical projects.
