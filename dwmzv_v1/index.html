Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Persecutory and grandiose thinking (PGT) is evident in mental disorder, religion, and politics. This paper offers an interdisciplinary explanation of PGT with a focus on common causes, mechanisms, and functions. First, psychopathological and ideological variants of PGT are introduced and compared. Theories of stress-induced psychological and social change are invoked next to explain shifts towards PGT in individuals and groups. These include a Status Threat/Reward Model of psychopathological PGT and a relative deprivation theory of political violence. Potential functions and dysfunctions of PGT are considered next, with a focus on how persecutory and grandiose thinking may orient individuals towards mitigating social threats and gaining social rewards, respectively. Conditions for collective PGT are then explored, including systemic stress and exposure to redemptive ideological narratives. Finally, we consider how religious and political institutions regulate PGT.

Persecutory and grandiose thinking (PGT) involves inferences about social threats (e.g., attacks, exploitations, conspiracies, humiliations, and injustices) and social rewards (e.g., recognition, special purposes, roles, or missions) -that is, threats and rewards mediated by other agents. This paper argues that similar causes and mechanisms contribute to psychopathological and ideological (e.g., religious and/or political) PGT.

We proceed with a selective review of PGT within mental disorders (see Part 1), followed by a broad conceptual comparison to ideological PGT (see Part 2). Three case vignettes of lone-actor PGT (Elliot Rodger), messianic PGT (Hong Xiuquan), and political PGT (Adolf Hitler) are presented next, highlighting some common features of PGT as well as developmental factors (e.g., personal distress or humiliation) that might contribute to its emergence (see Part 3).

Our analysis then moves from description towards explanation of the biopsychosocial causes and mechanisms of PGT (see Part 4). Here we introduce a Status Threat/Reward Model of psychopathological PGT, relate it to a compatible relative deprivation theory of political violence, and present a broader stress-based model of socioemotional belief updating that highlights how stress-facilitated perception of extra agency (PEA) can contribute to PGT. Part 1. Persecutory and Grandiose Thinking (PGT) in Mental Disorders

PGT is exemplified by persecutory and/or grandiose delusions, which are a defining feature of psychotic disorders (e.g. schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder) and mood disorders with psychotic features (e.g., bipolar disorder I, depression with psychotic features). The International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (World Health Organization, 2022) defines a delusion as:

A belief that is demonstrably untrue or not shared by others, usually based on incorrect inference about external reality. The belief is firmly held with conviction and is not, or is only briefly, susceptible to modification by experience or evidence that contradicts it. The belief is not ordinarily accepted by other members or the person's culture or subculture (i.e., it is not an article of religious faith). MB26.0 Two of the most common types of delusions are persecutory delusions and grandiose delusions.

The ICD-11 defines a persecutory delusion as "a delusion in which the central theme is that one (or someone to whom one is close) is being attacked, mocked, harassed, cheated, conspired against, or persecuted" (MB26.07) and a grandiose delusion as "a delusion of inflated worth, power, knowledge, identity, or special relationship to a deity or famous person" (MB26.05).

Persecutory and grandiose delusions are -by definition -clear expressions of PGT, but other features of psychosis can also reinforce or reflect PGT. These include visual hallucinations of threatening faces, shadow figures, and other entities (Toh et al., 2024), or auditory verbal hallucinations of persecutory (e.g., mocking, threatening) or supportive (e.g., guiding, inspiring) voices. See Ng et al. (2025) for a perspective on voice hearing as a social barometer and an examination of how cultural factors influence voice content in psychosis (see also Luhrmann et al., 2015).

Senses of felt presence (Barnby et al., 2023) and feelings of being watched or followed are also common in psychosis and can contribute to specific persecutory beliefs (e.g., being spied on, harassed, or targeted) or specific grandiose beliefs (e.g., having a special mission, special abilities, or secret knowledge). Delusions of reference and control may likewise contribute to PGT by increasing perception of extra/hidden messages, signs, or social forces relevant to the self. In general, an increase in the social salience of stimuli increases capacity for PGT, and the examples given above suggest that perception and cognition in psychosis fixate on socially salient objects and themes (Bell et al., 2021; Crespi & Badcock, 2008; Pappa et al., 2026; see also critique from Corlett et al., 2025 and reply by Razavi et al., 2025).

Emotions in psychosis are also clearly associated with moral cognition (Chiappelli et al., 2025), social objects, and social evaluations. So, for example, paranoia often involves a feeling of anxiety or fear related to inferred social threats and/or negative valuations of the self by others. Although not as well recognized, a counterpart to paranoia called pronoia has been posited to summarize the grandiose tendency to believe that one's actions are well received and praised by others (Goldner, 1982).

It appears then that the psychotic subject often feels the self to be an object (e.g., target) of observation, evaluation, judgment, and manipulation by other minds, whether real or imagined. The inferred negative valuations of the self by others and the inferred harmful intentions towards the self by others may feel justified (e.g., as punishment), for example, in delusions of guilt (e.g., in psychotic depression), but they are often felt to be unjust in paranoid psychosis, reflecting a sense that one is being unjustly evaluated or targeted, or in other words, persecuted.

Given that social judgements, evaluations, and intentions are also relevant to morality, ideology, religion, and politics, it is not surprising that religious and political themes are common in psychosis (Başaran et al., 2025; Collin et al., 2023). Religious delusions, for example, may reflect a culturally influenced subtype of PGT. A person may believe that they are being persecuted by demons, Djinn, or Satan (Brouwer & Raison, 2026; Pietkiewicz et al., 2021), or alternatively, that they are in special contact with benevolent spirits, angels, or God (Isham et al., 2021).

In a recent phenomenological investigation of grandiose delusions by Isham et al. (2021), eleven out of fifteen participants believed that they were God, were related to God, were the messiah, or had special spiritual abilities. Two others believed that they worked for government or security agencies, and one mentioned an unspecified special mission (Isham et al., 2021).

When looking at the content of PGT in psychosis then, it is apparent that religious and political themes are common.

Delusions or Ideological PGT

Two general criteria for determining whether PGT is delusional or ideological are whether (1) the beliefs conform to cultural norms and (2) are accompanied by significant distress or dysfunction (see also Bentall, 2018; Cunningham, 2018 for more comprehensive distinctions).

However, what is considered normal or dysfunctional varies between cultures and subcultures.

For example, magical, spiritual, and religious explanations of psychotic symptoms are common in countries with less access to western-style healthcare (Ingall et al., 2025).

Historically, individuals whose beliefs diverged from prevailing religious or political norms have sometimes been condemned as heretics or, in other contexts, diagnosed with mental disorders (Van Voren, 2010). Colloquially calling ideological opponents 'schizophrenic', 'mad', 'psychotic', 'delusional', 'crazy', or 'narcissistic' is also common and can be observed readily in the media (e.g., see Duckworth et al., 2003).

Distinctions between delusional and ideological thinking are further complicated by the existence of subcultures that reify paranoia and persecutory thinking, such as online communities that share beliefs about issues such as gangstalking (Lustig et al., 2021) or involuntary celibacy (Ciocca et al., 2022; Sparks et al., 2022). Taken together, these considerations indicate that clear boundaries between psychopathological, religious, and political forms of PGT cannot be drawn, and that similar causes and mechanisms might contribute to diverse forms of PGT. This view is consistent with spectrum perspectives on psychopathology (Van Os & Reininghaus, 2016), with persecutory and grandiose delusions representing extreme and dysfunctional manifestations of otherwise potentially benign or even adaptive psychological processes (Bell et al., 2021; Hagen, 2008; Raihani & Bell, 2019). The DSM-5 states that "Paranoid personality disorder is a pattern of distrust and suspiciousness such that others' motives are interpreted as malevolent" and that "Narcissistic personality disorder is a pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration, and lack of empathy" (American Psychiatric Association, 2013, p. 645). However, patterns of distrust and grandiosity apply to many individuals, so the DSM-5 includes the criterion that a personality disorder must be "An enduring pattern of inner experience and behavior that deviates markedly from the expectations of an individual's culture" (American Psychiatric Association, 2013, p. 646).

PGT in Personality Disorders

Variability of PGT in Psychopathology

Although PGT is common to psychotic, bipolar mood, and certain personality disorders, it presents in a variety of forms that differ both between and within diagnostic categories. Two dimensions of variation are particularly relevant for subsequent comparisons to religious and political PGT; namely, PGT can appear more or less persecutory versus grandiose, and more or less context-specific versus connected (see clarification below).

Persecutory Versus Grandiose

Persecutory thinking is more common than grandiose thinking in psychopathology and often presents in the absence of grandiosity. Conversely, pathological grandiosity is typically accompanied by some degree of persecutory thinking. For example, preliminary research suggests that a majority of individuals with persecutory delusions do not experience grandiose delusions, but that a majority of individuals with grandiose delusions do at some point experience persecutory delusions (Garety et al., 2013).

Likewise, while there is little correlation between vulnerable (e.g., insecure, rejection sensitive) narcissism and grandiose narcissism at a subclinical level, individuals with high levels of grandiose narcissism are also more likely to display vulnerable narcissism (Jauk et al., 2017), which is linked to paranoia and persecutory (e.g., conspiracy) thinking (Cichocka et al., 2022).

These observations suggest an asymmetry: grandiosity is more often accompanied by persecutory thinking than vice versa in mental disorder. There are various potential explanations for these observations, but one is that functional correlates of grandiosity (e.g., elevated mood, confidence, self-esteem) are not typically considered grandiose if they appear justified to others. It is possible, therefore, that individuals with an inflated sense of self-worth or purpose are especially susceptible to persecutory thinking when they perceive that others do not value them as highly as they desire or expect. This perceived discrepancy between expected and inferred social evaluations may generate feelings of unjust treatment, potentially explaining why grandiosity is positively associated with persecutory thinking whereas high self-esteem is not (Collett et al., 2016; see also below). Since there is no consensus term that captures the possible functional correlates of grandiose-only thinking, we will simply refer to it here and below as elevated/expansive-only thinking.

Context-Specific Versus Connected PGT

PGT also varies to the extent that it is context-specific: whether perceived social threats and opportunities appear disconnected and limited to specific persons, specific places, and specific times, or alternatively, highly connected and generalized to the largest possible coalitions, all places, and all times.

As an example of context-specific PGT, some narcissists may be self-aggrandizing opportunists but appear to lack a moral or ideological framework. These individuals might view social threats and opportunities as context-specific, malleable, and exploitable. As an example of connected PGT, some individuals with persecutory and grandiose delusions may view many threats as connected to some nefarious force or enemy alliance, or many social opportunities and rewards as connected to a common cause or group objective (Bell et al., 2021).

An example of non-clinical connected PGT is conspirituality (Walker et al., 2023; Ward & Voas, 2011), a construct that reflects the common co-occurrence of conspiracy and new-age spiritual beliefs in the general population. More connected forms of PGT, in contrast to context-specific PGT, are often accompanied by a high degree of moral concern and/or ideological fixation (Kristinsdottir et al., 2025). This tentative distinction between context-specific and connected PGT, however, does not necessarily imply that they are subserved by entirely distinct causes and mechanisms. To the contrary, it appears that in many cases context-specific PGT precedes connected PGT; that is, a sensitivity to personal pain, social loss and threats, and/or personal pleasures and social rewards, often precedes the progressive expansion and elaboration of a more connected persecutory and/or grandiose worldview.

Part 2. PGT in Religion and Politics

Scholars have used terms like popular delusion (Mackay, 1841 (Mackay, /2019) ) and collective narcissism (De Zavala et al., 2009) to highlight features of PGT that are common to certain collectively shared and value-laden belief systems (or ideologies). However, by simply using the PGT construct, we hope to avoid overemphasis on abnormality, falsity, selfishness, or entitlement implied by extension of the specific labels of delusion or narcissism.

Expressions of PGT can be normative and motivate self-sacrificial behavior in the name of some greater cause. Persecutory thinking, according to our broad conceptualization, can reflect real persecution and facilitate self-sacrificial resistance to an enemy coalition. Therefore, it is worth highlighting other variables, such as degree of connectedness and ratio of persecutory versus grandiose thinking, that are relevant to comparisons of pathological and ideological PGT.

Ideological PGT is Connected and Shared

As a general rule, ideological variants of PGT, whether religious or political or both, reflect some degree of connected PGT. Note that this variation (context-specific versus connected PGT) is meant to capture how people think and not necessarily how they act. Political or religious opportunists can be deceptive and speak or act in ways that signal (deceptively) a greater degree of connected PGT than context-specific PGT. A politician might, for example, publicly invoke a conspiracy theory that they privately doubt is true for some short-term, selfserving political aim. Also, as mentioned above, connected PGT does not necessarily reflect an absence of context-specific PGT. An individual can simultaneously be motivated by a larger worldview and by context-specific self-interests.

Participation in political or religious communities can facilitate shifts from contextspecific to connected PGT. That is, if PGT reflects a general sensitization to potential social threats or rewards (see below), then increased participation in political or religious institutions or movements, to the extent that they value and reward ideological and group commitments, might create more alignment between context-specific and connected (e.g., group) interests. Indeed, one plausible function of religious and political institutions is to align perceptions of social threats and rewards between individuals. See Snow et al. (1986) for a relevant discussion of frame alignment.

Intense connected PGT is often deemed pathological, abnormal or deviant, except in cases where it reflects the ideas of a group, in which case words like ideologue, radical, extremist, fanatic, and zealot are more commonly applied. Boundary cases are those in which connected PGT is either minimally shared with others (e.g., in isolated, small, or loosely-knit groups) or also accompanied by context-specific PGT that appears pathological (e.g., delusional). An example of the latter would be an individual who believes in a popular conspiracy narrative but also believes, in a way not shared by others, that specific agents (e.g., family members) are persecuting them.

Ideological PGT is Persecutory and Grandiose

The overlap between psychopathological, religious, and political PGT is greatest when both persecutory and grandiose thinking are pronounced. In the absence of persecutory thinking, elevated and expansive utopian, progressive, spiritual or religious thought lacks the emphasis on opposition and social threats that are common to adversarial politics and distressing psychopathology. When religious or progressive worldviews do reflect a high degree of persecutory thinking, such as is evident in historical messianic or witchcraft narratives or certain radical or revolutionary ideologies, they also often reflect (explicitly or implicitly) adversarial or paranoid sociopolitical beliefs. Conversely, persecutory thinking in the absence of grandiose or elevated/expansive thinking is common in psychopathology and in some social or ideological critiques, but is less characteristic of fully developed religious or political ideologies that envision some social aspiration, objective, or solution. One factor that differentiates Nazism, for example, from simple anti-Jewish paranoia or conspiracy thinking, is its grandiose political vision, as conveyed by Adolf Hitler in the following passage from Mein Kampf (My Struggle): every attempt to combat a WELTANSCHAUUNG [worldview] by means of force will turn out futile in the end if the struggle fails to take the form of an offensive for the establishment of an entirely new spiritual order of things. (Hitler, 1925 (Hitler, /1939, p. 149) , p. 149)

Part 3. Three Case Vignettes-Elliot Rodger, Hong Xiuquan, and Adolf Hitler

Having introduced PGT and its variations at an abstract level, it is worth providing a few specific real-world examples. The following case vignettes of Elliot Rodger, Hong Xiuquan, and Adolf Hitler overrepresent the violent potential of PGT. However, by doing so, they also highlight one important behavioral correlate and possible consequence of PGT at individual and collective levels. These three case vignettes were also chosen because details about the personal development of the protagonist are known, and because the PGT of the protagonist, revealed in words and deeds, displays some blend of psychopathological, religious, and/or political content.

In the following cases, personal failures, humiliations, or unmet ambitions precede the manifestation of connected and/or ideological PGT. Highlighting this common developmental trajectory prepares us for an examination of the psychosocial causes and mechanisms underlying PGT in the section that follows.

Elliot Rodger: Lone-Actor PGT

Before the lone-actor Elliot Rodger killed six and wounded fourteen in Isla Vista, California, in 2014, he wrote a lengthy autobiographical manifesto entitled My Twisted World:

The Story of Elliot Rodger (Rodger, 2014). Rodger's manifesto follows the trajectory of a young man who became fixated on sex, ashamed of his sexual status, and then resentful and hateful towards women.

As the manifesto comes to an end, Rodger describes a desperate attempt to win the lottery and thereby gain wealth and appeal to women. When this plan fails, Rodger's narrative becomes noticeably more delusional and ideological. Themes of persecution, grandiosity, and religious/political world-transformation abound in the last pages of his writing: I had been rejected, insulted, humiliated, cast out, bullied, starved, tortured, and ridiculed for far too long. . . . Women's rejection of me is a declaration of war, and if it's war they want, then war they shall have. . . . I am superior to them all. I am Elliot Rodger . . . Magnificent, glorious, supreme, eminent . . . Divine! . . . On the Day of Retribution, I will truly be a powerful god, punishing everyone I deem to be impure and depraved. . . . there must exist a new and powerful type of government, under the control of one divine ruler, such as myself . . . [and an] army of fanatically loyal troops. . . The first strike against women will be to quarantine all of them in concentration camps. . .. (Rodger, 2014, pp. 131, 135-136) Debating the extent to which Rodger was delusional, narcissistic, or simply radicalized by a fringe set of ultra-misogynistic ideas floating around the internet serves little purpose for the current analysis, other than to reveal how difficult it can be to distinguish between these categories and relevant processes. This difficulty is reflected in research findings, based on different methodologies and databases, that suggest very different rates of psychopathology among mass shooters (see Glick et al., 2021 versus Peterson et al., 2022). See also Lambe et al. (2018 Lambe et al. ( , 2024) ) for analysis of violence in relation to psychosis and narcissism.

While the alleged persecutors (women) and victims/saviors (men, allies, Rodger) of Rodger's narrative may be somewhat unique, the general structure of the manifesto reflects connected PGT at the time of writing (at least by the end of the narrative) -a commonality it shares with many other lone-actor manifestos. Connected PGT is often coupled with a personal power motive (context-specific PGT) that appears to drive many cases of lone-actor violence (see Schibler et al., 2025).

Hong Xiuquan: Messianic PGT

In 1837, Hong Xiuquan (born Hong Huoxiu) failed the Chinese Imperial Examination for a third time, precipitating a mental breakdown. During this breakdown, Hong had a vision of a father figure, who bequeathed him a sword, and bid him to rid China of "demon-devils" (see Spence, 1996). A few years passed and after failing a fourth examination attempt, Hong realized with the help of a few religious pamphlets what his prior vision meant. He was the brother of Jesus Christ, sent by God to liberate China from the demonic Qing dynasty.

Hong gathered a small community of God worshippers, and when the authorities arrested some of their members, they launched the Taiping rebellion. Their aspirations to create a heavenly kingdom of equality are alluded to in this jingle translated by Spence:

Those with millions owe us their money, Those who are half poor -half rich can till their fields.

Those with ambitions but no cash should go with us: Broke or hungry, Heaven will keep you well. (Spence, 1996, p. 161) The Taiping rebellion succeeded in establishing the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom in Southeastern China, which operated as an independent state until 1864. However, Hong's erratic leadership, paranoid purges, increasing social withdrawal, and grandiose sense of entitlement vis-à-vis foreign powers contributed to the rebellion's defeat in 1864. An estimated 20 million people died as a result of the Taiping rebellion.

The case of Hong and the Taiping rebellion is again unique in some ways, but parallels with other messianic and millenarian movements also exist (see Cohn, 2011; Landes, 2011). Hitler explains the development of his ideology in the autobiographical manifesto Mein Kampf (My Struggle). However, the fact that Hitler began writing Mein Kampf (Hitler, 1925 (Hitler, /1939) ) while imprisoned in 1924, many years after he suffered as a young man in Vienna (see chapter 2), served in World War 1 (see chapter 5) and entered politics (see chapter 8), raises questions about whether Mein Kampf accurately reflects the development of Hitler's ideology or rather serves as a piece of Nazi propaganda.

One reason to believe that Mein Kampf contains genuine autobiographical details is that Hitler recounts humiliating events from his years of suffering in Vienna (see Chapter 2). These include a second failed attempt to get into the Vienna Academy of Fine Arts and his living in a hostel and men's home with few close friends. See also Kershaw (1999, pp. 29-69).

The timing of Hitler's suffering is noteworthy because it does not fit neatly with the idea that childhood adversity increases the risk of adult psychopathology and violence. Rather, Hitler, like Rodger and Hong, had a relatively normal childhood but then suffered socially -in part from unmet hopes/ambitions -in adolescence and young adulthood, a potentially sensitive period for the development of social emotions, non-familial relationships, morality, and identity (Blakemore & Mills, 2014).

Returning to the question of whether Mein Kampf also functioned as propaganda, the narrative likely contains some invented elements that Hitler retroactively inserted into his life story (see Kershaw, 1999, pp. 29-69). However, many of these textual additions are not fabricated events so much as descriptions of thoughts, explanations of behavior, and interpretations of events that connect disparate experiences and themes into a coherent and motivating ideological narrative.

These retrospective psycho-explanatory additions serve two plausible functions. First, they appear to justify Hitler's own failings and disappointments (e.g., as learning experiences) and thus appear to serve a self-propaganda or self-motivating function in addition to other ideological purposes. Second, they serve as an important basis for Hitler's anti-Jewish Nazi ideology.

The Jewish threat in Mein Kampf is largely conveyed via appeals to unobservable thought processes and hidden conspiracies. This reflects something striking about Nazism as a case of popular rather than fringe political PGT: the extent to which the inferred Jewish threat did not map onto reality. Consider one of the few passages in Mein Kampf that purports to describe Jewish behavior and notice how the threat is conveyed via appeals to unobservable thought processes.

Their [Jews] blatant behaviour in their relations with other people shows how clearly they envisage their day of triumph in the near future. The black-haired Jewish youth lies in wait for hours on end, satanically glaring at and spying on the unsuspicious girl whom he plans to seduce, adulterating her blood and removing her from the bosom of her own people. (Hitler, 1925 (Hitler, /1939, pp. 269-270) , pp. 269-270) Describing the satanic glare of a Jewish youth is not simply a rhetorical device disconnected from perception and imagination. It also reflects and/or generates a perception or imagination of others as imbued with extra (e.g., malevolent) qualities. This raises the possibility that individuals susceptible to connected PGT are not only exposed to certain types of ideology that reflect connected PGT but are also psychologically predisposed to perceive extra agency, motivations, and intentions behind actions and events (see below).

Part 4. Common Biopsychosocial Causes and Mechanisms of PGT

There is a popular tendency to view mental disorder as a product of brain dysfunction and genetic predisposition and to view ideology as the product of sociocultural environment. This dichotomy may be stronger in the popular imagination than it is in reality. We suspect that the biological and sociocultural factors that contribute to PGT at an individual level also contribute to PGT at a collective level. This view assumes as a prerequisite that the PGT observed in various mental disorders is also caused in part by similar factors and processes.

Common Causes of PGT in Mental Disorder: A Status Threat/Reward Model

Biological, psychological, and social/environmental factors all likely contribute to PGT in mental disorders. Given the infeasibility of reviewing and synthesizing all relevant literature, we prioritize developing one explanatory causal framework. This framework builds on a recent explanation of how schizophrenia and bipolar I disorder are biologically related but phenomenologically different (Brouwer et al., 2026). Brouwer et al. (2026) suggest that common features of schizophrenia, such as persecutory ideation, social withdrawal, and feelings of passivity or loss of control, reflect low status and social threat during development (Selten & Cantor-Graae, 2005; Selten & Ormel, 2023), whereas certain features of classic mania, such as elevated mood, expansive thinking, hypersociality, and feelings of dominance, reflect mixed or high status and social opportunities during development.

They define status as "an individual's value in the minds of others" and this definition is adopted here. Status can therefore be distinguished from power or influence, although high status generally confers greater power or influence, and greater power or influence can in turn confer higher status (Anderson, 2025).

Common biological predispositions to schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, such as increased dopamine synthesis capacity (Jauhar et al., 2017; Jauhar et al., 2025) and release in response to social stress or reward (Ashok et al., 2017; Mizrahi et al., 2012), could facilitate pathological social learning that orients either around social threats, social rewards, or both.

The developmental conditions most conducive to a combination of persecutory and grandiose thinking in predisposed individuals, according to a Status Threat/Reward Model, would therefore include fluctuating experiences of low and high status, as well as social threats and rewards, during development (Table 1). We should highlight that a mixture of low and high status, or a fluctuating experience of low and high status, is not equivalent to neutral or average status. Average status, in contrast to low or high status, may be associated with a relative absence of anticipations of social threats and rewards if, for example, individuals of average status are less socially salient and less relevant to the goals of others (Foulsham et al., 2010; Lassetter et al., 2021; Neel et al., 2023).

The idea here is that status, defined as "an individual's value in the minds of others", influences others' attention, intentions and behaviors towards the individual, which in turn, shapes the individual's inferences about others' minds, whether or not these inferences are accurate.

Recent losses and gains in status may also exert a disproportionate influence on current inferences about social threats and rewards and on related persecutory and/or grandiose thinking.

This suggestion fits well with the case vignettes presented above, with evidence of increased mania following recent success or achievement (Johnson et al., 2000), and with evidence of increased paranoia associated with social rejection (Bagrowska et al., 2026; Stewart et al., 2017).

A loss/gain perspective could also potentially help explain why paranoia sometimes follows grandiose or elevated states in the context of clinical mania (Carlson & Goodwin, 1973) or dopamine-enhancing drug use. The idea here is that an unexpected plateauing at, or fall from, an elevated or grandiose state can give way to paranoia or persecutory thinking akin to experiencing losses from a more neutral state.

In addition to recency, drastic, sudden, or unexpected changes in status, affective state, or related brain (e.g., dopaminergic) activity may increase paranoia or its positive counterpart pronoia (Goldner, 1982) more so than mild, gradual, and expected changes. This idea is generally compatible with prediction error models of learning (Schultz, 2016), predictive coding accounts of psychosis (Harding et al., 2024), and empirical evidence that drastic, sudden or unexpected changes in cognitive-emotional state are often accompanied by perception of extra (inferred, imagined, or hallucinated) agency (PEA) in psychedelic, spiritual, and psychotic experiences (Brouwer et al., 2024; see also below).

Beyond psychosis, sensitivity to social threats/rewards and attention to status in relation to others are also implicated in narcissism (Grapsas et al., 2020) and could help explain the apparent link between vulnerable narcissism and persecutory ideation (Cichocka et al., 2022) and grandiose narcissism and grandiose thinking, respectively. While less is known about the biological and social-environmental risk factors that contribute to narcissism, as opposed to more severe mental illnesses like schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, it is reasonable to speculate that PGT in personality disorders and severe mental illnesses shares some etiological basis (Nagel et al., 2023).

The Status Threat/Reward Model of PGT introduced above requires further development and validation but is relatively compatible with "paranoia as defense" models of persecutory delusions (Murphy et al., 2018). A key difference is that the current model shifts emphasis from self-esteem to status, defined again as "an individual's value in the minds of others." This allows for anticipations of social threats to arise from inferences about others' evaluations of the self without requiring threats to self-esteem or negative self-evaluations as intermediate mechanisms.

It also accommodates mood-congruent grandiose thinking (Knowles et al., 2011) that may not be "defensive" and is therefore applicable to a broad range of persecutory and/or grandiose thinking.

It follows that individuals can respond in various ways to inferences about their status, for example, by accepting, questioning, or rejecting the legitimacy of others' supposed evaluations of them. The three case vignettes provided above suggest that mismatches between desired status and perceived status are particularly conducive to PGT and potentially related violence.

Relative Deprivation and Ideological PGT

The importance of status and status changes in driving PGT fits well with some social psychological and sociological theories of political violence. For example, in his book Why Men Rebel, Ted Gurr (1970) posits that relative deprivation (RD), defined in his work as the difference between value aspirations and value capabilities, drives political violence. According to Gurr, value aspirations are what an individual or group thinks that they deserve, are entitled to, or could get: the money, relationships, sense of community that they think it is possible to have.

Value capabilities are what they have now. The larger the difference between what an individual or group thinks it can or should have, and what they have now, the more likely they are to be frustrated and lash out at the source of their frustration.

Formulated as such, relative deprivation (RD) reflects a calculation, implying some degree of conscious comparison between self or ingroup and others, which is consistent with the use of the construct by various scholars. For example, Power et al. (2020) write that "The theory of relative deprivation states that when an individual or group compares themselves to other salient individuals or groups and in this comparison find themselves lacking, discriminated against, or disadvantaged, this leads to feelings of angry frustration".

Smith et al. (2012) likewise emphasize the necessity of conscious comparison and define

RD in three steps. "First, there must be comparisons made by an individual. If one does not compare, there can be no RD. Second, there must be a cognitive appraisal that leads the individual to perceive that the individual or his/her ingroup is at a disadvantage . . . Third, the perceived disadvantage must be viewed as unfair."

These views imply that RD is a deliberate and reflective process of conscious appraisal, and indeed such appraisal could contribute to feelings of unjust persecution or grandiose ideas about how to solve injustices. However, it is also likely that the mind/brain evaluates threats and rewards, and losses and gains in status, and facilitates PGT, in ways that are partially obscure to self-consciousness.

For example, one can feel ostracized or paranoid or entitled in a social setting without deliberately comparing oneself to others. Or take the example of Hong Xiuquan, whose visionary call to fight against "demon-devils" in China arose somewhat spontaneously in the context of mental distress. In the case of Elliot Rodger, we might wonder to what extent feelings of humiliation arising from interactions (or lack thereof) with women were either the cause or the consequence of more deliberate social comparisons.

This issue of the importance of deliberate comparison, a capacity that most individuals possess, raises another question that RD theories have yet to fully resolve, which is why certain individuals come to perceive their status vis-a-vis others as unjust while others do not. Here persecutory and grandiose thinking, and the potential psychological mechanisms that contribute to PGT, may have some explanatory value.

Stress, Perception of Extra Agency (PEA), and PGT

The Status Threat/Reward Model of PGT and relative deprivation theory of political violence mentioned above fit reasonably well with the general idea that stressors can contribute to PGT via bottom-up psychobiological and cognitive processes (Brouwer & Carhart-Harris, 2021). Here we examine one potential mediating mechanism in this pathway.

Brouwer et al. (2024) suggest that stress, uncertainty, and arousal increase perception of extra agency and the ascription of extra capacities and intentions onto people (self and others) and events (present and historical). Brouwer and colleagues (2024) state that . . . Perception of extra agency refers both to the perceptual or visionary experience of supernatural agents (e.g., God, angels, demons, ghosts) as well as the feeling that other people, objects, and events may possess extra human-like capacities and intentions.

Suspecting others of conspiring or colluding, feeling that others can read or influence one's thoughts, perceiving objects or events as potential "signs," or feeling that spiritual forces are intervening in or guiding events, therefore, also fall under the umbrella of PEA

[Perception of Extra Agency]

The construct of PEA is derived from phenomenological analysis of psychedelic (Brouwer et al., 2025a), spiritual (Brouwer et al., 2025b), and psychotic experiences (Brouwer et al., 2024), and it is invoked as a mechanism to explain socioemotional belief updating under conditions of stress, arousal, or uncertainty, or when psychedelics engage parts of the same underlying stress circuitry in the brain (Brouwer et al., 2024; see also Brouwer & Carhart-Harris, 2021; Carhart-Harris & Nutt, 2017). A related construct of "hyperactive agency detection" has been invoked to explain aspects of religion (Guthrie, 1993) and conspiracy thinking (Van Der Tempel & Alcock, 2015).

The definition of PEA provided above can be slightly expanded to include the perceptual or imaginative experience of other people (e.g., conspirators, spies, secret allies, cabals), and the belief that non-spiritual forces (e.g., racial, material/economic) are also guiding events towards some intended outcome. In regard to the perception of hidden non-spiritual forces, it is the linking of these forces (e.g., biological or economic) to specific types of consciousness (e.g., racial or class consciousness), intentions (e.g., exploitation, deception), teleological purposes, and value judgements that tentatively justifies a label of PEA. It is worth noting that PEA may or may not contribute to PGT, and if it does, this PGT could be more or less idiosyncratic or culturally normative, respectively. When PEA is ascribed to real-world actors or influential hidden forces in a way that contributes to the formation or intensification of idiosyncratic beliefs in imminent threats and rewards, this in turn contributes to more stress and uncertainty, and a perpetuation of the stress-PEA-belief pathway in a way that facilitates symptoms of psychosis (e.g., hallucinations, and persecutory and grandiose delusions).

Culturally normative PGT reflects shared beliefs in imminent threats and rewards and could also reinforce this pathway, albeit generally to a lesser extent than idiosyncratic PGT.

Alternatively, PEA can contribute to feelings of relief and acceptance and the adoption or entrenchment of shared beliefs that temporarily diminish this causal process. An example would be an experience of a benevolent God that reassures an individual and reinforces mainstream religious beliefs (e.g., reward in heaven) and authority structures (e.g., the power of the clergy).

The feelings of relief and belief alignment that result from an experience of this type could actually make it less likely that individuals ascribe harmful intent onto real-world others. Brouwer et al. (2024) suggest that religious traditions regulate the stress-PEA-belief pathway in order to promote shared beliefs and suggest that political institutions and ideologies could function similarly.

Perception of extra agency is a relevant construct for investigating the psychological mechanisms that drive the formation of connected PGT, because it follows that the more negative (e.g., malevolent, degenerate) qualities one perceives, imagines, or ascribes to the other or outgroup, and the more positive (e.g., righteous, powerful) qualities one perceives, imagines or ascribes to the self or ingroup, the more capacity there is for perceiving antagonism and/or injustice in the relations between these groups, and for believing that one's self and allies are being persecuted, and in turn for believing that one's self and allies have the imperative and/or capacity to resist or destroy the enemy coalition.

Note that the construct of PEA implies a relationship between affect-laden and social hallucinations, imaginations, and perceptions (Rasmussen, 2024). The extent to which this relationship is justified is bracketed for future consideration but has implications for a comparison of pathological and ideological PGT. Another relevant question is the extent to which social stressors or stressors relevant to the social self, versus stressors more broadly, drive increases in PEA. The logic behind this question may appear tautological -stressors that increase the potential for social threats increase PEA, which in turn facilitates belief-updating about social threats/rewards -but it is not, because preceding stressors (e.g., social isolation, injury, illness, death of a family member or ally) need not be consciously appraised as socially threatening to increase PEA, and subsequent updating of beliefs about social threats is often general rather than context-specific. For example, delusions and extreme overvalued beliefs (Kristinsdottir et al., 2025; Rahman, 2018) often reflect generalizations about the intentions of other agents across contexts. Intense emotional states (of fear, shame, anger, or pride) related to status (value in the minds of others), and that often accompany PEA, likely facilitate such social generalizations.

Alongside the Status Threat/Reward Model and relative deprivation theory described above, the stress-PEA-belief pathway outlined here overlaps with predictive coding perspectives on psychosis (Sterzer et al., 2018), conspiracy beliefs (Leclercq et al., 2024), and ideology (Wheeler et al., 2020; McGovern & Otten, 2024), but places greater emphasis on socioemotional factors, in addition to uncertainty, in shaping relevant belief-updating processes. We believe these perspectives are broadly compatible and point towards common mechanisms underlying PGT. Having outlined the causes and mechanisms that contribute to PGT, we turn next to the potential functions and dysfunctions of these processes at the individual level.

Part 5. Individual-Level Functions and Dysfunctions of PGT

This section will attempt to explain why, from a functional perspective, we observe that persecutory and/or grandiose thinking is amplified in certain contexts (see also Table 1).

Functionality here refers to whether PGT is sensitive to functionally relevant inputs (e.g., social conditions), generates appropriate outputs (e.g., behaviors), and contributes to consequences that are beneficial to the individual and/or group.

In general, low status, loss of status, and increased social threat increase the likelihood of persecutory thinking. If an individual's life experiences and social context do not also contribute to expectations that status gains or social rewards are likely, then the mind will focus on avoiding further status losses and potential threats (see also Keltner et al., 2003). Thus, in high potential threat and low potential reward contexts, some degree of persecutory-only thinking might be functional. Common behavioral outputs of persecutory-only thinking include social withdrawal and avoidance (see also Freeman et al., 2007; Lambe et al., 2025).

In general, high status, gains of status, or increased potential for social rewards will increase elevated/expansive thinking. If an individual's life experiences and social context do not also contribute to expectations that status losses and social threats are likely, then the mind will focus on obtaining further status gains and social rewards (see also Keltner et al., 2003). Thus, in high potential reward and low potential threat contexts some degree of elevated/expansive-only thinking might be functional. Common behavioral outputs of elevated/expansive-only thinking include social approach and engagement (see also Eisenhut et al., 2024).

In general, fluctuating or mixed experiences of low and high status, and increased potential for social threats and rewards, are implicated in PGT. If a person's life experiences and social context contribute to expectations that both losses and gains of status, and social threats and rewards, are likely, then the mind will focus both on avoiding further status losses and potential threats, as well as on obtaining further status gains and social rewards. Common behavioral outputs of PGT variously include social withdrawal and avoidance, social approach and engagement, and/or aggression/violence (see also Lambe et al., 2018).

While a certain degree of persecutory-only thinking or PGT can be functional in certain social contexts, persistent amplification of these types of thought could also facilitate interpersonal dysfunction. Amplification of elevated/expansive-only thinking may be less obviously detrimental to the individual who experiences it, but could increase interpersonal dysfunction by promoting behavior that is perceived by others to be grandiose, selfish, entitled, or exploitative, and by facilitating social inequalities or social hierarchical changes that are perceived by others to be oppressive, unfair, or threatening. Emergent antagonism towards highstatus individuals in turn represents a social threat, which helps explain why objectively wealthy or powerful individuals can nevertheless experience persecutory thinking when challenged, mocked, threatened, or disliked by others. According to our adopted definition of status as "an individual's value in the minds of others", many public and powerful individuals would be defined as having mixed status as a result of their being both positively and negatively evaluated by many others.

Beyond contributing to interpersonal dysfunction in non-clinical contexts, recurrent and intense persecutory and grandiose thinking also likely contributes, in a causal manner, to some cases of severe psychopathology. In cases where PGT itself is a causal driver of psychopathology, it need not represent a dysfunctional response to an abnormal input, such as neurological damage, but may instead reflect a mental response that becomes increasingly engaged by, sensitized to, and eventually partially decoupled from functionally relevant inputs.

Just as recurrent activation of immune or pain systems might contribute to autoimmune disease or chronic pain, with these systems becoming increasingly engaged despite the absence of functionally relevant inputs (e.g. pathogens or injury), excessive activation of systems involved in detecting social threats and opportunities may contribute to increasingly maladaptive, inflexible, or reality-incongruent (e.g., delusional) forms of persecutory and grandiose thinking.

Returning to a discussion of potentially functional PGT, there are additional group-level social dynamics that make PGT, and specifically connected PGT, more likely at an individual level. For example, unstable social contexts with a high degree of social contention (e.g., groups competing for influence and power) provide additional possibilities for status losses and gains which are often conditional upon aligning oneself with a coalition. It might be functional in such contexts to perceive social threats and rewards as contingent upon coalitional dynamics.

Coalitional PGT could facilitate black-and-white distinctions between allies and enemies that reduce uncertainty about how one should act and how others are likely to act, while also signaling one's own affiliations and intentions to others. Common behavioral outputs of connected coalitional PGT include selective social withdrawal from enemy coalitions, selective cooperation with allied coalitions, and selective aggression towards enemy coalitions. These dynamics invite consideration of the conditions and correlates relevant to collective PGT.

Part 6. Collective PGT: Conditions and Correlates

Innate (e.g., genetic) or relatively stable (e.g., trait-level) psychobiological predispositions to PGT are unlikely to substantially explain differences in levels of collective PGT between groups, with possible exceptions being cult-like or fringe-political organizations where self-selection into the group may relate to these predispositions. Rather, it is in contexts of recurrent systemic stress or increased exposure to widely shared value-laden ideas conducive to PGT, where a large segment of a population is likely to experience a state of heightened PGT.

Common types of systemic stress that could plausibly increase persecutory thinking within a population include: active war or defeats in war; oppression (recognized as such); economic challenges or uncertainties; famine; and large-scale destabilization or change of social networks, structures, and worldviews. The difficulty in distinguishing the influence of these systemic stressors from the influence of ideological factors in the development of collective PGT is that ideological factors influence the subjective appraisal of stressors.

So, for example, defeats in war, social hierarchical inequalities, economic hardship, and periodic famine will be felt to be more aversive, and perceived as more unjust or amenable to change, if there is an alternative idea of what things ought to be like. This alternative idea may refer to an idyllic or better past (see Midlarsky, 2005 below on reference points), an idyllic vision of the future, or to contemporary ideals and alternatives observed in other places (e.g., see Beck, 2011 on revolutionary waves).

One can apply these general points to explain the rise of collective PGT in Nazi Germany. Objective German losses in World War I (e.g., casualties and territorial losses) and subsequent economic deprivations are often cited as reasons for the rise of the Nazi Party (De Juan et al., 2024). But subjectively, at least for Hitler, the losses were arguably more profound.

In Mein Kampf, Hitler writes . ..

"THE DEPTH of a fall is always measured by the difference between the level of the original position from which a body has fallen and that in which it is now found. The same holds good for Nations and States... For only the profound decline or collapse of that which was capable of reaching extraordinary heights can make a striking impression on the eye of the beholder…the REICH had fallen from a height which can hardly be imagined in these days of misery and humiliation. The Second REICH was founded in circumstances of such dazzling splendour that the whole nation had become entranced and exalted by it." (Hitler, 1925 (Hitler, /1939, p. 161) , p. 161) The quote above from Mein Kampf exemplifies what Midlarsky (2005) refers to as a reference point. Midlarsky writes . . . "Losses and gains are defined for each person relative to some reference point unique to that individual . . . Accordingly, after suffering losses political leaders have a tendency not to renormalize their reference point but instead to gamble in the hope of eliminating those losses and returning to the reference point, even at the risk of suffering a larger loss." (Midlarsky, 2005, p. 104) Midlarsky (2005) argues that Hitler's reference point was Germany in 1914, but if we read Hitler's descriptions of pre-war Germany in Mein Kampf it becomes apparent that his reference point was a highly romantic, somewhat imagined, vision of Germany -what Germany was aspiring to be, would have become, or could still become in the absence of the cultural degeneration which was insidious (in Hitler's mind) even prior to World War I.

In addition to these visions of a glorious past and future, Hitler and like-minded Germans could also refer to contemporary alternative political ideals exemplified, for example, in the fascist regime of Mussolini in Italy. The rise of Nazi ideology in Germany was thus aided both by the occurrence of recent systemic stressors and losses (e.g., defeat in war, economic collapse) and the perception that these stressors and losses could be ameliorated or reversed by the adoption of Nazi ideology. A similar temporal pattern can be observed in the post-Civil War United States, where relative losses in status and power for white southerners preceded the emergence and spread of new white-supremacist movements. In both cases there is a time lag between the onset of collective losses and subsequent intensification or spread of collective PGT, which fits theoretically with the idea that chronic stress primes psychological transformation in response to acute stress at the individual level (Brouwer & Carhart-Harris, 2021).

While systemic stress and collective losses appear to play a role in many cases of collective PGT, ideological exposure can also be a primary proximal driver. Exposure to social justice-related ideas and revelations contained within the Bible, for example, has contributed to countless individuals and communities coming to view their social position as intolerable, unjust, and/or changeable (e.g., during the Protestant Reformation). Exposure to nationalistic, democratic, socialist, or communist ideas has played a similar role in the last three centuries.

That ideas can drive PGT is evident at the individual level as well. Contemporary phenomena of online radicalization (Binder & Kenyon, 2022) and so-called "AI psychosis" or "ChatGPT psychosis" (Morrin et al., 2025; Østergaard, 2023) suggest that iterative reinforcement of persecutory and grandiose ideas can play a prominent role in the development of PGT.

However, while ideas can potentially facilitate PGT in the absence of new stressors, it is still worth emphasizing that collective PGT often spreads in contexts of high systemic stress. For example, collective PGT is often reflected in apocalyptic and millenarian narratives that become popular during or after historical periods of perceived collective threat, loss, or disruption.

Textual examples include the Book of Daniel and the Book of Revelation, which are linked historically to the persecution of Jews and early Christians, respectively. Relevant movements include the American Indian Ghost Dance and Xhosa cattle-killing movement in present-day South Africa, and the Taiping Rebellion and Yellow Turban Rebellion in China, which are linked historically to various systemic stressors including disruptive foreign influence and dysfunctional political systems.

Apocalyptic and millenarian narratives, and redemptive ideological narratives more broadly, reflect a collective problem-solving process akin to the problem-solving nature of some psychotic and religious experiences at the individual level (Boisen, 1936; Brouwer & Carhart-Harris, 2021). This problem-solving structure has both diagnostic and prognostic elements: reflecting inferences about current status and social threats/rewards while also providing a value hierarchy that specifies how status and social losses/gains could, should, and will change across time.

For example, certain redemptive ideologies privilege the divine, race/ethnicity, or production as a primary organizing (but hitherto hidden or underrecognized) source of value.

These ideologies legitimate the elevation of a group (e.g., the righteous, Aryans, or the proletariat) with a special relationship to the organizing source of value (e.g., the divine, race, or production) by portraying it as uniquely capable of maximizing the realization of other collective values (e.g., prosperity, security, stability, or belonging) and therefore as deserving of the highest status and power in the future. Conversely, prevailing and rival ideologies organized around alternative value hierarchies are diagnosed as illegitimate, with their associated proponents held responsible for recent and current crises, as well as the maintenance or promotion of unjustified social hierarchies (see Table 2 below). While the prognostic, and often grandiose, element may be privileged in some of these narratives, the diagnostic, and often persecutory, element may precede it mechanistically. This suggestion fits our analysis of the expressions and causes of PGT at an individual level where grandiosity, as opposed to elevated/expansive thinking, often co-occurs with persecutory thinking. Conversely, persecutory thinking often occurs in the absence of grandiosity at an individual level, as do shared social critiques or diagnoses of existing societal problems at a collective level, although these critiques do not always involve an attribution of persecutory intent.

While collective problem-solving may be the object of redemptive ideological narratives, the mechanisms driving engagement with these narratives can be understood in terms of individual-level reinforcement: whether engagement with an ideology produces pleasant or unpleasant feelings, positive or negative affect, or feelings of control/power/dominance or their opposites -consequences related but not exclusive to anticipations of social gains or losses.

PGT can variously be a cause, structural component, or consequence of engagement with redemptive ideologies; in the latter case, these ideologies are often disruptive and invite opposition, which then further contributes to PGT.

Institutional Regulation of PGT

The potential for individual and group dysfunction resulting from increased persecutory and/or grandiose thinking is made evident, indirectly, by observing that religious and political institutions attempt to regulate PGT by variously 1) promoting beliefs that make sense of the world, including but not limited to beliefs about shared human interests, aspirations, and sufferings 2) promoting prosocial emotions and ideals such as love, trust, equality, modesty, freedom, justice, dignity, social harmony, service, charity, and camaraderie 3) upholding laws that diminish or regulate the capacity for status inequality or change 4) offering services that protect a population from basic stressors and scarcity 5) coercively suppressing non-normative PGT, which could manifest in a variety of forms depending on context 6) aligning or redirecting PGT to facilitate ingroup cohesion and promote common objectives; sometimes in opposition to an outgroup. An example of redirected PGT is the tendency for political regimes to blame outgroups for internal problems and failures. In general, when religious and political institutions fail to negatively regulate (diminish) or redirect PGT in a stressed and/or confused population, interpersonal dysfunction, social schisms, protest, revolt, and the embrace or emergence of alternative ideologies become more likely.

Conclusion

Here we have made a preliminary attempt to show that persecutory and grandiose thinking in mental disorder, religion, and politics can be subserved by similar causes (stressors, status changes, ideological influences) and psychological mechanisms (perception of extra agency, anticipations of threat and reward). The scope of the current project, however, precludes a comprehensive analysis, and the proposed framework will require further theoretical development and empirical evaluation.

Many relevant ideas and theoretical frameworks, as well as countless pieces of evidence and case examples, were not included due to space constraints and a desire to make basic claims accessible to a wide readership. Nevertheless, attending to the expressions and mechanisms of PGT at an individual level, for example, in relation to psychopathology, can provide scholars with a large body of phenomenological, psychological, biological, and epidemiological evidence that might profitably inform explanations of certain types of collective belief and behavior.

For example, stress-based accounts of social behavior, such as the relative deprivation theory (Gurr, 1970), have been criticized for lacking sufficient explanation and evidence of mediating psychological mechanisms (Goldstone, 1980; Tilly, 1975). More specifically, Gurr's (1970) reference to frustration as a mediating mechanism between relative deprivation and political violence is not specific enough to differentiate between political violence and violence writ large. Additional mechanisms and constructs, such as PEA and connected PGT, need to be invoked to explain why individuals are primed to target individuals or groups based on group identity rather than, or in addition to, personal relationship dynamics or convenience.

A fuller account could relate the present framework to other relevant social psychological or sociological constructs and theories regarding, for example, power (Keltner et al., 2003), shared grievances (Simmons, 2014), identity fusion (Swann et al., 2012), cognitive liberation (McAdam, 1982), framing processes (Benford & Snow, 2000), and radicalization (Doosje et al., 2016). Like relative deprivation theory, each of these concepts is potentially relatable to psychopathological and ideological PGT.

Future work might also investigate how symbolic thinking, cognitive biases, thought disorganization, and relevant psychobiological processes contribute to the generalization of values and anticipated social threats and rewards across contexts-a process presumably implicated in connected PGT. For example, both serotonergic and dopaminergic systems are known to encode information about values, threats, and rewards (Harkin et al., 2025; Schultz, 2010) and may therefore contribute to both psychopathological and ideological PGT. Future work could also investigate how religious and political institutions regulate the expression, reinforcement, and consequences of PGT.
