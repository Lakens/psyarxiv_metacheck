Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Many people want to change their personality in some way and personality traits have been found to predict various important aspects of life, including well-being, health, relationship quality, and work success. As a result, volitional personality interventions designed to elicit personality trait change in desired directions have recently gained momentum in research and public discourse.

Although the initial evidence on the effectiveness of these interventions is promising, concerns regarding their benefits, risks, and societal implications remain underexplored. In this review, we discuss 10 concerns regarding methodological, ethical, and practical limitations of volitional personality change interventions. Drawing on research across different areas of psychology, we provide a balanced discussion of each concern and outline directions for future research and practical applications of these interventions. Volitional personality change interventions have the potential to improve people's life and increase satisfaction with the self. However, realizing this potential requires sustained research efforts, thoughtful study design, and careful implementation of these interventions to maximize benefits and mitigate concerns.

Ten Concerns About Volitional Personality-Change Interventions-and How to Address Them

Personality traits predict outcomes across various important life domains, such as relationship quality, work-related success, mental health, and mortality (Beck & Jackson, 2022; Bleidorn et al., 2019; Roberts et al., 2007; Soto, 2019; Wright & Jackson, 2023). The effect sizes of these personality-outcome associations are on par with those of other commonly studied predictors, such as cognitive abilities or socioeconomic status (Roberts et al., 2007). Thus, it is perhaps not surprising that many people report a desire to change some aspects of their personality, such as becoming more extraverted, more self-compassioned, more emotionally stable, or more industrious (Baranski et al., 2021; Miller et al., 2019). Due to these far-reaching consequences of personality traits and people's motivation to invest time and effort to change their personality traits, volitional personality change interventions have recently gained increased attention as tools to improve people's life and increase health and well-being in the general public (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Bleidorn et al., 2019; Küchler et al., 2025; Stieger et al., 2021; Wagner et al., 2025).

Emerging research suggests that volitional personality change interventions can elicit desired trait change (Haehner et al., 2024; Stieger et al., 2021; Wright, Haehner, et al., 2025), supporting their theoretical and practical relevance. However, potential drawbacks of these interventions and limitations of studies examining them have largely been neglected in the existing literature. In this review, we first provide a brief overview of the current state of research on volitional personality change interventions. Then, we discuss 10 concerns regarding these interventions belonging to three broader domains: uncertain benefits, intervention risks, and societal implications. For each domain, we derive directions for future research and discuss recommendations for the practical application of volitional personality change interventions. In doing so, we clarify key concerns about these interventions and outline ways to improve their benefit-risk ratio.

Personality Trait Change and Volitional Personality Change Interventions

Personality traits describe relatively stable individual differences in thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that generalize across situations and contexts (Allport, 1961). While different conceptualizations of personality traits have been proposed, recent approaches converge on the idea that traits represent an intertwined, dynamic system of affect, behavior, and cognition that may reinforce and stabilize each other depending on social-cognitive mechanisms such as emotion regulation, motivation, and situation interpretation (Baumert et al., 2017; Cramer et al., 2012; Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015; Kuper et al., 2025). Accordingly, personality traits are not just aggregates of momentary experiences or self-perceptions, but enduring systems of interconnected processes.

Although personality traits are relatively stable, a large body of research has shown that personality traits remain malleable across the entire lifespan (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Haehner, Hopwood, et al., 2025; Wright, Krämer, et al., 2025). This evidence has sparked growing interest in whether personality traits can be changed through targeted interventions. Initial evidence supporting this idea stems from clinical studies. Integrating the results from 207 studies (N = 20,024), Roberts et al. (2017) found that psychotherapy and other clinical interventions predicted lasting personality trait change from pre-to post-intervention assessments, such as increases in emotional stability (d = 0.57) and conscientiousness (d = 0.19). Similarly, research in educational contexts shows that school-based interventions can elicit changes in traits such as grit, empathy, or mastery (Chelouche-Dwek & Fonagy, 2025; Santos et al., 2022; Vanacore et al., 2023). However, both lines of research considered personality trait change only as by-products of the interventions and focused on selective samples (e.g., schoolchildren, people with clinically relevant disorders).

In this review, we focus on volitional personality trait change interventions, that is, interventions that aim to change personality traits in line with people's personal change goals (see Haehner et al., 2024, for an overview). These interventions are of particular practical relevance for the general public, because many people-approximately 60% to 75%-report a desire to change aspects of their personality (Baranski et al., 2021; Miller et al., 2019). Studies investigating volitional personality trait change typically require participants to rate their current personality traits and state their desired changes, with most studies focusing on volitional changes in the Big Five traits (Hudson, Briley, et al., 2019; Stieger et al., 2021), narrower personality facets (Stieger et al., 2020), character strengths (Wagner et al., 2025), or moral traits (Mendonça et al., 2023).

After indicating their goals, participants are assigned to an intervention condition that matches their personality change goals. Most interventions then include strategies that raise discrepancy awareness between actual and desired personality trait levels. In addition, interventions typically include if-then plans (e.g., for extraversion, "If I meet one of my neighbors in the elevator again, I will have a brief small-talk with them"), which aim to elicit changes in personality-relevant behaviors (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Wright et al., 2026). Through practice and repetition, these changes in personality-relevant behaviors are theorized to become a habit, generalize to other contexts, and become incorporated into people's self-concept so that lasting personality trait change occurs over time (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Hennecke et al., 2014; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). Finally, participants rate their personality traits again after the intervention phase and at potential follow-up assessments, which allows an evaluation of whether the intervention was successful in eliciting lasting personality trait change (see Figure 1).

The emerging evidence for the effectiveness of volitional personality change interventions is promising. A recent meta-analysis (7 studies, N = 3,356) found that, on average, volitional personality change interventions are associated with small but significant trait changes in desired directions from pre-to post-intervention assessments (d = 0.22; Haehner et al., 2024). For example, Wright and colleagues (2025) found that 426 individuals who participated in a 6-week smartphone intervention study were successful in reducing their neuroticism consistent with their personal change goals, and that neuroticism changes in the intervention condition were significantly stronger than changes in an active control condition (d = 0.24). Similarly, Wagner et al. (2025) found that an 8-week online intervention was successful in increasing a character strength that participants (N = 3,434) selected and that these increases exceeded changes in a randomized control group (d = 0.19). Although this research thus supports the potential utility of volitional personality change interventions, concerns about their benefits, risks, and social implications remain underexplored. These concerns require careful consideration and discussion before widely applying these interventions.

Furthermore, we outline directions for future research and ways to advance volitional personality change interventions that alleviate these concerns (see Figure 2).

Uncertain Benefits of Volitional Personality Change Interventions

Volitional personality change interventions promise to change core patterns of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors in line with people's personal change goals (Bleidorn et al., 2019; Haehner et al., 2024). Many people are motivated to take part in personality change interventions because they expect to improve their lives, enhance their well-being, and increase person-environment fit (Baranski et al., 2017; Miller et al., 2019). However, the benefits of these interventions are less clear than typically claimed in personality intervention research (Bleidorn et al., 2019; Wright et al., 2026). Four concerns raise questions about the potential benefits of volitional personality change interventions.

Concern 1: The Effects of Volitional Personality Change Interventions Are Biased

The emerging evidence on volitional personality change interventions has been interpreted as support for the effectiveness of these intervention approaches (Haehner et al., 2024). However, existing research on this topic suffered from several methodological limitations that may have led to biased effect size estimates. In a typical personality intervention study, participants are aware of the intervention goal (i.e., no blinding) and a randomized control condition is missing (Allan et al., 2018; Hudson, Derringer, et al., 2019; Hudson & Fraley, 2015; Massey-Abernathy & Robinson, 2021; Mendonça et al., 2023). For example, in a recent digital intervention study, 255 individuals were asked to choose whether they wanted to increase their openness or their conscientiousness and were then assigned to the two intervention conditions based on their personality change goals (Stieger et al., 2020). Changes in openness and conscientiousness were then compared between these non-randomized groups. Such a design, however, can lead to biases like expectancy effects and selection effects (Krämer et al., 2025; Nichols & Maner, 2008; Paulhus & Vazire, 2007; Rosenthal, 1994). For example, participants aware of the intervention goal may be more likely to report personality trait change without actually having changed, because they expect to change or would like to-consciously or unconsciously-please the experimenter or justify their own time investment (Lilienfeld et al., 2014). Similarly, participants in certain intervention conditions may differ systematically from individuals in other intervention conditions due to the non-random assignment, complicating the disentangling of intervention effects from pre-existing individual differences. For instance, people who have the goal to increase their conscientiousness typically have below average levels on this trait before starting the intervention (Miller et al., 2019; Thielmann & de Vries, 2021). Thus, when comparing changes in conscientiousness between non-randomly assigned groups of people with and without the goal to increase their conscientiousness, regression-to-the-mean and intervention effects are confounded (Barnett, 2004; Morton & Torgerson, 2005). Together, these biases may threaten the internal validity of volitional personality change interventions and definite conclusions about their effectiveness.

Risk of bias is not specific to personality intervention studies but a common challenge in intervention research (Corneille & Lush, 2023; Ghanbari Noshari et al., 2023; Lilienfeld et al., 2014; Munder & Barth, 2018). For example, in psychotherapy research, many scholars have long emphasized the importance of carefully designed randomized control groups and have debated their optimal implementation (Cuijpers et al., 2024; Hsu, 1989; Kinser & Robins, 2013). The development and adoption of clear quality standards for randomized-controlled trials has substantially improved the informative value of psychotherapy research (Chalmers et al., 1981; Moher et al., 1995). Similarly, personality intervention research has also recently moved towards more rigorous that can address limitations of earlier studies and provide more robust evidence regarding intervention effectiveness. For example, recent personality intervention studies included randomized control conditions such as wait-list control groups (Küchler et al., 2025; Stieger et al., 2021) or active control groups (Wagner et al., 2025; Wright, Haehner, et al., 2025), allowing to disentangle selection effects from intervention-induced changes. The few studies with randomized control conditions support that intervention effects remain when controlling for biases like expectancy and demand effects (cf. Krämer et al., 2025). However, the evidence base for volitional personality change interventions varies across traits. Intervention approaches targeting certain traits, such as neuroticism, are more firmly established, whereas methods aimed at other traits, such as openness or moral characteristics, remain less well understood (Haehner et al., 2024).

Overall, research on the effectiveness of volitional personality change interventions is still in an early stage. While the emerging evidence suggests that these interventions can be successful in eliciting desired personality trait change, future research with rigorous randomized-controlled designs is needed to replicate these effects and control for multiple threats to internal validity.

Concern 2: The Effects of Volitional Personality Change Interventions Do Only Show in Self-Reports

A second concern is that volitional personality change interventions may not lead to "true" personality changes, meaning that it is difficult to assess whether an intervention was successful in changing the intertwined system of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors along with relevant socialcognitive mechanisms. So far, existing research on personality interventions has primarily used self-reports to capture personality trait change (e.g., Hudson, Derringer, et al., 2019; Hudson & Fraley, 2015; Massey-Abernathy & Robinson, 2021; Mendonça et al., 2023). Self-reports can be seen as a useful criterion to evaluate the success of volitional personality change interventions, because these interventions are typically offered to participants who are unsatisfied with their selfreported trait levels. However, drawing exclusively on self-reports to capture trait change is problematic, because self-reports can be biased by socially desirable responding and capture only one perspective on trait change (Mõttus & Henry, 2025; Paulhus & Vazire, 2007).

Existing research shows that different personality assessment methods such as self-report, peer-report, and behavioral observation provide complementary perspectives on personality, with each method demonstrating incremental validity beyond the other (Ashton & Lee, 2025; Bleidorn et al., 2020; Creed & Funder, 1998; Mõttus & Henry, 2025). At the same time, each method suffers from limitations (e.g., peer-reports can also be biased by socially desirable responding). Thus, to determine whether personality interventions produce "true" personality trait change and to mitigate the limitations of specific assessment methods, it is essential to combine multiple methods. A small number of personality intervention studies have included assessment methods beyond selfreports-such as peer-reports or implicit assessments (Stieger et al., 2020 (Stieger et al., , 2021;; Wright, Haehner, et al., 2025). However, intervention effects assessed with non-self-report measures generally appear to be smaller compared than those obtained with self-reported (e.g., dpeer-report = 0.08 vs.

dself-report = 0.24; Wright, Haehner, et al., 2025). These findings may either indicate that intervention effects fail to generalize beyond self-reported changes or that other methods such as peer-reports are not sufficiently sensitive to capture subtle changes in people's thoughts, feelings, and behaviors (Haehner et al., 2024; Lenhausen et al., 2021). Overall, convincing evidence that personality intervention effects generalize to non-self-report methods is still needed.

Concern 3: Volitional Personality Change Interventions Do Not Lead to Lasting Personality Trait Change

A third concern is that volitional personality change interventions may lead to temporary but not lasting trait change. It could, for instance, be the case that observed personality changes quickly attenuate after the intervention period, because the intervention failed to produce enduring changes in the expression of trait-relevant thoughts, feelings, and behaviors (Roberts et al., 2017; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). Lasting intervention effects are, however, critical for the practical utility of volitional personality change interventions to exert sustainable positive effects on people's lives (Bleidorn et al., 2019). Problematic fade-out of intervention effects has, for example, been found for educational interventions where effects on school performance and intelligence attenuate by 50% to 75% in the first few years after the intervention (Abenavoli, 2019; Bailey et al., 2020; Lee & Loeb, 1995; Protzko, 2016).

A few pioneer studies have examined the persistence of intervention-related personality changes (e.g., Allan et al., 2018; Wright, Haehner, et al., 2025). For example, Stieger et al. (2023) found that personality trait change in response to a volitional personality change intervention persisted through a 1-year follow-up (d = 0.52 to 0.78, N = 157). Similarly, a recent meta-analysis found that the effects of volitional personality change interventions were stable or even increasing after the intervention period (d = 0.37; Haehner et al., 2024). However, the number of personality intervention studies that have a follow-up assessment is still limited. For example, the metaanalysis by Haehner et al. (2024) included only four studies with follow-up assessments.

Furthermore, existing intervention studies suffer from methodological problems such as selective drop-out, potentially overestimating intervention effects at follow-up (Eysenbach, 2005; Stieger et al., 2023). Finally, longer follow-up intervals of several years are needed to fully evaluate the longevity of intervention effects.

Concern 4: Volitional Personality Change Interventions Are Not Practically Relevant

A fourth concern is that volitional personality change interventions fail to hold their promise of improving important outcomes such as health, well-being, or work-success in the general public. For many participants, improving these outcomes represents a main reason to take part in these interventions (Baranski et al., 2017; Miller et al., 2019). Missing effects on these outcomes may thus limit the practical relevance of volitional personality change interventions.

This concern is fueled by recent research on growth mindset interventions. These interventions aimed to foster people's belief that they can change important characteristics of themselves.

However, meta-analytic research showed that these interventions failed to elicit changes in relevant outcomes such as academic achievement or social functioning (Burnette et al., 2023; Macnamara & Burgoyne, 2023).

For personality traits, there is robust evidence for between-person associations with important life outcomes, which has led to early optimism about volitional personality change interventions as levers for improving health and well-being in the general public (Beck & Jackson, 2022; Bleidorn et al., 2019; Jokela et al., 2013; Roberts et al., 2007; Wright et al., 2026). However, few studies have examined whether increases or decreases in personality traits are also linked to these outcomes at a within-person level. For example, it is well-established that individuals with higher levels of conscientiousness tend to have better health (Beck & Jackson, 2022; Haehner, Shirima, et al., 2025; Weston et al., 2015), but it is less clear whether increases in conscientiousness over time predict health improvements at a within-person level (Wright & Jackson, 2023). The available research on such within-person effects suggests that they are smaller and less consistent than between-person associations (Jokela et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2022).

To evaluate the practical utility of volitional personality change interventions, it is thus important to directly investigate the downstream consequences of such interventions. Emerging research by Allemand et al. (2024) suggests that intervention-related increases in extraversion and emotional stability are associated with within-person increases in self-esteem (r ≥ .64, N = 1,179).

Similarly, Olaru et al. (2023) found that intervention-related personality changes are linked to increases in life satisfaction and satisfaction with oneself (r ≥ .13, N = 418). Furthermore, Soto et al. (2025) showed that intervention-related increases in socio-emotional skills were linked to positive changes in social relationships and well-being (r ≥ .16, N = 470). Together, these studies provide initial evidence for the practical utility of volitional personality change interventions.

However, it is still unclear whether these interventions also predict improvements in other outcomes such as physical and mental health or work success (Haehner et al., 2024). Furthermore, it remains an open question whether targeting personality traits is as effective or more effective than targeting certain life outcomes directly (Barlow et al., 2014; Bleidorn et al., 2019).

Summary

In summary, volitional personality change interventions are designed to elicit lasting personality trait change and thus improve health, well-being, relationships, or work success in the general public (Bleidorn et al., 2019; Wright et al., 2026). However, their potential benefits are not yet fully established. While initial research supports that volitional personality change interventions can elicit personality trait changes, which are associated with increased self-esteem and well-being, future research on these interventions is crucial to replicate these effects, combat potentially biases, and investigate the persistence and downstream consequences of interventionrelated personality changes.

Directions for Future Research

There are at least four directions for future research to critically test and fully establish the benefits of volitional personality change interventions. First, personality intervention research should more regularly apply designs with multi-method assessments of personality traits and randomization to intervention and control conditions to reduce the risk of biased effect sizes (Krämer et al., 2025). In this context, careful consideration needs to be given to the type of control group, because wait-list control groups may lead to over-estimating intervention effects (Cuijpers et al., 2024). A strong design for personality interventions includes the randomization of participants with the same personality change goal to groups that differ in their intervention approaches, including an (active) attention-matched control condition that does not receive the theorized active ingredients of the intervention. With such designs, it would be possible to evaluate the effectiveness of intervention conditions relative to one another and thus to control for demand, expectancy, and selection effects. Furthermore, the inclusion of alternative assessment methods such as peer-reports and behavioral observation (e.g., based on mobile sensing indictors) is needed to test whether personality trait changes generalize beyond self-reports. Ideally, future research should combine multiple assessment methods to separate method variance from "true" effects and counteract the limitations of individual assessment approaches (Mõttus & Henry, 2025).

Second, future research needs to investigate the persistence of intervention-related personality changes over several months or years. In this context, it is of particular importance to account for selective drop-out as a substantial proportion of participants drops out of self-help interventions-including volitional personality interventions-and this drop-out does not occur at random (Eysenbach, 2005; Stieger et al., 2023). Investigating who continues to use an intervention, conducting intention-to-treat analyses, and handling missing data adequately are important steps to ensure unbiased estimation of the long-term effects of volitional personality change interventions (Gupta, 2011; Swift & Greenberg, 2012). Furthermore, intervention studies should examine whether and how intervention effects can be sustained by using booster sessions, because psychotherapeutic research has shown that booster sessions can prevent the attenuation of intervention effects over time (e.g., Karyotaki et al., 2016). Including booster sessions into volitional personality change interventions may be a fruitful way to increase the longevity of intervention effects as these sessions can easily be implement in digital intervention settings. Fourth, future research on how to advance the effectiveness of volitional personality change interventions is needed, because this may be a strategy that can address all four concerns regarding their practical utility. For instance, researchers need to establish which intervention strategies are most effective in eliciting desired personality trait change (Haehner, Wright, et al., 2025). Currently, many interventions combine multiple strategies, without clear indications on which strategies are effective, leading to the development of potentially inefficient and burdensome interventions (Collins et al., 2024). Identifying successful intervention strategies helps researchers to amplify intervention effects and uncover causal mechanisms of successful personality trait change (Haehner, Wright, et al., 2026). One problem with this approach, however, is that many psychological interventions are "fat-handed", meaning that they lack specificity in targeting certain outcomes (Eronen, 2020; Eronen & Bringmann, 2021). Breaking down intervention approaches into the smallest possible components could be one strategy to overcome this problem in the future (Wright et al., 2026).

Finally, research on whether and how the effectiveness of volitional personality change interventions can be increased through personalization is warranted (Matz et al., 2024; Modersitzki et al., 2025). Developing a personalized just-in-time adaptive personality change intervention represents an important direction for future research. Just-in-time adaptive interventions aim to provide the right support, at the right time, by adapting the intervention to internal or external states and contexts of an individual, such as their current capacity for an intervention or their current environment (Nahum-Shani et al., 2015 , 2018). Tailoring volitional personality change interventions in this way promises to increase intervention effectiveness and may thus boost their practical utility.

Practical Implications

For practical applications, researchers need to be transparent about the changes participants can realistically expect from volitional personality change interventions. For example, the informed consent should include a brief statement noting that the effectiveness of volitional personality change interventions is not yet fully established and that, if effective, participants can expect relatively small changes in their personality trait levels. These changes may have positive implications for well-being and self-esteem, but a comprehensive understanding of the longer-term consequences of these interventions requires more research. Furthermore, researchers and practitioners should also carefully monitor intervention effects during the intervention period and at follow-up assessments to better understand the benefits of these intervention.

Risks of Volitional Personality Change Interventions

In addition to examining and communicating the potential benefits of volitional personality change interventions, possible risks of participating in such interventions need to be considered as well. Investigating risks of volitional personality change interventions is relevant because these interventions aim to alter core patterns of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, and because most interventions are implemented in an online setting without personal support (Haehner et al., 2024).

However, so far, potential risks and adverse effects of volitional personality change interventions have largely been neglected in existing research. Here, we discuss findings from clinical psychology and digital health interventions to draw attention to three concerns regarding possible risks of volitional personality change interventions.

Concern 5: Volitional Personality Change Interventions May Backfire for Some People

A possible risk of volitional personality change interventions is that the interventions may backfire and produce unwanted personality trait change for some individuals. Meta-analyses on the effects of psychotherapy, for example, show that 5-25% of participants tend to experience symptom deterioration during therapy (e.g., Barlow, 2010; Berk & Parker, 2009; Cuijpers et al., 2018; Linden & Schermuly-Haupt, 2014). Similarly, a recent review of 23 digital health interventions found that intervention participation was associated with new symptoms or symptom deterioration in about 5-20% of their users (Taher et al., 2023). A similar pattern seems to emerge for volitional personality change interventions. For example, Wright, Haehner, et al. (2025) found that approximately 10% to 20% of participants reported increases in neuroticism after completing a digital smartphone intervention designed to decrease neuroticism. Similarly, in data published by Stieger et al. (2021), approximately 25% of participants who aimed to increase a specific trait reported decreases in this trait, while 27% of those who aimed to decrease certain trait levels reported increases in this trait.

There are several possible reasons for why some people may show undesired trait change during volitional personality change interventions. For example, Hudson and Fraley (2015) posited that taking part in an intervention could undermine change motivations for some individuals, as they may perceive taking part in the intervention as (partial) goal fulfillment, reducing their motivation to implement actual changes in trait-relevant behaviors (see also Fishbach et al., 2006). Furthermore, volitional personality change interventions may backfire if they provide participants with insufficient support to reach their change goals. Hudson and Fraley (2015), for example, found that a structured goal setting approach with clear instructions on how to implement changes in trait-relevant behaviors did not backfire whereas an unstructured approach did. However, it could also be the case that external factors-unrelated to the intervention-elicit undesired personality trait change in some participants. For example, if participants experience negative life events like a job loss during the intervention period, these events could be responsible for the observed personality changes in unwanted directions (Haehner et al., 2024; Haehner, Krämer, et al., 2026). Similarly, changes in reference group comparisons across the intervention period may explain undesired changes if participants start to compare themselves with people who already show the desired trait expressions (Lenhausen et al., 2023; Lira et al., 2022). Thus, it has yet to be established whether changes in undesired directions are causally linked to the intervention. In summary, although volitional personality change interventions predict desired personality trait changes on average, individual differences in participants' responses to these interventions highlight the need for careful consideration before broadly disseminating these interventions to the general public. It is thus of critical importance to understand for whom and under which conditions these interventions may be ineffective or even backfire.

Concern 6: Volitional Personality Change Interventions May Have Side Effects

Because psychotherapy and other interventions that change individuals' behaviors and everyday routines have side effects, it seems likely that volitional personality change interventions, which rely on similar intervention strategies (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017), have side effects as well. Side effects refer to unwanted and potentially negative consequences for participants that occur during the intervention (Klein et al., 2024). For example, a previous experimental study has shown that 147 highly introverted individuals who deliberately behaved in an extraverted manner experienced increased fatigue and emotional strain (d = 0.24; Jacques-Hamilton et al., 2019). This finding suggests that volitional personality change interventions may tie up important resources of participants because acting out of character seems to require energy and self-control. Furthermore, confronting participants with unpleasant aspects of their personality during the intervention can be emotionally discomforting. The repeated focus on one's neuroticism, for example, may evoke shame, distress, or other adverse emotions. Although psychotherapy research suggests that such emotional reactions are likely transient and may even drive desired therapeutic effects (Gassmann & Grawe, 2006; Hayes et al., 2007), they could be a problem for volitional personality change interventions to the degree that such negative emotions undermine intervention adherence in a self-guided intervention and thus threaten lasting intervention success (Bidargaddi et al., 2018; Haehner, Wright, et al., 2026).

Finally, volitional personality change interventions may lead to potentially unwanted disruptions in people's life circumstances. Psychotherapy has, for example, been linked to a deterioration of work-related productivity or relationship satisfaction for about 10% of patients (Strauss et al., 2021). These changes can be explained by psychotherapeutic disruption of current habits and relationship patterns, potentially leading to major life events like job loss or divorce (Linden & Schermuly-Haupt, 2014). Given that personality traits have been found to affect many life domains (Beck & Jackson, 2022; Bleidorn et al., 2019; Soto, 2019), similar effects could occur for volitional personality change interventions. For example, even though lower agreeableness may be desired to better assert oneself at work and reject additional tasks, these changes could have negative implications for one's partnership or friendships, potentially causing the deterioration or dissolution of these relationships. Although such consequences may generally be rare given that volitional personality change interventions predict only small trait changes (Haehner et al., 2024), they call for a careful consideration of intervention effects beyond the targeted personality trait.

Overall, based on findings from psychotherapeutic and health intervention research, it is conceivable that volitional personality change interventions have side effects. However, empirical evidence on such side effects is virtually absent, highlighting the need of future research. A careful assessment of side effects is essential for drawing informed conclusions about the cost-benefit ratio of volitional personality change interventions and for determining the conditions under which, and the individuals for whom, such interventions should be offered.

Concern 7: Volitional Personality Change Interventions Are Unsafe for Some Individuals

Currently, volitional personality change interventions are offered to the relatively broad population of adults who want to change aspects of their personality (Baranski et al., 2021; Hudson & Fraley, 2016). This broad application, however, raises concerns that the interventions may be unsafe for some individuals. This concern refers to potential contraindications of the intervention, describing conditions under which adverse effects are particularly likely to occur (Linden, 2013).

Two contraindications of volitional personality change interventions may be of particular relevance: (1) presence of severe impairments in personality functioning and (2) misaligned or maladaptive personality change goals.

First, individuals interested in a volitional personality change intervention may suffer from clinically relevant disorders or severe impairments in their personality functioning. Personality functioning refers to an individual's capacity to manage themselves and their relationships with others (Sharp & Wall, 2021; Zimmermann et al., 2023). According to the DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) and ICD-11 (World Health Organization, 2021), severe impairments in personality functioning are indicative of personality disorders and thus constitute clinically relevant mental health conditions that are associated with risks of harm to oneself or others, including suicidality (Bach & Simonsen, 2021; Sharp & Wall, 2021). For individuals with such severe impairments in personality functioning, self-guided personality change interventions, even if rooted in psychotherapeutic principles, may provide insufficient support (Simon et al., 2022) or have a higher risk of drop-out (Kerber et al., 2023). For instance, such individuals might negatively distort feedback, overgeneralize failures during exercises, and ultimately lose hope for, or access to, more appropriate professional help. It is pivotal that these individuals get professional help as fast as possible, and that self-guided personality change interventions are not misperceived as suitable alternatives.

Second, the motivations for personality change may be misaligned or maladaptive in a way that renders volitional personality change interventions inappropriate or even counterproductive.

Personality change goals may arise from external societal pressures to achieve certain goals or meet unrealistic behavioral or affective standards (see also Concern 9; Hudson & Fraley, 2017), such as constantly engaging with friends or reaching perpetual states of happiness (Dejonckheere et al., 2022). Engaging in personality change interventions with the goal to fulfill such unrealistic standards may exacerbate pressures to conform and lead to internalized failure. Similarly, a desire for personality change might result from an overgeneralization of more specific difficulties and thus be unaligned with individual needs. For example, challenges in a particular work task might be misinterpreted as a fundamental personality flaw (e.g., "I need to be more industrious overall") when a targeted skill-building or problem-solving approach would be more suitable (Soto et al., 2021). In such cases, an intervention focused primarily on broad trait change could inadvertently distract from underlying problems or reinforce a belief that fundamental aspects of the self are inherently deficient, rather than fostering genuine well-being or targeted skill development.

Directions for Future Research

An important direction for future research would be a systematic risk assessment of volitional personality change interventions. Only if personality intervention research systematically investigates unwanted trait change, side effects, and contraindications, it is possible to fully evaluate questions about their indication, ethical feasibility, and benefit-risk ratio.

Researchers should thus routinely include measures in their studies that allow participants to report possible adverse effects of the intervention. To do so, it needs to tested whether validated measures to capture adverse effects in psychotherapeutic research (e.g., Fazhan et al., 2023; Rozental et al., 2019) can be used for volitional personality change interventions or whether they need to be adapted.

Furthermore, future research needs to directly investigate possible contraindications of volitional personality change interventions. An important first step into this direction would be to examine underlying motivations of people interested in personality change interventions to uncover the relevance of misaligned and maladaptive change goals. To do so, measures that can capture problematic change motivations need to be developed and validated. Relatedly, understanding how participant characteristics like self-efficacy, beliefs about personality trait change, or mental health are related to intervention effects is needed to identify further possible contraindications and advance intervention effectiveness (Hudson, 2021a; Hudson et al., 2021; Krämer, Wright, et al., 2026; Olaru et al., 2024). For example, it needs to be tested whether and under which circumstances people with mental health problems can profit from volitional personality change interventions and when they should be redirected to psychotherapeutic interventions. Similarly, self-efficacy or beliefs about personality traits might influence how the intervention should be designed as these characteristics may be related how people react to certain intervention strategies (Dweck & Yeager, 2019). Assigning people to different intervention approaches based on their personal characteristics could increase intervention effectiveness, reduce the risks of adverse side effects, and be the starting point for a more scientifically-based diagnostic assessment of intervention indication.

Practical Implications

Concerns about possible risks of volitional personality change interventions also have important practical implications. First, participants should be informed about possible side effects in the consent form (Hudson & Fraley, 2015; Jacques-Hamilton et al., 2019; Strauss et al., 2021).

Due to the lack of systematic research on side effects of volitional personality change intervention, such information can currently only be given at a fairly general level, for example, by providing a transparent description of potential intervention outcomes and possible side effects such as transient negative emotions or potentially unwanted changes in external life circumstances. Once there is evidence for side effects of volitional personality change interventions, the informed consent should be updated to offer descriptions of these effects and their probabilities (perhaps as an appendix, similar to a package insert of a medication). However, at the same time, the consent forms should be kept as concise as possible-focusing on risks that actually go beyond risks of everyday life-to avoid overburdening consent forms and to increase the likelihood that participants read the form (Haggerty, 2004).

Second, researchers and practitioners need to examine the eligibility of participants for the intervention to exclude possible contraindications. Examining the underlying motivation of personality change goals and impairments in personality functioning would be a reasonable first step to identify possible contraindications. Personality functioning can, for example, be assessed with self-report measures such as Level of Personality Functioning Scale -Brief Form 2.0 (Weekers et al., 2019) or Personality Disorder Severity ICD-11 Scale (Bach et al., 2021).

Individuals with moderate to severe impairments in their personality functioning might better be directed to professional help. Similarly, people with unrealistic change goals (e.g., individuals who aim for sweeping trait-level changes), change goals due to societal pressure, or specific behavioral problems might better be excluded from volitional personality change interventions.

Third, during and after the intervention, researchers and practitioners should monitor the intervention effects to uncover possible non-responses, personality trait change in undesired directions, and side effects. Digital personality change interventions could, for example, include automatized safeguards that direct participants to personalized support if they repeatedly report low well-being or personality trait change in undesired directions (Nahum-Shani et al., 2015 , 2018). Personalized support-may it be online or in-person-ensures that participants can ask questions regarding the intervention, that they can receive additional assistance, and that they are redirected to professional help, if needed. Finally, a careful design of psychoeducation modules may help to reduce side effects like feelings of internalized failure. Participants should be informed that transient emotional distress, non-linear progress, and fluctuating motivation and success are normal parts of a volitional personality change intervention.

Societal Implications of Volitional Personality Change Interventions

Offering volitional personality change interventions to the general public also raises concerns that extend beyond the individual level, even affecting individuals who do not (yet) take part in these interventions. At a societal level, questions about the equity of volitional personality change interventions, stigmatization, and social pressure to change require careful consideration (Allemand & Flückiger, 2022; Bleidorn et al., 2019; Nehring & Röcke, 2024). We discuss three concerns regarding the societal implications of volitional personality change interventions.

Concern 8: Volitional Personality Change Interventions May Contribute to Excessive Self-Optimization

Volitional personality change interventions may be classified as self-optimization interventions, because they are addressed to individuals from the general public who seek to move closer to their ideal self (Nehring & Röcke, 2024). Self-optimization can, however, become excessive and detrimental (Brandt & Straub, 2024; Meißner, 2016; Schneickert et al., 2026).

Recent years have seen a surge of self-optimization interventions, fostered by commercial selfhelp products and social media, with many approaches lacking theoretical grounding and empirical validation (Al Dahdah, 2022; Bergsma, 2008; S. Butler, 2024; Gross, 2025; Kent, 2020; Krämer, Asselmann, et al., 2026). This trend reflects a broader cultural climate in which constant selfoptimization has become a normative ideal, often equating personal worth with productivity, efficiency, and performance (King et al., 2019). Intentionally changing personality traits could be seen as a particularly far-reaching form of self-optimization, as it involves efforts to alter deeply rooted patterns of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors as well as core aspects of identity. Critically, the mere availability of such interventions could encourage some individuals-especially those who are dissatisfied with their lives and themselves (Miller et al., 2019)-to engage in excessive self-optimization. Potentially amplifying this concern, current discussions of volitional personality change interventions in the scientific literature and public discourse are highly positive, which may unintentionally discourage alternative pathways to well-being (Allemand & Flückiger, 2022; Haehner et al., 2024; Schnydrig, 2025).

The goal of volitional personality change interventions is, however, not-as often misconceived in popular discourse-to radically or fundamentally alter a person's character.

Rather, such interventions aim to facilitate subtle adjustments in specific traits, in line with individuals' self-defined change goals. However, to date, little is known about how volitional personality change interventions compare to alternative approaches that aim to improve people's well-being, such as increasing self-acceptance (J. M. Butler & Haigh, 1954; Rogers, 1961; Williams & Lynn, 2010). Ideally, the availability of different intervention approaches can ultimately offer people a rich toolbox to reach desired changes in their lives. Mitigating this concern therefore requires a realistic scientific and public discussion of advantages, limitations, and risks of various available interventions, including volitional personality change interventions.

Concern 9: Volitional Personality Change Interventions Lead to Stigmatization and Pressure to Conform

Another societal concern is that volitional personality change interventions may unintentionally promote a narrow definition of the "ideal" personality, thereby stigmatizing traits that fall outside of this norm (Klimstra & McLean, 2024). Individuals who do not conform to these ideals may feel marginalized or pressured to change. Institutionalizing personality change interventions-for example, as part of school-based programs (e.g., Santos et al., 2022; Sigmundsson et al., 2024)-could create pressures for people who are functioning well. Such pressures may undermine psychological well-being or even distort core aspects of identity (Higgins et al., 1994; Kiecolt, 1994). Moreover, some personality traits-such as high neuroticism or low self-efficacy-are already stigmatized (McCord, 2021; McCord & Joseph, 2020), and volitional personality change interventions may inadvertently reinforce societal biases and reduce appreciation for personality diversity in the general population.

However, most intervention approaches let participants pursue change in either direction (e.g., increasing or decreasing extraversion) and frame all trait levels as value-neutral, thereby avoiding the notion of a single "ideal" personality. A more general problem may, however, be that people's personality change goals-which are typically used as an entry criterion for volitional personality change interventions-are influenced by social pressure and stigmatization. For example, in a recent intervention study (Stieger et al., 2021), in which 1,523 individuals could freely choose whether they wanted to increase or decrease a certain Big Five trait, the vast majority of participants either selected to decrease their neuroticism (27%), increase their conscientiousness (26%), or increase their extraversion (25%). Carefully assessing the underlying motivation of these change goals (see Concern 7) and valuing intrinsic change goals thus seems necessary to prevent societally pressured personality trait change.

Concern 10: Volitional Personality Change Interventions Exacerbate Existing Disparities

A final concern pertains to the accessibility and equity of volitional personality change interventions. Most personality interventions-particularly app-based programs, coaching, or selfreflection protocols-have been developed and tested primarily in high-resource, highly educated, Western, student populations (Haehner et al., 2024), raising concerns about their generalizability and inclusivity (Nehring & Röcke, 2024).

For example, most volitional personality change interventions draw on trait models and intervention approaches that have been developed in Western samples (Bleidorn, 2024; Hudson, 2021a; Stieger et al., 2020), neglecting that characteristic and adaptive personality traits as well as ideal approaches to address them may differ across cultural contexts (Haehner, Shirima, et al., 2025; Thalmayer et al., 2022 Thalmayer et al., , 2025)). Furthermore, the time and effort required to engage with such interventions often pose substantial barriers, especially for individuals in full-time employment, caregiving roles, or with limited digital literacy. In their current form, volitional personality change interventions may therefore risk becoming tools that disproportionately benefit highly educated and resourceful individuals-those with the financial means, time, and personal capacities to invest in self-development-thereby potentially amplifying existing disparities in mental health and well-being (e.g., Weinberger et al., 2018). This risk may be further exacerbated when scientifically sound tools are commercialized and widely disseminated without sufficient attention to equity and accessibility. At the same time, volitional personality change interventions could also be harnessed to reduce disparities-if they are thoughtfully adapted, specifically targeted, and made accessible to disadvantaged populations, highlighting a crucial direction for future research and application.

Summary

Taken together, the increasing interest in volitional personality change interventions raises questions regarding their implications beyond the individual level. Excessive self-optimization, social pressure to change, and exacerbation of existing disparities are three important societal concerns regarding volitional personality change interventions. A careful empirical investigation of these societal implications is necessary to ensure that offering volitional personality change interventions to the general public is beneficial for individuals and society as a whole.

Directions for Future Research

Future research on societal implications of volitional personality change interventions is needed to better understand how to minimize societal risks and maximize possible benefits. First, researchers need to investigate how people perceive these interventions and how different intervention framings influence this perception. Intervention perception is important because it may influence the consideration of alternative intervention approaches and stigmatization of personality traits. Furthermore, alternative framings could help to reduce the risk of unintentionally promoting excessive self-optimization and social pressure to change. For example, instead of communicating that volitional personality change interventions are designed to change people's habitual levels of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, personality interventions could emphasize flexibility of personality expression in daily life, illustrating that different thoughts, feelings, and behaviors can be adaptive depending on the situational context (Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015; Soto et al., 2021).

Second, personality intervention research needs to overcome the overreliance on student samples recruited in Western countries. Investigating volitional personality change interventions in other cultural contexts is an important direction for future research to identify universal and differential mechanisms of volitional personality change and develop approaches tailored to different target groups, while accounting for potential differences in the structure, malleability, and adaptivity of personality traits across cultural contexts (Fan et al., 2011; Haehner, Naudé, et al., 2026; Haehner, Shirima, et al., 2025; Thalmayer et al., 2022).

Third, diversifying the design of volitional personality change interventions may increase their accessibility across heterogeneous demographic groups. Research on how intervention characteristics such as the application modality or timing and dosage of intervention prompts may influence intervention effects is thus an important direction for future research (Haehner, Wright, et al., 2026; Hudson, 2021b). A profound understanding of the relevance of such intervention characteristics may foster the development of a diverse set of personality interventions tailored to different target groups. For example, volitional personality change interventions varying in dosage and corresponding time commitment could reduce barriers to intervention access and enabling individuals to select formats that align with to their personal availability. Recent psychotherapeutic research has, for example, shown that single-session intervention designs can be successful in reducing depression and anxiety, increasing the accessibility for a broad audience (Schleider & Weisz, 2016; Venturo-Conerly et al., 2022). It is an important direction for future research to test whether brief volitional personality change interventions are sufficient to produce desired trait change or whether there is a minimal necessary intervention duration.

Practical Implications

Societal-level concerns regarding volitional personality change interventions also have implications for applying and promoting these interventions. For example, researchers and practitioners should carefully consider how they advertise volitional personality change interventions. The way personality traits are described in the public discourse likely influences how laypersons perceive personality traits and personality trait change, which may contribute to social pressure or stigmatization (Klimstra & McLean, 2024; McCord, 2021). A useful promotion strategy may be a strength-based approach that focuses on the potential benefits of certain trait levels without devaluating alternative trait levels (Flückiger et al., 2023; Niemiec & Pearce, 2021). Finally, once a positive benefit-risk ratio of volitional personality change interventions for diverse populations is scientifically established, researchers and practitioners should make efforts to reduce entry barriers for potentially disadvantaged populations. This requires information about volitional personality change interventions to be presented in an accessible way for laypeople, that personality interventions are offered at affordable prices, and that researchers clearly communicate the advantages and disadvantages for different people. In addition, community-based outreach and partnerships with trusted organizations, culturally adapted and participatory intervention development, and integrated or low-threshold service delivery models could facilitate engagement across socio-ethnic groups (Alam et al., 2024; Lee-Tauler et al., 2018; Nwokoroku et al., 2022).

Similarly, a careful design of intervention materials can ensure inclusivity for heterogenous populations. For instance, psychoeducational content should incorporate scenarios that reflect diverse life circumstances rather than focusing primarily on highly educated young adults.

Personalization strategies may further enhance inclusivity by adapting intervention materials to individuals' lived realities, thereby increasing relevance and accessibility for people from different backgrounds.
