Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Assessing others' pain relies heavily on facial expressions, yet how individuals translate observable facial features into pain judgments remains poorly understood. We combined a retrospective analysis of spontaneous pain expressions across three cohorts (Study 1, N = 398) with a reverse correlation experiment using synthetic facial stimuli (Study 2, N = 63) to investigate which facial muscle contractions (Action Units, AUs) drive pain judgments. Across the two studies, brow raising (AUs 1-2), brow lowering (AU 4), nose wrinkling/upper lip raising (AUs 9-10), lips corner depressor (AU 15) and mouth aperture were identified as key contributors. Multivariate pattern analysis from Study 1 indicated that pain judgments rely on a distributed set of facial AUs that partly generalize across independent cohorts and stimulus sets. Tailored co-engagement analysis from Study 2 identified AU pairs that, when combined, facilitate (AUs 7 + 43) or inhibit (AUs 4 + 9, 10 + 27) pain assessments. Finally, Study 2 also revealed AU information that were taken into consideration depending on the Sex of the displayed face, although the overall contribution of this factor was modest and no systematic effect was observed in Study 1. These findings provide a first systematic mapping of the observable facial muscle contractions that drive human pain decoding. The identified facial features are best interpreted in terms of flexible pain processing, whereby individuals base their inference on distinct, partly-redundant patterns of facial responses.

Introduction

Assessing others' pain is crucial in many clinical and everyday contexts [1, 2]. Unlike most medical conditions, pain is difficult to quantify objectively, and is mainly assessed using patients' selfreports and indirect reactions. Facial responses are particularly informative in this respect, due to both their sensitivity to affect and to their communicative relevance, especially when verbal selfreports are unavailable [3]. Many studies investigated painful expressions through the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) [4], which decomposes facial responses into coordinated muscle groups, or Action Units (AUs). Several AUs have been consistently implicated in pain: brow lowering (AU 4), eye narrowing (AU 6/7), nose wrinkling/upper lip raising (AU 9/10), mouth opening (AU 25/26/27) and eyes closing/blinking (AU 43/45) [5, 6]. Importantly, strong inter-individual variability has also been observed, to the point that some scholars refer to distinct pain expressions, rather than to a single prototypical one [7, 8].

Most studies investigated pain expressions in terms of encoding (which AUs are engaged during pain?), whereas less is known about decoding (which AU engagements influence observers' assessments?). Patrick et al. [9] recorded spontaneous reactions to electric stimulation, and found that movements associated with AUs 4 and 45 predicted observers' intensity ratings. Unfortunately, this research considered only a limited set of AUs, which often co-occurred with one another [9]. Other studies applied visual noise (reverse correlation [10, 11]) or spatial filtering [11, 12] to the face images, identifying which visual features explained pain judgments in a fully data-driven fashion. This approach, however, characterizes facial information only in terms of regions (eyes, mouth), rather than underlying musculature. To our knowledge, no study has combined the methodological strengths of reverse correlation [10, 11] with a careful assessment of the role played by facial muscle contractions.

Meta-analyses and multi-center studies document sex and gender disparities in how pain is acknowledged and treated, with females being more likely to have their symptoms underestimated and less likely to receive medications [13] [14] [15] [16]. The literature on facial expression has also addressed this issue: albeit adult females generally exhibit stronger facial muscular responses to painful stimuli than males [17], findings on the decoding are mixed, with some suggesting that female facial expressions are associated with lower ratings than male expressions (even when controlling carefully for the intensity of facial contraction) [18, 19], and others showing instead opposite effects or no sex differences [20] [21] [22]. Conceivably, sex biases might in part reflect the fact that individuals hold separate representations for male and female pain expressions. However, this possibility remains untested.

Here, we investigated which facial AUs observers rely on to evaluate pain. First, we ran a largescale analysis on multiple datasets to examine observers' evaluations of spontaneous facial reactions to pain. Second, we devised a modified reverse correlation task [10, 11] using synthetic expressions with pseudo-random AU engagement, enabling direct inference about the contribution of facial musculature to observers' judgments. Together, these approaches offer a comprehensive account of how facial information influences pain decoding.

Study 1: Spontaneous Facial Expressions

Methods

Participants

We used data from three independent cohorts, each obtained by combining multiple experiments. In particular, Cohort 1 was composed of N = 166 individuals (121 females, average age = 25.09 ± 5.92 [sd]) drawn from the studies by from Dirupo et al. [23, 24] plus an unpublished dataset from the same authors. Cohort 2 was composed of N = 119 individuals (73 females, average age = 23.49 ± 4.49) from Riontino et al. [25], plus an unpublished dataset from the same authors. Instead, Cohort 3 was composed of N = 113 individuals (66 females, 46 males, 1 missing value; mean age = 26.33 ± 4.58 years), obtained by combining four experiments that are part of an ongoing unpublished project. Supplementary Information provides full information about each cohort. Data from Cohorts 1-2 were acquired at the University of Geneva (see original publications for information about ethical approval). Data from Cohort 3 were acquired at the Center for Mind/Brain Sciences (CIMeC) of the University of Trento, in accordance with the local regulations and the "Comitato Etico per la Ricerca" of the University of Trento.

Facial Expressions

Cohort 1 participants were exposed to the same set of spontaneous expressions of pain. These were videos of variable duration (average 7.61 sec) recorded when neurotypical individuals received thermal painful stimuli [26]. Details about how these videos were created and validated are available in previous research [23, 24, 26]. Cohort 2 participants were exposed to 3 sec video-clips selected from the UNBC-McMaster Shoulder Pain Expression Archive Database [27]. This database depicts adults suffering from spontaneous shoulder pain due to a wide range of etiologies (arthritis, bursitis, tendonitis, subluxation, etc.), who were video-recorded while undergoing active or passive movements of their painful shoulder (abduction, flexion, internal/external rotation). Details about how the videos were selected and validated are available in Riontino et al. [25]. Cohort 3 participants were exposed to short (1 sec) video-clips from the Pain E-motion Faces Database (PEMF) [28]. In this dataset, neurotypical individuals were recorded while undergoing one nociceptive procedure: tonic pressure stimulation via an algometer, or phasic heat stimulation via a 𝐶𝑂 2 laser. Supplementary Information provides full information about all facial-expression databases, including how the videos used in Cohort 3 were selected and validated.

Task

Cohort 1 participants were engaged in two tasks. The first was a classification task, where they had to assess whether each facial expression displayed "pain" or another state ("disgust" or "neutral"), by pressing a key associated with the chosen label. The second was a rating task, where they were asked to quantify the degree of unpleasantness experienced by the person in the video. Ratings were provided using a visual analogue scale (VAS) where the two endpoints were labeled as "neutral" and "extremely unpleasant", and a cursor could be moved along the scale by pressing two different keys. Participants from Cohorts 2 & 3 were engaged in a task where they had to quantify the intensity of pain attributed to each facial expression through a VAS ranging from "no pain" to "extreme pain". Importantly, in Cohort 2, face ratings were carried out following either a cognitive demanding task (e.g., an incongruent Stroop task), or a tailored control [25]. As the cognitive exertion had detrimental effects on the sensitivity to painful expressions [25], we decided to include in this analysis only the control condition with no exertion. See Supplementary Information for full information about the tasks used in each cohort.

Facial Action Units (AUs)

Information about facial reactions in the videos from the three cohorts was obtained using Open-Face 2.2.0 (https://github.com/TadasBaltrusaitis/OpenFace) [29], which has predefined algorithms for video-stream decoding and quantification of specific AU engagements [30]. Specifically, multivariate models allow estimating the involvement of AUs, not only in terms of engagement vs. non-engagement (classification models) but also in terms of strength of contraction (regression models). For each frame of each video-clip, we obtained regression estimates for relevant AUs. The only exception was the estimation of eye blinks (AU 45). As these movements are usually interpreted as an all-or-none response, a classification-based estimate of this AU was chosen instead. Although the original databases were subjected to human FACS scoring, this approach differed in terms of the measure assessed (only occurrence vs. also intensity) and in terms of resolution (cumulative engagement across the clip vs. frame-by-frame analysis) making it difficult to compare results from the three cohorts. In this respect, OpenFace estimates have the advantage of providing the same AU assessment for all video-clips. Supplementary Information reports the results of a follow-up validation analysis, where the human scoring was compared with OpenFace estimates. For all databases, there was good overall agreement between human FACS coding and OpenFace output for homologous AUs. However, in line with prior studies comparing human and computerbased AU scoring [26], we also found cross-predictions between dissimilar AUs (see Supplementary Figure 1 for more details).

Having established that OpenFace output shows fairly good agreement with human FACS scoring, we took the Area Under the Curve of the OpenFace output as a measure of the cumulative effect of each AU within the whole clip. In particular, we followed studies showing the benefit of combining AUs underlying similar facial movements together, both when assessing the reliability of algorithms for facial feature extraction [31], and in the human coding of the facial expression of pain [5, 7, 8]. Hence, we decided to treat as equivalent and sum facial AUs related to eyebrow raising (AUs 1 & 2), eye narrowing (AUs 6 & 7), nose wrinkling (AUs 9 & 10), and mouth opening (AUs 25 & 26; no AU 27 output is provided by OpenFace). This led to 13 estimates: AUs 1/2, 4, 5, 6/7, 9/10, 12, 14, 15, 17, 20, 23, 25/26, 45. For AU 45, which was defined as a dichotomic variable, we calculated the percentage of frames in which it was estimated to be engaged.

Data Analysis

For the purpose of this work, we chose as dependent variables individual judgments about pain or unpleasantness. Specifically, for Cohort 1 we took unpleasantness ratings (on a VAS) and pain classifications (provided as a dichotomic response), whereas for Cohorts 2-3 we took pain ratings (on a VAS). In line with prior work [32], rating scales were recoded into proportions p (ranging from 0.025 [lowest possible score] to 0.975 [highest possible score]) and logit-transformed: 𝑙𝑜𝑔𝑖𝑡(𝑝) = 𝑙𝑛( 𝑝 (1-𝑝) ), to improve conformity to a normal distribution. As independent variables, we chose the 13 AU estimates provided by the OpenFace algorithm. Additionally, we controlled for the Sex of the participants (between-subjects factor: Sex) and that of the person displayed in the videos (withinsubjects factor: FaceSex).

Univariate Analysis.

We ran linear mixed models (LMMs) where single-trial judgments were assessed as a function of AU engagement, Sex, FaceSex and their interaction. The identity of the participants and that of the video-displayed person were specified as random factors (see Supplementary Information for full details on the random structure). Significance of the fixed effects was calculated with a Type II Analysis of Variance using the Satterthwaite approximation of degrees of freedom [33]. For pain classification, we exploited a Generalized LMM with a binomial distribution and Laplace approximation, and significance was assessed through Type II Analysis of Deviance and Wald 𝜒 2 statistics. For each cohort, separate models were run for each AU. Effects were considered significant at 𝛼 < .05 Bonferroni-corrected for 13 repeated tests (corresponding to each of the AUs used as predictor). The analysis was carried out as implemented in the lmerTest [33] and car packages of R software (https://cran.r-project.org/).

Multivariate Pattern Analysis.

We also adopted a multivariate approach, and trained Random Forest (RF) regression (for unpleasantness/pain ratings; Cohorts 1-3) and classification (for pain classifications; Cohort 1) to predict participants' assessments of facial expressions. Random Forest involves the implementation of decision trees, which perform recursive partitioning of the facial (feature) space to lead to a non-linear predictive model. Because decision trees are susceptible to small perturbations in the dataset, RF reduces prediction variance by aggregating the outcomes from trees fitted to bootstrap resamples of the original dataset [34]. To ensure out-of-sample prediction, a five-fold cross-validation scheme was implemented on a per-subject basis. Data were split into five parts, with all trials of a given participant assigned to the same fold. Data from one fold were then predicted using a model trained on the remaining four folds. The analysis was run as implemented in the randomForest package in R using 1000 trees per model.

Model performance for classification outcomes was summarized using classification accuracy (proportion of correct responses) and d-prime (d') [35]. For continuous outcomes, two primary evaluation measures were used: the fixed-effect slope (b) obtained from an LMM, assessing the agreement between observed and predicted scores; and the mean squared error (MSE), averaged first within and then across participants. To assess whether model performance exceeded chance levels, permutation tests were performed using 1000 data shuffles of the original datasets. Critical thresholds were defined as the 95 𝑡ℎ percentile of the null distribution for classification accuracy, d' and regression slopes (in order to ensure greater-than-chance prediction), and the 5 𝑡ℎ percentile for MSE (in order to ensure lower-than-chance error). After evaluating model performance using cross-validation, the final model was refitted using the entire dataset to extract variable importance scores. Importance measures included the average decrease in accuracy for classification models, and the percentage MSE increase. The importance estimate for each feature was also validated using permutation techniques, in order to identify features whose contribution to the overall prediction was greater than the one observed under null hypothesis.

Finally, regression models were run and also validated across cohorts, in order to establish whether a model tailored to one cohort could also predict responses in a different cohort. For each pair of cohorts, models were trained and validated across two independent folds, in each of which the model was trained on one cohort and tested in the other one.

Interim Discussion

This retrospective analysis of three independent cohorts allowed us to identify a core set of AUs that significantly influence participants' pain-related judgments. In the univariate analysis we found no core set of facial responses implicated in all cohorts, although AUs 1-2, 9-10, 15, and 25-26 were observed in all analyses except that of Cohort 3 which, instead, implicated only AU 4 (Figure 1). It should be stressed that univariate results were constrained by correction for multiple compar-isons, which considers as relevant only the most pronounced influences, disregarding weaker (but potentially converging across cohorts) effects. Multivariate pattern analysis, instead, identified a higher degree of cross-cohort convergence (Table 2) suggesting that individuals assess pain using a partially shared set of AU patterns that univariate analyses failed to capture. Importantly, however, cross-cohort predictive performance, albeit higher than chance, is substantially lower than the one obtained when modeling each cohort alone. It is possible that AU contributions in this analysis are context-sensitive and influenced by idiosyncratic cohort characteristics, task structure, or differences in the facial expression databases (luminance, duration, angle, etc). Furthermore, previous work on pain expression encoding stresses how AU engagement tends to co-occur based on distinct patterns of pain reactions [7, 8]. Hence, although all datasets are characterized by facial reactions to pain, these do not necessarily depict exactly the same muscular information. It is therefore not surprising that, in some cases, AUs 9/10 appear more relevant (Cohorts 1-2), whereas in others it is AU 4 that best explains behavior (Cohort 3).

It is important to stress that, as multiple pain-relevant AUs might co-engage following the same painful event [5] [6] [7] [8], it is often difficult to distinguish those AUs that are most relevant for pain decoding from those that are merely part of the same overall expression but are not actually taken into consideration by the observer. Overall, albeit relevant from an ecological point of view, these results underscore the inherent limitations associated with investigating pain expression decoding through spontaneous pain reactions, and call for alternative approaches where AU information is administered in a randomized (or pseudo-randomized) fashion.

Study 2: Synthetic Expressions

We ran a new experiment to complement existing findings, which was inspired by well-known reverse correlation paradigms, used to investigate people's representation of facial features informative of pain [10, 11]. This approach revealed that pain attributions are driven by facial information around brows, nose and upper lip [10, 11], without providing insight into the underlying musculature. Furthermore, these paradigms rely on applying random noise patches to the same face, hence providing little information on whether the results observed are generalizable across different identities.

To overcome these limitations we carried out an experiment where we adapted the reverse correlation paradigm using FACSGen, a validated facial synthesis tool [36] in which AUs can be parameterized to generate photorealistic synthetic facial expressions. Rather than applying random noise patches to a predefined face, we manipulated FACSGen parameters in order to obtain pictures where AU engagement was pseudo-randomized and applied to different identities of both sexes (Figure 2A, for an example).

Methods

Participants

This cohort was composed of N = 63 individuals (40 females, 22 males, 1 sex non-reported; mean age = 25.65 ± 4.17 years). All participants provided written informed consent prior to the experiment and were compensated for their time. The study was conducted at the Center for Mind/Brain Sciences (CIMeC) of the University of Trento, in accordance with the local regulations and the "Comitato Etico per la Ricerca" of the University of Trento.

Facial Expressions

The facial expression stimuli used in this study were generated using FACSGen software [36] to create synthetic expressions with predefined AU engagements. In particular, in FACSGen each AU is implemented with its own independent "blend shape", which was in turn sculpted by graphic designers (the company "Trait d'Esprit", http://www.traitdesprit.ch/) on the basis of reference pictures and videos, and interactive validation with certified FACS coders [36]. Here, we created an initial picture database of 400 facial expressions, from 20 distinct identities (10 males and 10 females) each associated with 20 unique facial configurations. Each picture was created with a unique pseudorandomized set of AU engagements, where each blend shape can be assigned an index ranging from 0 (not engaged at all) to 1 (full facial response). It is important to underscore that AU blend shapes were created on an individual basis, and subsequently mixed together using linear interpolation to obtain a full facial expression. It is possible that combining different (independently validated) blend shapes might lead to implausible expressions, especially for AUs with opposing effects (e.g., lip pucker AU 18 and lip opener AU 20) which seldom occur in the same expression.

To minimize this possibility, AU configurations were generated using constrained pseudo randomization. First, we focused on a set of 23 AUs (i.e., 1-2, 4-7, 9-12, 14-18, 20, 22-27 & 43) based on those available in the FACSGen software. For each picture, we randomly chose two AUs to be fully engaged (1), two partially engaged (0.6) and one minimally engaged (0.1), whereas all the others were set to 0. This procedure ensured that all stimuli were matched in terms of total activation (as assigned by the software) while varying its allocation across facial movements. We then discarded the configurations with co-engagement of AUs with opposing effects (e.g., AUs 1-2 & 4, AUs 12 & 15, AUs 18 & 20). The only exception was the co-engagement of AUs 1 & 4, as FACSGen has an ad hoc blend shape which combines the two movements in a plausible fashion. In this specific case, if these AUs were co-engaged under the same parameters (e.g., both at 0.6), the random configuration was not discarded. All discarded images were replaced with a new randomization until the desired 400 stimuli were obtained. All resulting images were then visually inspected to ensure they represented plausible facial expressions.

Task & Procedure

Participants completed a two-alternative forced-choice task implemented in MATLAB using the Psychtoolbox library. On each trial, two randomly selected facial expressions from the same identity were simultaneously presented side by side on a computer screen. Participants were asked "which face displays the higher degree of pain?" and were instructed to answer by pressing either the left or right arrow key on the keyboard (Figure 2B). Prior to the main task, participants completed a brief training phase to familiarize themselves with the procedure. The main experimental phase consisted of 200 trials in total, corresponding to 10 trials for each of the 20 different identities. Reaction times and selected responses were recorded for each trial. The overall experimental session lasted approximately 15 minutes.

Data Analysis

While Study 1 included realistic expressions with naturally co-engaged AUs, here each specific facial movement was pseudo-randomly parameterized. Hence, we did not combine those AUs underlying common macro-movements. Instead, each of the 23 AUs was analyzed as independent predictor.

Univariate Analysis.

Each trial is characterized by the presence of two randomly-selected pictures from the same identity, each associated with a unique facial configuration of AU engagements. In all analyses, AU parameters (originally recorded as a proportion ranging from 0 to 1) were logittransformed following the same procedure as in Study 1. In this framework, to establish whether any of the 23 AUs were predictive of participants' judgments, we considered right/left choices as a dichotomous dependent variable: specifically, trials where the right picture was deemed more painful were labeled as "1", whereas trials the left picture was deemed more painful were labeled as "0". As main predictor of interest, we considered the differential AU parameters obtained by subtracting the values associated with the left stimulus from those associated with the right stimulus. This resulted in a differential facial configuration, with positive values reflecting those AUs more engaged in the right face, and negative values reflecting those AUs more engaged in the left face. We ran Generalized LMMs with a binomial distribution and Laplace approximation, with choice as dependent variable, differential AU, Sex and FaceSex as fixed factors, and the identity of participants and of the displayed person as random factors (see Supplementary Information for full random structure). Significance of fixed effects was established through Type II Analysis of Deviance and Wald 𝜒 2 statistics as for Study 1. This model was repeated for all 23 AUs, and effects were considered significant if associated with an 𝛼 < .05 Bonferroni corrected for multiple comparisons.

AU co-engagement analysis.

We also assessed potential co-engagement effects, that is, AU combinations that were more likely to trigger pain classification when engaged together. This analysis was carried out through the same Generalized LMM approach described above, except that we did not model only information about one AU. Instead, we modeled simultaneously the differential parameters of two separate AUs, their interaction term (described as the right -left difference of their products) as well as further high-level modulations of Sex/FaceSex (see Supplementary Information for full details). The interaction between the two AUs was the main predictor of interest in the co-engagement analysis. If two AUs contribute to pain judgments in a totally additive fashion, then the interaction term should be = 0. Positive interactions suggest that AUs co-presence further increases the likelihood of pain classifications beyond the contribution of each AU alone. Negative interactions suggest instead a sub-additive integration. The analysis was carried out for every possible AU combination (253 potential pairings), although due to the pseudo-randomization procedure implemented for stimuli generation, some AUs never co-occurred (see above) and only 246 pairings were conducted. Results were considered significant if they survived Bonferroni correction for all repeated tests.

Multivariate Pattern Analysis.

As for Study 1, we exploited a multivariate Random Forest (RF) algorithm to identify whether participants' responses could be predicted by combined facial information [34]. Specifically, in this case, we exploited RF classification to distinguish chosen images from unchosen ones. The classification pipeline was identical to that described for Study 1.

Results

Univariate Analysis

Figure 3A displays significant effects which survive rigorous correction for multiple comparisons. Most notably, faces with stronger engagement in AUs 1, 4, 9-10, 11, 15 and 27 were systematically more likely to be selected as painful. Instead, faces with stronger engagement in AUs 12, 14 and 18 were systematically more likely to be discarded. Figure 3B-C displays effects associated with FaceSex*AU interaction, highlighting AU influences that were more pronounced in male faces at the level of nose wrinkler (AU 9) and lip funneler (AU 22, Figure 3C, left subplot). Instead, female faces were classified as more painful if they displayed engagements of the lip stretcher (AU 20) and eye closure (AU 43, Figure 3C, right subplot). Other facial responses were also implicated (AUs 18, 25 and 26) although in this case effects were interpretable in terms of differences in the facial information associated with lower pain judgments (Figure 3C, middle subplot, see also Supplementary Figure 6 for all AU plots). Finally, no effects of Sex*AU or FaceSex*Sex*AU were found (see Supplementary Table IV for full statistics).

Co-engagement Analysis

We also ran a co-engagement analysis to assess whether people's choices could be best explained by combined information from two AUs. Figure 4A displays the pairs associated with interactions surviving correction for multiple comparisons in network format. Positive effects (green edges) reflect AUs associated with increased classification likelihood when paired together: AUs 7-43 (see Figure 4B, top subplot), 2-22, 6-26, and 1-17 (Supplementary Figure 7). Negative interactions also emerged (Figure 4A, red edges), indicating that some AUs contributed less to pain judgments when accompanied by another AU. For example, the contribution of AU 27 was weaker at higher AU 10 engagement (Figure 4B, middle subplot), as was that of AU4 at higher AU 9 engagement (Figure 4B, bottom subplot). Similar patterns emerged for AU11 paired with AU 16 or AU 20, and for AU 7 or AU 10 paired with AU 12 (Supplementary Figure 8). We also investigated potential modulatory effects of FaceSex, by testing the interaction between this factor and the AUs pairings. Figure 5A reports full results in network format. Although the interpretation of such high-order interactions is often challenging, several AU pairings are clearly associated with increased classification likelihood for male faces: this is the case of AUs 2-6 (see Figure 5B, top subplot), but also AUs 2-17, 6-11, 7-11, 7-18, 10-24 (Supplementary Figure 9). Negative effects were also found: e.g. AU 4 contributions decreased when associated with a female face with strong AU 20 engagement (Figure 5B, bottom red subplot). Likewise, AU 1 effects are attenuated when associated with male faces with strong AU 25 engagement (Figure 5B, bottom green subplot; see Supplementary Figures 9 10for more details about other AU pairings). Overall, the sex of the displayed face changes the degree to which pairs of AU interact with one another, with male faces associated with multiple co-engagement effects, and both male and female faces showing their unique negative patterns. Finally, we tested modulations associated with Sex or Sex*FaceSex interactions, but found no suprathreshold effects.

Multivariate Analysis

Finally, in line with the retrospective analysis, we trained a Random Forest (RF) classification algorithm to predict participants' choices from facial information. Cross-validated predictive accuracy was 64.50% [permutation-based cut-off = 51.11, 𝑝 < 0.001], and sensitivity to pain decision was 𝑑 ′ = 0.75 [cut-off = 0.05, 𝑝 < 0.001]. We also repeated the analysis by including FaceSex and Sex, which, however, had a negligible impact on the model predictive proficiency (Accuracy = 64.30% [cut-off = 51.10], 𝑑 ′ = 0.74 [cut-off = 0.05]). The analysis of feature importance (Supplementary Figure 11) argue that FaceSex (but not Sex) contributed to the prediction with higher proficiency than chance, albeit with low ranking compared to many AUs.

Discussion

This study provides novel, convergent evidence that pain judgments rely on a distributed set of observable facial muscle contractions (Action Units, AUs). Combining a retrospective analysis of spontaneous pain expressions across three different cohorts (Study 1) with a reverse correlation experiment using synthetic facial stimuli (Study 2), we confirmed the key contribution of inner brow raising (AU 1), brow lowering (AU 4), nose wrinkling (AU 9), upper lip raising (AU 10), lip corner depressor (AU 15) and mouth opening (AUs 25-26 in Study 1; AU 27 in Study 2) to pain assessment. Multivariate pattern analysis indicated that pain judgments rely on a distributed set of facial AUs, partially consistent across cohorts (Study 1). Co-engagement analysis (Study 2) suggests that pain classifications are also explainable in terms of AU combinations (e.g., AU 7-43), rather than isolated facial responses, alongside evidence that some AUs interfere with each other's contributions (e.g., AUs 4-9, 10-27). To our knowledge this is the first investigation of the mental representation of pain expressions through the lens of the Facial Action Coding System [4], showing that individuals draw on pain-relevant information flexibly, exploiting distinct, partly-redundant patterns of facial responses.

In Study 2, positive univariate effects of AUs 1, 4, 9-10, 15, and 27 (Figure 3A) mirror those from Study 1 (Figure 1) for AUs 1-2, 9-10, 15, 25-26 (Cohorts 1-2), and 4 (Cohort 3). These data converge with encoding research on pain expressions, as many of these AUs were part of the early model proposed by Prkachin [6] later confirmed by a meta-analysis of experimental and clinical studies [5], as well as in research investigating variations in pain expressions [7, 8]. Study 2 also identified negative associations (not observed in Study 1), with AUs 5, 12, 14, 18, 23-25 systematically more engaged in faces that participants judged as less painful (Figure 3A). These data converge with prior research investigating the mental representation of pain by applying visual degradation to face stimuli [10] [11] [12], showing that participants systematically attend to the brow, nose, and mouth regions when interpreting painful expressions. Most critically, however, our results add interpretative value to this previous research, by shifting the focus from facial regions to the underlying muscle contractions, and disentangling local information that points to higher pain from that pointing to lower pain. Indeed, while we confirm the key role of the mouth region from the literature [10] [11] [12], we distinguish AU 27 (mouth stretch) that increases classification likelihood, from a wide range of alternative mouth movements (AUs 12, 14, 18, 23-25) that instead decrease it. Particular attention should be paid to the case of mouth aperture: in the retrospective analysis, this movement was modeled through the combined information of many AUs (see Methods of Study 1), consistently with prior encoding research that has difficulty separating the effects of lip parter (AU 25), jaw drop (AU 26) and mouth stretch (AU 27) [5, 7, 8]. In Study 2, however, we could build synthetic expressions in which each of these AUs were parameterized pseudo-randomly, and could determine that it is the mouth stretcher (AU 27) that drives the decisions the most. Paradoxically, in Study 2 AU 25 is associated with a negative main effect (opposite to mouth aperture effects in Study 1), suggesting that, when presented alone, lip separation does not contribute to, but rather undermines, pain classification.

Importantly, the role of many AUs goes beyond that of a simple main effect, but is conditional on the movements of other parts of the face. This was observed in the co-engagement analysis (Study 2) that provides a first mapping of the AU combinations that influence pain classification (Figure 4A). Such an approach could not be done meaningfully in Study 1. Despite their strong ecological relevance, real spontaneous expressions are characterized by systematic AU co-engagement patterns related to pain experience [5, 7, 8]. However, if many facial movements are all part of the same expressions, it becomes difficult to ascertain which are taken into consideration by the observer, and how the different sources of information interact with one another. In this perspective, by devising stimuli that do not match typical pain response patterns but follow pseudo-random configurations, Study 2 allows us to explore the role of each AU in a more reliable fashion. Hence, it is not surprising that, in Study 1, AUs like lid tightener (AUs 6-7) and eye closure were described as main effects (Figure 1), but in Study 2 the role of AUs 7 and 43 is redefined in terms of co-engagement, as it is their combination that increased classification likelihood (Figure 4B, top subplot). Most interestingly, we also found negative co-engagements, such as those between AUs 4 & 9 and AUs 10 & 27. Albeit all these facial movements are reliable signs of pain (as outlined by the main effect in Figure 3A), their combination attenuates classification likelihood in comparison to the sum of their isolated effects (Figure 4B). Other AU pairings also allow for a similar interpretation (e.g., 11 & 16, supplementary Figure 8) underscoring the variability and redundancy of facial information, and observers' ability to flexibly assess pain from a wide range of face muscles that sometimes interfere with one another in the information they provide.

Study 2 also provides a comprehensive mapping on the degree to which the sex of the observed person influences the facial information taken into consideration, both in the main univariate model and the follow-up co-engagement analysis. On the one hand, we identified malepreferential (AUs 9 and 22) and female-preferential (AUs 20 and 43) facial responses (Figure 3B-C). On the other hand, we identified several co-engagement patterns that were male-preferential (Figure 5B, top subplot; see also Supplementary Figure 9), as well as both male-and female-preferential negative co-engagement effects (Figure 5B, low subplots; Supplementary Figures 9 10). It has been recently suggested that individuals attribute more masculine traits to pain expressions [37], possibly reflecting the fact that pain representation is male dominant. Our findings are not necessarily incompatible with this interpretation, although the presence of female-preferential AU patterns speaks more in favor of variability and flexibility in the decoding strategy, with partially different facial information considered relevant for male and female expressions. It could be tempting to relate our findings to prior evidence of sex disparities in clinical settings [13] [14] [15] [16], as caregivers might interpret female pain through an ad hoc facial template that makes them more prone to underestimating symptoms. Albeit this is an intriguing possibility, clinical pain assessment is not based exclusively on facial decoding, but on a comprehensive evaluation of behavioral reactions, verbal reports and contextual information. As such, studies focusing exclusively on the assessment of facial expressions yield mixed findings regarding sex disparities [18] [19] [20] [21] [22].

It is important also to underscore that Study 1 describes AU modulations that were conditional to the sex of the observed face. These, however, were inconsistent between the different cohorts as well as with those observed in Study 2. The variability in Study 1 effects most likely reflects idiosyncratic properties of the three databases, such as orientation and luminosity, as well as the inter-individual differences in pain sensitivity and responsiveness. In this perspective, the synthetic stimuli from Study 2 provide more reliable AU effects, as the software used was optimized to provide clearly recognizable AU engagements regardless of the face to which they are applied [36]. Still, it is important to underscore that multivariate Random Forest classification in Study 2 confirms that the inclusion of FaceSex had trivial impact on the overall prediction of participants' choices. Likewise, feature importance analysis (Supplementary Figure 11B) confirms that this factor contributes to the classification prediction with higher reliability than chance, but with low ranking compared to the other features. Hence, although Study 2 describes reliable modulations of FaceSex in the GLMMs, its overall impact in participants' decisions should not be overstated.

Emotion research has repeatedly debated whether affective inference from facial reactions relies on prototypical template-matching or, alternatively, on decomposing the expression into idiosyncratic and variable combinations of emotion constituents, such as contextual appraisals [38]. Caution is warranted in extending this debate to pain, which previous studies have rarely addressed directly. This being said, our findings point to flexibility and variability in facial decoding strategies, with multiple AUs interfering with one another in the degree to which they convey pain information, and to an extent also sex-preferential facial patterns. Hence, pain decoding appears to rely on multiple, redundant face patterns, something that is more in line with a multicomponential organization of the pain response. Interestingly, multi-componential accounts could offer an explanation as to why participants focus on some facial cues over others. Indeed, prior work links AU 1 engagement and mouth opening to appraisals of novelty, and AUs 4, 9 and 15 engagement to appraisals of unpleasantness and low control [39] [40] [41], suggesting that participants may have read facial signs that are informative of something "bad" or "sudden". As these assessments are not specific to pain, future studies should test whether these findings generalize to other affective experiences.

Despite its advantages, the reverse correlation task implemented here has some limitations. First, the FACSGen software [36] used to generate the stimuli was optimized for isolated AU engagements and emotional expressions, rather than random AU parameter assignments. To minimize potential artifacts, we used constrained pseudo-randomization to avoid selected combinations of facial movements with opposing effects and visually checked that each stimulus represented a plausible facial expression (see Methods). However, some AU combinations may appear different on synthetic avatars than on real faces. Second, to minimize the risk that participants' choices were driven by overall engagement (rather than specific facial configurations), all stimuli in Study 2 were created with matched total AU activation parameters, while varying its allocation across facial movements. However, controlling overall activation during stimulus generation does not necessarily ensure that perceived expressiveness or the magnitude of visible movement is matched. Furthermore, allocating activation to one AU necessarily restricted its allocation to others, introducing dependencies among AUs. Consequently, the contribution of each AU to pain classifications should not be interpreted in absolute terms, but rather in relation to all other available facial information.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our study provides a first clear mapping of the facial muscle contractions that drive individual pain judgments. These findings provide strong support for the idea that pain decoding is represented flexibly, relying on variable, partly-redundant patterns of facial responses.

In summary, 68 individuals (aged 18-61) were recorded under standardized laboratory conditions while displaying four types of facial expressions: neutral, simulated pain, and two types of spontaneous pain expressions elicited through distinct nociceptive procedures: tonic pressure stimulation via an algometer and phasic heat stimulation via a CO 2 laser. From these recordings, short dynamic video-clips of 1 sec were extracted to capture the peak of the painful expression, resulting in a database of 272 validated micro-clips, which have been normatively rated for pain intensity, valence, and arousal by over 500 participants from multiple European universities. For the purpose of this analysis, we focused on data from a subsample of 28 video-clips displaying spontaneous pain expressions as induced by either an algometer (11 clips) or CO 2 laser-stimulations (17 clips). All these clips were edited to be 1 sec long and sound free. Participants were exposed also to simulated pain expressions (all sub-cohorts) and neutral expressions (sub-cohort 3.1 & 3.4), although these were not taken into consideration in keeping with Cohorts 1-2.

3) Task: Participants viewed each video clip and subsequently assessed the facial expression according to two dimensions: pain intensity and authenticity. The first question was: "Rate how much pain you perceive in the expression on a scale from 1 (no pain) to 10 (extreme pain)". The second question was: "Rate how authentic the expression seems on a scale from 1 (not authentic at all) to 10 (completely authentic)". In sub-cohort 3.2, 3.3 and 3.4, the videos were presented following a predictive cue of variable intensity and uncertainty. However, the role of this predictive information was not taken into account in the present study. As for the other cohorts, the potential confounding effects in the experimental design were controlled during the analysis.

II. Comparison between human and algorithm-based assessment facial Action Units coding for Study 1

To establish that OpenFace estimates of AU engagement were indeed comparable to that from certified human FACS scorers, we ran a validation analysis for each database. For cohort 1, two certified FACS scorers evaluated a subgroup of short clips (6 painful, 6 disgusting and 6 neutral) by selecting AU onset/offset time and the intensity (on a scale from 1 to 5). Full details about the human scoring procedure are provided in Dirupo et al. [4]. For cohort 2, full certified human FACS coding was available for the videos of the UNBC-McMaster Shoulder Pain Expression Archive Database [6]. As for the PEMF database video-clip used in cohort 3 [7], these were manually assessed by six independent coders trained to identify AUs commonly associated with pain expressions. Differently from cohorts 1-2 where scoring occurred on a frame-by-frame basis and included both information about engagement and estimated intensity, here coders only assessed AU occurrence. Furthermore, this procedure was conducted at the whole-clip level, meaning coders judged the overall presence of AUs across the entire video, rather than in each isolated frame. Within this framework, an AU was considered as engaged if at least four out of the six coders agreed on its occurrence [7].

We then compared the human scores with OpenFace output from the same clip subselection. In cohorts 1-2 clips, for each AU, and for each video, we assessed the consistency between the time-course of the human scoring and OpenFace output. Furthermore, we followed studies showing the benefit of combining AUs underlying similar facial movements together, both when assessing the reliability of algorithms for facial feature extraction [8], and in the human coding of the facial expression of pain [9]- [11]. Hence, we decided to treat as equivalent facial AUs related to the eyebrows raising (AUs 1 & 2), eye narrowing (AUs 6 & 7), nose wrinkling (AUs 9 & 10), lips tightening/pressing (AUs 23 & 24), mouth opening (AUs 25, 26 & 27) and eye closing/blinks (AUs 43 & 45). Please note that, whereas human coding provides estimates of any potential AUs, OpenFace output is constrained to an AU subselection, and specifically has no estimates of AUs 24, 27 and 43. Thus, while human coding of mouth opening was based on AUs 25, 26 and 27, OpenFace homologue is based only on AUs 25 & 26 (AU 27 movements are likely classified by the algorithm as either 25 or 26). In a similar vein, whereas human coding of lips tightening is obtained from AUs 23/24, OpenFace homologue is derived on AU23. Finally, whereas human coding of eye closure/blinks is obtained by combining both AUs 43/45, OpenFace homologue is based only on AU 45 (the algorithm most likely does not distinguish between eye closures and blinks).

In Cohort 3, where human FACS scoring was not carried out at a frame-by-frame level, we took comprehensive measure of each algorithm-based AU across the whole clip (the Area under the curve and, for AU 45, the percentage engagement within the whole clip) and modelled as a function of human-scored engagement from the original study. We then ran a Linear Mixed Model (LMM) with the OpenFace estimates as dependent variable, the intensity of the homologous AUs information from FACS scorers as continuous predictor, and the identity of the participant in the video as random factor (with random intercept and slope for the human score). A simpler random structure (characterised only by random intercept) was chosen in case of nonconvergence. Given that in cohorts 1-2, the prediction of OpenFace estimates of AU 45 was expressed as a dichotomic variable (reflecting engagement vs. non-engagement), we exploited instead a Generalized LMM with a binomial distribution and Laplace approximation. The LMMs were run following the same processing pipeline described in the main text.

Supplementary Figure 1 reports the results of the analysis in matrix form, with each cell reflecting the prediction of one OpenFace AU from a human scored one. Effects highlighted are considered statistically significant. Predictions from homologous AUs (e.g., Open Face AU 4 estimate predicted by human scored AU 4 engagement) were considered significant if associated with α ≤ 0.05 one-tailed with the hypothesis constrained for positive relationships. All other predictions were considered significant if associated with α ≤ 0.05 two-tailed with no directional hypothesis. Overall, this validation analysis shows good agreement between human FACS coding and OpenFace output for homologous AUs. The only notable exceptions were AUs 1/2, 6/7 and 23 (Cohort 1) and AU 20 (Cohort 3) for which OpenFace estimates could not be predicted by homologous human scores. Additionally, many cross-predictions between dissimilar AUs are observed, with both negative and positive directions (see Dirupo et al., [4] for in-depth commentary on this issue).

We tested the influence played by AU engagement in pain assessment. This was achieved by running the (G)LMMs each time with a different AU estimate as within-subjects predictor. We also tested potential interactions between AU and Sex/FaceSex. Supplementary Table I reports detailed results, which were quite idiosyncratic to the specific Cohort. In particular, the analysis of FaceSex*AU interaction revealed that AUs classification effects in Cohort 1 were more pronounced in female faces, and the same was the case for unpleasantess rating, limitedly for AUs 6-7. In all cases, male participants rely to a lesser extent than females on information from these facial responses (Supplementary Figure 2). Finally, the analysis of the three-way Sex*FaceSex*AU interaction revealed only an effect associated with AU 14 for pain classification in Cohort 1 (χ 2 (1) = 17.92, corrected p < 0.001, reflecting the fact that female participants rely to a larger extent on this facial information when observing an expression of the same sex (Supplementary Figure 3).

3) Task: Participants were engaged in two tasks. The second was a rating task, where they were asked to quantify the degree of unpleasantness experienced by the person in the video. The evaluation happened through a visual analogue scale (VAS) where the two extremities were labeled as "neutral" and "extremely unpleasant", and a cursor could be moved along the scale by pressing two different keys. In sub-cohorts 1.1 & 1.5 the two tasks were implemented sequentially, namely participants first saw the facial expressions, then classified it and subsequently rated the associated unpleasantness. In sub-cohorts 1.2 & 1.4 the two tasks were administered as separate sessions (balanced in order). In sub-cohort 1.3 only the unpleasantness rating was implemented. Finally, some sub-cohorts were characterized by idiosyncratic manipulation testing for social (sub-cohort 1.5) or contextual (sub-cohorts 1.2 & 1.3) influences.
