Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Autistic adults are under-investigated in psychedelic research, despite speculated elevated likelihood of substance use for therapeutic and recreational purposes.

This study explored contexts of naturalistic psychedelic use and wellbeing and mental health indicators in a group of Autistic adults using psychedelics for therapeutic purposes, and compared these with the broader population of naturalistic psychedelic users.

Secondary analysis of Global Psychedelic Survey 2023 data (N = 6,378) was undertaken with a sub-sample of naturalistic psychedelic users who self-indicated being Autistic adults who used psychedelics therapeutically. A convergent parallel mixed-methods study design investigated quantitative between-group differences in contexts of naturalistic psychedelic use and mental health indicators, and explored qualitative implications for wellbeing in the Autistic sub-sample.

Compared with other psychedelic users (n = 6,166), the Autistic sub-sample (n = 212) reported higher rates of lifetime use of less common psychedelic substances and use in structured therapeutic settings, lower perceived psychedelic knowledge and experience, and greater prevalence of persisting hallucinations (65.09%) and clinically significant symptoms of anxiety (57.35%) and depression (60.66%) at the time of survey completion. Concurrently, three qualitative themes described largely beneficial, yet context-dependent implications for wellbeing within the Autistic sub-sample: Shifting Perspectives, Therapeutic Utility, and Conditional Affordances.

Integrated mixed-methods findings highlighted that wellbeing-related affordances of psychedelic use in the Autistic sub-sample were sometimes contingent on context and support, and adverse mental health indicators could co-exist with personal growth. Future research should further evaluate quality-of-life and adverse outcomes from psychedelic use in Autistic populations.

[div-01]

The modern resurgence in psychedelic science has fostered growing clinical and community interest worldwide regarding the therapeutic properties of psychedelic substances. Whilst legal and affordable access to psychedelic-assisted therapies remains limited globally, therapeutic motivation is frequently reported within naturalistic or 'real-world' psychedelic use contexts (Lake and Lucas, 2024). However, understanding the therapeutic potential and safety of psychedelic substances across historically under-represented populations remains limited, due to systemic exclusion and limited sub-group analyses in clinical trials, and reduced focus within naturalistic investigations (Jacobs et al., 2024). Autistic adults represent one such under-investigated population (Imoh et al., 2025), with evidence suggesting an elevated likelihood of endorsing therapeutic motives for naturalistic substance use more broadly, including psychedelics (Weir et al., 2021).

Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition characterised by differences in social communication, sensory processing, and cognition (Bottini et al., 2024). Autism is increasingly conceptualised through a neuroaffirming lens, which positions autism not as a pathology requiring treatment or cure, but as a meaningful expression of human neurological diversity (Hunt and Procyshyn, 2024; Walker and Raymaker, 2021).

Investigating psychedelic use among Autistic adults through a neuro-affirming lens requires including Autistic lived experience and moving beyond deficit-based models that frame autism as pathology, instead evaluating therapeutic outcomes in relation to wellbeing.

Autistic adults experience disproportionately high rates of co-occurring mental health conditions and barriers to accessing autism-informed supports (Camm-Crosbie et al., 2019; Lai et al., 2019). Conventional mental health interventions demonstrate reduced efficacy or accessibility for Autistic adults (Curnow et al., 2023; El Baou et al., 2023; Oakley et al., 2021). These systemic gaps may underlie increased engagement with alternative therapeutic pathways, including naturalistic substance use (Weir et al., 2021).

Research suggests that psychedelics may hold therapeutic promise for Autistic adults' wellbeing, in addressing anxiety, distress, and psychological flexibility (Afsharnia et al., 2025; Danforth et al., 2018; Stroud et al., 2025). However, further investigation of risk and safety in this population is necessary, due to evidence indicative of adverse outcomes impacting a substantial minority of Autistic adults within naturalistic psychedelic use contexts (Stroud et al., 2025). Improved understanding of the contexts, risks, and affordances of psychedelic use among Autistic adults is critical to informing harm minimisation strategies and supporting equitable access to psychedelic-assisted therapies.

Despite growing interest in the intersection of psychedelics and autism, few studies have systematically characterised naturalistic therapeutic psychedelic use among Autistic adults or considered the first-person perspectives of Autistic psychedelic users (Afsharnia et al., 2025; Stroud et al., 2025). In particular, it remains unclear whether Autistic individuals demonstrate distinct patterns of psychedelic substance use, motivations, contextual factors, risks and benefits compared to the broader psychedelic user population. This exploratory mixed-methods study sought to address these gaps through investigation of data from a large-scale global survey.

Specifically, this study explored the following questions in a group of self-identified Autistic adults who reported engaging in naturalistic therapeutic psychedelic use: 1) What are the contexts of naturalistic psychedelic use?

2) How do wellbeing and mental health indicators present in the context of naturalistic psychedelic use?

3) Are there meaningful differences in the contexts of naturalistic psychedelic use and mental health indicators compared with the broader population?

METHODS

Data was collected from May 19 to June 2, 2023, through the Global Psychedelic Survey (GPS) 2023a cross-sectional online study of English-speaking adults aged ≥ 21 (n = 6,378). Self-reported use of 11 substances was targeted, including 'classic' (ayahuasca, N,N-DMT / 5-MeO-DMT, LSD, mescaline, psilocybin), and 'atypical' psychedelics (2C-B, ibogaine / iboga, ketamine, MDMA / MDA, nitrous oxide, Salvia divinorum).

Survey development occurred collaboratively across North American, European and Australian institutions, with sponsorship from various non-government organisations. The study was reviewed by Advarra (Protocol # Pro00071490), which oversaw Canadian participants and classified the broader study as exempt from oversight in other jurisdictions under Department of Health and Human Services regulation 45 CFR 46.104(d). Secondary data analysis received approval from the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (MUHREC # 47832). The secondary analysis reported in this paper was pre-registered through the Open Science Framework (OSF) in full before statistical analysis (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/Z8PKB).

Sample

The Autistic sub-sample was derived from all participants who selected 'Autism spectrum disorder' within a survey item asking, "which physical health condition have you treated with psychedelics" (n = 212). The remaining overall sample (n = 6,166), which served as the comparison group, therefore cannot be conclusively identified as a 'non-Autistic' group, but rather a representation of the broader population of naturalistic psychedelic users who did not report using psychedelics for 'Autism spectrum disorder'. This distinction has implications for interpreting between-group comparisons throughout this study and is explored further as part of study limitations within the discussion. OSF pre-registration reported a sample size of 213 for the Autistic subsample; however, one duplicate response was subsequently identified during analysis and removed.

Neuro-Affirming Language

To address discrepancies between the survey item wording and this study's neuro-affirming lens, consultation was undertaken with six Autistic individuals working across research, healthcare, education, and advocacy. Consultees endorsed the use of the term 'self-identified Autistic adults who reported engaging in therapeutic psychedelic use' to describe participants in a neuro-affirming manner without altering the reason for their inclusion.

Measures

All GPS 2023 survey items described below are provided within supplementary material. In addition to demographic characteristics, the following measures were analysed.

Psychedelic Use Contexts

Lifetime psychedelic substance use was assessed using a single item, with responses dichotomously coded into variables for each substance. Dosage was captured through a single item indicating use of regular doses, microdoses, or both across lifetime, and responses were aggregated into two dichotomous variables reflecting lifetime regular dose use and lifetime microdose use of any psychedelic. Past-year frequency of psychedelic use was assessed via two single items asking whether psychedelics had been used in the past 12 months and, if so, how frequently. Responses to both items were coded into a single variable with eight ordinal categories (1 = Not in the past year,8 = Daily).

Self-reported exposure to psychedelics was assessed via two single items respectively asking participants to rate their "current level of knowledge" or "experience" with psychedelic substances on a scale from 0-100, with higher values indicating greater knowledge or experience. Motivations aside from therapeutic use (e.g., personal growth or self-exploration) were assessed through a single item, with responses coded into dichotomous variables per motivation.

Social and environmental settings were each assessed through single items asking participants, "with whom" and "in which setting" they typically used "regular doses" of different psychedelic substances. The variable structure planned and reported within OSF pre-registration was flagged by analysis software for potential approximation inaccuracies when the proposed statistical tests were run, so these variables were re-structured to allow for more accurate analysis. Responses were instead aggregated across substances and re-coded into dichotomous variables for each type of social and environmental setting (e.g., use of any psychedelic substance indoors in a familiar setting: yes = 1, no = 0).

Wellbeing and Mental Health Measures

Mental health at the time of survey completion was assessed using the 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7) and 8-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8), measuring past two weeks' self-reported anxiety and depression symptoms, respectively. Total scores ≥ 10 on the GAD-7 or PHQ-8 indicated clinically significant (moderate-to-severe) symptoms of anxiety (Spitzer et al., 2006) or depression (Kroenke et al., 2009), respectively. The GAD-7 and PHQ-8 were not framed in relation to psychedelic use but were included in this study to characterise participants' current mental health.

Hallucinations persisting beyond 24 hours after drug effects subsided following any psychedelic use were assessed via three self-reported single items evaluating frequency, duration, and perceived impact. Frequency responses were re-coded into a dichotomous variable characterising endorsed experience of persisting hallucinations (1 = any occurrence;0 = never). Duration (in days) was coded into a single variable with seven ordinal categories (listed from 1 to 7): 1-2 days, 3-7 days, 8-14 days, 15-30 days, 31-60 days, 61-90 days, > 90 days. Perceived impact was rated on a 5-point Likert scale and coded into an ordinal variable (1 = very negative;5 = very positive).

Broader implications of psychedelic experiences upon wellbeing were assessed through responses to three free-text response survey questions. Participants who reported slight, moderate, or strong positive changes to personal wellbeing or life satisfaction following an intense psychedelic experience on a single item using a 7point Likert scale were invited to describe these "specific changes" further. A second item prompted elaboration on "other" specific benefits arising from intense psychedelic experiences. A final item invited any closing reflections before survey completion.

Data Analysis

All quantitative analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). To reflect Autistic phenotypic heterogeneity, all outliers were retained and missing data were addressed using pairwise deletion.

Descriptive statistics characterised psychedelic use contexts and mental health indicators within the Autistic subsample. Between-group (Autistic sub-sample vs. remaining overall sample) differences in demographic characteristics (Table 1) were examined descriptively, without correction for multiple comparisons, using Pearson's chi-square tests for all variables except age, for which an independent-samples t-test was used. All other between-group comparisons addressing the research questions were conducted using Pearson's chi-square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, and Mann-Whitney U tests for ordinal or continuous variables. To control family-wise error, Holm-Bonferroni correction (target α = .05) was applied to all of these between-group comparisons (Giacalone et al., 2018). Effect sizes were reported for significant results, including Cohen's w for chi-square tests and rank-biserial correlation coefficients (rrb) for Mann-Whitney U tests, interpreted as small (0.10-0.29), medium (0.30-0.49), and large (≥ 0.50) (Cohen, 1988), with directionality of effects indicated by whether the value was positive or negative.

Qualitative data were analysed through reflexive thematic analysis as per Braun and Clarke (2006). This process involved 1) familiarisation with the data; 2) three iterative rounds of coding using ATLAS.ti version 25.0.01 (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2025); 3) initial theme generation; 4) theme development and review; 5) naming, defining, and refining themes; and 6) written presentation of findings (Braun and Clarke, 2006). An inductive, iterative approach was adopted within a critical realist framework, whereby responses were interpreted as reflecting both observable experiences and underlying explanatory mechanisms (Botha, 2025). A subset of responses (n = 2) was coded collaboratively by A.S., A.S.R., A.D., and P.Li. Participant quotes were presented using original language to preserve authenticity, with minor grammatical edits to enhance readability. Ellipses '(...)' indicate removed text for brevity.

Attending to researcher positionality is essential within qualitative research to understand how researchers' backgrounds, lived experiences, and disciplinary training have shaped the research process and its findings (Holmes, 2020). The first author, A.S., has lived experience of being an Autistic adult, allowing for a first-person perspective to be actively integrated within analysis and interpretation of Autistic experiences communicated within qualitative data, aligning with a neuro-affirming approach to research. Additionally, the research team includes both researchers and clinicians with backgrounds in the fields of psychedelics and autism, who collectively bring familiarity with the nuances of both the psychedelic and Autistic experience to their interpretation of the data.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings occurred at the interpretation stage, through development of a visual joint display, which allowed systematic side-by-side comparison of quantitative findings and qualitative themes (Guetterman et al., 2015). Quantitative findings were mapped alongside the qualitative theme most analytically relevant to it, and the nature of the relationship between findings was explicitly stated.

Relationship types between quantitative and qualitative findings were reported as per the following definitions: convergence, indicating agreement between findings; divergence, indicating inconsistency or contradiction of findings; complementarity, indicating qualitative findings contextualising rather than contesting quantitative findings; and expansion, indicating qualitative findings exceeding the explanatory reach of quantitative instruments (Skamagki et al., 2024). For each pairing, an integrated meta-inference, which could not have been derived from either quantitative or qualitative analysis alone, was developed.

Psychedelic Use Contexts

All findings regarding naturalistic psychedelic use contexts among self-identified Autistic adults who reported engaging in therapeutic psychedelic use are reported in Table 2. Psilocybin was the most reported psychedelic substance used across lifetime (64.15%), followed by MDMA or MDA (58.02%), and LSD and ketamine (both 53.30%). The Autistic sub-sample differed significantly from the remaining overall sample, with small effect sizes, in lifetime use prevalence of ibogaine or iboga (36.79%, Cohen's w = 0.22), psilocybin (Cohen's w = -0.17), other psychedelic substances (40.09%, Cohen's w = 0.11), and 2C-B (43.40%, Cohen's w = 0.10). A majority endorsed lifetime use of regular (97.64%) or micro (95.28%) doses of any psychedelic substance, and 65.09% reported psychedelic use within the past year.

Among participants from the Autistic sub-sample, the median perceived level of knowledge of psychedelics was just below 'moderate', at 40.00 (IQR = 27.00) and experience with psychedelics was just above 'moderate', at 56.00 (IQR = 11.00). Compared to the remaining overall sample, the Autistic sub-sample reported significantly lower perceived level of knowledge (rrb = -0.55) and experience with psychedelics (rrb = -0.14).

Beyond therapeutic use, the most common motivations for psychedelic use were general wellbeing (44.34%), closely followed by personal growth or self-exploration (43.40%), both of which differed significantly from the remaining overall sample with small effect sizes (Cohen's w = -0.10 and -0.22, respectively). Reduction of use of another substance (26.42%, Cohen's w = 0.05) was the only motivation significantly more prevalent in the Autistic sub-sample, though with negligible effect.

Within the Autistic sub-sample, psychedelic use most commonly occurred with a spouse, companion, or a few friends (46.23%), closely followed by alone (45.28%). The most prevalent environmental setting was indoors in a familiar setting (51.89%). Significant differences, with small effect sizes, were identified relative to the remaining overall sample in environmental settings of a licensed clinic, wellness centre, or hospital (39.15%, Cohen's w = 0.18) and an unregulated therapist or practitioner's office or clinic (32.55%, Cohen's w = 0.14).

Theme 1: Shifting Perspectives This theme illustrates the profoundly transformative ways in which psychedelic experiences facilitated Autistic participants' wellbeing, through the opportunity to shift perspectives and reshape understanding of oneself, one's relationships, and one's place within the world. Sub-Theme 1.1: Reconfiguring and Cherishing the Self The psychedelic experience fostered an opportunity for Autistic adults to experience a renewed sense of self-identity, through a shift from self-criticism, confusion, or disconnection towards self-understanding, selfacceptance, and self-compassion. Psychedelic experiences provided Autistic participants with a medium through which inquiry and reflection upon their conceptualisation of their self-identity could occur. For many, this prompted an epistemic shift through deepened introspection, in which they developed a new, more genuine, and strengths-based understanding of themselves. One participant (38, Female, Canada / US, with no adverse mental health indicators) described gaining a "deeper, more complete understanding of some core 'self' ideas", while another (40, Male, Australia / NZ, with persisting hallucinations, and clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms) shared that he felt like a "totally new person, with new mental abilities and capabilities". This shift away from deficit-focused self-appraisal supported Autistic participants in developing profound and enduring feelings of self-compassion. As one participant (32, Female, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations and clinically significant depression symptoms) reflected, "I had never in my life experienced self love until the first time (and most intense) I used LSD. I felt compassion and empathy for myself and I have carried this with me to this day". For some participants, this compassion was tied specifically to validating their neurodivergent identity, and reframing it as an explanatory lens rather than a flaw. One participant (34, Female, Europe / UK, with persisting hallucinations) shared, "I accepted my neurodiversity and the challenges it brings", while another (25, Male, Canada / US, with no adverse mental health indicators) explained that the experience "led me to recognise how my 25 years of undiagnosed autism was affecting my life and let me examine every memory with a new perspective and love for myself". This process also supported Autistic adults in relinquishing the hold of external judgement and unmasking their true, authentic selves, as noted by one participant (24, Male, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations, and clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms) who explained, "I felt more like myself. That people's judgements didn't matter because in the end, we are all one and are all equal. I stopped caring as much about what others think of me and started being more true to myself". For some, this shift in selfperception translated into greater agency and self-determination in everyday life: one participant (32, Female, Canada / US, with no adverse mental health indicators) shared that "the experience led me to realise I was stagnant in my life. It caused me and my husband to move out of his mom's house and get our own apartment, leading to increased independence and self-sufficiency. It is the single most impactful experience I have ever had".

Ultimately, this sub-theme emphasises the capacity for naturalistic psychedelic experiences to act as a catalyst for enduring, positive reconfiguration of self-identity among Autistic adults.

Sub-Theme 1.2: Cultivating Connection Beyond the Self

Beyond the self, the psychedelic experience offered Autistic adults an enriched sense of connection with others and the surrounding world. Participants described shifts in relational dynamics that facilitated meaningful interpersonal connection for them and opportunities for relational repair. One participant (36, Female, Canada / US, with no adverse mental health indicators) shared that "I was unable to bond with my infant son prior to my ayahuasca journey. After, I felt a sense of compassion and the resentment was gone".

This sense of connection extended beyond interpersonal relationships to the natural world and broader society. Some participants described a deepened connection to nature, like one (31, Female, Canada / US, with no adverse mental health indicators) who reported, "I am truly here to make the world a better place and do small things to keep myself happy, like take hikes and take care of plants". Others described an elevated sense of prosocial responsibility towards the greater good. One participant (44, Non-Binary, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations) described how the psychedelic experience fostered their sense of justice while also helping them disengage healthily when needed, sharing that "my increased empathy for the suffering of others and my understanding of the systemic nature and perpetuation of their suffering have made me more effective in my justice work, and also less overinvested".

For Autistic adults seeking to cultivate connection, this sub-theme suggests that psychedelic experiences can not only deepen understanding of how to do so, but also translate that understanding into sustained action beyond the acute experience itself.

Sub-Theme 1.3: Mystical Experiences

Mystical experiences occasioned through naturalistic psychedelic use served as a vehicle for Autistic adults to experience wellbeing through a shift towards a more contextualised and meaningful existence.

Characterised by ineffability and boundlessness, these experiences left a profound imprint that participants carried with them beyond the acute experience. For some, this manifested as spiritual connection that fostered a sense of purpose and strength. One participant (24, Male, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations) shared that "It was comforting, because the experience had a spiritual nature to it. I felt and still feel more connected to the universe as a spiritual entity". Others described undergoing unitive experience -a profound sense of oneness and interconnection -including one participant (50, Female, Australia / NZ, with clinically significant depression symptoms) who recounted "an experience where my sense of identity as this human vanished for a few seconds and I was simply 'the light'. It was profoundly freeing, joyful, expansive, and peaceful".

These mystical experiences also brought about a sense of profound acceptance and trust in participants' circumstances, fostering drastic reframing of their perspectives on both themselves and their surroundings. The same participant quoted above (50, Female, Australia / NZ, with clinically significant depression symptoms) shared that the mystical experience they underwent helped them "to see my life circumstances as 'right' and 'perfect', despite how difficult and challenging they actually were". Another participant (44, Non-Binary, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations) reflected that, "I have a better sense of my proportions in the universe and feel safe being a tiny and integral part of this beauty".

This sub-theme highlights the propensity of naturalistic psychedelic experiences to facilitate existential wellbeing among Autistic adults through a fundamental shift in their understanding of the nature of existence and their role within it.

Theme 2: Therapeutic Utility

The potential of naturalistic psychedelic substance use to address co-occurring health concerns among Autistic adults was highlighted in Theme 2, with 32 out of 49 participants (65.31%) either describing underlying therapeutic mechanisms or directly endorsing use for a health condition. Here, psychedelics were not only conceptualised as agents of symptom reduction, but also as catalysts for developing skills that support long-term wellbeing.

Sub-Theme 2.1: Disrupting Distress and Relieving Symptoms

Psychedelic use was conceptualised by participants as a means of alleviating distress and symptoms of co-occurring health conditions, particularly where other treatment approaches have lacked efficacy. For many, the psychedelic experience facilitated a rapid and profound shift towards relief and safety, with these affective shifts, at times, described as life-saving. One participant (32, Male, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations) recalled feeling like "everything was going to be okay for the first time in my adult life", while another (44, Non-Binary, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations) stated simply "I no longer want to die".

Beyond this relief, participants described reductions in symptoms of co-occurring health conditions, with enduring changes often attributed to improvements in self-regulation. One participant (44, Male, Europe / UK, with persisting hallucinations) shared that "It has lessened my overall anxiety. Whenever I feel anxious, it's a lot easier to shake it and move on. Before I started using psilocybin, it would take weeks to recover from the smallest incident of anxiety". Similar patterns emerged in the context of addiction, alongside broader behavioural change.

One participant (44, Male, Australia / NZ, with no adverse mental health indicators) reflected, "I detoxed from alcohol, but never actually ever thought that I would be able to sit in a bar or similar environment. After using DMT for about 6 months, I have found that I no longer crave alcohol nor do I mind if people drink around me.

These used to be triggers for me and now as such, have disappeared".

Some participants framed these benefits against a backdrop of conventional treatments that had been ineffective for them. One participant (44, Female, Canada / US, with no adverse mental health indicators), shared that "I have had symptoms of PTSD and treatment resistant depression and have completed medication trials, TMS, and have done a variety of other holistic and traditional psychotherapy treatments…psychedelics have provided the most prolonged symptom relief and least amount of side effects and discomfort; namely psilocybin".

While therapeutic benefit was most discussed in relation to mental health, some participants reported comparable relief for physical health conditions, with one participant (39, Female, Other Catchment Area, with no adverse mental health indicators) indicating that "Knowing about psychedelics completely changed my life. I still cannot believe that after trying drug after drug for the last 18 years or so, I finally found some relief for cluster [headaches]".

Ultimately, participants overwhelmingly reflected on their psychedelic experiences with positive regard and expressed clear acceptance of psychedelic use, with one (34, Female, Europe / UK, with persisting hallucinations) remarking that "I'm not sure how I can express in words how beneficial the few trips I have experienced have been".

Together, findings within this sub-theme highlight the perceived meaningfulness and effectiveness of naturalistic psychedelic use for therapeutic purposes among Autistic adults, and suggest that the relief experienced was qualitatively different from prior support accessed by participants.

Sub-Theme 2.2: Developing Tools and Adaptive Capacity

The psychedelic experience provided a medium through which Autistic participants could develop skills and perspectives to return to and utilise in daily life, supporting maintenance of therapeutic gains beyond the acute experience. For some, this learning was facilitated by embodiment, allowing for development of novel insight through first-hand experiences of 'being' and 'feeling' beyond the confines of traditional therapeutic dialogue. As one participant (43, Male, Europe / UK, with clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms) described, "[Psychedelics] help me to feel that I am one with all things and to experience this as a deep abiding love for everybody and all that is. They melt my preoccupation with myself".

This process was sustained for some through Psychedelic Proximity -an enduring sense of closeness to features of their psychedelic experiences, which participants could revisit as anchors in their daily life. One participant (50, Female, Australia / NZ, with clinically significant depression symptoms) framed a psychedelic experience as "a reference point I carry with me, to this day. This experience happened nearly half a lifetime ago".

This proximity facilitated improved access to adaptive emotional states beyond the acute experience, with another participant (28, Non-Binary, Europe / UK, with no adverse mental health indicators) sharing that "after the experience, I can now more easily tap into that feeling of contentment and gratitude, which makes daily life more manageable".

For other participants, adaptive capacity was cultivated through decentring -the ability to reframe experiences of distress or undesirable affect from a position of greater psychological distance. One participant (34, Female, Europe / UK, with persisting hallucinations) reflected, "I do experience feelings of disappointment and feeling left out in certain social situations, but I employ mindfulness practices more readily and am able to distance myself from the situation, thus gaining a different perspective".

More broadly, participants described the psychedelic experience as fostering greater psychological flexibility and openness, which they perceived positively. As one participant (24, Male, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations) noted, "I became more open minded to what is possible. The experience destroyed my previously determined biases and allowed me to perceive life from a fresh, unadulterated perspective".

Participants also described an improved sense of groundedness and presence extending beyond the acute experience, with one participant (28, Non-Binary, Europe / UK, with no adverse mental health indicators) sharing that "the substances have [taught] me a lot about presence and being in the present. I am Here I am Now".

Ultimately, this sub-theme suggests that the psychedelic experience supported Autistic participants in developing psychological resources that they could draw upon within their everyday lives, facilitating expansion of their toolkit of adaptive skills beyond the boundaries of the acute experience.

Theme 3: Conditional Affordances

This theme describes the conditional nature of affordances that can be reaped through naturalistic psychedelic use for Autistic adults, and the contingencies that can influence outcomes.

Sub-Theme 3.1: Integration Beyond the Trip

Whilst most Autistic participants spoke of the beneficial implications of psychedelic use, some highlighted the precarious nature of integrating knowledge learned within the acute experience into life beyond the trip. The psychedelic experience can prompt recalibration of one's expectations of wellbeing, and this was challenging for some participants to integrate within their everyday lives beyond the 'afterglow' period of the psychedelic experience. One participant (21, Male, Australia / NZ, with clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms) shared that "everyday life does seem a little bit more dull and boring after moments like that. I feel like there's lots more out there. I wish I could microdose on MDMA everyday without any side effects".

For some Autistic adults who experienced enduring Psychedelic Proximity, this did not necessarily support more rapid or effective integration, as it instead became an extended process across several years. This was particularly the case for those who were exposed to challenging content within their acute psychedelic experience, like one participant (46, Female, Canada / US, with clinically significant anxiety symptoms) who shared that "I was 19 (now 46) and experienced ego death without knowing what it was then. Now I see the bigger picture and what I considered the worst bad trip of my life started to make sense as a middle aged adult".

However, these perspectives did not present psychedelic use as being an innately negative experience and highlighted the importance of integration in being able to support affordances through the psychedelic experience. Importantly, integration was discussed as being a process requiring active engagement and commitment, as noted by one participant (30, Other Gender, Canada / US, with persisting hallucinations, and clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms) who reflected that they "reunified my personal

beliefs. Took ten years of devout study and introspection on my trip though".

This sub-theme highlights that meaning-making and adapting to the knowledge and insights gained through the psychedelic experience can be a challenging, effortful, and extended process for some Autistic participants, though an important facilitator of affordances.

Sub-Theme 3.2: Contextual Contingencies

Autistic participants highlighted the role of context in shaping outcomes of naturalistic psychedelic use, whether these be risks or affordances. These contexts were reflected at individual, environmental, and systemic levels. Some Autistic adults highlighted the potential influence of individual characteristics upon the ability to experience affordances from the psychedelic experience. This reflected a need to evaluate the suitability of psychedelic use on a case-by-case basis, as epitomised by one participant (34, Female, Canada / US, with clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms) who shared, "I have C-PTSD and diagnosis of Autism, ADHD, [Bipolar II Disorder]. It was offered to me to support healing, but I don't think it was properly suited for my mind and body. I stayed in the experience much longer than anyone else in the room had experienced". Another participant (63, Male, Australia / NZ, with clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms) also cited concern "about the effects [of psychedelics] on my ageing brain".

Whilst most Autistic participants endorsed benefits to wellbeing through psychedelic use, some also highlighted the importance of access to knowledge and supportive settings in facilitating harm minimisation within and beyond naturalistic psychedelic experiences. One participant (34, Female, Canada / US, with clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms) indicated that "My experience with 5MeO has been the most dramatic and impactful in moving me towards the idea of psychedelics for therapy. It has such significant power, but without proper setting and support for the aftermath -it was a traumatic experience in many ways. It feels critical that more information be shared about how to navigate your experience and how to reground".

Contextual contingencies in outcomes were also structurally produced through the legal status of psychedelics. Some Autistic participants reflected upon the risks of engaging with psychedelic substances where there is limited regulation and knowledge of harm minimisation practices. One participant (50, Female, Australia / NZ, with clinically significant depression symptoms) reflected on their early naturalistic psychedelic experience, sharing that "... I'm not sure that it even was LSD, same with the MDMA. I did not know about testing facilities at the time. They were not positive experiences."

Parallel to this, some Autistic adults advocated for change to these systemic contexts to foster affordances. Participants voiced a need for legal and safe access to psychedelics, citing the efficacy and therapeutic properties of psychedelic substances as a pertinent rationale, as highlighted by one participant (56, Male, Canada / US, with no adverse mental health indicators) who reflected, "psychedelics are medicines. Responsible use is possible and should be legal. Safe access is a priority, as are safe havens where those who need to can make progress in their own healing, with the right group (community and/or family) and professional support".

Collectively, findings within this sub-theme emphasise the need to consider safety of psychedelic use among Autistic adults both at an individual and systemic level, highlighting the importance of considering the conditions of naturalistic psychedelic use, and emphasising a need for systems that facilitate harm minimisation.

DISCUSSION

This study generated novel insights into naturalistic psychedelic use contexts alongside wellbeing and mental health indicators among self-identified Autistic adults who reported engaging in therapeutic psychedelic use. Contextually, the Autistic sub-sample reported greater lifetime use of less common psychedelic substances; lower perceived knowledge and experience with psychedelics despite this broader engagement; and more frequent use within structured therapeutic settings than the remaining overall sample. Regarding wellbeing and mental health, self-identified Autistic adults who reported engaging in therapeutic psychedelic use reported significantly poorer mental health at the time of survey completion and more frequent and distressing persisting hallucinations than the broader population of naturalistic psychedelic users. Yet, qualitative accounts from the same Autistic sub-sample were largely positive, describing profound shifts in self-understanding, connection, and therapeutic benefits. Rather than reflecting divergent findings, integrated interpretation suggested that these patterns within wellbeing co-exist, whereby benefit and distress were not mutually exclusive, but were rather facets of a more complex, non-linear process of change.

Naturalistic Psychedelic Use Contexts

Psilocybin was the most commonly used psychedelic substance within the Autistic sub-sample, consistent with prior survey research on the Autistic population (Afsharnia et al., 2025). The Autistic sub-sample endorsed greater lifetime use of less common psychedelic substances, like ibogaine and 2C-B, than the broader population of naturalistic psychedelic users (Lake and Lucas, 2024), despite lower perceived knowledge and experience with psychedelics relative to the remaining overall sample. This combination of broader exploratory engagement with psychedelics alongside lower perceived exposure may indicate reduced access to, or confidence in relevant informational resources, possibly reflecting the under-representation of Autistic individuals in both clinical and naturalistic psychedelic research (Imoh et al., 2025), and a subsequent limitation of tailored harm minimisation guidance (Munday et al., 2026).

Motivations for psychedelic use among Autistic sub-sample participants were broadly consistent with prior research, with wellbeing and self-exploration emerging as common motivators (Afsharnia et al., 2025).

Reduction in other substance use was the only motivation more prevalent among the Autistic sub-sample than the remaining overall sample. This may reflect a preference for psychedelics as part of a broader self-directed harm reduction strategy (Glynos et al., 2024), as noted by qualitative participant narratives of managing addiction through psychedelic use.

The greater prevalence of licensed and unlicensed therapeutic settings for psychedelic use in the Autistic sub-sample relative to the remaining overall sample likely reflects the therapeutic motivations inherent to how the sub-sample was defined, but may also suggest a broader pursuit of predictability and support during the psychedelic experience through structured environments. This interpretation is supported by qualitative findings, which highlighted the importance of setting and adequate integration support in shaping positive outcomes from the psychedelic experience. This preference for structure is also consistent with broader findings that Autistic adults favour therapy with a clear agenda and well-defined goals (Mazurek et al., 2023). Future research and clinical frameworks may benefit from explicitly incorporating Autistic perspectives to improve accessibility, safety, and relevance of psychedelic-assisted supports.

Risks and Affordances through Wellbeing and Mental Health Indicators

The coexistence of risks and affordances identified through integrated mixed-methods findings raises questions around what facets of wellbeing are influenced by psychedelic use in this population, and under which circumstances and conditions risk becomes most pronounced.

One of this study's most novel and clinically-salient quantitative findings was the substantially higher prevalence of persisting hallucinations in the Autistic sub-sample compared to the remaining overall sample.

Nearly two-thirds of the Autistic sub-sample reported hallucinations persisting beyond 24 hours, with these hallucinations being more negatively appraised than those reported in the remaining overall sample and lasting 15 to 30 days for 78.26% of affected Autistic individuals. One explanation for this potentially distinct risk profile may be regarded at the intersection between psychedelic-induced perceptual amplification and atypical sensory processing in autism, given that Autistic individuals show elevated rates of anomalous perceptual experiences and psychosis-related symptoms even outside of substance use (Kiyono et al., 2020; Milne et al., 2017). Perceptual differences arising at the intersection of autism and psychedelics may also relate to overlapping neurobiological mechanisms identified in both autism and psychedelic research, including reduced connectivity within the default mode network and greater connectivity between different networks (Roseby and Osborn Moar, 2025). The interaction between these neurobiological mechanisms is yet to be understood with regard to the effects of psychedelics upon Autistic adults. Whether altered sensory processing among Autistic individuals contributes to unique psychedelic outcomes compared to the general population remains an open question for future research to explore.

Despite this elevated risk, qualitative narratives described psychedelic experiences that facilitated selfcompassion, agency, emotional insight, connection, and spirituality, with perceived benefits extending beyond cooccurring conditions to broader wellbeing and quality of life. Several participants described a phenomenon previously termed Psychedelic Proximity -an enduring sense of closeness to features of the psychedelic experience that could be revisited as an emotional anchor in daily life, distinct from either acute drug effects or traditional symptom relief (Payne et al., 2021). The presence of this construct, alongside processes like decentring and increased psychological flexibility noted among participants, suggests that perceived wellbeing gains in the Autistic sub-sample may operate on a dimension at least partially independent from standardised screening measures. Participants reported meaningful gains even when GAD-7 and PHQ-8 scores indicated ongoing clinical-level distress. Wellbeing improvements derived from psychedelic use among the Autistic sub-sample hence appear to reflect a more holistic and multidimensional construct (Keyes, 2002) related to notions like flourishing and eudaimonia (Deci and Ryan, 2008) as opposed to discrete symptom reduction.

Broader wellbeing affordances described also present implications for therapeutic psychedelic use to support specific challenges experienced by Autistic adults which are inadequately targeted by existing supports (Huang et al., 2024). This could include a fragmented sense of self (Kong et al., 2025), loneliness (Ee et al., 2019), interoceptive difficulties like alexithymia (Oakley et al., 2022), cognitive inflexibility (Hollocks et al., 2025), and difficulties in integrating Autistic identity following late diagnosis (Lamash et al., 2024; Meldrum et al., 2026).

This is particularly salient given evidence that a stronger, more positive relationship with Autistic identity is itself associated with better psychological wellbeing (Cooper et al., 2023), suggesting that broader wellbeing-related constructs like these may serve as equally-relevant primary outcome measures when evaluating therapeutic benefit of psychedelic use in Autistic populations.

It is important, however, that these affordances are not mistaken as resolution of well-documented systemic mental health disparities between the Autistic and general population. Over half of the Autistic subsample reported clinically significant anxiety (57.35%) or depression (60.66%) symptoms at the time of survey completion -prevalences that were significantly greater than the remaining overall sample, and also exceeded documented pooled prevalence estimates for anxiety (20%) and depressive (11%) disorders in Autistic individuals more broadly (Lai et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the qualitative affordances described by Autistic participants closely parallel those described by the general population of naturalistic psychedelic users (Neitzke-Spruill et al., 2024; Rupiński et al., 2025; Thomson and Thomacos, 2025), which is notable precisely because Autistic individuals typically experience poorer baseline wellbeing and greater psychosocial marginalisation compared to the general population (Weir et al., 2022). Together, this suggests that psychedelic use may generate genuine benefit for Autistic adults without necessarily functioning as a corrective for the elevated mental health burden this population carries relative to the general population. This is consistent with growing recognition within the literature that psychedelic-assisted approaches may address co-occurring symptomatology without resolving broader systemic forces like stigma, discrimination, or inaccessibility that amplify mental ill health among Autistic individuals in the first place (Cluck et al., 2026; Danforth et al., 2018). Clinically, this emphasises the need to frame psychedelicassisted therapies as a complement to structural gaps in autism-informed mental health support as opposed to a substitute.

Consistent with this, qualitative narratives suggested that affordances were not typically automatic.

Several participants described conditional or even traumatic outcomes tied to inadequate setting, insufficient integration support, or poor personal fit, echoing considerations already established in the broader psychedelic literature (Argyri et al., 2026; Palmer and Maynard, 2022). These concerns are likely amplified for Autistic individuals given well-documented preferences for predictability, the disproportionate impact of unpredictability upon wellbeing in this population (Lin et al., 2025), as well as potential for difficulty tolerating open-ended, nondirective approaches, which are commonplace in much of psychedelic-assisted therapy (Cluck et al., 2026; Mazurek et al., 2023). The psychedelic substance itself therefore appears to function as a necessary but not sufficient condition for benefit. Practically, this points towards specific, actionable adaptations of psychedelicassisted therapy with Autistic clients, like extended preparatory sessions that explicitly address the likely unpredictability of the experience, and integration support structured to extend well beyond the acute experience, potentially over a period of months rather than weeks, given participants' accounts of multi-year integration processes.

Collectively, these findings support conceptualising psychedelic experiences within the Autistic subsample as dynamic, non-linear processes rather than discrete events with uniform outcomes. Reduced wellbeing captured through quantitative screening may not solely indicate an adverse event, but may reflect pre-existing or systemic difficulties, or a transitional phase within a longer process of psychological change. Nevertheless, without adequate support, qualitative accounts suggest that destabilising experiences may persist unresolved rather than resolving into growth. Longitudinal research capable of tracking this trajectory over time, rather than capturing a single cross-sectional snapshot, is needed to distinguish these possibilities in this population.

Strengths and Limitations

This study's strengths lie in its mixed-methods approach, global scope, and contribution of novel findings pertaining to Autistic adults' experiences of psychedelic use. Findings offer insight into how a group of Autistic adults using psychedelics for therapeutic purposes may differ from the broader naturalistic psychedelic user population, with implications for guiding safe clinical practice with this population. Specifically, findings suggest that there may be characteristics warranting accommodation when working with Autistic adults in psychedelicassisted therapy settings, including differential levels of prior psychedelic knowledge, elevated risk of persisting hallucinations, and a potentially greater need for structured preparation and integration support.

Nevertheless, several limitations constrain interpretation. Most notably, as acknowledged within the methods section, the Autistic sub-sample was determined not by respondents directly identifying as Autistic, but through an indirect measure: respondents indicating they used psychedelics specifically to support their experience of autism. This meant that any potential Autistic participants who did not indicate such intentions for psychedelic use were not able to be identified as Autistic in this study, and were therefore retained in the comparison group, precluding interpretation of findings as reflecting strictly Autistic vs. non-Autistic differences.

Between-group differences identified may also reflect confounding factors, including high rates of co-occurring conditions within the Autistic sub-sample and significant demographic differences between the groups across characteristics like gender, race and ethnicity, and income. Despite the global scope of data collection, overrepresentation of White participants from Canada and the US further limits generalisability across cultural and sociopolitical contexts. Additionally, recruitment through psychedelic-supportive channels, the predominantly benefit-focused qualitative items, and the retrospective study design potentially introduced a positive reporting and recall bias.

Conclusions

Self-identified Autistic adults who reported engaging in therapeutic psychedelic use may represent a distinct sub-group of naturalistic psychedelic users, characterised by greater lifetime use prevalence of less common psychedelic substances, lower self-perceived knowledge and experience with psychedelics, more frequent use in structured therapeutic settings, and greater prevalence of persisting hallucinations following psychedelic use. Through a novel mixed-methods approach to studying psychedelic use among Autistic adults, this study's findings suggest that risks and affordances related to wellbeing within this population can co-occur and may unfold across extended, non-linear trajectories. Future research should investigate a broader range of potential outcomes of psychedelic use among Autistic adults, alongside further exploration of Autistic first-person perspectives. This work can improve understanding of how to best tailor psychedelic use contexts and therapeutic processes to Autistic needs to ensure equitable access to psychedelic-assisted therapies for Autistic adults. Future psychedelic research should also consider mixed-methods approaches as a means of holistically understanding the experiences of other unique sub-populations of interest.
