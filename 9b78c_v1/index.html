Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Social determinants of health (SDH) are increasingly recognized as essential for understanding health outcomes across public health and social sciences. In pediatric health, the importance of addressing SDH is particularly evident, as early-life exposure to adverse social conditions can have lifelong impacts on health. However, there is significant variation in how researchers conceptualize and measure SDH, which presents considerable challenges in translating evidence into practice. Using the PROGRESS-Plus framework, we conducted a scoping review to explore how research conceptualized and measured SDH in pediatric studies. We identified 10,268 empirical, peer-reviewed studies that were published in English, focused on populations under 18 years of age, and examined social determinants of health. A total of 316 articles were included in this review, highlighting the widespread use of the term in the literature. Results indicated substantial variability in the SDH factors and measurement approaches used across studies, with few studies examining two or more factors (21%). Additionally, many researchers were not explicit about what they considered SDH. They often used the term without a definition and explanation of what the term meant in the context of their research. Socioeconomic status (26.8%), education (17.9%) and social capital (13.1) were among the most frequently used factors when assessing SDH. Based on this review, we present recommendations for strengthening the conceptualization and measurement of SDH in future research involving pediatric populations.

Mapping Social Determinants of Health in Pediatric Populations: A Scoping Review

Social determinants of health (SDH) has gained increasing recognition as an essential framework for understanding health outcomes. Defined by the World Health Organization (2023) as the social, economic, and political factors that shape individual and population health, SDH encompass a wide range of factors, from income and education to community environments and access to healthcare. While SDH is a widely used term across public health and social sciences, different disciplines conceptualize and measure SDH in diverse ways, leading to variability in research findings and implications. For instance, while some researchers emphasize material factors, such as income, employment status, and housing quality (House et al., 2018; Willcox-Pidgeon et al., 2021), others focus on more interpersonal or social interactional factors, including experiences of discrimination, social support, and stress (Berger-Jenkins et al., 2019; Morrow, 2004). This variation in approaches reflects the complexity of SDH and the multifaceted ways in which social environments influence health outcomes. These effects are especially consequential in childhood, when developmental systems are most sensitive to environmental input (Shonkoff & Garner, 2012). SDH influence health outcomes through multiple interconnected pathways that operate at societal, psychological and biological levels. At the societal level, factors like education quality and employment opportunities shape health behaviours, access to resources, and the ability to navigate healthcare systems (Cutler & Lleras-Muney, 2010). At the psychological level, racism and discrimination or poverty may affect self-esteem, self-efficacy and sense of safety, resulting in chronic stress (Williams et al., 2019). At the biological level, chronic exposure to adverse social conditions can lead to sustained stress responses that dysregulate multiple physiological systems (McEwen, 2017). The constant activation of stress pathways contributes to inflammation, immune system dysfunction, and accelerated cellular aging, increasing vulnerability to chronic diseases (Miller et al., 2011). Furthermore, factors such as racism, economic inequality, and geographic disadvantage create persistent health disparities between different population groups (Bailey et al., 2017). For example, racial and ethnic minorities and individuals living in poverty consistently experience worse health outcomes across numerous measures, from higher rates of chronic diseases to shorter life expectancies (Braveman & Gottlieb, 2014; Williams & Mohammed, 2013). Emerging neurobiological research demonstrates how early-life adversity can alter brain development, affecting cognitive function and emotional regulation well into adulthood (Luby et al., 2013; Noble et al., 2015). These findings underscore how social inequalities become biologically embedded, with lasting consequences for physical and mental health (Danese & McEwen, 2012).

The importance of addressing SDH is particularly evident in pediatric health, where early-life exposures to adverse social conditions can have lifelong consequences for health and well-being in youth due to their ongoing neurodevelopment and dependence on caregivers and social systems (Shonkoff & Garner 2012). For example, children growing up in poverty are more likely to experience food insecurity, inadequate housing, and exposure to environmental toxins, all of which can negatively impact their overall development (Do et al., 2023; Hastings et al., 2023). Similarly, experiences of discrimination or trauma during childhood can lead to chronic stress and mental health disorders, with long-term implications (Lockwood et al., 2018; Palacios-Barrios & Hanson, 2019; Taylor et al., 2020).

As SDH is relevant across diverse fields, its operationalization varies, thus shaping how findings are generated and applied in practice. The lack of standardized definitions and measurement approaches has significant implications for health research and policy, especially the identification of priority areas for intervention, evaluation of the effectiveness of policies, or tracking of progress in reducing health inequities. To begin to address these gaps, the Cochrane Collaboration developed the PROGRESS-Plus framework (Oliver et al., 2008) to assess whether studies address a broad range of social factors. The acronym PROGRESS stands for: Place of residence, Race/ethnicity/culture/language, Occupation, Gender/sex, Religion, Education, Socioeconomic status, and Social capital, while PLUS refers to additional factors contributing to an individual's physical or mental health (Oliver et al., 2008; O'Neill et al., 2014). Though widely cited, its application remains varied, with studies selectively emphasizing certain domains (e.g., socioeconomic status) while neglecting others (e.g., religion or social capital; Holland et al., 2024).

Present Review

As a step toward developing guidelines for research, we conducted a scoping review to examine current practices related to the operationalization and measurement of SDH in current pediatric health research. The primary objectives of this review were to: (1) clarify ways that researchers across disciplines conceptualize SDH; (2) identify and synthesize the measures used to assess SDH in the literature (O'Neill et al., 2014); and (3) investigate methodological differences and their implications for SDH research. By systematically reviewing the literature, we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of the ways in which SDH is defined and measured, identify best practices, and offer recommendations for future research.

Method

The scoping review protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework Scoping Review Protocol Guidelines and Template (osf.io/f3sdv).

Search Strategy

We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for conducting the search strategy for this scoping review (Tricco et al., 2018; See Figure 1). Peer-reviewed journal articles in English with no limits placed on publication year using Medline, EMBASE and Scopus were included. A search strategy was developed with the aid of a university librarian. Searches were conducted using the following terms: "social determinants of health", "adolescen*", "child*", "youth*", "infant*", "baby", and "babies" (see Appendix A for more details regarding the logic of the searches across databases). Searches were limited to titles and abstracts for the MEDLINE and EMBASE databases, and to titles, abstracts, and keywords through Scopus.

All the relevant hits were imported into Covidence, a systematic data management tool (Covidence systematic review software, 2024). Title and abstract screening were completed first by two independent reviewers. Following the initial screening, full texts of the articles that were accepted and the inclusion criteria were re-applied by two independent reviewers. Inter-rater agreement was calculated by taking the average of the proportionate agreement between all reviewer combinations and was 93.8% for the title and abstract screening and 91.7% for the fulltext review. Any disagreements were resolved by a third independent reviewer.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion criteria included: 1) empirical studies; 2) participants under 18 years of age to be consistent with UNICEF's (2023) criteria for pediatric populations in Canada; 3) mention of the term "social determinants of health" in the title or abstract and elaborated on in the full text; 4) social determinants of health factors were explicitly stated and measured. Detailed inclusion criteria can be found in Appendix B.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

In this review, we examined the conceptualization of SDH using the PROGRESS-Plus framework developed by O'Neill et al. (2014). Oliver et al, (2008) conceptualized "Plus" in terms of three additional categories: i) characteristics that can invite discrimination (e.g. age, or disability); ii) specific features of interpersonal relationships (e.g. parents who smoke); and iii) time dependent relationships where an individual may be at an temporary disadvantage (e.g. leaving the hospital, respite care, other instances where a person may be temporarily at a disadvantage) (Oliver et al., 2008). We also used definitions and examples provided by Karran et al. (2023) in their scoping review on using the PROGRESS-Plus framework as a guideline in the creation of subcategories when presenting our results in each of the PROGRESS characteristics, as well as the Plus category.

The following information was extracted and entered into a shared Microsoft Excel file: (1) article title, (2) authors' names, (3) publication year, (4) journal name, (5) demographic information (number of participants, age, gender), (6) SDH factors measured, and (7) outcomes measured.

Further, the SDH factors measured and the tools used in each article were then categorized according to the PROGRESS-Plus framework. We calculated the total number of studies that measured relevant SDH factors. We also identified and extracted the variables (referred to as terms for this paper) that fit under each broader SDH category (e.g., years of education for the SDH factor of Education). Articles included multiple terms to discuss the relevant SDH factors. The specific terms used (denoted by n) to refer to the different variables that were extracted to highlight the variability in concepts measured are presented.

When synthesizing different measures used by researchers, we included any questionnaires created for a study as measures/tools developed by researchers or composed of items from multiple questionnaires, regardless of whether they were standardized. Where possible, we highlight specific measures/tools that were commonly used as indicated in the literature. Results

To examine the conceptualization of SDH in pediatric research, a total of 10268 articles were retrieved across three databases and imported into Covidence. After identifying duplicates (2103 articles) and applying the inclusion criteria, 7849 articles were excluded. 316 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in this scoping review. A breakdown of the number of articles that included factors falling into each category is presented in Figure 2. Appendix C has more information about the different variables.

Place of Residence

Place of Residence (P) is defined as the location where one lives, such as their community, region, or city and their housing type (O'Neill et al., 2014). In all, 98 articles out of 316 (31%) included Place of Residence as an SDH factor. The Place of Residence category demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in how constructs were operationalized across studies. Across these articles, a total of 143 terms related to place of residence were extracted. Indicators spanned multiple levels, including geographic location (e.g., region, urban-rural classification, n = 60), housing characteristics (e.g., structure, quality, security, and materials; n = 35), and broader neighbourhood context (n = 30). Access to infrastructure and sanitation (n = 18) also included indicators related to sanitation, pollution, and availability of neighbourhood resources.

A variety of national surveys were used to identify factors related to the place of residence. Seven articles included place of residence based on the National Survey of Children's Health, which measured terms such as perceived neighbourhood safety (Aysola et al., 2011), poorly built environment (Yang et al., 2023), and neighbourhood conditions (Ji et al., 2023).

Additionally, some authors used the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey to examine terms such as household sanitation (Adeyinka et al., 2020) and region of residence (Adeyinka & Muhajarine, 2024), while other authors created their own measurement tool specific to their research questions. For example, Costa et al. (2019) created their own questionnaire of SDH based on the hierarchical model of social determinants of health, which encompasses individual, behaviour and lifestyle, social and community, living and working conditions, and structural and socioeconomic conditions of the environment.

Race, Ethnicity, Culture, and Language

Race, Ethnicity, Culture, and Language (R) is defined as one's racial, ethnic and cultural background (O'Neill et al., 2014). This variable was examined in 97 of 316 articles (30.7%), making it one of the more frequently assessed PROGRESS factors. From these articles, a total of 125 individual terms were extracted and grouped into four distinct categories: race and ethnicity (n = 89), language and English proficiency (n = 19), cultural identity and social contexts (n = 15), and discrimination based on minority status (n = 2). Twenty-five articles included two or more terms across these categories.

Of the 92 articles, 37 involved measures developed for the study. Of these, 29 involved measures of participant race or ethnicity. Further, national survey measures, including the US National Survey of Children's Health (NSCH, 2018) to measure race (Victorino & Gauthier, 2009), the National Survey of Children with Special Health Care Needs (NS-CSHCN) to measure language and race (Pankewicz et al., 2020) and the American Community Survey (US Census Bureau, 2018) were used to measure the racial makeup of a community (Weller et al., 2021).

Gender and Sex

Gender and Sex (G) refer to biological and social differences across the gender spectrum. Sex denotes the biological differences between males and females, while gender encompasses the socially constructed roles and identities of an individual (O'Neill et al., 2014). While commonly framed in dichotomous terms (i.e., men and women), other categories and identifiers are in use ( Levitt et al., 2024; van Anders, 2025). Fifty-seven articles, out of 316 (18.03%), included effects of Gender and Sex with a total of 57 terms used. Terms were categorized into child gender (n=20), child sex (n=33), parental gender/sex (n=3), and gender equality (n=1).

Child gender and child sex were frequently collected using health database information (e.g., medical chart review), city health department information (e.g., 2017 Baltimore City Neighborhood Health Profile; Baltimore City Health Department, 2017), larger studies (e.g., TARGet Kids! COVID-19 Study of Children and Families), and surveys (e.g., The Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey; UNICEF, 2015) as reported across several studies (Alston et al., 2022; Baker-Smith et al., 2021; Dahab et al., 2020; Margol et al., 2024). Parental gender/sex, which specifically considers the gender or sex of the parent or head of household as a social determinant of health (Adeyinka et al., 2020; Graham et al., 2020; Kibuchi et al., 2024). Gender equality was examined in only one study, in which the aim was to understand the potential mitigating effect of gender equality on maternal and under-5 child mortality using the Gender Parity Index of the United National Global SDG Indicators Database (El-Shal et al., 2022).

Across the 57 articles, 20 included lab-created measures, with many studies not including additional information about the structure of their questions. Given the inclusion of infants and young children in many of these studies, child gender and sex were likely reported by parents or caregivers. Furthermore, 15 articles used an already developed survey measure, such as the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS; UNICEF, 2015) or National Survey of Children's Health (NSCH, 2018).

Religion

Religion (R) is defined as one's religious affiliation, or lack thereof (O'Neill et al., 2014).

Religion was one of the least explored variables in the literature, assessed in only 13 out of 316 articles (4.11%). Despite the limited attention, how religion was examined across articles varied enough to be grouped into four distinct categories: child religion (n = 5), maternal religion (n = 4), maternal religious involvement (n = 3), and child spiritual health (n = 1).

Regarding measurement tools, questionnaires developed to assess religious terms were used in 5 of the 13 studies. Previously adapted instruments such as the 2013 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) and the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS: UNICEF, 2015), were used to study maternal religion in two studies (i.e., Barua et al., 2023; Levano et al., 2024).

Additionally, administrative or health databases were used to explore child religion in three studies (e.g., Baker-Smith et al., 2021), while national data sources such as the Nairobi Crosssection Slums Survey (NCSS) and the 2013 Urban Health Survey (UHS; NIPORT et al., 2015) were used to evaluate child and maternal religion (e.g., Barua et al., 2023; Kibuchi et al., 2024).

Education

Education (E) is defined as the level of educational attainment, including literacy and years of education (Karran et al., 2023; O'Neill et al., 2014). From a total of 316 articles, 140 (44.3%) included terms related to education, with a total of 148 different terms being measured. The category of parent/caregiver's education was the most commonly used (number of different terms = 115). The parents' or caregivers' education was examined in the majority of the articles (67 out of 140) without further specification (i.e., mother, father). Among the studies of caregiver education, 46 articles were focused on mother's education and only three on fathers' education (Dahab et al., 2020; Huda et al., 2016; Kumari et al., 2019). Other relevant terms included family education (highest level of education achieved by any family member in the household; n = 8), population-level education (percentages of the population with formal education in the community; n = 10), and child/youth education attainment and support (n = 15).

Among the 140 articles that fell into the education category, 79 involved lab-created measurements, while 37 involved demographic or household-based surveys, such as the National Survey of Children's Health (NSCH, 2018) or the American Community Survey (ACS; U.S.

Census Bureau, 2012). Details regarding the specific wording of the questions presented to participants were often not included. However, the authors frequently described education based on the highest level completed (e.g., high school, some college, college, more than college, etc.), with the specific breakdown of education levels varying across articles (e.g., Aristizabal et al., 2023; Call et al., 2024).

Socioeconomic Status

Socioeconomic status is defined as any measure related to income, including welfare, benefits, and affluence (Oliver et al., 2008). A total of 209 (66.14%) articles involved socioeconomic status (SES) as a social determinant of health, with the focus being on parent SES as a reflection of the child's circumstances. Of these, 78 articles examined two or more SES-related terms, resulting in a total of 435 terms. While a broad range of terms were included, they were categorized into six subgroups: poverty and economic stability (e.g., structural and perceived indicators of socioeconomic disadvantage; n = 104), income and wealth (e.g absolute, relative, and perceived measures of household/family income and wealth; n = 199), food security and insecurity (n = 60), housing, material conditions, and basic needs (n = 49), insurance and financial assistance (n = 11), and other (e.g., medical records, EMS clinician free-text notes; n = 12).

Measures used to assess SES were grouped into six categories: lab-created tools, surveys and questionnaires, datasets, census data, indexes, and other sources. Lab-created tools were the most commonly used, with 115 factors. Similar to education, few details were provided by the authors about these lab-created tools, but data were frequently presented in a way that suggests that participants were asked to select the income range that best reflected their circumstances.

Further, previously developed surveys and questionnaires were used 80 times, with frequently cited examples including the National Health Interview Survey and the 2006 Aboriginal Children's Survey (ACS). Additionally, datasets were used in 31 articles, while census data, such as those from the U.S. Census Bureau and patient census tracts, were used in 28 studies. Twentyfour measures were drawn from previously developed indices, including the Social Vulnerability Index (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, n.d.), the Food Atlas (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2022), and the Ontario Marginalization Index (Matheson et al., 2023). Forty-nine measures were classified under "other," including medical records, EMS clinician free-text notes, and the China Health Statistics Yearbook.

Social Capital

Social capital is defined as measures related to social relationships and networks, including interpersonal relationships, civic participation, and neighbourhood involvement (O'Neill et al., 2014). This factor was included in 102 articles out of 316 (32.28%), with a total of 148 terms measured. Terms were categorized into marital status of caregiver (n = 52), familial social support (n = 27), family and household structure (n = 40), child-specific social environment (n = 9), and community and social networks (n = 20).

The marital status of caregivers category focused on single-parent households, absence of a caregiver (i.e., divorce or loss), and the marital status of the caregivers. This was most commonly measured by demographic questionnaires such as the 2016 cross-sectional Medical Expenditures Panel Study (MEPS; e.g., Rosenburg et al., 2024), Urban Health Survey 2013 (NIPORT et al., 2015; e.g., Barua et al., 2013), and the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS; UNICEF, 2015; e.g., Adeyinka et al., 2020). The social support category focused on levels of social support from friends and family, social isolation, and childcare support systems for caregivers. The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; Zimet et al., 1988) and lab-created measures were most frequently used to assess this category (Alijanzadeh et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2024). Family and household structure focused on the number of people in the home or family size, the number of children, and relationships between family members, including complicated or strained relations and heavily relied on lab-created questionnaires (Kumari et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020). The child-specific social environment category encompasses bullying, the child's relationship with parents, peer relationships and influence, and school environment. The most frequently used assessment tools were lab-created questionnaires (e.g., Wang et al., 2020) and the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC; Currie et al., 2012; e.g., Költő et al., 2021). The category of community and social networks focused on social cohesion, social networks, neighbourhood social capital, sense of community, and involvement in community programs, which were most frequently measured by The Delaware Survey of Children's Health ([DSCH]; Nemours Children's Health, 2017; e.g., Zhao & Settles, 2014) or lab-created tools (Manes et al., 2016).

Overall, of the 102 articles, 41 involved lab-created measures, and 22 involved demographic surveys or questionnaires, such as the American Community Survey (ACS; U.S.

Census Bureau, 2012) or the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS; UNICEF, 2015).

"Plus" Factors

Of 316 articles reviewed, 244 (77.22%) involved terms that fell into the Plus category, with 140 of them involving two or more terms in the same article. A total of 684 distinct terms were measured across these articles. As the Plus category encompasses a very wide range of factors, subcategories were developed to organize them, informed by the approach used by Karran et al. (2023). These subcategories included: (1) age, which captured any terms or measures related to the age of the parent or child; (2) insurance status, referring to terms related to insurance coverage or healthcare payors; (3) immigration/citizenship status, which included any reference to place of birth, migration, or legal status; (4) parent/family health-related factors, covering a broad range of health-related issues affecting parents or family members, including but not limited to healthcare access, physical or mental health, and substance use; (5) Child Health and Wellbeing, covering a broad range of health and wellness issues affecting children, including but not limited to child physical or mental health and general childhood opportunity (e.g., school type, kindergarten attendance); (6) socioeconomic and environmental context, which encompassed factors related to the broader social and physical environment, particularly those that did not clearly fall under 'place of residence' or 'SES', including multi-dimensional index measures; (7) diet and nutrition, covering a variety of nutrition-related terms and practices; (8) family dynamics, including aspects of household relationships, caregiving, and interpersonal conditions within the family; (9) physical and behavioral activity, referring to general activity levels and related behaviors; and (10) other, used for factors that did not align with any of the categories above (e.g., legal issues, president's political party, recessions).

Figure 3 shows the frequency of terms explored within each of the subcategories across the 316 articles included in this scoping review, and Table 1 provides an overview of the variability of factors explored within the Plus domain. The most frequently used category was socioeconomic and environmental factors, with 169 terms included in this category. This was followed by Parent/Family Health-Related (n = 139) and Child Health and Wellbeing (n = 84). Index scores were widely used in the Socio-Economic and Environmental Context subcategory. For example, the Social Vulnerability Index (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, n.d.), the Area Deprivation Index (Singh, 2003), and the Social Deprivation Index (Butler et al., 2013) were widely used to measure social vulnerability, area deprivation and social deprivation, respectively. Regional indices such as the Ontario Marginalization Index (Matheson et al., 2023) or the New Zealand Index for Deprivation of Individuals (Salmond et al., 2006) were also employed to measure a range of factors, including population and neighbourhood deprivation.

A variety of tools were employed to measure factors within the Parent/Family Health-Related Terms and Child Health and Wellbeing subcategories. Notably, UNICEF's Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (2015) and the National Survey of Children's Health (2018) were commonly used to capture a range of factors within these categories. The Child Opportunity Index (diversitydatakids.org, n.d.) was the most frequently applied index, both within these categories and across the review as a whole. The Child Opportunity Index was also used more extensively in some studies to assess specific domains such as educational opportunity, health and environmental opportunity, and social and economic opportunity (Crooks et al., 2024; Kolwaite et al., 2024). Researchers also used tools to measure parent mental health, including the Washington State Department of Social and Health Services Integrated Client Database (Mancuso & Huber, 2021), the Parental Stress Index-Short Form (Abidin, 1995), the 25-item Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (Goodman, 1997), and electronic medical records. In many cases, researchers developed their own questionnaires or interview protocols to capture these factors.

Factors within the Age, Insurance, Diet and Nutrition, and Physical/Behavioral Activity categories were primarily measured using lab-created tools that asked simple questions about variables such as age, insurance status, and habits including breastfeeding, sports participation, and screen time. Age and insurance information were also collected from medical records and demographic surveys, including the U.S. Census, the National Socioeconomic Characterization Survey (Ministry of Social Development et al., 2018), and the Bangladesh Health and Demographic Survey (NIPORT et al., 2015). Factors within the Family Dynamics category were measured using a combination of validated questionnaires, databases, demographic surveys, and researcher-developed tools. For example, domestic violence was assessed with instruments such as the Washington State Department of Social and Health Services Integrated Client Database, the Survey of Wellbeing of Young Children (Perrin et al., 2016), and the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (Squires & Bricker, 2009). Other studies, such as Pedrosa et al. (2021), developed their own questionnaires to capture family-level factors, including domestic abuse, sexual abuse, and family abandonment.

With regard to the measures, lab-created tools were used in 78 studies, while established indexes, most commonly the Child Opportunity Index (diversitydatakids.org, n.d.) and the Social Vulnerability Index (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, n.d.), were used in 42. The third most common measure in the plus factor, appearing in 10 articles, was UNICEF's Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (2015).

Discussion

The focus of this scoping review is on how SDH has been conceptualized and measured among pediatric populations using the PROGRESS+ framework. Overall, we identified 316 published articles that met our inclusion criteria of explicit discussion of SDH, indicating notable engagement with the topic. Socioeconomic status, education, and social capital were among the most commonly used factors in the pediatric studies (210 articles; 66.45 %). Additionally, factors categorized under the "Plus" rubric (244 articles; 77 %) were the most examined, highlighting the diversity of ways researchers attempt to conceptualize and measure SDH. The term "Social Determinants of Health" was often used as a 'buzzword', that is to say, the term was mentioned without further explanation or definition of what it meant in the context of the research questions. Further, the specific characteristics of the rubric SDH were often not explicit, nor were its conceptualization, operationalization, or measurement.

SDH Factors

When reviewing individual factors according to the PROGRESS+ framework, we found that many studies (66 of 316 articles; 21%) included only two or fewer social determinants, primarily including parent/caregiver sex or gender and parental education. This is consistent with Nwankwo et al.'s (2024) findings of a systematic review of SDH in pediatric dermatology, where SDH factors collected during clinical trials were frequently omitted, and in cases where they were included, approximately only one or two factors were considered. In order to support a holistic approach in understanding factors that may impact pediatric health and wellbeing outcomes, a variety of factors should be included.

Consistent with the WHO definition of SDH, socioeconomic status, education, and social capital were among the most frequently explored areas in the pediatric studies included in this review. Similar or overlapping factors within these domains were included in many studies, demonstrating some consistency in what researchers consider to be SDH variables. For example, concordant with evidence that parental education and household income have been consistently linked to various health outcomes, including obesity, chronic illnesses, cardiometabolic risk and mental illness among children, (Homs et al., 2022; Martín-Matillas, 2022; Ribeiro et al., 2020 : Xiang et al., 2024), caregiver education appeared in 115 different terms, and poverty or economic status a in 104 terms. These numbers highlight that certain factors could be considered the standard "go-to" variables that researchers use when assessing SDH. In contrast, other factors with established links to health outcomes were notably underrepresented; despite evidence of the positive impact of religion and spirituality (Koenig, 2012), few articles included these variables, suggesting that this remains an underexplored area warranting further attention in future research.

Consistent with a previous review (Karran et al., 2023), the "Plus factors" in the residual category not explicitly included in the PROGRESS acronym were the most frequently examined SDH (77%), highlighting the diversity of ways that researchers attempt to conceptualize and measure SDH beyond the more obvious categories. The Plus category included diverse terms, including age, factors linked to diet and nutrition, and information about physical and behavioural activity. Notably, certain frameworks include certain social determinants (e.g., age, insurance) as intermediary factors (Krieger, 2001; WHO, 2008), rather than explicitly including them in the framework. Their appearance in this review underscores the heterogeneity of frameworks observed in the literature. Additionally, some factors were context-specific. For example, insurance status may serve as an important health inequity in some countries (e.g., United States), but may not be as relevant or have as much of an impact in countries like Canada that provide universal health coverage and additional coverage to those without health insurance.

There were also inconsistencies in what authors considered to be demographic factors and factors related to SDH. For example, while 'Sex and Gender' is considered a SDH in the PROGRESS+ framework, not all articles that measured gender/sex identified it as a measure of SDH. We concluded from our analyses that 223 articles included gender or sex in their demographic information, but gender/sex was identified as an SDH factor in only 53 of the articles.

Measures

We also examined different measurement tools used to measure SDH factors. As expected, the measures used varied greatly from one study to another. For example, when measuring income, some researhers developed their own measures to capture perceived economic income, while others relied on proxy measures such as postal code data to estimate household income. These differences in the measurement can affect findings among child health outcomes associated with different SDH factors.

In 58 articles, index scores-which conceptualize SDH as cumulative risk scores to understand how different factors may impact the outcome of interest (e.g., Child Opportunity Index; diversitydatakids.org., n.d; Social Vulnerability Index; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, n.d.)-were used to examine SDH factors. These indices offer a practical method for operationalizing a complex, multifactorial construct like SDH (Butler et al., 2013). However, index scores may not allow the separation of the aggregated scores into specific factors that have independent effects and distinct pathways of influence. For example, an index of the factor of neighbourhood would be based on the participants' postal codes or census records, reflecting averages rather than an individual child's unique environment.

Taken together, our findings emphasize that even as some SDH are frequently considered in pediatric research, its study is complicated by variations in conceptualization and the lack of standardization in measurement. While many authors agree on the World Health Organization's definition of SDH, the findings from our review using the PROGRESS-Plus categorization highlighted that the "Plus" category (with 246 articles) included a very wide array of factors with widely variable measurement strategies.

Limitations

The current study is limited in a few ways. One, we only included articles in which the term "social determinants of health" was explicitly used. Articles with specific terms such as "social determinants of anemia" or named specific social factors (e.g., SES, education, etc.) were excluded. This narrow operationalization may have led us to miss conceptually relevant studies and, in turn, to underestimate the breadth and depth of the evidence on social influences on pediatric outcomes. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting only that portion of the literature that explicitly adopts an SDH framing, rather than the full range of work examining social and structural drivers of pediatric health. Two, we limited the age range from 0 to 17 years. Three, we only included peer-reviewed articles, potentially overlooking articles in the grey literature that may have provided additional insights into how SDH is conceptualized.

Four, because of the restricted scope of our search, we were not able to conduct an in-depth examination of specific factors or of study-specific details (e.g., respondent types, disciplinary contexts) that could inform more specific recommendations.

Recommendations

These findings underscore the importance of developing a toolbox of SDH measures based on the PROGRESS+ framework, which could provide a valuable resource for future researchers and professionals, helping them select the most context-appropriate measure. This is not a call for strict standardization, as a single fixed measure risks overlooking the diverse and context-specific ways in which each SDH is experienced across populations. Rather, we advocate for a baseline set of core questions or indicators that could be consistently included across studies to allow for greater comparability and cumulative data, while still leaving room for researchers to adapt or supplement these measures to fit the specific population, cultural context, or setting being studied. This balance between consistency and flexibility would allow the field to move toward more comparable data without sacrificing sensitivity to the populations being studied.

This study revealed that certain essential factors, such as child and parent gender/sex (57 out of 316 articles) and child and maternal religion (13 out of 316 articles), need to be included in more studies of SDH. As gender and sex intersect with health in meaningful ways, gender is recognized as a critical determinant shaping access to resources, opportunities, and power, and gendered divisions of labour and caregiving responsibilities contribute to differential health risks and access to care (Hossain et al., 2025). Furthermore, gender-based discrimination intersects with other factors such as ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and age, compounding health inequalities (World Health Organization, 2019). Similarly, religion has been demonstrated to positively influence health outcomes, and religious affiliation in youth has been associated with better psychological well-being (Chiswick & Mirtcheva, 2013; Pearce et al, 2019). Additionally, expanding this focus to include spirituality could yield further insights, as youth spirituality has also been associated with well-being (Alijanzadeh et al., 2024; Shin et al., 2024) and may be more prevalent than organized religion in certain communities.

Conclusion

In this review of studies explicitly including SDH in pediatric research, we found that the conceptualization of SDH varied substantially, with many authors not explicitly defining their use of the term. The most common SDH factors assessed were related to SES or fell within the broad "Plus" category. Across all of the categories, measures and indices created for the specific study were the most common. Indices allowed researchers to aggregate a large array of variables, but limited comparability across studies.

The systematic SDH is a promising direction for researchers to examine the interplay of social factors and health among children. Our review suggests three key recommendtions for future work: (1) more explicit reporting of the conceptualization and definition of SDH and the specific variables included under this umbrella; (2) the inclusion of a wider range of variables (e.g., religion, gender/sex); and (3) the continued development of a toolkit of measures, including standard indices to capture the complex interplay between these determinants and health outcomes and allow aggregation of larger datasets across pediatric studies.
