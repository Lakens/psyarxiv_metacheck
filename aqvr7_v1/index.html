Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 12 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Interpersonal connection emerges through conversation. Theories of interpersonal communication hold that conversational partners increasingly align in how they express meaning, build on one another's contributions, and experience connection. Yet how such alignment unfolds during a conversation, and how it relates to the experience of a "good" interaction, remains largely untested. Seventy dyads of previously unacquainted individuals held naturalistic conversations while dual functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) recorded neural activity. We measured semantic alignment using convergence entropy and substantive exchange, the number of adjacent turns in which both partners contributed contentful talk.

Contrary to a convergence account, dyads broadened their semantic range over time. Subjective experience was predicted not by semantic alignment but by substantive exchange, particularly in shallow-prompt conversations. Neurally, inter-brain synchrony tracked substantive exchange, whereas semantic alignment was reflected in partners' matched turn-to-turn neural dynamics.

First conversations build connection through reciprocal, substantive exchange, with distinct linguistic and neural signatures.

BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 3

Building Connection Through Conversation:

Linguistic Convergence and Neural Complexity in Dyadic Interaction

Scholarship on interpersonal communication rests on the assumptions that conversation is a strategic and planned endeavor (Berger, 1997; Kellermann, 1992) and alignment in speech content and style is beneficial to the development of a bond (Giles et al., 1991; Altman & Taylor, 1973). Research suggests that individuals mobilize cognitive effort to accomplish comprehension, prediction, and production of speech in conversation, and a predisposition toward efficiency underlies these activities (Gasiorek & Aune, 2021). As conversations that accomplish social goals are ones that successfully align the minds of the interlocutors on situationally important dimensions, communicative behaviors can be viewed as the iterative presentation and exchange of stimuli that evoke particular constructs that coalesce into a coherent mental representation of the conversation (Gasiorek & Aune, 2021).

Decades of theorizing about interpersonal communication endorse these fundamental assumptions, though scholars have noted that empirical research on how the exchange of messages between individuals produces interaction outcomes has been surprisingly scant (Solomon et al., 2021). Following extant theorizing and assumptions about interpersonal interaction, to investigate how interactive alignment occurs, research needs to attend to dyad members as autonomous and interdependent partners and the communication patterns that emerge over the course of the interaction (Abney et al., 2014; Cappella, 1985; Solomon et al., 2021). Further, scholars have advocated for examining the neurobiological substrates that underwrite communication patterns such as interactive alignment (Dragojevic & Giles, 2014; Gasiorek & Aune, 2021). Whereas the field of interpersonal communication has heavily relied on monadic, summative, and self-report data to draw inferences about conversations, support for BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 4 theoretical claims is strengthened by analysis of dyadic, intensive longitudinal, and multimodal data.

In this study, we investigate the emergence of semantic alignment in initial conversations between previously unacquainted strangers. Specifically, we draw on communication accommodation theory (Giles et al., 1991), a dynamic dyadic systems perspective on interpersonal communication (Solomon et al., 2021 (Solomon et al., , 2023)), and the process model of creating understanding (Gasiorek & Aune, 2021) to develop hypotheses about the form, neurobiological underpinnings, and outcomes of getting-to-know-you conversations. Building on these perspectives, we incorporate advances in computational methods and social neuroscience in our tests. In what follows, we elaborate on the theories and methods that informed this project.

Communication accommodation theory (CAT) conceptualizes convergence as the tendency for interactants to adapt their communicative behaviors toward one another in order to manage social distance and shared identity (Giles et al., 1991; Soliz et al., 2021). Empirically, linguistic convergence has been observed across a wide range of communicative features, including lexical choice, syntactic structure, stylistic markers, and semantic framing (Branigan et al., 2000; Pickering & Garrod, 2004; Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010). These adaptations are not limited to surface-level mimicry; speakers also converge at the level of meaning, progressively aligning on conceptual structures and shared interpretations of conversational topics (Bradac et al., 1988; Doyle et al., 2017; Dragojevic & Giles, 2014). From a dyadic perspective, convergence reflects an increasingly interdependent system in which each speaker's contributions are shaped by-and predictive of-the other's speech. As shared understanding increases, interlocutors become more capable of anticipating how their partner will talk about a given topic, indicating greater mutual information in the way meaning is jointly constructed over time.

Complementing CAT, the present study adopts a dynamic dyadic systems (DDS) perspective on interpersonal communication (Solomon et al., 2021 (Solomon et al., , 2023)). Recognizing that individuals in conversation are simultaneously autonomous and interdependent actors, the DDS perspective treats conversation as a dynamic system that manifests qualities like selforganization and self-stabilization; as a dyadic system, the trajectory of interaction takes shape in the exchange of speaking turns between interactants. By tracking the multimodal dynamics constructed by dyad members in an interaction, the DDS perspective privileges the emergent qualities and phase shifts in conversation, such as the alignment of meaning in dialogue. From this perspective, semantic alignment and substantive exchange are distinct signatures of a dyadic system. Semantic alignment is a property of the exchanged turns, concerning how closely the meaning expressed by one partner relates to what the other has just contributed in the adjacent turn. Substantive exchange, by contrast, focuses on the accumulation of reciprocal participation, referring to how often both partners contribute contentful talk across adjacent turns. These properties need not vary together. Partners may sustain extensive back-and-forth while continually introducing new meanings, or remain closely aligned in meaning across relatively few substantive exchanges. Because interactional systems are constituted through repeated exchanges between partners, we therefore treat the amount of substantive exchange as a feature of the dyadic interaction in its own right, alongside semantic alignment.

Further, the process model of creating understanding describes the emergence and functions of alignment in interaction (Gasiorek & Aune, 2021). Gasiorek and Aune (2021) noted the limitations in a conventional, code-based model of communication (i.e., meanings are encoded into and decoded from specific signals). Rather, interaction is viewed as a process of aligning situation models between interlocutors through the presentation of stimuli and activation BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 6 of meme states. Individuals are motivated to align their situation models because the accomplishment of social goals hinges upon the creation of understanding, and humans display an inherent social orientation that drives attempts at seeking alignment. This theoretical process implicates predictive inference-making as a key step in creating understanding: individuals venture informed guesses about what comes next in the conversation, and this activity provides an ongoing basis for evaluating and correcting the situation model of the interaction. Further, as generating predictions and responding to a conversation partner are cognitive activities that require computational resources, the process of interactive alignment is constrained by a disposition toward efficiency. These assumptions about the process of alignment, in turn, manifest patterns of entrainment in neurobiological markers between interactants during a conversation.

Recent work has increasingly framed convergence in explicitly information-theoretic terms, emphasizing predictability, redundancy, and incremental novelty in conversational dynamics. Speaking turns typically introduce new information relative to the immediately preceding context, while simultaneously preserving and building upon stable shared meanings established earlier in the interaction (Enfield, 2017). Skilled conversationalists are perceived as such not because they maximize novelty, but because they introduce just enough new information while maintaining coherence with prior turns. Convergence entropy formalizes this balance by quantifying how much uncertainty remains in one speaker's verbal-semantic contributions given a conversation partner's prior verbal behavior (Rosen & Dale, 2023). Lower convergence entropy indicates a more tightly coupled dyadic system in which meaning unfolds predictably yet flexibly, reducing the degrees of freedom interlocutors must manage during coordination (Kelso, 1994; Riley et al., 2011). Importantly, such alignment has been repeatedly BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 7 linked to smoother interaction, greater interpersonal affiliation, and more successful joint action (Dale et al., 2013; Soliz et al., 2021; Gasiorek & Aune, 2021).

Notably, the research reviewed thus far poses contradicting predictions with regard to how entropy changes over the course of an interaction. The accommodation and alignment framework implies that as partners converge, adjacent turns should become progressively more predictable from one another, resulting in decrease in entropy. The information-exchange account implies the opposite trajectory: as strangers get to know each other, they would strive to maximize novelty while maintaining alignment, hence conversational turns should carry progressively more new information, signaled by an increase in entropy. A getting-to-know-you conversation is thus a suitable context to examine this theoretical debate. In this way, convergence entropy provides a principled bridge between classic communication theories of accommodation and contemporary computational approaches to modeling meaning-making as an emergent, dyadic process.

In addition to documenting the pattern of change in semantic alignment, an aim of this investigation was to examine the neurobiological underpinnings of these changes. These turn-byturn processes of meaning-making are supported not only by the core language network in the brain, but also by broader neural systems involved in social, contextual, and internally oriented cognition (Zada et al., 2026). In this regard, the default mode network (DMN) might play a key role in processes that extend beyond the language-selective system, such as introspection, selfreflection, and the construction of mental narratives (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014). Not a specialized linguistic network, the DMN functions as an active and dynamic sense-making network, integrating incoming external information with internally generated knowledge to construct a coherent situational model of the interaction (Fedorenko et al., 2024; Yeshurun et al., BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 8 2021). Its long temporal receptive window enables it to track discourse-level structure, such as relationships across sentences and narrative segments, more robustly than the language network alone (e.g., Hasson et al., 2015; Lerner et al., 2011). Prior work shows that DMN regions are recruited more strongly for coherent discourse than for disconnected sentences and can integrate information across multiple sentences and conversational turns (Yeshurun et al., 2017). As a result, the DMN may also contribute to interpreting linguistic meaning at a higher level. For example, extracting global themes, maintaining context, or linking incoming utterances to prior knowledge. This joint operation of the language network and DMN suggests that understanding discourse requires not only linguistic decoding but also the domain-general processes that support continuity, integration, and contextual interpretation in communication.

The default mode network (DMN) is centrally involved in the internally oriented processes that support meaningful interpersonal connection. DMN regions are activated when people reflect on their own beliefs, values, autobiographical memories, and future plans (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014; Buckner & Carroll, 2007), and when they juxtapose another person's experiences with their own to construct a shared or comparable mental landscape (Gasiorek & Aune, 2021). During social interaction, this comparison process is essential: understanding another's disclosures requires mapping them onto one's own life history, evaluating their relevance, and anticipating likely future behaviors or intentions-all computations supported by the DMN's integrative architecture and long temporal receptive windows (Yeshurun et al., 2021). Accordingly, DMN activity increases during conversations that demand greater interpersonal perspective-taking, such as deep, self-disclosing, or emotionally rich exchanges, and during interactions that elicit stronger social bonding (e.g., Paunov et al., 2019; Stephens et al., 2010). These findings suggest that DMN engagement reflects the cognitive work BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 9 of constructing high-level relational meaning: the more an interaction requires aligning one's internal models with another's lived experience, the greater the recruitment of DMN systems, and the stronger the resulting sense of connection.

Interpersonal neural coordination, however, can take different forms. Inter-brain synchrony (IBS) asks whether two people's neural signals rise and fall together in time, and the strongest IBS finding from this dataset lies in DMN and predicted connection between partners (Miao et al., 2026). Complexity matching, on the other hand, asks whether two people's signals fluctuate by similar amount at a given timescale, without time-locking the specific fluctuations (Abney et al., 2014). Two partners can be closely matched in the magnitude of their turn-to-turn (i.e. several seconds) neural variability without their signals peaking at the same moment. If meaning construction in conversation is jointly contributed across alternating speaker and listener roles, successful experience may register less as simultaneous co-activation and more as partners' neural dynamics gradually resembling one another, reflecting as an interpersonal synergy (Fusaroli & Tylén, 2016). Distinguishing these two forms of coordination allows us to ask not only whether brains track conversation, but also which facet of conversation each form of coordination tracks.

Methods

Data and Participants

The study included 140 individuals who formed 70 dyads of previously unacquainted participants (20 male-male, 24 female-female, 23 male-female, two male-other, and one femaleother). Participants were recruited from the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Departments of Psychology and Communication subject pools, as well as through flyers posted on campus. All participants self-reported English as their primary language. Experimental procedures were approved by the UCLA Institutional Review Board (IRB #22-001209), and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

The study employed a between-dyads experimental design with two conversation conditions: shallow and deep. The shallow condition included 10 male-male, 13 female-female, BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 11 11 male-female, and two male-other dyads, and the deep condition included 10 male-male, 11 female-female, 12 male-female, and one female-other dyad.

Each experimental session involved two strangers engaging in a face-to-face, get-toknow-you conversation. Conversation prompts were displayed sequentially on a shared computer screen and consisted of either shallow or deep topics (see Table 1), adapted from Kardas and colleagues (2022). Prompts appeared in a fixed order for all dyads. Participants read each prompt simultaneously and volunteered to respond in the order they preferred. They were instructed to remain on topic, take as much time as needed for each prompt, and advance to the next prompt by pressing a button once they had finished discussing the current topic.

During the conversation, both participants wore functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) caps to allow for simultaneous measurement of neural activity (Figure 1). The interaction was audiovisually recorded to enable subsequent linguistic analyses. No experimenter was present in the room during the conversation. Each conversational session lasted approximately 20 minutes. Following the conversation, participants completed a series of questionnaires assessing subjective experiences of the interaction.

Linguistic data

Several methods have been developed to measure linguistic convergence, or the extent to which conversation partners become aligned in their language use (Giles et al., 2023; Duran et al., 2019). We measured linguistic convergence using Convergence Entropy (CE), an information-theoretic approach developed by Rosen and Dale (2023). Leveraging transformer language models, CE quantifies how predictable the semantic content of one speaker's turn is from the other speaker's preceding turn. In this framework, lower CE values indicate greater linguistic alignment, and higher values indicate that a turn is less semantically predictable from the preceding turn and therefore introduces more new semantic information.

Linguistic data preprocessing steps included extracting audio files from the video record of each interaction, transcribing with an automated speech-to-text system (Otter.ai), and verifying across multiple rounds of manual correction by trained research assistants. The preprocessed transcripts were segmented into conversational turns, defined as continuous stretches of speech by one speaker until the other began. Each turn was time-stamped at the onset of its first word.

To calculate CE scores, transcripts were processed with RoBERTa, a transformer language model that represents words and word fragments (i.e. tokens) as numerical vectors capturing their semantic context. These vectors were then used to compute CE for every crossspeaker turn pair, indicating linguistic alignment of two conversation partners on a turn-by-turn level. Since raw CE values are strongly affected by how long a turn is, all analyses below use corrected entropy values adjusted for turn length (referred to hereafter as "entropy"), so that BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 13 longer utterances did not automatically produce higher entropy values. For dyad-level analyses, entropy values were averaged across the conversation to index each dyad's overall level of semantic alignment. Further explanations of the CE method and details of this adjustment are provided in Supplemental Materials.

Because all conversations were designed to last approximately 20 minutes, we devised a measure to capture how dyads use that conversation time. To isolate reciprocal and contentful exchanges, we adopted the five-token threshold recommended by Rosen and Dale (2023) for statistically reliable entropy estimation. We defined a "substantive exchange" as an adjacent turn pair in which both speakers produced at least five tokens. This is a quantitative way to capture portions of conversation in which both speakers contributed sufficient linguistic content for their adjacent turns to be meaningfully compared.

Neural data

Neural activity was recorded simultaneously from both conversation partners using a mobile functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) system, with 40 measurement channels providing partial cortical coverage of four brain networks: the default mode network (DMN), the frontoparietal network (FPN), the ventral attention network (VAN), and the dorsal attention network (DAN; Figure 2). Our hypotheses concern the DMN; the FPN, VAN, and DAN were examined without a priori predictions. Neural data preprocessing steps followed the standardized pipeline reported in Miao et al. (2026), including addressing low-quality channels, motion artifacts, cardiac, respiratory, drift components, among other steps. Measurements that did not meet predefined quality thresholds were treated as missing. As a result, usable network-level data were available for 69 dyads for the DMN, 69 for the FPN, 70 for the VAN, and 49 for the DAN.

Two complementary measures of interpersonal neural coordination were examined. First, we used previously computed inter-brain synchrony (IBS) values from Miao et al. (2026), which quantify the extent to which partners' neural time series rise and fall together over the conversation with time-locked Pearson correlations. In addition to the four network-level groupings described above, the same 40 channels were also grouped into seven anatomically defined regions: medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), left and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (l-dlPFC and r-dlPFC), left and right temporoparietal junction (lTPJ and rTPJ), and left and right superior parietal lobule (lSPL and rSPL). IBS values were available at both the network and anatomical-region levels. The present study reused these existing values to examine their relationship with linguistic behavior.

Second, using the preprocessed channel-wise neural data, we characterized the dynamics of each participant's neural activity using Allan variance (AVAR), and compared partners at the timescale of conversational turn following the logic of complexity matching research, which compares the temporal organization of two interacting systems (Abney et al., 2014 (Abney et al., , 2021;; Rigoli et al., 2020). Complexity matching has also been applied to neural data (Harrison et al., 2018; Carpentier et al., 2020). Whereas conventional complexity matching compares dynamics across multiple timescales, we focused specifically on the timescale of conversational turns to align the neural analysis with our turn-level linguistic measures.

At the turn scale, AVAR captures how much a participant's neural activity changes from one turn-length interval to the next: higher values indicate greater fluctuations, and lower values indicate more stable activity. We then compared partners' AVAR values to derive an "AVAR matching" score, using the absolute difference between partners' AVAR values followed by a negative-log transformation consistent with conventions in the complexity-matching literature BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 15 (Abney et al., 2014). Higher values indicate more similar magnitudes of neural fluctuation between partners, and lower values indicate more differentiated dynamics. Importantly, AVAR matching does not require partners' neural signals to rise and fall together at the same moments (the time-locked coordination is captured by IBS). When linking neural and linguistic data, neural windows were shifted by 5 seconds to account for the hemodynamic delay (Tesler et al., 2023). Further computational details are provided in the Supplemental Materials.

Consider two stylized forms of dance to clarify what AVAR and AVAR matching measure. A waltz dancer might maintain a similar average movement intensity from one phrase to the next, producing low AVAR at the phrase scale. A freestyle street dancer might alternate sharply between restrained and explosive phrases, producing high AVAR. The distinction is not that waltz is regular and freestyle is unpredictable; it is that neighboring phrases differ less in the first example and more in the second. The dyadic comparison then asks whether the two dancers exhibit similar amounts of phrase-to-phrase change. Two steady dancers can be closely matched, as can two highly variable dancers; a highly variable dancer paired with a steady one would be less matched. Matching therefore concerns the resemblance of the partners' dynamics, not the dynamics of either partner alone.

Throughout the analyses, we used dyad as the unit (N = 70 unless otherwise noted).

Results

Across the 70 stranger dyads, conversations yielded 22,726 cross-speaker turn pairs, of which 11,100 (49%) met the criterion for substantive exchange. Dyads produced an average of 159 substantive exchanges (SD = 64, range = 44-325), with a median of 146 exchanges per dyad.

H1: Linguistic Convergence Entropy Over Time H1 posed competing predictions from two theoretical traditions: entropy would decrease if strangers progressively aligned, but increase if they progressively broadened the semantic ground of the conversation. Results supported the latter. We tested this pattern both continuously over the course of the conversation and by comparing entropy across four conversation quarters.

Entropy increased over conversation time across all exchanges (p < .001) and substantive exchanges (p < .001). The same pattern emerged when conversations were divided into quarters.

When each conversation was divided into four quarters, entropy differed significantly across quarters for all exchanges (F(3, 207) = 3.80, p = .011) and for substantive exchanges (F(3, 207) = 4.97, p = .002). Entropy was significantly higher in the final quarter than in the first quarter both across all turn exchanges (t(69) = 2.27, p = .026) and when focusing on substantive exchanges (t(69) = 2.73, p = .008). Results supported H1b over H1a, such that adjacent turns became less semantically predictable as conversations unfolded.

H2: Linguistic Signatures and Self-Reported Conversational Experience

H2 predicted that favorable conversational experiences would be associated with two linguistic signatures: semantic alignment (indicated by lower entropy) and the amount of substantive exchange. Results indicated that semantic alignment was not associated with selfreported conversational experience (all |rs| ≤ .12, all ps ≥ .34). However, greater amount of substantive exchange was associated with greater perceived similarity (partial r = .28, p = .021), engagement (r = .27, p = .023), friendship potential (r = .26, p = .028), and liking (r = .25, p = .041). Similarity, engagement, and friendship potential remained significant after falsediscovery-rate correction within the focal outcome family (qs = .047), while liking was marginal (q = .052). Substantive exchange was not associated with felt depth itself (r = -.09, p = .45), BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 17 suggesting that reciprocal, contentful exchange tracked positive evaluations of the interaction rather than how deep the conversation felt (Figure 3A). Thus, H2 was partially supported for substantive exchange, but not semantic alignment.

We then examined how the amount of substantive exchange differed by conversational depth, operationalized both by assigned prompt condition and by experienced depth (median split). Dyads assigned shallow prompts accumulated more substantive exchanges (M = 184) than those assigned deep prompts (M = 132) (t(68) = 3.66, p < .001, d = 0.87); the same pattern emerged for experienced depth (Ms = 177 vs. 134) (t(68) = 2.91, p = .005, d = 0.70) (Figure 3B).

The assigned conditions also differed in how talk was organized: deep-condition dyads produced longer turns (M = 17.2 vs. 14.3 tokens) (t(68) = 2.30, p = .024, d = 0.55), and fewer total language tokens overall (t(68) = 2.42, p = .018, d = 0.58), while the proportion of turn pairs meeting the substantive-exchange criterion did not differ (.50 vs. .53, p = .39). Thus, the difference in the amount of substantive exchange appears to reflect how conversational contributions were organized rather than simply whether turns were substantive.

Depth did not moderate the associations of substantive exchange with friendship potential, similarity, or liking (interaction Fs(1, 66) ≤ 1.12, ps ≥ .29), and the associations were directionally positive within both shallow and deep conversations (e.g., liking within experienced-deep conversations: r = .38, p = .037). However, engagement was the exception.

H3: Linguistic behavior associates with inter-brain coordination

H3 predicted that linguistic signatures are associated with interpersonal neural coordination. We examined two linguistic measures (convergence entropy and amount of substantive exchanges) against two neural coordination measures: inter-brain synchrony (IBS; previously computed values from Miao et al. (2026)) and AVAR matching. IBS and AVAR matching were not interchangeable measures of coordination: within-network correlation ranged from r = -.24 to .31 (DMN r = -.24, p = .051; FPN r = -.05, p = .66; VAN r = -.04, p = .73; DAN r = .31, p = .033), corresponding to at most 9.4% shared variance.

Convergence entropy was unrelated to IBS across all networks (all ps ≥ .36). In contrast, the amount of substantive exchanges was associated with greater IBS in the DMN (r = .29, p = .015), VAN (r = .32, p = .007), and FPN (r = .26, p = .028), with all three effects surviving Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction across the four networks tested. This association was not significant in the DAN (r = .16, p = .27). Thus, greater inter-brain synchrony was associated with a larger amount of substantive exchanges, but not with greater semantic alignment between turns.

AVAR matching showed a different pattern. In turn-level analyses, greater matching of partners' neural dynamics was associated with lower entropy, indicating greater semantic alignment, in the DMN (p = .007) and right TPJ (p = .008), with no comparable effects in other networks or regions. Discussion

Conversation looks effortless but is cognitively demanding. Conversation requires people to continuously comprehend a partner's contribution, anticipate what may come next, and formulate a response, all while pursuing interpersonal goals under real-time cognitive constraints (Berger, 1997; Gasiorek & Aune, 2021; Kellermann, 1992). This complexity is also reflected at the neural level. Real-life conversation recruits not only the core language system but also broader brain networks involved in constructing meaning across context, mentalizing, and social cognition (Zada et al., 2026). Communication theories have therefore long treated alignment and adaptation as central to successful interaction (Giles et al., 1991), yet considerably less is known about how these processes actually unfold from one exchange to the next and how they relate to interpersonal outcomes (Solomon et al., 2021 (Solomon et al., , 2023)). This study examined this process across linguistic, experiential, and neural levels during first conversations between strangers.

Three findings emerged. First, strangers did not become progressively more semantically predictable over the course of a 20-minute conversation. Instead, adjacent turns broadened in semantic range over time (H1). Second, how positively strangers experienced the conversation BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 20 was related not to their average level of semantic alignment, but to how much reciprocal and substantive exchange they accumulated. For engagement, this relationship was even more pronounced in shallow conversations (H2). Third, different linguistic signatures mapped onto distinct forms of neural coordination. Substantive exchange was associated with inter-brain synchrony in the DMN, VAN, and FPN, whereas semantic alignment was associated with matching of turn-to-turn neural dynamics in the DMN and more specifically, the right TPJ (an anatomically defined region within our DMN coverage) (H3). These findings suggest that conversational coordination should not be reduced to a single form of "alignment": how meaning develops, how much reciprocal and contentful exchange partners construct, and how their neural activity coordinates capture related but distinguishable aspects of interaction. Together, the findings tell a story that conversation is skilled work built turn-by-turn: prompts are only the starting points of conversations, but engagement is co-constructed with reciprocal and substantive exchanges by the conversation partners themselves.

H1 qualifies a straightforward convergence account of initial interactions between strangers. Communication accommodation theory conceptualizes convergence as interlocutors' mutual adaptation across communicative behaviors, often in ways that reduce social distance (Giles et al., 1991; Soliz et al., 2021). If this process manifested as progressively increasing semantic similarity, adjacent turns should have become more predictable as strangers talk.

Instead, entropy increased: strangers moved from relatively constrained early exchanges toward a broader semantic range as they got to know each other.

Instead, this trajectory resonates with social penetration theory, which proposes that relationship development involves an expansion in breadth and depth of self-disclosure during conversation (Altman & Taylor, 1973). Although social penetration theory primarily describes BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 21 changes that unfold over longer-term relationship development, the present findings suggest that a related process may already be observable within a single initial conversation. As strangers establish enough common ground to sustain the interaction, successive turns may become less constrained by the immediately preceding content, allowing the conversation to move into a wider range of meanings and topics. Convergence, in other words, need not imply that conversational content becomes progressively narrower or more redundant. This pattern does not imply an absence of accommodation. Rather, it suggests that accommodation during conversation need not take the form of progressively narrowing semantic content. Convergence can occur across multiple dimensions (such as in timing and other features of interaction), even while partners introduce increasingly varied ideas. The process model of creating understanding describes conversation as requiring both prediction and the continual updating of situation models (Gasiorek & Aune, 2021). Some predictability is necessary for partners to understand and respond to one another, but perfectly predictable conversation would leave little room for new information. The increase in entropy observed here may therefore reflect a balance crucial to developing good conversation: turns ought to be sufficiently connected to what came before to be understood while progressively opening new semantic ground that moves the interaction forward. H2 connects linguistic signatures with self-reported experiences of the interaction.

Average entropy was unrelated to participants' evaluations of the conversation. By contrast, dyads that accumulated more substantive exchanges tended to report greater engagement, liking, similarity, and friendship potential. This distinction is theoretically informative: semantic similarity alone was not what differentiated positively experienced conversations.

Notably, the moderation by conversational depth further clarifies this pattern. Deep prompts produced greater engagement on average, but within deep conversations, accumulating more substantive exchanges did not predict additional engagement. The relationship was instead concentrated among shallow conversations, whether depth was defined experimentally or by participants' subjective experience. These results suggest that deep questions may relatively directly provide conversational material that supports engagement; but when the material itself is shallow, the interaction appears to depend more on what partners construct from it. A question about the weather can still produce an engaging conversation, but doing so may require partners to repeatedly take up, elaborate, and return substantive contributions to one another. This finding complements a dynamic dyadic systems (DDS) perspective and conversational-motif analysis on interpersonal communication, which locates the consequential structure of conversation in the unfolding exchange between partners rather than in isolated messages or whole-conversation summaries (Solomon et al., 2021 (Solomon et al., , 2023 (Solomon et al., , 2025)). DDS treats a turn not as an isolated behavior but as both a response to what preceded it and a stimulus for what follows, such that interaction emerges through sequences of mutually contingent contributions. Our measure of substantive exchange captures one simple feature of this process: how often adjacent turns contain reciprocal and substantial contributions from both partners.

Unlike conversational-motif approaches, which distinguish recurring sequences based on the functions and linguistic characteristics of turns, substantive exchange does not identify what kind of interaction is occurring. Instead, it provides a scalable measure of how much reciprocal, contentful back-and-forth accumulates over a conversation. The present findings suggest that even this relatively simple property of turn-to-turn organization may be consequential for how strangers experience an interaction.

While the substantive exchange measure captures one important form of interaction, it should not be treated as a comprehensive measure of meaningful engagement. Conversation analysis has long emphasized that the function of a turn depends on its position within a larger sequence, and that interaction can be highly coordinated without symmetrical verbal contributions (Sacks et al., 1974; Schegloff, 2007). Storytelling is a clear example: one speaker may hold the floor across extended turns while the recipient contributes brief acknowledgments or backchannels that are interactionally consequential but fall below our five-token threshold.

Our measure therefore privileges reciprocal, contentful back-and-forth while underrepresenting other forms of sequence organization. This limitation may be particularly relevant to the deep-prompt condition, where participants produced fewer and longer turns, consistent with more extended telling-and-listening sequences. Because we did not formally distinguish storytelling, backchanneling, or other sequential structures, we cannot determine whether such patterns account for the weaker relationship between substantive exchange and engagement in deep conversations. Future work could combine scalable quantitative measures with conversation-analytic or motif-based approaches to sequence organization. With the support of recent development of dynamic data visualization dashboards such as DIMS (Miao et al., 2025), this allows different forms of interaction (i.e. reciprocal exchange, extended telling, active listening, and others) to be identified rather than collapsed into a single measure.

H3 extends this dyadic account beyond language. Substantive exchange was associated with time-locked inter-brain synchrony, whereas semantic alignment showed an association with similarity in partners' turn-to-turn neural dynamics. The key point is therefore not simply that conversation partners' brains coordinate, but that different forms of neural coordination appear to BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 24 map onto different features of the interaction. Inter-brain synchrony captures whether partners' neural activity co-fluctuates at corresponding moments, whereas AVAR matching captures similarity in the magnitude of turn-to-turn neural change without requiring the signals to move in lockstep. This distinction may be especially relevant for conversation, where partners alternate between speaking, listening, interpreting, and responding. Similarity in the dynamics of those processes may therefore capture forms of interpersonal coordination that conventional synchrony measures miss, broadening the methodological toolkit for studying naturally unfolding interaction.

The recurrence of the DMN and right TPJ across analyses is also theoretically suggestive.

Prior work in this sample linked IBS in DMN and right TPJ to felt interpersonal connection (Miao et al., 2026), while the present analyses connect it to both reciprocal exchange and semantic alignment through different neural metrics. This convergence is consistent with an important role for mentalizing-related systems in conversation.

More broadly, the study advances a dynamic dyadic approach by using the conversational turn as a common unit for linking linguistic and neural processes. This moves analysis closer to the interaction itself: rather than relying primarily on transcripts or post-conversation summaries, contemporary recording and computational methods make it possible to align multiple streams of interaction data (including language, self-reported experience, and neural activity) within the unfolding conversation. Such multimodal analysis can reveal how different forms of coordination emerge together, or diverge, from one exchange to the next (Solomon et al., 2021 (Solomon et al., , 2023 (Solomon et al., , 2025)).

A central implication is that interpersonal coordination is not a unitary phenomenon. Semantic alignment, reciprocal participation, time-locked neural synchrony, and similarity in BUILDING CONNECTION THROUGH CONVERSATION 25 neural dynamics did not simply rise and fall together. Their divergence is theoretically useful rather than problematic, suggesting that successful conversation can involve multiple, partly independent forms of coordination operating at different levels and timescales. Multimodal approaches can therefore refine communication theory by specifying what is coordinated during interaction, rather than treating alignment as a single underlying property of the dyad. For example, combining scalable computational measures with sequence-sensitive approaches, facilitated by dynamic data dashboards such as DIMS (Miao et al., 2025), would allow future research to distinguish reciprocal dialogue, extended telling, active listening, and other conversational organizations rather than treating one structure as representative of engagement as a whole.

Conclusion

Together, findings across linguistic, experiential, and neural dimensions portray conversation as skilled work built turn by turn. Prompts may provide the starting point, but they do not determine whether an interaction becomes engaging. Especially when prompts were shallow, engagement was associated with the reciprocal and substantive exchanges that partners constructed together over the course of the conversation. At the same time, strangers did not simply converge toward increasingly similar semantic content; their conversations broadened semantically as they unfolded. The brain registered both sides of the story: inter-brain synchrony tracked the accumulation of substantive exchange, while matching of turn-to-turn neural dynamics showed preliminary links to semantic alignment.
