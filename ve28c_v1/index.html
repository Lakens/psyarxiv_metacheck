To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 15 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate). This validation was made with an earlier, looser pattern; the pattern now requires “significant” or “significance” to follow the trigger word closely (#695).

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

Improved alcohol labels are a population-level intervention with the potential to reduce selection and consumption of alcohol. The primary aim of this study was to assess the acceptability of implementing (i) alcohol calorie labelling, (ii) alcohol health warning labels (HWLs), and (iii) both types of labels combined, each presented with or without images. The specific labels used were based on those being proposed for national implementation in Ireland.

Methods

Participants (n=994) were randomly assigned to one of six groups in an online experiment with a between subject 3 (Label Type: HWL only; calorie labelling only; combined label [HWLs and calorie labelling]) x 2 (Message Type: description; description plus example image) factorial design.

The primary outcome was acceptability of implementing the labelling policy. Secondary outcomes were perceived effectiveness and potential harms. Free-text comments were analysed using content analysis.

Results

There was a significant main effect of Label Type (F(2, 990)=4.27, p=0.014, η²=0.01), with ₚ acceptability lower for HWLs (p=0.01, d=0.23). There was no main effect of Message Type or interaction. All types of labels were perceived as relatively acceptable. There were no overall differences in perceived effectiveness or harms of the labels.

Conclusions

HWLs and calorie information were both perceived as relatively acceptable, although HWLs were less accepted. The way the labelling policy was presented did not affect acceptability. Although further evaluation with other labelling interventions is warranted, this suggests giving the public more detailed and visual information about a labelling policy does not decrease acceptability of the policy.

Short summary

In an online randomised experiment, 994 participants viewed alcohol calorie labelling, alcohol health warning labels (HWLs), or both types of labels combined, either presented with or without images. HWLs and calorie information were both perceived as relatively acceptable. The way the labelling policy was presented did not affect acceptability.

Background

The harmful use of alcohol is a key cause of non-communicable diseases worldwide (WHO, 2024). Reducing alcohol consumption would reduce conditions like heart disease and many cancers (Murray et al., 2020; Rehm et al., 2018). Current alcohol labelling on product packaging is inadequate, for example in the UK the only required information is overall volume, alcohol by volume (ABV) and allergen information, and many products do not include any additional voluntary information (AHA, 2026).

Labelling interventions are a promising and feasible population-level intervention across a range of health-harming products (Clarke et al., 2025; Noar et al., 2016). Given this, there is increasing policy interest in the implementation of improved labels for alcohol (IAS, 2021; WHO, 2023; AHA, 2026), which could encompass many types of labelling. The current study focuses on health warning labels (HWLs), which describe the adverse health consequences of drinking either in text-only form or with an accompanying image depicting the health consequence (often termed graphic health warnings), and nutritional labels that display the drink's calorie content (calorie labels). Initial evidence suggests the addition of health warnings and nutritional information can increase knowledge and awareness (Correia et al., 2024; Steptoe et al., 2024), and may reduce alcohol purchasing (Zhao et al., 2020 , Zuckerman et al., 2024). Alcohol product labelling interventions have been implemented or are currently being considered in many countries, including in Europe and the USA (IAS, 2025; Kokole et al., 2026 , WHO 2025). The government of Ireland have outlined plans to implement a warning that contains a health warning, calorie information, and a pregnancy warning (WCRF, 2024), although there has been a delay to their implementation from 2026 to 2028 (Eurocare, 2025).

Public perceptions of and support for a policy are key determinants of its implementation (Burstein, 2003; Schwaid, 2023; WHO 2024). This may be particularly important in relation to alcohol labelling; for example, concerns have been raised about the potential for calorie information to have unintended consequences, such as by causing harm in individuals with eating disorders (Raffoul et al., 2026) or encouraging drinkers to choose drinks with a high alcohol and low-calorie content (Maynard et al., 2018; Polden et al., 2023). A gap in understanding of the harms of alcohol in both the public and politicians may also undermine support for alcohol policies (Calnan et al., 2025). Initial evidence broadly suggests that improved labels that include health warnings are relatively acceptable to the public (Greene et al.,2026; Grummon et al.,2025; Reynolds et al., 2019). However, levels of public support for alcohol labelling policies do vary in the literature, with some evidence suggesting the public are opposed to these types of labels (Clarke et al., 2020; Pechey et al., 2020). These differences could in part be due to the way the policy is described or presented. For example, in a study that described the policy (Reynolds et al., 2019) there was higher support for graphic HWLs (78% of participants supporting) compared to a study that showed participants an image of the label (34% supporting) (Clarke et al., 2020). This may indicate that although the public do not oppose the concept of labelling, viewing the HWL may reduce support for them because they are designed to evoke negative emotions. Exploring differences in the way a policy is presented to participants can inform researchers and policymakers as to how best to describe and present these types of policies to the public, including when assessing their potential support.

Calorie labels on alcohol are also relatively supported and initial evidence suggests they may be more accepted than HWLs (Clarke et al., 2023; Grummon et al.,2025). Although studies that primarily aim to investigate the impact of alcohol labels on behavioural outcomes often include measures of acceptability (Clarke et al., 2021 (Clarke et al., , 2023;; Hobin et al., 2020), we are not aware of any randomised studies that directly compare HWLs and calorie labels that include a primary outcome of acceptability. Evidence also suggests that there might be an interaction between nutrient information and HWLs (Hobin et al., 2024). Given that labels suggested for implementation often include both calorie information and health warnings, more evidence is required on the perceptions and potential impact of a combined label that includes multiple components.

Both perceived effectiveness and the possibility for the policy to lead to unintended consequences have been shown to be primary drivers of public support for a policy in both empirical research (Cornwell & Krantz, 2014; Mantzari et al., 2022; Reynolds et al., 2018 Reynolds et al., , 2020) ) and in modern theories of policy support (Grelle & Hoffman, 2024; Reynolds et al., 2026). Previous studies suggest that drinkers believe their drinking patterns may change if these types of labelling policies were introduced (Steptoe et al., 2024), but we are not aware of any studies that directly compare calorie information to HWLs on this measure. There is also some evidence to suggest people may support policies when the target is people in general (versus themselves as an individual) and that they believe policies are more likely to be effective for others compared to themselves (Cornwell & Krantz, 2014). This highlights the importance of examining differences in perceived effectiveness and potential unintended consequences to both the individual and to others.

The primary aim of this study was to assess the acceptability of implementing (i) alcohol calorie labelling, (ii) alcohol HWLs, and iii) both types of labels combined (calorie labelling and HWLs). Each of these labels were described to participants, either with or without an image of the label, and the specific labels used were based on those being proposed for implementation in Ireland (Murray, 2025). The secondary aim was to explore perceived effectiveness and perceived unintended consequences of these labels.

Methods

The study protocol (https://osf.io/x4kr6/overview) and a statistical analysis plan (https://osf.io/tdcm4/overview) were pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF). This study was approved by the Bath Spa Ethics Committee (ref: 010724NC).

Design

Participants were randomly assigned to one of six groups (see Figure 1) in an experiment with a between subject 3 (Label Type: combined label [HWLs & calorie labelling] vs HWL only vs calorie labelling only) x 2 (Message Type: description vs description + example image) factorial design.

Setting

The study was conducted online, designed and hosted on the Qualtrics online survey platform (http://www.qualtrics.com/).

Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were adults (18+, the legal minimum age for alcohol use in the United Kingdom), residing in the UK. Participants were recruited to the study via Prolific, which has a pool of over 150,000 participants (https://www.prolific.com/). The sample was nationally representative based on Prolific quotas (for age, gender, and ethnicity). Recruitment occurred in September 2025.

Sample size

There was no direct evidence available within the literature from which to estimate the effect of these different label formats on acceptability. A maximum sample size of 1000 was possible with available resources (~166/per group). With 80% power and at alpha 5%, 1000 participants would detect Cohen's d of 0.2 or greater between the six groups with a 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Randomisation

Randomised assignment of participants was completed via the default algorithm in Qualtrics with a ratio of 1:1:1:1:1:1. Participants were unaware of their group assignment throughout the study.

Intervention

In the description only condition, participants were told that the study was aiming to elicit opinions on a potential policy that the government could implement and were then presented with a description of the policy. In the description plus image conditions, participants were given the same information but were also presented with an example image of the intervention (see Figure 1). The image included a label (depending on the condition the participant was randomised to) next to either a beer or wine product, depending on participant preference (see supplementary figure S1 for an example). A note highlighted that the policy would involve presenting the label on the alcohol packaging (e.g., "note that the label below will be displayed on alcohol packaging"). The specific health warnings were based on those being proposed for implementation in Ireland (WCRF, 2024), which contain a health warning, calorie information, and a pregnancy warning. In all conditions, the labels included a pregnancy warning. As the pregnancy warning is already currently often included on alcohol drink packaging (RSPH, 2018), we did not look at this label separately, and instead focused on the additional components being proposed. For the HWL and calorie labelling conditions, a section of the overall label was presented (i.e., either the HWL or calorie information depending on group allocation), alongside the pregnancy warning. Calorie information was given per serving as in previous studies on calorie labelling (Clarke et al., 2023), along with the product weight (in grams)foot_0 and Kilojoules.

Measures Primary outcome measure

Acceptability of the labelling policy.

Assessed by taking the mean of two items (Cronbach's alpha = 0.9; inter-item correlation, r = 0.82), adapted from previous research assessing the impact of sugar tax (Reynolds et al., 2018) and alcohol HWLs (Clarke et al., 2023). Participants were first asked: 'Do you support or oppose a government policy of putting this label on alcohol products?', with responses on a seven-point scale from 1 (strongly oppose) to 7 (strongly support). Then they were asked 'Would you find it acceptable or unacceptable if the government puts this label on alcohol products?', with responses on a seven-point scale from 1 (completely unacceptable) to 7 (completely acceptable). Mean overall ratings past the scale mid-point (i.e. >4), indicated that the label was acceptable.

Secondary outcome measures

Perceived effectiveness of the labelling policy

Two variables were created using single item questions adapted from a previous study on alcohol labels (Blackwell et al., 2018). Participants were asked 'Do you think a government policy of putting this label on alcohol products could make you feel motivated to drink less alcohol?' and 'Do you think a government policy of putting this label on alcohol products could make others feel motivated to drink less alcohol?'. Questions were answered on a seven-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Perceived unintended consequences of the labelling policy

Two variables were created using single item questions adapted from a study on public policies (Cornwell & Krantz, 2014). Participants were asked 'Do you think a government policy of putting this label on alcohol products could lead to harms to you?' and 'Do you think a government policy of putting this label on alcohol products could lead to harms for the public?'. Questions were answered on a seven-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Attention check

To ensure that participants were paying attention to the questions and reading them properly, after the primary outcome participants were asked 'Please select the response 'strongly agree'' from a set of options. Individuals choosing any other options were screened out of the study.

Other measures

Free response comments

Participants were asked open-ended questions where they could provide their thoughts on the government labelling policy, particularly in relation to acceptability, perceived effectiveness and potential adverse effects, as well as any further comments.

Demographic characteristics

Age, gender and highest qualification attained (with classification based on UK definitions (Office for National Statistics).

Drinking characteristics

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification test (AUDIT-C) was used to assess drinking behaviour. This questionnaire uses three questions to detect heavy and binge drinking behaviour in a general population (Bush et al., 1998). A score of 0 to 4 indicates low risk; 5 to 7 indicates increasing risk; 8 to 10 indicates higher risk; 11 to 12 indicates possible dependence (Bush et al.,1998).

Procedure

After reading the information sheet and consenting to take part, participants completed screening questions relating to their typical consumption of alcohol and indicated their preferred beverage (either beer or wine) to determine which drink type they viewed in the image conditions. If no preference was indicated, they were randomised to view either beer or wine. Participants were then asked questions regarding their demographic characteristics.

Participants were randomised to one of six groups (see 'Intervention' section). They were presented the description of the labelling policy, either with or without an example image, and were asked to answer the primary and secondary outcome questions (see measures section). They then completed the open-ended questions. Following completion, participants were debriefed, including receiving information about the study and the health consequences of consuming excess alcohol. Participants were reimbursed for their time via Prolific.

Inattentive participants were screened out via the embedded attention check. A captcha question was also used at the beginning of the survey to screen out bots and a threshold of 0.5 for excluding potential bots was used.

Statistical analysis.

Analyses were pre-registered in a statistical analysis plan (https://osf.io/tdcm4). Associated data and code are available (https://osf.io/h6bzx).

For the primary and secondary outcomes a 2-way ANOVA summary was reported, with Label Type and Message Type as the independent variables. The initial model included the two main effects of Label Type and Message Type respectively, at a threshold for significance of 0.025 (i.e., 5%/2). This model was run with and without an interaction term, and the main effects were interpreted in the models without an interaction term. If the interaction term was significant then post hoc tests were conducted.

All outcomes indicated some violations of normality, assessed via QQ plots and histograms. A non-parametric alternative to an ANOVA (Aligned Rank Transform [ART]) was therefore conducted for all primary and secondary outcomes. These are reported in the supplementary material 2 (Table S1). Results did not differ from the ANOVA results.

Free text comments were manually coded and sorted into categories through content analysis (see supplementary material 3).

There were minor changes to the registered analysis plan (effect sizes for ANOVAs were reported as partial η² rather than d).

Results

Sample characteristics

Figure 2 shows the flow of participants through the study and Table 1 the demographic characteristics by group. 1014 participants were recruited into the study; 7 of these dropped out, 9 failed attention checks and 4 were identified as potential bots using bot detection within Qualtrics. This resulted in a final sample size of 994 included in the analysis. The sample was 51% female and the mean age was 46.42 (standard deviation [SD] = 15.13). Groups were well balanced on all characteristics. Raw primary and secondary outcome data are shown in Table 2. ANOVA results are shown in Table 3.

Primary outcome

There was a significant main effect of Label Type (F (2, 990) = 4.27, p = 0.014, η² = 0.01) ₚ on acceptability. Post-hoc comparisons indicated a significant difference in acceptability between the calorie label and HWL label (p = 0.01, d = 0.23), with acceptability lower for HWLs (see supplementary material 2, Table S2 for marginal means and Table S3 for post hoc tests). There was no evidence for a main effect of Message Type [F(1, 988) = 0.63, p = 0.43] or an interaction between Label Type and Message Type F(2, 988) = 1.37, p = 0.25].

In general, the labels were all relatively accepted by participants. For all groups the proportion of participants that agreed to some extent with the labelling policies (indicated by a score of above 4) ranged from 77.8% (in the HWL only + image group) to 86% (in the calorie labelling only + image group).

Secondary outcomes

Perceived effectiveness

There was no evidence of an effect of Label Type or Message Type on perceived effectiveness of the labels at changing the participant's behaviour or the public's behaviour [Label Type: F(2, 990) = 0.28, p = 0.757; Message Type: F(1, 990) = 2.12, p = 0.14].

In general, most participants indicated they did not feel the labels would be effective in getting them to drink less (scores above 4 indicating that they agreed to some extent with the statement), with the proportion ranging from 36% to 44%. The proportions were higher for those who thought the labels might be effective in getting others to drink less, with proportions ranging from 59% to 65%.

Potential for harms

There was no evidence for main effects of Label Type or Message Type on perceived potential harms to the participant or to the public [Label Type: F(2, 990) = 0.85, p = 0.427; Message Type: F(1, 990) = 0.005, p = 0.946]. There was evidence for a significant interaction for perceived harms to self [F (2, 988) = 3.87, p = 0.021, η² = 0.01]. Post-hoc comparisons ₚ (see supplementary material 2, Tables S4 and S5) indicated a significant difference between the calorie labelling only + image group and the combined label + image group (p = 0.03), with higher scores for the combined label (indicating higher perceived harms). There was evidence for a significant interaction for perceived harms to others [F (2, 988) = 3.87, p = 0.009, η² = 0.01)]. Post-hoc comparisons indicated a significant difference between ₚ description of the calorie labelling only group (with no image) and the description of the combined label group (with no image) (p = 0.016), with lower scores for the calorie label (indicating higher perceived harms to others).

In general, the proportion of participants that perceived harms to either themselves or to others for any of the label types (indicated by scores over 4) was low, with the proportion below 10% for both secondary outcomes in all groups.

Exploratory analysis

As the overall analysis includes a label that combines both elements of the calorie and HWL label within the combined label, we conducted an analysis that included only the calorie and HWL labels in a 2 (Label Type: calorie vs HWL) x 2 (Message Type: description + image vs description only) ANOVA. The results were similar to the primary outcome analysis. There was a main effect of Label Type [F(1, 661) = 8.87, p = 0.003, η² = 0.01] and no evidence for ₚ a main effect of Message Type or interaction between Label Type and Message Type.

Free text comments

Overall, 988 participants left a free-text comment in response to at least one question. Support for all labels was relatively high, with 40% (n=394) of participants leaving a comment that suggested they found the labels acceptable, 25% (n=249) of participants implying that they supported their implementation in some form, and only 7% (n=73) of participants leaving a comment that explicitly opposed the labels. Overall, just over 50% (n=507) of participants emphasised that they thought the labels could reduce general drinking behaviour or have wider public health benefits, 17% (n=166) of participants highlighted they could lead to reductions in drinking for specific groups of people (e.g., heavier drinkers), and 32% (n=313) of participants thought they would not have any impact on drinking behaviour. In terms of other potential benefits, 49% (n=486) emphasised that the labels could be useful for increasing knowledge and awareness (i.e., of health risks and calorie content). Overall, 12% (n=116) of participants highlighted there may be potential for unintended harms either in general or for specific groups. Example comments can be seen in Table 4 and the full overview of the free text comment analysis and results -including the breakdown by groupcan be found in the supplementary material 3 (Table S6).

Discussion

Overall, adding health warnings that describe the risk of liver disease and cancer to alcoholic products were less acceptable than adding calorie information, albeit both label types were relatively acceptable. There was no evidence that there were any differences in acceptability, perceived effectiveness or potential for harms, between describing a labelling policy only versus describing with an accompanying image of the label.

The finding that health warnings are less acceptable than calorie labels is in line with other studies that compare support for these labelling policies (Clarke et al., 2023; Grummon et al., 2025), although -based on both the label ratings and the free text comments -all of the labels in the current study were relatively acceptable overall. Despite this, future work should focus on how to increase the acceptability of HWLs, given their proposed implementation in many countries and continued policy interest (WHO, 2025). Although there was a difference in acceptability between HWLs and calorie labels, there was no evidence of a difference in acceptability between these labels and the label that combined HWLs and calories. More specifically, adding a HWL to calorie information does not seem to reduce acceptability. Given many countries have implemented alcohol labels that contain multiple components (Kokole et al., 2026), this suggests labelling policies that combine multiple label types may be as acceptable to the public as the single label components.

There were no differences in acceptability between describing a labelling policy only versus describing with an accompanying image of the label (hereto described as visualising the labelling policy), which was not in the expected direction given previous study findings (e.g., 78% for a description of a labelling policy (Reynolds et al., 2019) versus 34% for an image of the policy (Clarke et al., 2020)). Current findings therefore suggest the acceptability of this labelling policy is similar irrespective of whether it is described in text form or with an accompanying image and these earlier findings may be attributable to different samples and methods rather than an effect of describing vs visualising. Many policy communications occur through different modalities that often use imagery, such as print, broadcast, and social media, with social media shown to be the most widely used globally (Mede et al., 2025). Current findings suggest that a visual policy instrument such as labels is unaffected by mode of communication: adding additional visual information does not decrease acceptability of the labelling policy compared to a text description. This is in line with other evidence for food policy research, which finds that communicating evidence via different modalities (visualising versus re-expressing) leads to similar levels of public support and perceived effectiveness (Reyolds et al., 2022). Given that imagery may have other wider benefits such as increasing understanding of a policy, this has important implications for policy communication. However, this finding needs to be replicated for other labelling types, as previous studies measuring the acceptability of health warnings have focused on graphic HWLs that contain an image alongside a text warning, whereas the current study used textonly HWLs. These warnings were selected as they are most likely to be introduced as a policy, but future research should also investigate any differences in acceptability for less accepted policies such as graphic HWLs, which have been shown to be less accepted in general and evoke stronger reactions -such as negative emotions -compared to text-only HWLs (Clarke et al., 2021).

In terms of secondary outcomes, there were no differences in the perceived effectiveness of the labels at changing the participant's behaviour or the public's behaviour by either label type or between describing versus visualising the labelling policy. Based on overall scores that agreed to some extent with labels being effective in reducing drinking, participants did not view the labels as being particularly effective in getting them to drink less (36-44%) but were more likely to say they were useful in getting others to drink less (59-65%). In the freetext comments, half of the participants thought the labels might have some benefits on reducing drinking at a population level and 17% of participants highlighted they may be effective for specific groups, such as heavy drinkers. This is in line with evidence that the public often believe policies are more effective for other than themselves (Cornwell & Krantz, 2014). Additionally, the majority of participants in the study were low risk drinkers who may not perceive their drinking as needing to change, and evidence suggests that heavier drinkers are more likely to say they will change drinking patterns in response to calorie labelling than low-risk drinkers (Steptoe et al., 2024). In the free-text comments, many of the participants highlighted that the labels could have a beneficial impact on increasing awareness or knowledge, which is in line with previous research suggesting labels may influence these cognitive factors (Correia et al., 2024; Steptoe et al., 2024). These potential impacts are important in their own right but may also serve as precursors to behaviour change.

There were no overall differences in perceived harms by either label type or between describing versus visualising the labelling policy. There were significant interactions for both perceived harms to self and others. For perceived harm to self this was due to a significant difference between the calorie labelling only and the combined label in the description plus image conditions. For perceived harms to others this was due to a difference between calorie labelling only and the combined label in the description only conditions. We cannot identify any plausible explanation for these specific findings, but this may suggest differences in perceived harms for calorie labels alone compared to when presented alongside HWLs. Given other research has found a difference in response to different combinations of nutrient information and HWLs (Hobin et al., 2024), future research should therefore investigate any differences in perceived harms by different label combinations. Overall, perceived harms to both self and others were low, with the vast majority of participants suggesting that they disagree to some extent that this policy could lead to any harms. This is in line with research that suggests despite some concerns that calorie information might have adverse consequences there is limited evidence that this is the case (Raffoul et al., 2026) and it may explain why they are a relatively accepted policy. However, several participants did raise concerns in the free-text responses about the potential impact of calorie information on individuals with eating disorders (9% of participants in the calorie labelling only groups), suggesting a perception that such labels may be harmful for specific populations. Similar concerns have been reported in relation to calorie labelling on food menus (Trompeter et al., 2025). Consequently, the potential for unintended harms among vulnerable groups should remain an important focus of future research.

Strengths and limitations

This pre-registered randomised study is the first to our knowledge to directly compare the acceptability of HWLs and calorie information. The sample was representative of a UK population.

This study has some limitations. First, as we were primarily interested in the differences in responses to different labels we did not include a control group. Future studies could therefore investigate attitudes towards labelling policies compared to a control label, for example if they seek to assess differences in responses compared to current information included on alcohol product packaging. Second, the majority of drinkers in the study were low risk. Given a potential difference in attitudes towards labelling by drinking level (Steptoe et al., 2024), further evidence is needed to investigate any differences in response to labelling interventions by drinking characteristics. Finally, the sample was representative of the UK population but participants were highly educated overall, although there is evidence to suggest that responses to labelling may not differ by SES group (Robinson et al., 2022).

Conclusions

Health warnings and calorie labels on alcohol Using labels based on those being proposed for implementation in Ireland, we found that overall alcohol HWLs and calorie information are perceived as relatively acceptable, although HWLs were less accepted than calorie labelling. We found no differences in acceptability when describing an alcohol labelling policy compared to when showing an example image alongside the policy description. Although further evaluation with other labelling interventions is warranted, this suggests giving the public more detailed and visual information about a labelling policy does not decrease acceptability of the policy.
