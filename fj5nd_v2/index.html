Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Importantly, DCD presumed this fragment knowledge to be conscious, since participants were conducting these identifications intentionally.

Such a conscious judgment that the string is correct does not imply, in any sense, that participants were conscious of prior memories or of explicit rules they learned.

Thus, we do not simply ask whether participants are aware of anything, but specifically whether they are aware of the bigrams that support their judgments.

Crucially, it allows us to assess awareness under the same level of sensitivity as DCD and P&P: the awareness measure is administered immediately after each grammaticality decision, and participants are explicitly encouraged to report as conscious even fragmentary knowledge they are unsure of (e.g., Newell & Shanks, 2014, 2023; Shanks & St. John, 1994).

A second factor was the specific grammar (A or B; see Figure 1) that participants were presented with, because we used a standard two-grammar crossover design that controls for possible confounds pertaining to the grammars (e.g., Dienes & Scott, 2005; Norman & Price, 2012).

Thus, participants were randomly assigned to one of the four groups resulting from the combination of stimulus type (letters and faces; see Figure 2) and grammar (A or B).

After giving their informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of the two stimulus conditions (letters: N = 145, with 102 included in the final analysis; faces: N = 128, with 86 included).

During the acquisition phase, participants were told they would complete a memory task and were instructed to memorize each displayed string as thoroughly as possible, without being informed about the underlying grammar.

Following the acquisition phase, participants were informed that the strings they had previously memorized were generated by a complex grammar.

Participants were presented with precise definitions of these response options (see Supplementary Materials, Appendix A), which, in short, indicated that guessing refers to a random response, intuition and familiarity refer to feelings they cannot further justify, and rules and remembering refer to knowledge that is conscious and that they could verbalize if asked.

Next, participants completed an unexpected comprehension test designed to assess their understanding of the response options for the awareness scale (see Supplementary Materials Appendix B), and we included in the analyses only participants who made a maximum of one confusion between implicit and explicit options but immediately corrected it after re-reading the instructions.

Participants had a maximum of 12 seconds to decide.

Finally, participants completed a brief post-experimental survey in which they reported any explicit knowledge of the grammars.

We selected the images from the FACES database (Ebner et al., 2010) and chose one young actor (to match our participants’ demographics), of male gender (since for female faces, the level of facial attractiveness interacts with the learning effect; Ziori & Dienes, 2015), and with a high (98.66%) mean accuracy in emotional identification among young participants in the database’s validation study (Ebner et al., 2018; see the online repository for the R code used in the selection procedure).

Essentially, we tested whether learning had occurred in the two conditions (i.e., whether participants had above-chance classification accuracy in the faces and letters conditions); further, we aimed to determine whether participants had above-chance classification accuracy when they relied on implicit knowledge and, separately, on explicit knowledge.

We tested whether participants were more unaware of the grammars in the faces condition than in the letters condition.

Before the test phase began, participants completed a comprehension test in which they were given explicit examples of thoughts that might arise during grammaticality decisions and were asked to label them using the appropriate decision strategy (see Appendix B for the full instructions).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

Humans often appear to possess knowledge they cannot fully articulate. For instance, in the landmark artificial grammar learning (AGL) task, participants acquire knowledge of a complex regularity (grammar) that structures letter sequences, while typically claiming to have little or no subjective access to the regularity, suggesting that learning has been largely unconscious. A highly influential argument against this conclusion comes from studies showing that participants can identify simple letter pairs (bigrams) that the grammar is composed of when asked to do so. Consequently, the unconscious status of learning in AGL has remained highly controversial. Crucially, however, these latter studies have never measured participants' subjective states when performing the bigram identifications but have assumed that bigram identification necessarily reveals conscious knowledge. In this paper, we test this critical assumption by employing novel bigram-based artificial grammars and, simultaneously, by measuring participants' subjective access to their bigram knowledge. For generalization, we employed both typical letter stimuli and face stimuli. Participants (N = 188) memorized strings (of faces or letters) constructed from the grammars; subsequently, they classified novel strings (half grammatical, half nongrammatical), reporting subjective awareness trial-by-trial. We found that letter strings were classified above chance based both on unconscious and on conscious knowledge (all ps < .001, Bayes factors > 10⁷), while data were insensitive for the faces condition (Bayes factors between 0.33 and 3). Our findings demonstrate unconscious learning of bigrams for letter strings, challenging the influential idea that accurate bigram knowledge necessarily reveals conscious knowledge.

Unconscious Bigram Knowledge in the Artificial Grammar Learning Task

Elucidating whether learning can take place without awareness has been the target of intense efforts within cognitive science. Reaching a resolution on the issue would contribute to mapping the capacities of unconscious cognition, which have been the object of perpetual controversies (e.g., Newell & Shanks, 2023). Additionally, it would stimulate important advances in understanding the functions of consciousness by clarifying whether consciousness is required for learning, at least in humans (e.g., McGovern & Baars, 2007).

Based on current research on natural and artificial neural networks, learning without awareness appears plausible. For instance, connection strength in such networks can change to mimic the statistical regularities that govern encountered stimuli; but there is no inherent reason why the calibrations of connection strength should be consciously represented (Alamia et al., 2020; Cleeremans & Dienes, 2008; Conway, 2020; Han et al., 2022).

Furthermore, some current theories of consciousness, especially versions of the "higherorder" approach, explicitly stipulate that becoming aware of these processes would require an additional metacognitive network which traces the calibrations of the initial (first-order) network (e.g., Cleeremans et al., 2020; Dienes, 2012). These theoretical analyses are consistent with observations from multiple real-life contexts: humans routinely use regularities they cannot articulate, such as grammatical rules or those involved in complex movements (e.g., the patterns of muscle activations involved in walking; see Reber & Allen, 2022, for additional examples). Despite its plausibility, demonstrating that humans are capable of such unconscious (or implicit) learning has been extremely difficult and controversial. The empirical study of this phenomenon was initiated by Arthur Reber's experiments on artificial grammar learning (AGL), which has become one of the most employed tasks for studying implicit learning (e.g., Reber, 1967 Reber, , 2022)). In an AGL experiment, participants attempt to memorize or transcribe several meaningless letter strings. Importantly, the strings are derived from a complex regularity, called an artificial grammar (see Figure 1), but this is not disclosed to participants. In a subsequent test phase, participants attempt to discriminate novel strings that follow the same grammar (grammatical strings) from strings not following it (nongrammatical strings). Typically, participants make these grammaticality judgments with above-chance accuracy, indicating that they have acquired knowledge of the grammar and can generalize it to novel strings. However, ample evidence was interpreted to suggest that most of their grammar knowledge is not conscious. For instance, the rules they can verbally report underpredict their objective classification performance (Dienes et al., 1991) and they show above-chance discrimination performance even when subjectively claiming to respond at random or based on feelings of familiarity that they cannot further justify (e.g., Dienes et al., 1995; Dienes & Scott, 2005; Ivanchei & Moroshkina, 2018; Jurchiș & Dienes, 2023; Norman et al., 2016; Wierzchoń et al., 2012). Nevertheless, as discussed below, the literature is far from settled on this matter.

The controversy surrounding the unconscious status of knowledge acquired in the AGL task has been triggered by two influential studies suggesting participants can consciously identify the basis of their response. Following each grammaticality decision (grammatical/nongrammatical), Dulany, Carlson, and Dewey (1984; henceforth, DCD) asked participants to underline (or cross out) the fragment making each string grammatical (or nongrammatical). Participants' accuracy in conducting this fragment identification predicted the accuracy of their grammatical classifications. Thus, it appears that participants possess accurate knowledge of the chunks (e.g., bigrams, trigrams) that make the strings (non)grammatical. Importantly, DCD presumed this fragment knowledge to be conscious, since participants were conducting these identifications intentionally. In a subsequent investigation, Perruchet and Pacteau (1990; henceforth, P&P) first presented participants with letter strings in a learning phase, and, in the test phase, asked them to discriminate bigrams that were present in those strings from bigrams that were not. Participants successfully performed this recognition task, and P&P argued-based on a simulation-that their conscious knowledge of the bigrams could account for the typical accuracy observed in standard AGL tasks. These two studies have exerted an important influence on the subsequent literature, being typically considered the strongest evidence against the unconscious status of knowledge acquired in AGL (cf. Dienes & Seth, 2022; Pothos, 2007; Shanks, 2005; Shanks & St. John, 1994). But how could we reconcile the fact that participants can identify the fragments that render strings (non)grammatical with their claims of being subjectively unaware of most of their knowledge (i.e., stating they guess or rely on vague feelings of familiarity, e.g., Dienes & Scott, 2005; Jurchiș & Dienes, 2023)? One straightforward possibility is that fragment knowledge does not necessarily indicate conscious knowledge of the bigrams, contrary to the interpretation advanced by DCD and P&P-but as already suggested by other authors (e.g., Dienes, 2012; Dienes & Seth, 2022; Reber et al., 1985). Our proposition is that participants might well be capable of objectively discriminating strings or bigrams based on their bigram knowledge without being subjectively conscious of them. For instance, one of the DCD participants might think: "I have a feeling of familiarity that I previously saw the fragment XM and this is what makes the string grammatical so I will highlight it, but I don't know what I rely on; I don't have any recall of seeing it previously and I don't know any rule stating that XM is grammatical." Such a conscious judgment that the string is correct does not imply, in any sense, that participants were conscious of prior memories or of explicit rules they learned. Prior literature assumed that objective identification indicates conscious bigram knowledge, and even though this assumption is central to the conclusion reached by the skeptics of unconscious learning, it has not been tested yet.

The Present Study

In this study, we question the notion that knowledge of simple fragments (i.e., bigrams) is necessarily conscious. Specifically, we ask whether participants have the conscious phenomenological experience of relying on bigrams when making grammaticality judgments. In this respect, our approach differs fundamentally from the classical studies of DCD and P&P, which took participants' objective ability to discriminate grammatical from ungrammatical strings or bigrams as evidence that they were aware of those bigrams. We argue instead that objective discrimination may be supported by subjectively unconscious bigram knowledge. Following Dienes and Scott (2005), we consider that a participant may consciously judge that "XMVTR is grammatical" because of unconscious knowledge that "MV is a grammatical bigram," while experiencing the basis of that judgment only as an intuition or feeling of familiarity ("I feel it is grammatical and I can indicate it, but I don't have the faintest idea why"). Thus, we do not simply ask whether participants are aware of anything, but specifically whether they are aware of the bigrams that support their judgments.

To observe such judgments, subjective awareness measures are needed.

Previous AGL studies using subjective awareness measures have already shown that participants claim to be unaware of knowledge for most of the trials while they perform consistently above chance (e.g., Dienes et al., 1995; Dienes & Scott, 2005; Ivanchei & Moroshkina, 2018; Jurchiș & Dienes, 2023; Norman et al., 2016; Wierzchoń et al., 2012).

However, standard artificial grammars provide several correlated cues, besides individual bigrams, on which participants may base their grammaticality judgments. These include positional chunk strength, or how frequently chunks occur in particular positions, positionspecific letter frequencies, global repetition structure, string length, and complex combinations of these features. Consequently, when participants perform accurately without being able to report the relevant knowledge, it is often unclear what type of information supports their judgments. While previous studies established that subjectively unconscious learning occurs, they do not specifically illuminate whether bigram knowledge specifically can remain subjectively unconscious.

To address this limitation, we conducted an otherwise typical AGL experiment using two custom grammars in which only bigram information could support accurate grammaticality judgments. We controlled for alternative cues, including global repetition structure, positional chunk strength, position-specific letter frequencies, and string length.

Any resulting learning effect could therefore be attributed to bigram knowledge. We do not assume, however, that this knowledge takes a particular representational form. It may be represented as chunks or transitional probabilities (Perruchet, 2019). Our question concerns only whether knowledge of the bigrams is conscious, regardless of the form in which it is represented. Moreover, we tested whether participants could generalize their bigram knowledge to novel strings; accordingly, none of the test strings had been presented during acquisition.

To examine the generalizability of our findings and to increase the chances of detecting unconscious learning, in addition to the typical condition where participants saw strings of letters, we also employed a second condition where participants saw sequences of emotional facial expressions, based on the same grammars. This was based on preliminary findings in the AGL literature showing that social stimuli tend to produce slightly higher proportions of implicit knowledge (Costea, 2018; Jurchiș et al., 2023; Jurchiș & Dienes, 2023; see also Norman & Price, 2012). Thus, participants first attempted to memorize strings of letters or facial expressions constructed from our artificial grammar. In a subsequent test phase, they were presented with novel strings of letters or faces, depending on the condition, where only half were grammatical. For each test string, they provided a grammaticality decision and a subjective report of awareness. Our main hypothesis was that participants would show learning of the bigram-based artificial grammar, even when their subjective reports would indicate the absence of conscious grammar knowledge.

We employed a subjective awareness measure widely used in AGL and other implicit learning paradigms (e.g., Dienes & Scott, 2005; Ivanchei & Moroshkina, 2018; Jurchiș et al., 2023; Scott & Dienes, 2008 , 2010). Briefly, it asks participants to report whether their grammaticality decision was based on a conscious memory or rule fragment, on a feeling (e.g., of familiarity) they cannot further explain, or on a pure guess (a subjectively random response). Crucially, it allows us to assess awareness under the same level of sensitivity as DCD and P&P: the awareness measure is administered immediately after each grammaticality decision, and participants are explicitly encouraged to report as conscious even fragmentary knowledge they are unsure of (e.g., Newell & Shanks, 2014 , 2023; Shanks & St. John, 1994). This circumvents the situation where evidence for unconscious bigram knowledge revealed by our study could be explained by our measure's lower sensitivity in capturing conscious knowledge, compared to those used by DCD and P&P. However, our measure differs from those employed by DCD and P&P in an important manner: we do not assume participants' objective performance (e.g., in discriminating strings or recognizing chunks) necessarily indicates conscious knowledge. Rather, we test this assumption; and, as an alternative awareness measure, we ask participants to report their mental states, that is, we use the subjective measure described above. The use of subjective measures is consistent with higher-order and global workspace theories, which imply that conscious information is accessible and reportable, either because it is meta-represented (i.e., knowledge that one knows one possesses) or because it is globally available, which includes availability for verbal reports (see, e.g., Dienes, 2012, for further theoretical considerations, and Dienes & Scott, 2005, for evidence that in AGL the trials classified as conscious and unconscious with this scale differ qualitatively).

Objectives and Hypotheses

Our objective was to assess whether, when the only regularity leading to accurate responding concerns bigrams allowed by an artificial grammar, participants can still acquire implicit knowledge. Thus, our main hypothesis was that in both the letters and the faces conditions, participants would have above-chance accuracy when reporting to rely on unconscious knowledge. As in typical AGL studies, we also expected above-chance performance for conscious knowledge, and based on the studies employing social stimuli (faces and body movements; e.g., Costea, 2018; Norman & Price, 2012; Ziori & Dienes, 2015), we expected a higher proportion of unconscious trials in the "faces" condition.

Methods

Participants

For this study, we had a maximum available pool of 572 students enrolled in a cognitive psychology class, who were invited to attend in exchange for course credit. Given that the grammars employed were entirely new, it was difficult to estimate a plausible implicit learning effect; thus, we aimed to recruit as many participants as possible from this available pool. A total of 273 undergraduates accepted the invitation and completed the task.

We excluded 31% of participants due to failing several data quality criteria, as we detail in the Analysis subsection; thus, the final sample was composed of 188 participants (M = 27.8 years, Mdn = 22 years, SD = 9.09; 12.5% males). Given this substantial number of excluded participants, in the online Supplementary Materials (https://osf.io/hruqx/?view_only=c2a32baca55e49f28f15e26a0d06b30d) we also report results without exclusion and show that they support the same theoretical interpretation.

Experimental Design

In our design, a first between-subjects factor was the stimulus type-letters or faces.

A second factor was the specific grammar (A or B; see Figure 1) that participants were presented with, because we used a standard two-grammar crossover design that controls for possible confounds pertaining to the grammars (e.g., Dienes & Scott, 2005; Norman & Price, 2012). This design involves presenting, in the acquisition phase, one group of participants with strings from one grammar (either A or B) and the other group with strings from the other grammar. In the testing phase, both groups classified novel strings from both grammars as grammatical or nongrammatical (A and B). For participants exposed to grammar A in acquisition, in the test phase the grammar A strings are grammatical, and the grammar B strings are nongrammatical; and vice versa for the other participants. If any confounding factor caused participants to prefer strings from one grammar, it would equally affect both groups but in opposite directions (correct for one, incorrect for the other), and its influence over the two groups would cancel out. Thus, participants were randomly assigned to one of the four groups resulting from the combination of stimulus type (letters and faces; see Figure 2) and grammar (A or B). Procedure

After giving their informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of the two stimulus conditions (letters: N = 145, with 102 included in the final analysis; faces: N = 128, with 86 included). All tasks were administered online via the Gorilla.sc platform (Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020).

The Acquisition Phase

Apart from differences in stimulus type (letters vs. faces) and string exposure time during the acquisition phase, participants in both stimulus conditions followed identical procedures.

During the acquisition phase, participants were told they would complete a memory task and were instructed to memorize each displayed string as thoroughly as possible, without being informed about the underlying grammar. Each string was presented individually in a block of 36 unique strings; this block was repeated four times, with a one-minute break between repetitions. Because during piloting face stimuli tended to be more difficult to process and learn, we set exposure times to 12 seconds/string for faces (as in Jurchiș et al., 2023) and 5 seconds/string for letters (as in Scott et al., 2014). Within each block, six attention checks were interspersed randomly. During these checks, participants indicated whether the current string was identical to the previous one.

The Testing Phase

Following the acquisition phase, participants were informed that the strings they had previously memorized were generated by a complex grammar. They were then asked to classify new, unseen strings as either grammatical or ungrammatical. Importantly, after each classification, we probed their awareness of the knowledge supporting it by asking them to choose from guess, intuition, familiarity, rules, or memory (Dienes & Scott, 2005).

Participants were presented with precise definitions of these response options (see Supplementary Materials, Appendix A), which, in short, indicated that guessing refers to a random response, intuition and familiarity refer to feelings they cannot further justify, and rules and remembering refer to knowledge that is conscious and that they could verbalize if asked. The instructions also emphasized that participants should choose "rules" or "remembering" even if they were aware only of some rule fragments and even if they were unsure that their knowledge was correct.

Following the previous literature, guess, intuition, and familiarity were pooled together in the analyses, as they indicate implicit knowledge of the grammar, or, in other words, that participants lacked conscious access to the grammar knowledge supporting their decision. Namely, their experience was that of a random response or of a feeling that is not bound to an identifiable piece of knowledge, such as a rule or fragment. Conversely, rules and remembering responses were also pooled together to form the explicit knowledge basis, as they indicate conscious access to the basis of their decision (e.g., Dienes & Scott, 2005; Jurchiș & Dienes, 2023; Norman & Price, 2012).

Next, participants completed an unexpected comprehension test designed to assess their understanding of the response options for the awareness scale (see Supplementary Materials Appendix B), and we included in the analyses only participants who made a maximum of one confusion between implicit and explicit options but immediately corrected it after re-reading the instructions. If a participant did not provide a correct response on the second attempt or erred more than once throughout this comprehension testing, their data were excluded. The awareness instructions and the corresponding comprehension test were administered after the acquisition phase, but before the beginning of the testing phase.

In the test phase, participants classified 36 new strings (18 generated by Grammar A and 18 by Grammar B). Participants had a maximum of 12 seconds to decide. After each classification, they reported the level of awareness of the knowledge supporting their decision (Dienes & Scott, 2005), with the instructions on the meaning of each strategy continuously displayed and without a time limit. Finally, participants completed a brief post-experimental survey in which they reported any explicit knowledge of the grammars. We added this check because the present study was the first to test these grammars, and we needed to confirm that participants' attention had not been systematically drawn to irrelevant aspects of the task.

Responses from the survey served as a rough manipulation check and were not used to draw formal conclusions about participants' awareness.

Stimuli

Grammars and Strings

We designed two distinct grammars (Figure 1) such that their strings could be accurately classified using only bigram knowledge. To achieve this, we controlled all factors known to influence learning so that strings differed solely in their bigram components. First, we ensured that the grammars were equivalent in terms of components and complexity. Each grammar comprised six nodes, yielding twelve bigrams that formed five-letter strings without self-loops.

Second, the two grammars shared no bigrams. Otherwise, grammatical bigrams could appear in nongrammatical strings (and vice versa), potentially diminishing the size of any observed effect of bigram learning.

Third, we eliminated distractors known to undermine grammar learning. One such distractor is the presence of repeating letters (e.g., XXMVVTR), which can distort grammaticality judgments, presumably by increasing processing fluency (Jamieson et al., 2016). Therefore, we ensured the grammars could not generate adjacent repeating letters.

However, we allowed at most one (nonadjacent) repeating letter per string to produce enough unique strings (e.g., PZKPV), but we controlled for the number of strings with repetitions present in the two grammars.

Fourth, we aimed for each bigram to be learned equally, so that accuracy would not hinge on a small subset of them. To this end, we generated both the acquisition and testing string sets such that the frequency of each letter and bigram was identical across the learning material. During the acquisition phase, each of the twelve bigrams was presented 12 times across 36 strings, and each of the six letters appeared 30 times, while during the testing phase each bigram occurred 6 times and each letter 15 times. Moreover, in standard AGL paradigms, an "anchor effect" may lead participants to focus disproportionately on the first and last bigrams (e.g., XMVTRVT; Lotz & Kinder, 2006). To counteract this, we maintained a constant frequency of each letter at each string position while ensuring that bigrams occurred with roughly equal frequency at each position (with a maximum deviation of ±1 from the mean frequency of each bigram). Thus, in the acquisition phase, each letter appeared 6 times per position, and each bigram appeared on average 3 times (max ±1) per position; in the testing phase, each letter appeared 3 times per position, and each bigram appeared, on average, 1.5 times (max ±0.5).

Fifth, we counterbalanced grammaticality with global repetition structure (GRS)that is, the pattern by which the same letter tends to appear in two or more positions of a string (e.g., PKTPN). In a pilot study using the emotional faces condition, we observed that participants responded "YES" more often to strings containing repeated elements. Rather than eliminating GRS, we incorporated it to systematically examine its influence and thus we balanced GRS across grammaticality to separate their effects. Specifically, in the acquisition string set, grammar A had half of its strings with no repetition structure (no-GRS; i.e., all five letters were different), while one-quarter featured a GRS in which the first and fourth letters matched (e.g., XMVXT). For grammar B, half of the strings were no-GRS, while one-quarter had a GRS where the second and fifth letters matched (e.g., XMVTM). Furthermore, both groups saw strings in which the first and last letters matched, making up the remaining onequarter of the strings (e.g., XMVTX). In the testing phase, we presented strings generated by both grammars that covered all GRS conditions: half were no-GRS, and the remaining half were divided equally among each GRS pattern (one-sixth from grammar A's GRS, one-sixth from grammar B's GRS, and one-sixth from the shared GRS). Thus, if participants learned the grammars but not the GRS, the probability of responding "YES" should reflect grammatical status rather than GRS. Conversely, if participants learned the GRS but not the grammars, they should more frequently endorse the familiar GRS without regard for grammatical status.

Because manual selection of strings that met all constraints was infeasible, we formulated the task as a constraint-satisfaction problem in Python 3.11 and solved it with a mixed-integer model using Google OR-Tools 9.12's Solving Constraint Integer Programs backend (Bolusani et al., 2024; Perron & Furnon, 2025); the code and final string sets appear in the online repository.

Surface Stimuli

In the letters condition, we used N, K, V, P, T, and Z. In the faces condition, each letter was replaced with an emotion displayed by the same actor: anger, fear, disgust, neutrality, sadness, and happiness (cf. Costea, 2018; Jurchiș et al., 2023; Norman & Price, 2012). We selected the images from the FACES database (Ebner et al., 2010) and chose one young actor (to match our participants' demographics), of male gender (since for female faces, the level of facial attractiveness interacts with the learning effect; Ziori & Dienes, 2015), and with a high (98.66%) mean accuracy in emotional identification among young participants in the database's validation study (Ebner et al., 2018; see the online repository for the R code used in the selection procedure).

Data Analysis Plan

We report below only the results for the included participants, but we also provide results of the entire sample in the Supplementary Materials (link: https://osf.io/hruqx/?view_only=c2a32baca55e49f28f15e26a0d06b30d). None of the conclusions drawn in the Results section changed when analyzing the full sample.

Essentially, we tested whether learning had occurred in the two conditions (i.e., whether participants had above-chance classification accuracy in the faces and letters conditions); further, we aimed to determine whether participants had above-chance classification accuracy when they relied on implicit knowledge and, separately, on explicit knowledge. Finally, we tested whether there were differences between the two stimulus conditions in the learning effects and in the proportion of implicit versus explicit trials. To this end, we conducted both frequentist and Bayesian analyses.

Frequentist Analyses

We tested our hypotheses by running generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) in R. We tested the learning effects in each stimulus condition by fitting one GLMM with response accuracy as the dependent variable (incorrect vs. correct) and the following fixed factors: (1) subjective awareness reported on each trial, (2) Global Repetition Structure (GRS), to account for the systematic variance it might induce, and (3) the interaction between subjective awareness and GRS to investigate if the observed effects depend on the GRS condition. To assess the overall learning effect (performance vs. chance), we examined whether the model intercept (logit scale) differed from 0 (logit[50% chance] = 0) and whether its associated Wald z test was significant. To evaluate implicit and explicit learning, we obtained estimated marginal means (EMMs) for each awareness condition (collapsing over the GRS conditions) and tested whether each exceeded chance (0 on the logit scale, i.e., > .50 probability).

To test for differences between the two stimulus conditions in the proportion of implicit versus explicit trials, we fitted one GLMM with awareness (implicit/explicit) as the dependent variable, and as fixed effects (1) stimulus condition and (2) GRS structure.

Additionally, we also assessed the differences in overall learning between the two stimulus conditions. We did this by fitting one GLMM with response correctness as the dependent variable and the following fixed factors: (1) subjective awareness, (2) GRS structure, and (3) stimulus condition. We extracted EMMs from the model to test the difference between stimulus conditions.

In all models, variables were effect-coded to ensure that the model intercept remained interpretable as the grand mean of the design. For full details on model specification, selection, and variable coding, please see the online Supplementary Materials, Appendix C.

Bayesian Analyses

We conducted Bayesian analyses using informed priors to quantify the evidence supporting the alternative hypothesis relative to the null. We computed Bayes factors (BF) for GLMM parameters using the bfrr R library (DeBruine & Dienes, 2025). For prior specification, we followed Dienes (2014) and Silvey et al. (2024), setting the H1 prior as a half-normal distribution centered at zero with a standard deviation reflecting the expected effect, and the H0 as a spike distribution centered at zero. The sole exception was for testing the presence of unconscious effects, namely for comparing the unconscious accuracies with the chance level. In this case, we used an interval null that corrects for the possible overestimation of unconscious effects due to their contamination with conscious knowledge: the interval null was from 0 to 0.29% for the faces and from 0 to 2.4% in the letters condition. 1 We interpreted BF values in accordance with Lee and Wagenmakers (2014), 2 as shown in Table 1.

In addition, we calculated the robustness regions (noted as RR) for each B, thereby defining the range of models of H1 that would yield the same substantive conclusion (i.e., support for H1 or H0, or insensitive data). For instance, an RRB>100 = [0, 4.595] would mean that a Bayes factor above 100 would also be obtained with priors with SDs ranging across all possible values, from 0 to 4.595 (transforming the log-odds into percentages, from the 50% chance level to 100%). 1 The rationale behind this more stringent test is as follows: random measurement noise could cause some conscious trials to be misclassified as unconscious (and vice versa). Thus, if conscious knowledge is associated with higher accuracy, the observed unconscious accuracy might be artificially inflated by the contamination of trials measured as unconscious with trials that are, in reality, conscious. Given the assumption that random noise produces symmetrical contamination in both directions, one can estimate the measurement error that would produce the highest level of contamination for unconscious trials (see Dienes, 2025, for the rationale). Using the formulae derived and detailed in Dienes (2025; see also Jurchiș & Dienes, 2023; Skora et al., 2023), we found that conscious contamination could increase the observed accuracy of unconscious trials to a maximum of 2.4% in the letters and to 0.29% in the faces condition.

Discussion

The present study examined whether bigram knowledge acquired in AGL tasks is necessarily conscious-a long-standing assumption in the implicit learning literature (e.g., Dulany et al., 1984; Perruchet & Pacteau, 1990; Shanks, 2005; Shanks & St. John, 1994). We employed two novel grammars ensuring any accurate classification could be attributed solely to bigram knowledge, since we eliminated or controlled alternative sources of learning (e.g., repetition structures, analogical similarities, non-adjacent dependencies) typically present in standard AGL paradigms (Lotz & Kinder, 2006). We aimed to investigate whether the conclusions could be generalized across different stimulus types; thus, we tested participants in both a standard letter condition and an emotional-faces condition, the latter having been shown to produce a higher proportion of unconscious responses (Jurchiș et al., 2023).

In the letters condition, we found strong evidence for learning bigram knowledge, both consciously and unconsciously. This challenges the traditional interpretation (Shanks & St. John, 1994) of the DCD and P&P findings, namely that acquiring bigram knowledge necessarily results in conscious representations. Importantly, our awareness measure circumvents most criticism typically directed at subjective measures: to ensure sensitivity, participants reported awareness trial-by-trial, immediately after each grammaticality judgment, and were encouraged to report as conscious even fragmentary knowledge held with low confidence (e.g., Newell & Shanks, 2014 , 2023; Shanks & St. John, 1994; Sweldens et al., 2017). Furthermore, we employed a recent modeling technique showing that unconscious accuracy could not be explained by contamination with conscious knowledge due to measurement noise (Dienes, 2025; Jurchiș & Dienes, 2023; Shanks, 2017). We thus provide reliable evidence that participants acquired substantial amounts of unconscious bigram knowledge. Of course, our study cannot escape other criticism inherent to any study employing subjective measures of awareness: namely, despite our insistence that participants report as conscious even fragmentary knowledge held with low confidence, we cannot be certain that their reports always reflected the true conscious or unconscious status of their knowledge, without bias (e.g., systematic underconfidence in some participants; Newell & Shanks, 2014 , 2023). Still, despite these limitations of subjective measures, past AGL research has shown that trials classified as conscious or unconscious with the scale that we employed capture different psychological phenomena, as they respond differently to experimental manipulations (Dienes, 2012). For example, loading working memory with a dual task in the acquisition phase leaves the accuracy of implicit trials intact, but decreases that of explicit trials (Dienes & Scott, 2005); similarly, temporal delays between acquisition and testing decrease conscious-based accuracy, but not implicit-based accuracy (Jurchiș et al., 2026).

At the same time, some features of our method may have increased the amount of explicit knowledge detected relative to other procedures. First, the acquisition phase used memorization instructions, which may have encouraged more conscious knowledge of bigrams than more incidental procedures such as passive viewing or string transcription (e.g., Scott & Dienes, 2008). Anticipating that the present grammars would be more difficult to learn (see below), we employed instructions likely to promote deep processing of strings and bigrams, but future studies could use more incidental acquisition phases. Second, previous AGL research has shown that assessing awareness trial-by-trial, with the scale we employed, tends to make participants more analytical and to lead them to develop more conscious knowledge of the grammars (Ivanchei & Moroshkina, 2018). This effect may have been amplified by the fact that we carefully instructed participants on the meaning of the scale and checked their understanding of it, which may have drawn even more attention to the presence of regularities during the test phase. Therefore, measuring awareness in this manner may ultimately lead to underestimating participants' capacity to operate with implicit knowledge.

Even so, despite possible detrimental effects, trial-by-trial measurement of awareness remains necessary for ensuring sensitivity. Third, we also increased sensitivity by instructing participants to classify even low-confidence, fragmentary knowledge as conscious. This may have encouraged them to search for conscious explanations even on trials where their Furthermore, we took several steps to reduce the likelihood that participants would confuse the decision strategies. Before the test phase began, participants completed a comprehension test in which they were given explicit examples of thoughts that might arise during grammaticality decisions and were asked to label them using the appropriate decision strategy (see Appendix B for the full instructions). These examples emphasized that "guess"

should be selected only in the absence of any thought about the correct decision, whereas "intuition" and "familiarity" should be selected only when participants felt they knew the correct answer but had no explicit recall from the acquisition phase and could not articulate the rules underlying their decisions, even with low confidence. During this comprehension test, participants received corrective feedback after each answer and could not proceed to the test phase unless they selected the correct response. We excluded participants who confused implicit and explicit decision strategies more than once. In addition, during the test phase itself, the definitions of the decision strategies remained visible on each trial, so participants could consult them before reporting the basis of their grammaticality decision. To the best of our knowledge, the present study deployed the most stringent test of participants' understanding of the awareness report options in the implicit learning field (see also Jurchiș & Dienes, 2023, for a preliminary version).

Given the stringent safeguards against misunderstanding and the extreme Bayesian evidence in favor of implicit learning, we consider it unlikely that the observed pattern resulted from participants misunderstanding the awareness report options. However, our subjective awareness measurements have several constraints typical of the AGL literature. For instance, the task typically does not allow us to distinguish the precise source of conscious knowledge. The conscious knowledge expressed on a given trial may have originated during acquisition, for example, if a participant noticed that a particular bigram was repeated across several training strings. Moreover, it may also arise during the testing phase, as participants begin to notice that some bigrams occur more often in strings that feel familiar (Scott & Dienes, 2010). In that case, awareness would emerge only after nonconscious learning had already been established, because it would be based on the familiarity signal produced by earlier learning. This would, in turn, risk overstating the role of conscious knowledge in the task. As a preliminary descriptive exploration of this issue, we asked participants in the post-experimental questionnaire (a) to rate the extent to which they were aware during acquisition that the strings followed rules (0 = not at all;7 = completely), and (b) to indicate when they became aware of the rules (0 = acquisition stage;7 = testing stage). The mean response to the first question was 2.51, below the midpoint of the scale (3.5), suggesting relatively low awareness during acquisition. For the second question, the mean was 3.18, close to the midpoint, suggesting that conscious knowledge emerged to a similar extent during both phases. These results are only descriptive, but they are consistent with the possibility that a substantial part of conscious knowledge arises during testing, after learning has already taken place. The implications of this possibility should be examined more directly in future work using methods better suited to separating awareness at acquisition from awareness that develops later during test (e.g., Scott & Dienes, 2008 , 2010).

Our main finding, that participants learn unconscious knowledge of bigrams, is consistent with those from an unpublished study by Zoltán Dienes, who, in a development of the DCD paradigm, asked participants to identify n-grams making strings grammatical (or nongrammatical), and then to report the subjective bases of their bigram identifications.

While, as in DCD, the identified n-grams predicted classification accuracy, most identifications were based on subjectively unconscious knowledge (Dienes, 2017, 1:10:27).

Thus, together with Dienes' results, our findings challenge the assumption that bigram knowledge or bigram identification performance necessarily reflects conscious knowledge.

The present findings are also congruent with results showing that humans are sensitive to adjacent dependencies even in conditions that are suboptimal for conscious processing (or where conscious processing might be absent), such as in infants (e.g., Basirat et al., 2014; Saffran et al., 1996), during sleep (Batterink & Zhang, 2022), or during coma (e.g., Herrera-Diaz et al., 2023). Thus, our results portray a broader scope of implicit knowledge, showing that even simple structures, when embedded in streams of sufficiently complex stimulation (e.g., among many other simple dependencies), can be the object of unconscious processing. Real-life examples could be the orthographic regularities-for instance, that if an English word starts with q, the next letter will almost always be u-or grammar rules, for instance, that some adverbs can determine comparatives, while others cannot (e.g., much better versus many better). Furthermore, the possibility of learning bigrams unconsciously is compatible with predictions of current theories of consciousness; foremost, global workspace theories suggest that unconscious knowledge should especially emerge regarding relatively simple stimuli that the cognitive system encounters over a short time span, such as the adjacent letters composing a bigram.

In contrast, the integration of stimuli over more extended time frames, in more complex regularities (e.g., non-adjacent repetitions), might rely more on conscious processing (e.g., Baars et al., 2013).

Unexpectedly, unlike previous investigations (Jurchiș et al., 2023; Ziori & Dienes, 2015), we did not find an unconscious learning effect in the faces condition. Since they were poorer in sources of information, our grammars were likely more difficult to learn than typical grammars. 3 In addition, letters were presented in a format consistent with their natural occurrence, that is, as multiple letters simultaneously composing a single string, as in real words. By contrast, facial expressions were presented in the same string-like format, although multiple expressions from a single individual are not naturally perceived all at once.

Combined with the increased complexity of the face stimuli, these factors likely resulted in the nondetectable learning effects. Furthermore, in the supplementary analyses we present evidence that participants' responses were influenced by repetitions only for faces, which are, arguably, more salient features. That is, they tended to endorse as grammatical stimuli with repetition structures -such as abcad -encountered in the acquisition. Together, these findings suggest that, in AGL tasks, participants might extract different types of information depending on stimulus nature or complexity-bigrams for letters and repetition structures for faces (see also Conway & Christiansen, 2006; Eitam et al., 2014; Frost et al., 2015; Jiménez et al., 2020).

In conclusion, we found empirical evidence that bigram knowledge in AGL tasks can be acquired without conscious awareness, challenging a widespread assumption in the implicit learning literature. In addition, we also found that bigram acquisition operates differently depending on the surface stimuli and, more generally, that different surface stimuli might favor the acquisition of different types of knowledge.
