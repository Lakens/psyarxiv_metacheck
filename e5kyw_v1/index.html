How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Whose deposit is each link? Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Empathy plays a crucial role in social connectedness, clinical care, and moral decisionmaking (Cecchetto et al., 2018; Decety, 2020; Eisenberg & Miller, 1987), but empathy is not always allocated to everyone to the same extent, particularly when the target is an ostensible outgroup member (Rösler & Amodio, 2022; Vanman, 2016). Racial outgroup membership, in particular, has been associated with attenuated recognition of emotional and physical pain, further perpetuating ingroup favoritism and empathy biases (Han, 2018; Mende-Siedlecki et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2009).

Situational cognitive demands may further intensify empathy biases. In general, biases are exacerbated under cognitive load (Gilbert & Hixon, 1991; Kleider-Offutt et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2020; Wigboldus et al., 2004). Cognitive load refers to the amount of working memory used during a task (Sweller et al., 1998). Moreover, empathy itself is a motivated and effortful process that relies on cognitive resources (Cameron et al., 2019; Keysers & Gazzola, 2014; S. K. Lin et al., 2010; Zaki, 2014). However, the extent to which these empathy biases persist or change under cognitive load remains unclear. Here, we tested whether cognitive load and target racial outgroup status interact to amplify empathy biases. Furthermore, we investigated how cognitive load affects the two distinct, yet interacting, routes of empathy -experience sharing and mentalizing (Zaki & Ochsner, 2012).

Two Routes to Empathy

To understand how cognitive and social demands can impair empathic processing, it is critical to differentiate between the different components of empathy. We define the emotional component of empathy as experience sharing, an affective form of empathy involving vicarious emotional resonance with others (Cameron et al., 2019). By comparison, mentalizing is the cognitive component of empathy and involves inferring the thoughts, intentions, and beliefs underlying others' mental states and actions (Amodio & Frith, 2006; Kliemann & Adolphs, 2018). According to the dual-route model of empathy (Decety & Lamm, 2006; Yu & Chou, 2018), system one operates rapidly and automatically, outside of conscious awareness, and facilitates experience sharing through shared representations (Budell et al., 2010; Gallese & Goldman, 1998; Rizzolatti et al., 1996; Sowden et al., 2022; Stueber, 2010). System two, in contrast, engages more cognitively demanding empathic processes, including mentalizing, and relies on controlled, effortful, and resource-intensive processing supported by prefrontal regions (De Vignemont, 2008; Goldman, 2011; S. K. Lin et al., 2010; Yu & Chou, 2018). These differences in the degree of controlled processing underlying each route suggest that experience sharing and mentalizing may differ in their susceptibility to cognitive demands.

However, although experience sharing is relatively more automatic than mentalizing, this does not imply that affective empathic responses are cognitively cost-free or immune to cognitive demands. The cognitive cost hypothesis of empathy proposes that empathy can be experienced as effortful and that empathic engagement is shaped, in part, by the perceived cognitive costs associated with engaging with another person's experiences (Cameron et al., 2019; Scheffer et al., 2022). Thus, even relatively automatic experience-sharing processes may vary as a function of available cognitive resources and the cognitive effort required to engage empathically.

Recent evidence is consistent with this idea. Yang et al. (2026) found that prior cognitive effort attenuated neural empathic responses to others' pain. Because the basic depictions of pain are thought to elicit relatively rapid and automatic somatosensory and affective resonance (Fan & Han, 2008; Lam et al., 2011) rather than requiring explicit inference about another person's mental state, these findings suggest that even relatively automatic forms of experience sharing remain sensitive to cognitive effort. remain sensitive to cognitive effort. Yang et al. interpret this effect as reflecting changes in motivation and attention rather than depletion of a finite cognitive resource, consistent with the process model of ego depletion (Inzlicht et al., 2014).

Studies imposing concurrent cognitive or attentional demands similarly indicate that experience sharing can be influenced by cognitive demands, although the direction of these effects has been inconsistent. Participants performing a concurrent cognitive task while viewing images of others in pain show decreased activation in neural regions involved in empathic pain processing (Gu & Han, 2007), and high cognitive load has been associated with lower caregiving intentions, partly through reduced empathic concern (Hiraoka & Nomura, 2016). In contrast, other studies have reported stronger shared pain responses and greater sympathy under cognitive load (Chambers & Davis, 2012; Tremblay et al., 2021). This variability in findings may reflect the fact that cognitive demands can influence affective empathy through multiple pathways:

Cognitive effort may reduce empathic responding by increasing the perceived cost of engagement and shifting motivation and attention away from others' experiences. In comparison, relatively automatic experience sharing responses might be less susceptible to the effects of cognitive load or might even become stronger when load limits the controlled, top-down processes that would otherwise down-regulate excessive sharing. Despite the mixed evidence, we predicted that cognitive load would attenuate affective empathic responding, consistent with the cognitive cost account of empathy and evidence that cognitive effort can reduce attentional and motivational engagement with empathic information.

Nonetheless, we expect cognitive load to have stronger and more consistent impairing effects on mentalizing because mentalizing depends more directly on executive and working memory resources. Unlike experience sharing, mentalizing requires the active representation and inference of another person's thoughts, intentions, and beliefs, all processes that compete with a concurrent task for limited cognitive resources. This reliance on controlled cognitive resources suggests that resource competition may be a primary mechanism through which cognitive load disrupts mentalizing. When executive and working-memory resources are occupied by a concurrent task, fewer resources remain available for representing and reasoning about another person's mental state. Consistent with this account, increasing executive demands through cognitive load impairs perspective taking (Epley et al., 2004) and Theory of Mind performance (S. K. Lin et al., 2010; McKinnon & Moscovitch, 2007; Schneider et al., 2012), while executive functioning supports the ability to represent and reason about others' mental states (Apperly et al., 2005 (Apperly et al., , 2009)). Neuroimaging evidence similarly demonstrated reduced activation in regions associated with mentalizing during empathic processing under cognitive load (Morelli & Lieberman, 2013).

Together, these findings suggest that cognitive load is unlikely to influence all forms of empathy through a single mechanism. Affective empathic responses may be altered through motivational and attentional regulation, whereas mentalizing may be particularly susceptible to direct competition for executive and working-memory resources. We therefore predicted that high cognitive load would reduce both affective empathy and mentalizing, but that the effect would be stronger for mentalizing because mental-state inference depends more directly on the cognitive resources being taxed. Cognitive demands, however, are only one source of limitation on empathic processing. Social factors, including the group membership of the target of empathy, may also shape the degree to which experience sharing and mentalizing occur. The influence of these social factors may itself depend on the cognitive resources available to regulate the immediate group-based responses.

Racial group membership has been shown to shape both experience sharing and mentalizing (Cikara & Fiske, 2011; Contreras-Huerta et al., 2013; Gutsell & Inzlicht, 2012; Han, 2018; Stürmer et al., 2006; Vanman, 2016). During experience sharing, racial prejudice reduces empathy for racial outgroup members' physical and emotional pain (Vanman, 2016). Neural evidence shows increased empathic neural resonance with ingroup compared to outgroup members (Avenanti et al., 2010; Gutsell & Inzlicht, 2012; Krautheim et al., 2019; Sessa et al., 2014). During mentalizing, people more readily infer the mental states of ingroup members compared to outgroup members (Hackel et al., 2014). Furthermore, mentalizing about ingroup members engages the ventral medial prefrontal cortex, an area associated with self-referential thinking, while mentalizing about outgroup members engages the dorsal medial prefrontal cortex, suggesting that mentalizing is influenced by top-down social processes (Mitchell et al., 2006). Examining potential race-based empathy biases is crucial, given their link to improper diagnoses and racial health disparities (Drwecki et al., 2011; J. D. Lin et al., 2024; Pelaccia et al., 2020; Torres et al., 2022).

However, racial group membership does not necessarily produce a uniform reduction in empathy toward outgroup members. Although, people rapidly categorize others into ingroup and outgroup members (Tajfel & Turner, 2004), initial automatically activated group stereotypes can be modified through controlled processing (Devine, 1989). For example, Pryor et al.'s (2004) dual-process model proposes that controlled processing can override automatic negative responses toward outgroup members, facilitating prosocial responding rather than avoidance.

Such regulation may be particularly likely among individuals who are internally motivated to respond without prejudice (Plant & Devine, 1998). When sufficient cognitive resources are available, motivated individuals may regulate initial group-based responses, thereby reducing the extent to which group membership translates into observable differences in empathic responding. This framework suggests that the expression of racial empathy biases may depend not only on racial group membership itself, but also on the availability of sufficient cognitive resources to regulate initial group-based responses. Under low cognitive load, controlled processing may dampen initial group-based differences in empathic responding. Under high cognitive load, however, fewer resources are available for this regulation, allowing relatively automatic group-based responses to have a greater influence. Therefore, we predicted that the effect of racial group membership will depend on cognitive load, such that ingroup-outgroup differences in empathy will be amplified under load.

The Current Study

The goal of the current study was to investigate whether and how cognitive load influences experience sharing and mentalizing and whether these effects depend on target racial group membership. To induce cognitive load, participants completed a paradigm designed to tax the updating component of the central executive network of working memory. Working memory supports the short-term storage and manipulation of information but has limited capacity (Baddeley, 2012; Cowan, 2008; Kessler & Oberauer, 2014). When people perform two tasks at once, this limited capacity is taxed because they must process and maintain multiple streams of information simultaneously (Cowan & Morey, 2007). Performance typically declines under such dual-task conditions, even when the tasks use different sensory modalities (Cowan, 2008). Our goal was not to create a dual-task paradigm requiring continuous responding during narrative viewing and mentalizing, but rather to examine whether maintaining information in working memory while processing an emotional narrative influences empathic responding.

We predicted that requiring participants to engage in an updating task while empathizing would reduce both experience sharing and mentalizing performance. Previous studies have typically imposed cognitive load before the empathy task -arguably tapping into ego depletion rather than cognitive load (Bajouk & Hansenne, 2019; Meiring et al., 2014) -or relied on static stimuli to measure mentalizing ability (Handley et al., 2019 (Handley et al., , 2023;; Kinderman et al., 1998). In contrast, the present study used a more naturalistic paradigm to examine how concurrent cognitive load influences both experience sharing and mentalizing. Participants completed a spatial n-back task that extended across the entire trial, requiring them to maintain spatial location information. While maintaining this information in working-memory, participants watched racial ingroup and outgroup members deliver emotionally negative first-person narratives, rated their state affective empathy, and answered Theory of Mind (ToM) questions requiring them to infer the narrators' thoughts and intentions. This approach provides a novel way to examine how cognitive load influences empathic processing while offering greater ecological validity by approximating the competing demands of real-life contexts.

While the effects of cognitive load on empathy and racial bias have been studied independently, their interaction remains unexplored. Related research points towards potentially serious consequences of this interaction. For example, cognitive load has been shown to interact with patient race and physician gender in shaping pain treatment decisions (Burgess et al., 2014) However, it remains unclear whether cognitive load increases empathy biases in experience sharing and mentalizing toward racial ingroup versus outgroup members. To address this gap in the literature, the present study investigates whether cognitive load and racial group membership jointly amplify empathy biases.

We predicted that high cognitive load would undermine empathic responding and amplify differences in empathic responding toward racial outgroup members. Specifically, we tested three preregistered hypotheses.

H1a: We predicted that cognitive load would significantly reduce empathic responding.

Specifically, compared with the no-load condition, participants in the high-load condition were expected to show: a) lower affective empathic responding, as indicated by less negative valence ratings, b) lower compassion ratings, and c) worse mentalizing performance, as indicated by slower reaction times and lower accuracy.

H1b: We predicted that cognitive load would have a larger negative effect on mentalizing than on affective empathic responding.

H2:

We predicted that cognitive load would interact with racial group membership such that the ingroup-outgroup differences in empathic responding would be amplified under high cognitive load. Specifically, we expected a) lower affective empathic responding, b) low compassion, and c) worse mentalizing performance from no load to high load for both ingroup and outgroup targets, with a larger decline for outgroup targets, producing a larger ingroupoutgroup difference under high cognitive load.

Methods and Materials

Participants

Participants were recruited through the CloudResearch Connect (Hartman et al., 2023) platform and were paid $7.00 for their participation, which took approximately 50 minutes. All participants were between 18 and 35 years of age. Participants were required to be able to read English, have access to a computer with working audio and stable internet access, and be able to operate a keyboard. Recruitment was restricted using CloudResearch Connect's demographic prescreening to participants who identified as either Black/African American or White. Because Connect does not screen for mixed-race identities, participants were asked to report their race again at the end of the study. Participants who identified as both Black/African American and White were excluded because racial ingroup and outgroup membership could not be unambiguously defined in relation to the Black and White video targets. Participants whose endof-study racial identification did not match their prescreened racial identification were also excluded from analyses. Estimates for the required sample size (N = 80, 50% Black/African American and 50% White/Caucasian) were based off of a mixed-effects power analysis (supplemental materials 1g). Ethical approval was granted by the research university's Institutional Review Board (#23212R).

Design

The study uses a within-participant 2 X 2 factorial design examining the effects of cognitive load and target racial group membership (ingroup vs. outgroup) on empathic responding. The first factor, cognitive load, was manipulated using a spatial n-back task with two levels: no load (0-back) and high load (2-back). The second factor, target racial group membership, was manipulated by varying the race of actors in the EmpaToM video stimuli.

Racial group membership (ingroup vs. outgroup) was determined by the correspondence between participants' self-identified race and the race of the target. Targets who shared the participant's racial identification were classified as ingroup members, whereas targets of the other racial group were classified as outgroup members. Dependent variables included affective empathic responding (experience sharing), compassion, and mentalizing performance. Affective empathic responding was calculated as the participants' average rating of their own affective state following the narratives within each experimental condition. Compassion was calculated as the average rating of compassion felt toward the target within each condition. Valence ratings were reverse scored prior to analysis so that higher scores indicate greater empathic responding, consistent with the direction of the compassion measure. Thus, higher valence and compassion scores reflected greater affective empathy. Mentalizing performance was measured with accuracy and reaction times for theory of mind questions. The primary analyses test (a) the main effect of cognitive load on empathic responding and (b) the interaction between cognitive load and racial group membership, indicating whether cognitive load amplifies group-based empathy biases.

EmpaToM Task and Race Manipulation

We used the EmpaToM task (Kanske et al., 2015), which allows for simultaneous investigation of affective and cognitive empathy using the same naturalistic video stimuli. The videos depict autobiographic narratives that are either emotionally negative (e.g., experiences of loss or threat) or neutral (control condition). After each video, participants rate the valence of the target's emotional state (affective empathy) and the degree of compassion they felt towards the target. Mentalizing was assessed via subsequent questions about the target's thoughts, goals, or intentions (ToM condition).

Affective empathic responding was operationalized using participant's ratings of their own affective state following each narrative, consistent with the original EmpaToM paradigm (Kanske et al., 2015). Within the EmpaToM, this rating captures affective empathic responding because participants report their own affective state after observing another person's emotional experience. Greater negative affect following emotionally negative narratives reflects greater sharing of the target's affective experience. The affective experience sharing measure is different than the compassion measure, because the valence rating measures the extent to which participants' own affective state (self-oriented) corresponds to the negative experience of the target, while the compassion rating assesses an other-oriented feeling of concern for the target.

In the original EmpaToM, affective empathic responding was assessed by comparing participants' affective responses following emotional and neutral videos (Kanske et al., 2015).

We are primarily concerned with responses to the emotionally negative videos; however, six neutral narrative videos were included in the no-load condition to provide a comparison for evaluating whether the emotional narratives elicited the expected shift toward negative affective state ratings. The neutral narratives featured the same actors who appeared in the emotionally negative narratives, allowing affective responses to emotional and neutral content to be compared while holding target identity constant. Specifically, we expected participants to report more negative affect following emotional than neutral narratives.

Building on Lantos' et al (2023), who adapted Kanske's et al. (2015) original German scripts and videos into English (Australian dialect), we created the Multi-Racial American version of the EmpaToM, with video stimuli depicting Black and White identifying actors and speaking in American dialect (Franks & Gutsell, 2026, in prep). Sixteen stories (supplemental materials 1a) were slightly modified to consider Australian and American dialectical differences (e.g., changing "pub" to "bar"). The Videos depict 12 men and 12 women, ages 15-70, telling approximately 15-second emotionally negative stories describing moments of distress or suffering (age estimates from FaceReader 10). The stories told in the videos are meant to resemble excerpts from seemingly two-person conversations. The videos were naturalistic and presented as autobiographical narratives, depicting 12 Black and 12 White actors. Videos were analyzed in FaceReader 10 using the default facial analysis settings (see supplemental materials 1b). No significant differences between Black and White actors' videos were found on measures of valence and emotional expression: neutral, happy, sad, angry, surprised, scared, disgusted.

In a separate online validation study conducted prior to the present study (supplemental materials 1c), participants from diverse racial backgrounds rated the videos on emotional intensity, arousal, attractiveness, expressions of realism, perceived race, gender, and age.

Character-level mean ratings of arousal, emotional intensity, attractiveness, trustworthiness, and expression realism were initially compared between Black (n = 12) and White (n = 12) characters. Ratings did not significantly differ by character race for arousal, attractiveness, trustworthiness, or realism of expression. Character names were also selected to convey racial identity based on naming data reported by Gaddis (2017) and matched on mother's educational attainment. The EmpaToM has been validated as a paradigm for assessing distinct components of social cognition, with emotional videos eliciting greater negative affect and compassion than neutral videos and mentalizing questions producing distinct behavioral and neural responses from factual reasoning questions (Breil et al., 2021; Kanske et al., 2015; Lantos et al., 2023).

Cognitive Load Manipulation

We used a spatial n-back task to induce cognitive load, because complex working memory tasks, such as the n-back, require both storage and control processes to maintain information. The n-back task has strong face validity and is a widely used and well-established working memory and cognitive control task (Jaeggi et al., 2010; Kane et al., 2007; Peterson et al., 2022). In the spatial n-back task, participants view sequences of a highlighted square location and judge whether the current stimulus location matches the one presented n-steps earlier (Cowan & Morey, 2007). This design manipulates working memory demands by comparing a 0back control condition, which does not require active maintenance or updating of information, with a 2-back condition requiring sustained maintenance and updating of spatial information (Figure 1).

Procedure

The experiment, built using PsychoPy v2025.2.4 and hosted on Pavlovia, was presented on each individual's laptop or desktop computer (Peirce et al., 2019). After consenting in Qualtrics (Provo, UT), participants received detailed instructions and had multiple opportunities to practice the spatial n-back task and the pacing of the empathy task. A complete experimental trial consisted of one EmpaToM video and its associated valence, compassion and ToM question response, with n-back square presentations interspersed throughout the trial. Individual square presentations within the n-back task are referred to below as n-back presentations.

Participants first received extensive practice of just the 0-back and 2-back tasks.

Participants saw a screen displaying 12 randomly scattered white squares and, on each presentation, one of the squares turns black for 500ms. In the "left or right task" (0-back) trials participants indicated if the square is on the left side or on the right side of the screen. In "remember 2-back task", participants needed to use the arrow keys to indicate if the square is in the same location as two trials back. Participants completed 20 practice trials of just the squares presentation (0-back and 2-back) and were required to reach an accuracy of 60% on the practice trials before proceeding. Failure on all three attempts resulted in termination of the program.

Next participants were introduced to the combined 2-back and video trials, which required tracking the spatial locations and maintaining their locations while also completing the empathy task.

Two extended practice trials included sections of the empathy task, and reminders about which arrow keys correspond to what choices. The final two practice trials included example videos at the actual experimental pacing. Before starting the main task, participants were instructed that both the memorization and the perspective-taking tasks are equally important and should receive equal effort.

At the beginning of each experimental trial, participants were informed whether they would perform the 0-back ("left/right") or 2-back ("remember 2-back") task. A green fixation cross presented for 500ms marked the beginning of a new trial and, in the 2-back condition, instructed participants to reset the 2-back sequence; the next square presentation was therefore treated as the first item in a new sequence. Each trial (see figure 2 for a depiction of a typical trial) then proceeded with a black jittered fixation cross for 1-3 seconds, followed by two to six seconds of the n-back task. Next, participants saw the character's name and heard it in the corresponding actor's voice for two seconds, after which they watched a video of the actor telling an emotionally negative or neutral story for approximately 15 seconds. Following the story, a fixation cross proceeded another six seconds of the n-back presentations. Using the arrow keys, participants then provided a static valence rating of "How do you feel?" for four seconds.

This was described to participants as a rating scale "on which you should indicate how you are feeling after this particular video. Please rate whether you are feeling rather positive (e.g., happy, cheerful, pleased) or rather negative (e.g. sad, anxious, angry)". The scale ranged from 0-100 with "negative" being 0, 100 being "positive", and 50 being "neutral". After valence, participants provided a compassion rating of, "How much compassion do you feel?" This was described as a rating scale, "on which you should indicate how much compassion you are feeling for the person in the video. Compassion is defined here as feelings like affection, care, warmth, goodwill, and loving kindness. In understanding what this means, some people find it helpful to think about a child who is in pain and for whom they feel compassionate, or to think about a close relative who isn't feeling well." The compassion rating ranged from 0-100, with "none" being 0, 100 being "very much" and 50 being "neutral". Participants had four seconds to respond.

After seeing another fixation cross for 500ms, participants were presented with 6 seconds of n-back presentations and then given a Theory of Mind question. They had 14 seconds to read and chose what they thought the person in the video was thinking from a list of three options, only one of which was correct answer. Chance-level accuracy for an individual ToM question was 33.3%. This was followed by a fixation cross and the final presentation of 2 seconds of the n-back presentations.

Half of the trials were no load, and the other half, the high load condition (12 high trials and 12 low). Within each trial, 25% of the N-back presentations were match trials, with match positions predefined to ensure balance and to avoid back-to back matches. Each sequence of square locations ranged between 2 and 6 seconds. The order of videos, questions, and answer choices were randomized. At the end of the experiment, participants were asked to identify the perceived race of each character. Participants also reported their relative prioritization of the nback and EmpaToM tasks and described any strategies they used to complete the n-back task.

Participants were also asked whether they used any external memory aids during the experiment, including recording square locations or information presented in the narratives.

Feasibility of the Study

To test the feasibility of online data collection in the context of the current study, we first ran a pilot study on CloudResearch's Connect platform (N =20; supplemental materials 1f). Ten Black and 10 White participants were able to complete the study in an average time of ~60 minutes. All participants met the required 60% accuracy threshold during the practice trials, which was necessary for their data to be included. One participant did not complete the task and was not included in analyses. Participants demonstrated an ability to learn the task, and 60 minutes did not appear to be too long, because all but one participant continued answering the questions. In the cognitive load task, participants on average showed a lower mean accuracy on the high load n-back task (M = 0.668, SD = 0.229) than in the no load condition (M = 0.939, SD = 0.116). A paired-samples t-test indicated that this difference was significant, t(18) = -6.3843, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.360, -0.181], Cohen's d = -1.46, 95% CI [-2.11, -0.80], indicating that the high load cognitive task is more difficult than the no load task.

Sample Size Determination

Sample size was determined using a simulation-based power analysis for the linear mixed-effects model specified for our primary hypothesis (supplemental materials 1g). To obtain empirically informed parameter estimates for the simulations, we first fit the planned mixedeffects model to pilot data collected from 19 participants. The pilot model included fixed effects of cognitive load, group membership, and their interaction, as well as crossed random effects for participants and stimuli. Estimates from the pilot data were used to inform the intercept, participant-and stimulus-level random-effect variances, and residual variance used to generate the artificial datasets. Because the pilot sample was relatively small and therefore provided an imprecise estimate of the Load × Group Membership interaction, we did not use the observed pilot interaction as the effect size for the power analysis. Instead, we specified a smallest effect size of interest (SESOI) for the Load × Group Membership interaction of 5.70 points, indicative of a small to medium effect (d = β / SD residual = 5.70 /14.43 ≈ 0.395) on the 0-100 affective empathic responding scale. Under the effect coding used in the analyses, Load was coded -0.5 = no load and +0.5 = high load, and Group Membership was coded -0.5 = outgroup and +0.5 = ingroup. Thus, an interaction coefficient represents a 5.70-point change in the ingroup-outgroup difference across levels of cognitive load. Fixed-effect values for the remaining model parameters were specified as: intercept (69.25), Load (-2.50), and Group Membership values (2.85). The simulation indicated that N = 75 participants provided 92% power to detect the specified interaction. We therefore established a target final analytic sample of 80 participants (40 Black and 40 White). To account for potential exclusions, we planned to recruit 100 participants.

Data Exclusion and Analysis Plan

Participant data were excluded if the participant failed to respond to more than 50% of trials in any task domain (compassion ratings, valence ratings, or mentalizing trials). Data were retained when participants demonstrated an understanding of the task structure, as indicated by appropriate response attempts to "match" trials in the high load n-back condition. Previous research using spatial n-back tasks indicates that accuracy is typically high in the 0-back condition and decreases as working-memory load increases, although absolute performance varies across task implementations and samples. For example, McMillan et al. (2007) reported mean accuracies of approximately 86% (SD = 12.7) in a spatial 0-back condition and 78% (SD = 13.0) in a spatial 2-back condition in a younger adult sample. Consistent with this pattern, our pilot data (n = 19; supplemental materials 1f) indicated that participants achieved an average accuracy of approximately 94% in the no-load (0-back) condition and 67% in the high-load (2back) condition. Given the greater difficulty of the 2-back condition observed both in previous research and in our pilot data, we established condition-specific performance thresholds.

Participants were required to achieve an average accuracy of at least 85% across the no-load blocks and at least 55% across the high-load blocks for inclusion in the primary analyses. These thresholds were selected to identify participants demonstrating adequate engagement with each task while accounting for the substantially greater difficulty of the 2-back condition observed in our pilot data. Participants who reported using an external memory aid during the task, or whose free-response description of their strategy indicated use of an external memory aid, were excluded.

Model assumptions were evaluated prior to hypotheses testing. Potential outliers at the participant level were examined using box plots of the dependent variables, with values exceeding 1.5 interquartile ranges flagged for inspection. We excluded participant data only if flagged as outliers across all three dependent variables. Flagged observations were not excluded solely on the basis of this threshold. Participant data were excluded only when extreme values were accompanied by evidence of data-quality concerns, such as response error or noncompliance with study procedures. For linear mixed effects models, we evaluated model assumptions visually using quantile-quantile (QQ) plots with approximate linearity taken as evidence that assumptions were met. Homoscedasticity was assessed by inspecting residuals versus fitted values (and versus key predictors). For generalized linear mixed-effects models, we evaluated distributional assumptions based on the specified link function and outcome distribution (e.g., binomial for accuracy). Random-effects structures were examined for convergence and singular fits. Performance on the n-back task, regardless of target race, served as the manipulation check of the cognitive load manipulation, with lower accuracy and longer response times expected in the high-load condition relative to the no-load condition.
