How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Data from repeated-measures experiments (e.g., response time data, electrophysiological data) can show systematic changes in outcomes over time, so-called (temporal) trends. When analyzing experimental effects between conditions, different trends per condition can cause the experimental effect to vary with time, for example, to decline over the course of the experiment. Why and How to Model Trends and Time-Varying Experimental Effects Using Mixed-Effects Models: A Simulation Study and a Tutorial

In experimental psychology and cognitive neuroscience, it is common to measure outcomes multiple times for each participant (e.g., recording response times, eye tracking data, fMRI or EEG across several trials). The experimental effects of interest are typically computed as the average differences in these outcomes across experimental conditions. This procedure is grounded in the notion that averaging across trials can yield more reliable estimates of experimental effects by mitigating unsystematic noise.

However, this approach is based on the assumption that all experimental trials are interchangeable and that the variance in the outcome across trials over time is introduced by nuisance variables alone. We propose that a different perspective be adopted by considering repeated-measures data as longitudinal data and explicitly examining systematic changes in the outcome over time, referred to as (temporal) trends. Previous research has already investigated trends in various outcome measures across different experimental paradigms, demonstrating the potential for longitudinal analyses to uncover meaningful patterns such as habituation or attention processes (e.g., Dignath et al., 2019; Fink et al., 2024; Kalus et al., 2015; Langner et al., 2010; Lorist et al., 2000; McLaughlin et al., 2023; Muller et al., 2013; Pattyn et al., 2008; Van Rij et al., 2019; Volkmer et al., 2022; Von Gunten et al., 2018; Wetzel et al., 2021).

In longitudinal data with trends, the outcome value systematically depends on the specific time point of its observation, making the temporal order of the observations non-ignorable. In designs with multiple experimental conditions, there may even be different trends for each condition. Consequently, the difference in outcomes across these conditions (i.e., the experimental effect) should also be considered conditional on a specific time point, thus leading to a time-varying experimental effect, which occurs when different trends per condition lead to the experimental effects changing over time. This point is crucial because time-varying experimental effects can no longer be adequately described by the so-called marginal experimental effect, which is the average experimental effect across time points. 1 Consequently, these marginal experimental effects can no longer be meaningfully compared between studies of different durations (for a discussion see e.g., McLaughlin et al., 2023; Volkmer et al., 2022; Widmann et al., 2025). Volkmer et al. (2022) provided a specific example of this phenomenon in their analysis of data from an auditory-visual distraction experiment (i.e., an oddball paradigm). In this experiment, each trial required a forced-choice response to a visual stimulus, which was preceded by either a frequently occurring standard sound or a randomly interspersed distractor sound. Participants exhibited slower responses in trials following distractor sounds versus standard sounds, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the distraction effect. Crucially, the distraction effect decreased over time due to different trends in response times (RTs) for each condition (Volkmer et al., 2022; Wetzel et al., 2021). Figure 1 illustrates the consequences of these trends across participants.

Specifically, here, RTs for distractor trials reduced during the initial trials (dark blue trials in Figure 1a), whereas RTs for standard trials showed only a slight decrease (light blue trials in Figure 1a). As a result, the so-called conditional experimental effect -the experimental effect for a specific trial -varied between the beginning and the end of the experiment. We use this simple RT data example in the remainder of the article to make the content accessible to a wide audience. The same basic principles can be applied to any other psychophysiological measure.

Leaving these different trends unaccounted for in the analysis (i.e., calculating the marginal effect) imposes a strict assumption that the experimental effect remains constant over time (as depicted in Figure 1b). When this assumption does not hold, erroneous conclusions may be drawn when comparing marginal effect sizes between experiments, because the marginal experimental effect then depends on the duration of the experiment. Practically speaking, in our example, the marginal effect would appear larger in an experiment ending after only 50 trials than after 240 trials (see the effect sizes shown on the y-axis in Figures 1c and 1b). From a statistical perspective, this situation resembles a special case of omitted-variable biases, with time being a hidden, omitted moderator. Each dot is the average of trials 1 to 10, 11 to 20, and so on. This approach was used to make the figure less crowded while still accurately representing the overall trend.

This issue arises in all data with different trends per condition. The conditional experimental effect is only constant and equal to the marginal effect if the trend is identical in the two conditions. Although this assumption might be justifiable in some cases, there are several compelling reasons to explicitly investigate trends in experimental data: (a) Even when the marginal experimental effect is not biased, misspecifications may manifest in other model parameters and distort substantive conclusions (e.g., Bauer & Cai, 2009; Keintzel et al., 2026; Widmann et al., 2025). For example, similar trends in different measures induce correlations between those measures. (b) Accounting for time as a covariate increases statistical power by reducing the residual variance (e.g., Thul et al., 2021). (c) Time-varying experimental effects can represent meaningful and interesting phenomena, resulting in new theories about the underlying cognitive or psychophysiological processes (Baayen et al., 2022; Kalus et al., 2015; Keintzel et al., 2026; Lorist et al., 2000). Changes in experimental effects are not caused by the mere passage of time but by time-varying covariates (Falkenström et al., 2017) and theorizing about such covariates (e.g., arousal, training effects) opens up new directions for substantive research questions. Similarities and differences in temporal trends across measures (e.g., behavioral, pupillometric, and EEG data) may indicate shared and distinct underlying processes, respectively.

Several different mixed-effects models can be used to model trends per condition, allowing researchers to make their choice on the basis of their prior knowledge and desired flexibility: 1.

Exact parametric mixed-effects models can be used when the exact shape of the trend per condition can be mathematically specified a priori.

2. Linear and polynomial growth curve models can be used to approximate various simpler smooth trends.

3. Generalized additive mixed models can be used to model nonlinear trends, even without any prior knowledge about the shape.

4. Random-effects-expectation-maximization (RE-EM) trees

can be used to reveal arbitrarily complex nonlinear interactions between multiple time-varying covariates and time.

Next, we report two simulation studies in which we investigated the performance of these models in several realistic scenarios. This approach allowed us to explore each model's data requirements and performance. To our knowledge, no systematic comparison of these models has been conducted before. Finally, we illustrate the implementation and interpretation of all these models in a tutorial using a real data example with annotated R-code. We conclude with a recommended workflow for analyzing time-varying experimental effects. 2 Recap: The Basics of Mixed-Effects Models

Mixed-effects models (MEMs, also known as hierarchical models, multilevel models, and random coefficients models) are widely used to analyze data from repeated-measures designs. We assume that the reader is familiar with the basics of these models, and we only briefly reiterate the basic notation to enable a better understanding of the trend models (for a detailed introduction tailored to experimental psychology, see e.g., Bagiella et al., 2000; Maxwell et al., 2018). MEMs account for the systematic dependencies among observations within participants and can also be used to investigate interindividual differences in experimental effects, extending repeated-measures analyses of variance, which is a special case of MEMs (e.g., Brauer & Curtin, 2018). Crucially, the MEM framework can easily be expanded to incorporate complex interactions of time-varying predictors, making it suitable for investigating time-varying effects.

MEMs are used to distinguish sources of variance on two levels: participant-level variance (between-person) and trial-level variance (within-person; Maxwell et al., 2018).

3.1 Exact Parametric Mixed-Effects Models

Sometimes, theory predicts a very specific trend (e.g., the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve, Ebbinghaus, 1913), which can be directly specified as a (non)linear MEM by using either a transformed time variable (e.g., the log of the trial number, number of experimental blocks) or by specifying a particular (nonlinear, e.g., sine, exponential) function (Lindstrom & Bates, 1990; Pinheiro & Bates, 2000). However, these models impose a strict assumption on the shape of the trend, making it difficult to detect unexpected or deviating shapes. At worst, true trends may be obscured by the specified shape, resulting in a false sense of confidence in the model's parameters and potentially false conclusions. The same problem arises with nonlinear transformations of the time metric. Additionally, nonlinear MEMs featuring functions such as exponentials or sines are notoriously difficult to estimate (e.g., McCormick, 2023). Given these disadvantages, we recommend that explicit parametric models should be used only when a specific functional form of the trends is expected. Otherwise, we think the following approximate models are more preferable.

3.2 Linear and Polynomial Growth Curve Models

MEMs that model linear (first degree polynomial) or higher-order polynomial growth over time (trends) are referred to as linear or polynomial growth curve models (GCMs) (for an introduction see also Kristjansson et al., 2007; Mirman, 2017).

Linear GCMs are a popular choice. Researchers often have to assume a linear model for model identification, especially with limited numbers of measurement occasions (Mirman, 2017). However, linear GCMs are a special case of an exact parametric model. They impose a specific linear shape, thus sharing the limitation of being unable to capture deviating trends (Beller & Baier, 2013). This limitation is illustrated in Figure 2a, where the linear trend fails to adequately represent the observed trend in the data. By contrast, more flexible polynomial GCMs can approximate various nonlinear shapes (e.g., U-shapes, logarithmic, or exponential functions, as shown in Figure 2b).

Theoretically, any smooth function (i.e., no discontinuities, differentiable) can be reformulated as a higher order polynomial according to Taylor's theorem, allowing for a very flexible representations of trends.

3.2.1 Advantages and Disadvantages

Linear GCMs are appealing because they are parsimonious and their parameters have a straightforward interpretation that follows the principles of regression analysis. For quadratic, cubic, or even higher order GCMs, the interpretation becomes less intuitive because the number of parameters increases rapidly with the order of the polynomial.

Additionally, the parameters do not intuitively translate into the shape of the curve.

For instance, a significant quadratic term does not necessarily indicate a U-shape over the observed time range (Simonsohn, 2018). To gain a better understanding of the polynomial, inference about meaningful characteristic points (e.g., maxima or slopes at specific time points) can be derived. Additionally, all fixed coefficients in GCMs can also be added as random coefficients. GCMs can easily be extended by incorporating random coefficients for the linear or polynomial interaction, thus allowing trends to vary by condition and individual (random trends).

The challenge with polynomial GCMs lies in determining the appropriate degree.

Higher order polynomials tend to fit the data too closely (overfitting), resulting in overly complex ("wiggly") trends (e.g., Runge's phenomenon, an "artificial wiggliness" at the margins of the data or time range, Runge, 1901). Conversely, lower order polynomials can be too simplistic (underfitting) or even introduce artifacts caused by the inherent limitations of the mathematical function rather than the actual process in the data. For example, when attempting to approximate exponential decay with a negative cubic function, the cubic function is determined to eventually fall again, regardless of the underlying process in the data (see the falling trend in final distractor trials in Figure 2b).

3.3 Generalized Additive Mixed Models

Mixed-effects splines, more frequently known as Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs; Baayen & Linke, 2020; Hastie et al., 2009; Wood, 2017), are a semi-parametric approach for modeling smooth relationships of arbitrary shapes (that might occur in psychophysiological data) and can be applied to repeated-measures data (see also Van Rij et al., 2019, for an extensive tutorial). GAMMs allow for very flexible approximations by using splines to model trends per condition. The general idea of splines is to combine simple functions into a flexible curve that can approximate complex trends without specifying their exact overall shape in advance. Because this shape emerges from the combined contributions of these functions, individual coefficients generally do not have a straightforward substantive interpretation in terms of the overall trend. Consequently, the fitted trends are typically inspected graphically to understand their shapes. For example, the lines in Figure 2c represent the actual GAMM results and there are no "simple" model terms describing these shapes. This way, the fit to the data is maximized while simultaneously constraining the curvature. The basic principle of the penalization can be understood with a minimal squared residuals example: model fit = sum of squared residuals + λ • curvature penalty. One very commonly applied penalty accumulates the squared second derivative across the observed time range. Recall that the second derivative describes how rapidly a curve's slope changes. Therefore, a straight line receives no curvature penalty because its slope does not change, whereas pronounced bends increase the penalty. The smoothing parameter λ determines how strongly the penalty affects the estimation. Larger values force less curved fits because an additional bend must improve the fit enough to justify its additional penalty. The basis functions and knot positions remain unchanged; the model just penalizes the spline coefficients. Whereas λ is typically optimized automatically during model fitting, researchers specify the basis dimension and should check whether increasing it allows a substantially better fit to the data (Wood, 2017, see also the tutorial section). Their flexibility (over-and underfitting) is governed both by the hyperparameters (basis dimension and by the smoothing penalty), so these choices and the adequacy of the resulting fit need to be considered. However, splines alone can not yet account for the multilevel structure of repeated-measures data. 3.3.1 Advantages and Disadvantages

GAMMs are sometimes favored over higher order polynomials because they tend to produce smoother fits and fewer artifacts at the margins of the data range (Perperoglou et al., 2019). They can be used to approximate any continuous, unknown trend separately per experimental condition (Sóskuthy, 2017). Random trends per participant can also be included (i.e., participant-specific splines for each condition), but these require additional consideration, such as substantially higher computational demands, the choice among different random-effects structures, and the handling of residual autocorrelation (Sóskuthy, 2017). The challenge in fitting splines lies partly in avoiding over-or underfitting, that is, choosing settings that allow enough flexibility without fitting noise. If the basis dimension is too small or the smoothing penalty too strong, the curve may miss real changes in the data. Conversely, a very large basis dimension combined with too little smoothing can fit random fluctuations and produce an overly wiggly curve. Thus, although smoothing parameters are often estimated automatically, the basis dimension and the adequacy of the resulting fit still require consideration; model diagnostics and sensitivity to reasonable alternative settings can help assess these choices (Wood, 2017). As with higher-order polynomials, spline flexibility also increases model complexity and can require more data and computation. The resulting model can therefore be harder to summarize and compare, even though plots make the fitted trends accessible. As explained above, the estimated spline parameters can not be interpreted directly. Instead, researchers typically rely on visual inspection to gain an understanding of the trend per condition. This issue becomes even more problematic when individual trends per condition are allowed, resulting in one graph per condition and participant. However, the parametric fixed and random effects have a clear interpretation (see tutorial section).

3.4 RE-EM Trees

Random effects expectation maximization (RE-EM) trees are a semi-parametric algorithm used to detect complex nonlinear interactions in repeated-measures data (Hajjem et al., 2011; Sela & Simonoff, 2012). There are many different variants and extensions of this basic idea (Fu & Simonoff, 2015; Gottard et al., 2022; Hajjem et al., 2014 Hajjem et al., , 2017;; Jing & Simonoff, 2022; Miller et al., 2017; Nestler & Humberg, 2022; Ngufor et al., 2019; Pellagatti et al., 2021). For the sake of comprehensibility, we limit our introduction to the RE-EM tree as implemented in Sela and Simonoff (2021). So far, RE-EM trees have been used in clinical psychology (e.g., (Smith et al., 2023)), but never to our knowledge in experimental psychology. In the following, we briefly explain the general idea behind RE-EM trees (see Strobl et al., 2009, for a more in-depth technical introduction to tree-algorithms).

RE-EM trees may be conceived of as MEMs in which the fixed-effects part is replaced by a regression tree, which partitions (splits) the data into segments called nodes on the basis of the predictor variables (Breiman et al., 1984). The estimated value is usually the mean outcome across all observations in this node and identical for all observations in each node (the median or other sample statistics are also possible).

In contrast to splines, split points may occur on any of the predictor variables (e.g., experimental conditions, time, other potential covariates). The splitting is done recursively and is entirely data-driven. An approximation for our data example is depicted in Figure 2d, illustrating the final nodes on the basis of experimental condition and time, resulting in a step-function approximation of the trend.

Alternatively, regression tree results can be depicted as a tree plot (Figure 5).

One prominent approach is to choose the splitting variable and split point such that the sum of squared errors is minimized within each node (i.e., the squared difference between estimated and observed outcome values).

This approach is known as the classification and regression tree (CART; Breiman et al., 1984) algorithm. When applying regression trees, it is crucial to control the tree complexity to avoid overfitting (Breiman et al., 1984). Otherwise, CART trees would grow until each observation ends up in its own node, which is undesirable because the results would not generalize beyond the current data set. Stopping rules and pruning are two methods used to achieve this objective (Breiman et al., 1984). They operate at different stages of the tree-building process.

Stopping rules are criteria set during the tree-growing process to decide when to stop splitting (hyperparameters). Common stopping criteria include requiring a minimum number of observations in each node or specifying a complexity parameter that determines the minimum amount that the total explained variance must increase in order for a split to be made (Sela & Simonoff, 2012). Pruning, on the other hand, is applied after the tree has been fully grown and involves removing some of the splits to simplify the tree and improve its generalizability. Typically, cross-validation is used to decide how much a tree must be pruned (Sela & Simonoff, 2012). Cross-validation is a technique for estimating how well models fit on new data (estimating generalizability).

The observations in the dataset are randomly split into subsets where a model (here a tree with a certain number of splits corresponding to a certain complexity parameter) is estimated on one subset and used to predict outcomes from a different subset. This process is typically repeated 10 times (widely used default in machine learning, balancing estimation accuracy and computational cost; e.g., Hastie et al., 2009), and the resulting prediction errors are combined into a cross-validation error.

By choosing the complexity parameter value that minimizes the cross-validation error, a better balance between model fit and model complexity can be achieved (Breiman et al., 1984). Pruning based on cross-validation is less dependent on arbitrary decisions by researchers compared with implementing a stopping criterion. We explain in more detail how the complexity of the trees can be controlled in practice in the tutorial section.

The tree algorithm described so far can be applied to independent observations only. RE-EM trees therefore combine regression trees with random effects to allow the trees to be fit to repeated-measures data (Sela & Simonoff, 2012). The tree and the random effects (here, random intercept and random slope for condition, for random trends see next section) are combined iteratively, resulting in the RE-EM tree model:

3.4.1 Advantages and Disadvantages

The primary advantage of the RE-EM tree lies in its ability to include any number of fixed-effect predictors and (nonlinear) interactions while still providing a systematic way to understand its splitting rules. This approach could be particularly beneficial for designs with multiple dichotomous predictor variables, such as experimental conditions, where the tree can be used to determine which (combinations of) conditions have different trends (variable selection). Similar to GAMMs, RE-EM trees can be used without any prior knowledge about the shape of the trend, as they can approximate any smooth function using a step-function. The step-function approximation can be used to infer smooth shapes (see also simulation and tutorial section). Moreover, they can also approximate non-smooth functions, such as when a sudden change in the outcome is expected, but the time point is unknown. Still, it has been shown that regression trees are worse at approximating (quasi-)linear functions than nonlinear functions (Gottard et al., 2022).

However, the flexibility of RE-EM trees relies on selection of stopping criteria and is limited to the tree part (fixed effects) of RE-EM trees. Random effects, such as individual differences in trends, can (so far) only be specified using parametric functions (Sela & Simonoff, 2012). Moreover, CART algorithms have a tendency to select variables that offer many splitting points, resulting in a bias toward including continuous or polytomous predictors over dichotomous ones. There are alternative algorithms that adopt different splitting criteria to avoid this bias (Fu & Simonoff, 2015; Hothorn et al., 2006). Furthermore, unlike the previously explained approaches, RE-EM trees do not provide any formal inferential statistics, such as significance tests or confidence intervals. This omission is especially detrimental because trees are often unstable, and results depend heavily on the specific sample. Split points and selected variables can differ tremendously across samples, but this uncertainty is not apparent in the model output. However, there are approaches for mitigating this problem such as conditional inference trees (Hothorn et al., 2006), stable trees (Michel et al., 2016), or random forests (see Capitaine et al., 2021; Hajjem et al., 2014, for an implementation for longitudinal data).

All the methods described above are theoretically capable of modeling time-varying effects. We expect simplistic linear GCMs to underfit non-linear trend shapes, whereas higher-order GCMs are at risk of overfitting simpler trends. GAMMs and RE-EM trees are suited to recover various shapes of trends (non-linear, RE-EM tree also stepwise), but their performance is known to depend on the settings of the hyperparameters. Given that data from psychological experiments are notoriously noisy at the trial level, it is important to assess whether these methods can recover (nonlinear) trends in experimental data under realistic scenarios. Additionally, we are not aware of any applications of tree-based methods to experimental data in psychology, and it remains to be seen if these methods can be useful in this context. To obtain initial evidence on these questions, we conducted two monte carlo simulations to derive practical guidelines for researchers who want to apply these methods. In a first simulation, we investigated the models' abilities to recover trends and detect experimental effects under various realistic data situations and shapes of fixed trends, that is, under the restriction that all participants share the same trend per condition.

In a second simulation, we investigated scenarios with random trends, that is, with realistic inter-individual variability in the temporal trends per condition.

Values below 100% indicate a worse fit than the benchmark model. GOF values above 100% suggest that the approximate model fit the data from the specific sample better than the benchmark model, which may indicate overfitting.foot_6

Generalizability -Goodness of Fit Out of Sample.

Model performance out of sample is a key metric for model evaluation, as researchers want to generalize their results to a population. We simulated one large test data set with N = 10000 for each simulation scenario to predict the RT for this test data set with each models fixed effects as a marker for generalizability of the fixed effects to new data from new participants. Similar to the GOF in sample we calculated a GOF out of sample , with test dataset RT and estimated ˆ︃ RT based on the models' fixed effects.

Recovery of Random Effects.

We investigated potential bias in the estimation of the random-effects parameters, specifically, the standard deviations of random intercepts and slopes, as well as their correlation.

Detection of Time-Varying Experimental Effects.

We also examined how often models detected a difference in trends between sound types. For GCMs and GAMMs, we used the number of significant model comparisons between models with the same trend for both sound types versus models that allowed for different trends per sound type (i.e. power or false positive rate -FPR -of the time-varying experimental effect). For RE-EM trees, there is no statistical significance test for the time-varying effect. Instead, we counted the number of trees containing a split for both sound type and trial number as a measure of detection rate. We also investigated the number of splits and splitting variables conducted to further assess the fitting behavior of RE-EM trees.

4.2 Results

Linear and polynomial GCMs, GAMMs, and RE-EM trees consistently achieved convergence in nearly 100% of the runs. Runs with non-converged benchmark (i.e., data-generating exact-parametric) models (0.38%), linear GCMs (0.005%) and polynomial GCMs (0.002%) were excluded. In addition, we observed occasional anomalous solutions for the benchmark model in rare cases. These were characterized by implausibly extreme values of the trend parameters and therefore could not be considered valid benchmarks. All models showed sufficient detection of the time-varying effect when a trend was present in the data generating model. Only two exceptions to this pattern occurred, both in "limited data" scenarios (i.e., conditions with high residual variance, small sample size N = 50, and low sampling frequency T = 90): First, for exponential trends, the time-varying effect was detected in only 86.41% of the samples by linear GCMs, 85.08% for GAMMs, 89.83% for polynomial GCMs, and 74.36% for RE-EM trees. Second, RE-EM trees had greater difficulty in detecting linear trends (90.00%). 4.3 Discussion

Overall, polynomial GCMs, GAMMs, and RE-EM trees were all effective in detecting and estimating various (fixed) time-varying effects in sample under various realistic conditions for noisy measures such as RT, while also accurately estimating random intercepts and slopes for the experimental condition. Notably, all models demonstrated sensitivity to the time-varying effect of almost 100%, under most realistic scenarios-featuring 50 participants, 90 trials (whereof only 18 interspersed distractor trials showed a trend, so the decisive number of trials per experimental condition was 18), random slopes for the experimental condition and skewed residuals with realistic RT variance. The only notable drop in detection rate for the time-varying effect occurred in the limited data scenarios (N = 50, T = 90, high residual variance SD = 108), particularly for the exponential trend. This provides an orientation for minimal data requirements for research involving very noisy data with few participants and numbers of observations, as is often the case in research on RT in young children for example. To further examine minimal data requirements, we reran the simulation with N = 20, T = 90 and high residual variance. We replicated the same pattern in model performance, but with lower detection rates (down to a minimum of 39% in the exponential scenario but still > 97% for the stepwise scenario). Additionally, we found a larger number of non-converging models for the benchmark (nlme) models (up to 25% in the exponential trend scenario) and GAMMs (up to 15 -18% for all trend scenarios).

Taken together, we encourage researchers to gain practical experience on detection and convergence in their data. For studies with (very) small sample sizes, we recommend pooling data from similar experiments together to achieve a larger sample size for analysis. Differences between models mainly emerged regarding the out-of-sample generalizability, that is necessary in order to draw valid conclusions about the underlying population.

Unsurprisingly, the linear GCM performed well only for linear or absent trends.

A strict linear model is inherently unable to represent any strong nonlinearity, especially non-monotonous shapes (see Figure 7, upper row) and always underfits in these scenarios. In contrast, the 3 rd -degree-polynomial GCM performed much better, providing reasonable approximations and good generalizability for all smooth trends.

Notably, in scenarios with a (quasi-)linear trend, small sample size, and small number of trials, the polynomial GCM tended to overfit a little, resulting in a "wiggly" appearance.

This result occured because the polynomial model, while subsuming linear trends as a special case, still retains higher order terms that in practice do not equal exactly zero in smaller samples. This overfitting tendency decreased with larger samples, leaving only minor artifacts at the edges of the time range due to the mathematical limitations of the 3 rd -degree-polynomial (e.g., two turning points). For the stepwise trend, approximation by 3 rd -degree-polynomials proved too coarse. However, this issue could be addressed by employing a higher order polynomial (e.g., 7 th degree) which achieves approximations comparable to GAMMs, but also suffers from Runge's Phenomenon (Runge, 1901). Overall, our results emphasize that lower order polynomial GCMs are effective for modeling various trends if a polynomial of sufficient degree is used. The primary challenge with GCMs lies in determining a reasonable degree to avoid under-or overfitting and still maintain interpretability (see Tutorial section).

GAMMs adequately approximated all smooth trends and also managed to approximate the stepwise trend. However, some systematic "wiggle" artifacts emerged preceding and following the sudden change. These artifacts can be explained when considering splines as filters (Kauermann et al., 2011): The stepwise trend resembles a step function, and it has been established that filters produce characteristic artifacts when encountering discontinuities (e.g., Widmann et al., 2015). Thus, the GAMM pattern observed for the stepwise trend resembles typical step responses of filters.

Regarding the FPR, we observed a surprisingly low FPR for GAMMs compared with the other models (around 1.1 to 2.8% vs the nominal 5%). The model comparison we used for inference of the time-varying effect is recommended for being the least liberal option (Sóskuthy, 2017), but this very low false-positive rate should be investigated in future research. Overall, in line with previous research, GAMMs proved to be a flexible and well-performing option for modeling trends (Sóskuthy, 2017; Van Rij et al., 2019).

RE-EM trees also achieved good approximations with high generalizability, even for smooth trends, when sample sizes were sufficiently large in terms of participants and trials, performing surprisingly well on average despite their reputation for limited effectiveness in modeling continuous functions (Gottard et al., 2022). Nonetheless, in scenarios with small numbers of participants and trials, RE-EM trees performed worse in terms of generalization of smooth trends and detecting differences in (quasi-) linear trends scenarios compared to the other methods. For stepwise trends, RE-EM trees outperformed GAMMs and GCMs as expected. Most notably, the estimated stepwise shapes from RE-EM trees were fundamentally different from the smooth shapes estimated by the other models across all scenarios. In this simulation, we know the underlying true shape, whereas in real world applications, we do not. It is a substantive question to determine which shape best resembles the underlying processes. Abrupt changes may occur, for instance, when participants change their response strategy (Ollman, 1977), learn a new concept or rule (e.g., in the wisconsin card sorting test, Dehaene & Changeux, 1991) or in the context of bistable perception effects (Blake & Logothetis, 2002), where qualitative changes in stimuli perception may happen at certain points in the experiment. Nonetheless, our results demonstrate that it is possible to infer a smooth shape from a sufficiently detailed stepwise shape (e.g., a sine from a tree with seven final nodes, as shown in Figure 7), and vice versa (e.g., the GAMMs' smooth approximation of the stepwise trend in Figure 7). Overall, RE-EM trees proved to be quite flexible across different trend shapes but provided no advantage over (correctly specified) exact parametric models, polynomial GCMs or GAMMs for smooth shapes. However, they require larger numbers of participants and trials than the other models to avoid overly simplistic fits (e.g., only one or two splits) and adequately represent smooth trends. When investigating the number of tree splits we also witnessed the instability of the CART algorithm (e.g., Hothorn et al., 2006; Strobl et al., 2009), leading to larger variability in GOF across samples compared to the other models.

While the uncertainty of the other MEMs is reflected in the provided standard errors, the instability and (increased) uncertainty of the RE-EM tree is not apparent by looking at only one sample. The practical implications are discussed in the tutorial section.

4.4 Limitations

We derived our simulation scenarios from a real data set to maximize ecological validity and designed simulation scenarios that are challenging for the models (e.g., noisier data than RT, various nonlinear trends). Consequently, the results clearly demonstrate that the proposed models can be effectively applied to data sets from experiments that are sufficiently similar to this relatively simple design even with noisy measures such as RT.

We think the recommendations derived from the simulation study can be confidently generalized to experimental data with a minimum of 18 trials per experimental condition and participant (with more trials recommended for the RE-EM tree), randomization on the trial level (no block-wise randomization), similarly or less noisy, skewed measures, and experimental data with unbalanced experimental conditions (e.g. 20% vs 80% of trials). As with any other experiment, the generalizability of monte carlo simulations depends on specific design choices. In the following, we want to reflect critically on some of our choices and their potential impact on the generalizability of our results: 1.

Most importantly, we assumed fixed trends, that is, one trend per condition with a fixed shape across participants. This assumption may be realistic for very common phenomena (e.g., habituation of pupil dilation, Fink et al., 2024), but individual trends per participant may occur for a lot of real processes. To address this, we conducted another simulation investigating model performance under participant-specific trends for each condition (see next section). We also discuss possible modeling options for random trends in the tutorial section.

2. Related to the first point, for all models, we assumed that the correct random effects structure was specified. It remains an open question how well the models perform when the random-effects structure is misspecified (see next section).

3. More complex experimental designs with multiple experimental conditions are quite common. However, as long as the number of trials per condition is sufficiently large, increased complexity should not per se have an impact on the models' performances. Options to handle more complex experimental designs are discussed in the tutorial section.

4. The simulation results for GAMMs and RE-EM trees depend on a careful selection of their hyperparameters (i.e., amount of penalization, stopping criteria, and pruning), which were tailored for this specific, yet realistic, experimental data context. For GAMMs, these choices typically have very limited influence on estimation (Perperoglou et al., 2019). By contrast, for RE-EM trees, we also ran the simulation with default pruning settings (pruning via one standard error, complexity parameter = 0.001), which resulted in overly simplistic trees with a very small number of splits. 9 We provide more information and recommendations regarding optimal choices for hyperparameters in the tutorial section.

5. Specifically, for RE-EM trees based on the CART algorithm, it should be noted that they are not tailored to satisfy a nominal significance level like conventional inferential statistics are. This means their FPR depends on the hyperparameters but can not be specified explicitly. Conditional inference trees (RE-EM ctrees, Fu & Simonoff, 2015) may be a suitable alternative that circumvents this limitation.

Additionally, CART trees tend to select continuous over dichotomous predictors (Fu & Simonoff, 2015). In theory, RE-EM ctrees should be less susceptible to this issue as well. Although we did not observe such problems in this simulation, we acknowledge that more methodological research on mixed-effects trees is needed.

Their use in any research project should be accompanied by appropriate critical reflection.

6. The results cannot be generalized to very different trend shapes (e.g., rapid oscillation) or changes in effects across trial sequences instead of the whole experiment. For these scenarios, other models may be more suited (e.g., Keintzel et al., 2026). It is important that the sampling frequency matches the temporal scale of the time-varying effect (temporal allignment; Mulder et al., 2026).

5 Simulation for Random-Trends Scenarios

In the previous simulation, our data generating model contained fixed trends and time-varying effects. However, individuals varying in their individual change in RT over time, or even random interactions between trends and experimental condition are plausible in a lot of processes (Bolger et al., 2019).

Random trends should be considered in the modeling process for two reasons:

First of all, individual differences in trends may provide interesting insights into the substantive phenomena (e.g., Bolger et al., 2019; Elbe et al., 2024; Sörqvist et al., 2012) and could also take on various (nonlinear) shapes. The aforementioned models can all be extended with parametric random effects, GAMMs also with non-parametric random effects to do so (see Tutorial section). Second, unmodeled random effects introduce heterogeneity into the model and thus may result in false conclusions due to distorted standard error estimates for the fixed effects (e.g., Barr et al., 2013). In the following simulation, we illustrate the impact of unmodeled random trends and time-varying effects on model performance using a simplistic linear trend minimal example. The impact of misspecified random trends structure is already visible in this simplistic scenario and can be generalized to more complex models, while maintaining a reasonable runtime for the simulation.

We used the same dependent measures for model fit, generalizability and recovery of random effects as before. Misspecified random-effects structures are known to impact FPRs (Barr et al., 2013). For GAMMs we used the test of the approximate significance of the smooth term in the standard sound type condition. For RE-EM trees, there is no straightforward way to determine if a trend in standard trials was detected. Therefore we used the number of trees that only split once and the splitting variable was trial number as a proxy. The FPR for the time-varying effect was determined as in the fixed-trends simulation for all models.

Fixed-trends linear GCMs, polynomial GCMs, GAMMs, and RE-EM trees always converged. 5.3 Discussion

We showed even in a simplistic scenario, the choice of random effects, especially random trends, substantially influences the conclusions drawn from the model results for GCMs, GAMMs and RE-EM trees. Replicating previous research, we found that unmodeled random effects do not change fixed-effects model fit, but increase FPR due to underestimated standard errors (Barr et al., 2013; Matuschek et al., 2017). The FPR for the experimental effect remained at nominal level, as the respective random effect was included in all models. For RE-EM trees, we also observed an especially increased FPR for continuous predictors (time variable), reflecting a known bias of the CART algorithm regarding selection of continuous over dichotomous predictors as splitting variables. Additionally, we observed that random effects structures including random interaction terms resulted in more singular fit warnings in lme4, that is, estimated variance-covariance matrices with (almost) less than full rank indicating a certain "redundancy" in the random-effects covariances (see the tutorial section for more details).

Overall, the results of our random-trends simulation underline that correct specification of the random effects is mandatory for adequate statistical inference even when the variance of the neglected random slopes may seem negligibly small in absolute values. Given these findings, we re-emphasize existing recommendations to carefully think about random effects (Matuschek et al., 2017) and include all random effects warranted by the design (including trends and interactions here; Barr, 2013; Barr et al., 2013; Park et al., 2020). We discuss how this can be achieved with each of the models in the tutorial section. We acknowledge that there is more research needed to provide more specific recommendations on modeling individual trends.

6.1 Increasing Convergence and Handling Singular Fit Warnings

When implementing MEMs, researchers are often confronted with convergence issues, that is, the estimation algorithm not arriving at a solution, returning no estimates (or estimates that should not be interpreted). Depending on the model and software, different strategies can be used to improve model convergence. First of all, centering variables can be useful. In our case, we created a centered time variable so that 0 refers to a time point in the middle of the experiment (trial 120). Secondly, it proved to be useful to rescale the time variable to not range from -119 to 120 but from -2 to 2 (time_scaled). This way, all predictor variables had a similar range, helping with convergence. Note that the rescaling influences parameter interpretation, as we will outline below. Furthermore, different estimation methods are differentially susceptible to convergence issues. We found GCMs to converge better with REML instead of ML estimation. Bayesian estimation has also been proposed to circumvent convergence (and singularity) issues (e.g., Bürkner, 2018). 14 Lastly, various model options can be changed. The number of iterations for the optimization algorithm can be increased and different optimizers can be chosen (e.g., "bobyqa" instead of "nloptwrap" for lme4, "optim" instead of "nlminb" for REEMtree). All packages used in this tutorial also allow custom starting values for the algorithm, which can be helpful. These values may be taken from descriptive statistics or simpler models.

Another frequent issue encountered by researchers is singularity, that is, estimated variance-covariance matrices with less than full rank indicating a certain "redundancy" in the random-effects covariances. The source of the singularity can be hard to detect with more than one random slope. Singular models provide a mathematically correct estimation, but have been argued to have poor power (Matuschek et al., 2017) and some inferential tests may be inappropriate (Bates et al., 2015). Although we found no evidence of these issues in our simulation study, we emphasize that further research is needed to fully understand the impact of singular fits. There is no straightforward recommendation for handling singular fits in general and how to interpret the random-effects estimates in these models. Based on our simulation for random trends, we suggest that (nearly) singular random-effects covariance matrices should be accepted if the focus is on interpreting the fixed effects.

This difference occurred because the polynomial trend more effectively captured the nonlinear shape and thus also the smaller conditional experimental effects in the middle of the experiment. Here, estimating a linear trend leads to overestimation of the conditional experimental effect in the middle of the experiment, highlighting the importance of modeling nonlinear trends with adequate models.

In this polynomial GCM, the trend per sound type is described by six parameters instead of two. The three fixed-effects parameters poly(time, 3, raw = T)1 to 3 in Figure 9 describe the polynomial trend in standard trials (i.e., when sound type equals zero) and describe the linear, quadratic, and cubic term, respectively. The interaction terms with sound type correspond to the differences in these parameters between sound types. Thus, the polynomial trend in distractor trials is calculated by adding the respective interaction term parameters soundType:poly(time, 3, raw = T)1 to 3 to the standard trial parameters. Although specific parameter interpretations can be derived analytically from the polynomial functions, they do not intuitively correspond to the trend's shape (Simonsohn, 2018) and should instead be interpreted as part of the whole equation (Humberg et al., 2019). Therefore, we emphasize that researchers should complement their analyses with a visual inspection of the trend (see Figure 9). However, maxima, minima, intersections, and other characteristic points can be analytically derived from the polynomial parameters, and confidence intervals for these points can be estimated using the delta method or a Bayesian approach.

Polynomials can also be reformulated such that parameters match substantively relevant characteristic points, as explained by McCormick (2023).

As with linear GCMs, the random-effects variances quantify how much the participants' individual parameters vary beyond the fixed effects. The random intercept and slope for sound type are interpreted the same as in the linear GCM: They quantify how much participants conditional standard trial RTs in the middle of the experiment and how much their conditional distraction effects vary. The random effects for the polynomial terms have no intuitive interpretation, but may result in very different shapes of polynomials per participant, as can be seen in the light dotted lines in Figure 9.

6.3.2 Model Interpretation

The fit$mer part of the output (upper output in Figure 10) is structured like the lme4 output and contains fixed-and random-effects parameters. The fixed intercept (407 ms) differs in both value and interpretation from the previously discussed GCMs: It now represents the estimated marginal RT in the standard trials, instead of the conditional RT. Similarly, the fixed slope for sound type indicates that the estimated marginal experimental effect is 17 ms. Inference for these parameters can be obtained from the fit$gam part of the output under parametric coefficients (bottom panel of Figure 10).

A notable peculiarity is that the splines per sound type (Xs() and Xr()), shown in the grey parts of the output in Figure 10, are listed in both the fixed and random-effects sections of fit$mer. This is because penalized splines can be represented within a mixed-model framework, with the unpenalized component treated as a fixed effect and the penalized component as a random effect, that has, however, nothing to do with between-person variability (Gurrin et al., 2005). Here, the grey Xs coefficients represent the unpenalized linear components for standard (23.44) and distractor (92.01) trials. Both use the same internally scaled, decreasing basis function.

Thus, the larger positive coefficient for distractor trials indicates a stronger linear component of the temporal decline. The grey Xr variances determine shrinkage of the nonlinear spline components (and not between-participant variability; Wood & Scheipl, 2020). For standard trials, the variance is effectively zero (displayed as 0.00), leaving an approximately linear trend (EDF = 1.00). For distractor trials, the positive variance (4.41) allows a nonlinear contribution (EDF = 5.31). In combination with the linear component this produces the sharp initial decline and subsequent flattening. We recommend visual inspection of the fitted trends to better understand the shapes of the splines.

The fitted values in Figure 10 resemble those of the 3 rd -degree-polynomial GCM in Figure 9, except for the decrease in distractor trial RT during the final trials. Thus, the decrease was likely an artifact of the lower order polynomial, which could not adequately approximate the flattening trend. Inference for the time-varying effect can be obtained using a Likelihood-Ratio test (Sóskuthy, 2017). We compared a GAMM with different splines per sound type (time-varying effect) with a GAMM with a common spline for both sound types (i.e., without the by argument in the smooth term s()), indicating a better fit for the model with different trends per sound type (χ 2 (2) = 40.737, p < .001).

It is also possible to further test the conditional experimental effect at specific time points using difference smooths (Sóskuthy, 2017). These compare the condition-specific smooths across the observed time range. By default, they show differences between the smooth components without including the difference in the conditions' overall mean levels (group_means = FALSE). Setting group_means = TRUE also includes the difference associated with the parametric condition term, giving the estimated total condition difference over time. The difference is plotted with pointwise confidence intervals, which can be used to identify time points at which the interval excludes zero. We implemented this approach using the gratia R-package (Simpson, 2024, see code on GitHub). In our example, the results indicate that differences between sound types were largest and significant for the initial trials.

Finally, the random intercept and random slope for sound type also have a marginal interpretation, because in this case, the random effects structure is less complex than the fixed effects structure (no random trends and interactions). They quantify the variation in participants' marginal RTs in standard trials and in the marginal distraction effect, respectively.

6.4.1 Model Specification

RE-EM trees can be fit using the REEMtree package (Sela & Simonoff, 2021), which depends on the rpart package (Therneau & Atkinson, 2022). The syntax (Figure 12) begins with the dependent variable RT, followed by all fixed predictor variables separated by a +. We chose to stick with the raw trial number here for a more intuitive interpretation. The random effects are specified in the random argument, again following a syntax similar to what was used before but without the parentheses. As mentioned before, RE-EM trees are only capable of including parametric random effects. In Figure 12 we chose to only model random intercepts and random slopes for sound type, omitting any random trends. We will discuss this decision below.

The following arguments address the stopping criteria and pruning. The stopping criteria are set using tree.control, where we set the complexity parameter to cp = 0.0001, allowing splits that contribute only 0.01% of total explained variance (i.e., an increment in R² of only 0.0001; Therneau & Atkinson, 2022). Although this value seems very small, consider that the total effect for a continuous predictor (e.g., trial number) consists of multiple splits. Each individual split might contribute a small proportion, but the total variance explained may still be substantial. The minbucket and maxdepth arguments can be used to define the "largest meaningful tree" based on design considerations. The minbucket argument specifies the minimum number of observations required in a final node. We set minbucket = 106, which ensures that data from at least two consecutive trials for all 53 participants is segmented into one final node. The maxdepth argument indicates the maximum number of splits allowed before reaching a final node, with a default setting of 30. Determining sensible values for these stopping criteria can be challenging. Alternatively, it is possible set cp = 0, minbucket = 1, and maxdepth = 30 to grow the full initial tree and determine the final tree by pruning alone.

Pruning is enabled by setting cv = TRUE. The default option is no.SE = 1, pruning to the simplest tree with a cross-validation error within one standard error from the minimal cross-validation error (Breiman et al., 1984; Sela & Simonoff, 2012). For our data, this setting resulted in overly simplistic trees that rarely detected time-varying or any experimental effects. Therefore, we recommend pruning such that the complexity parameter with the minimal cross-validation error is used (no.SE = 0), as described in the introduction. However, this recommendation may be specific to our data scenario, and further research on pruning recommendations for different data settings is needed. Finally, another useful setting is to change the optimizer to "optim" using the lme.control argument, as the default "nlminb" often failed to converge in our pilot simulations.

RE-EM trees can easily be expanded by adding more fixed predictor variables to model trends for multiple (combinations of) experimental conditions. The provided plot(fit) can help in understanding even complex interaction patterns in experimental predictors.

6.4.2 Model Interpretation

The tree output in text form (bottom right of Figure 12) can be difficult to read. The first part provides the estimated values for each node, which are better understood when visualized with plot(fit), as in Figure 12 in the bottom left panel. Nodes marked with * in the output correspond to the final node values in plot(fit) and the bold line segments in the fitted values plot. In the tree plot, each split is indicated by the the splitting variable and the split point. Observations that meet the decision criterion (e.g., soundType < 0.5 for the first split) are positioned to the left (standard trials coded 0), whereas those that do not meet the criterion are positioned to the right (distractor trials coded 1). For standard trials, the tree splits only once, indicating that estimated RTs in the last trials (trial number >= 107.5) are lower than in the first 108 (trialnum starts at 0) trials (402 ms vs 413 ms). This split shows that the tree models the previously observed slow decay in standard trials (see Figures 8 to 10) as a level change, demonstrating the tree's limitations in approximating smooth, especially quasilinear, functions. For distractor trials, the tree split twice, resulting in a shape similar to the models before, but again, stepwise in nature.

At the bottom of the fit output, we find the covariance matrix of the random effects. From this, we can calculate the random intercept SD = √ 1968 ms = 44 ms, the random slope SD = √ 171 ms = 13 ms, and the random effects correlation r = 249 44×13 = .44. The interpretation of these random effects now concerns marginal effects, because the random effects structure is less complex than the fixed effects structure (no random trends and interaction). Therefore, the random intercept variance quantifies the variation in participants' marginal standard trial RT and the random slope variance quantifies the variation in their marginal distraction effect. RE-EM trees can also be expanded to include more complex, though only parametric, random-effects structures, such as individual linear or polynomial trends per experimental condition. RE-EM trees can not model fully nonparametric individual trends because it is not possible to allow the tree structure to vary across participants.

Based on our simulation for random trends, we caution about using simple RE-EM trees as in Figure 12 when individuals vary substantially regarding trends and/or in situations with large number of trials. This is a clear limitation of the RE-EM trees.

However, we also caution against including parametric random trends and interactions as a fix, because they severely complicate interpretation and the impact on model estimation remains unclear. The only way to model different trees depending on person-characteristics in RE-EM trees is by including relevant person-level predictors for these subgroups as splitting variables, but research shows that RE-EM trees are not well suited for this purpose (Hany et al., 2026). Researchers interested in detecting subgroups of participants with similar trends might instead want to consider different tree-based methods that are explicitly dedicated to detecting these subgroups (Abdolell et al., 2002; Eo & Cho, 2014; Fokkema et al., 2017; Kundu & Harezlak, 2019; Segal, 1992; Stegmann et al., 2018).

As a final caveat, we want to point out again that no inferential statistics or measures of uncertainty are included in the RE-EM tree output, even though the tree algorithm is associated with considerable uncertainty. Trees produce largely different results for different samples, and their results also depend on random components, such as cross-validation, in the algorithm. Some approaches to mitigate these issues are also applicable to repeated-measures data, for example, RE-EM ctrees (Fu & Simonoff, 2015) and mixed-effects random forests (Hajjem et al., 2014). However, the latter can not be used with only two predictor variables.

7 Practical Recommendations

Investigating time-varying experimental effects can elucidate underlying (cognitive) processes, increase statistical power, and avoid the risk of misleading comparisons of marginal effects across experiments of varying length. Our simulations demonstrated that exact parametric models, lower order polynomial GCMs, and GAMMs can all effectively detect and model time-varying experimental effects across various trend shapes under realistic conditions provided an adequate random-effects structure. Only for stepwise trends, RE-EM trees and GAMMs outperformed lower order polynomial GCMs. For smooth trends, RE-EM trees showed no advantage over polynomial GCMs or GAMMs, as they also achieved a good approximation provided large numbers of trials and sample sizes, but showed worse generalization to new samples than the other models for small number of trials and participants and suffered from an increased FPR when random trends are not adequately modeled. In the tutorial, we illustrated that, in practice, these models differ in flexibility, interpretability, statistical inference, and expandability to more complex fixed-and random-effects structures.

Ultimately, the selection of a model depends on the research goal. If the goal is prediction, models from different classes can be compared using cross-validation, evaluating how accurately they predict observations that were not used for fitting. For repeated-measures data, the validation scheme should reflect the intended prediction task: For example, assessing generalization to new participants requires holding out entire participants rather than individual observations (Rabinowicz & Rosset, 2022).

With small samples, cross-validation estimates can be highly variable, so small differences in predictive performance should be interpreted cautiously (Varoquaux, 2018). Information criteria such as AIC and BIC offer another approach to model comparison by balancing model fit against model complexity. Both criteria have established asymptotic correspondences with specific cross-validation procedures for model selection, although the corresponding procedures and assumptions differ (Shao, 1997; Stone, 1977). Comparisons require models fitted to the same observations and response scale using comparable likelihood definitions. This is particularly important when comparing results across software packages, which may differ in the constants included in the reported log-likelihood and in how model complexity is counted. Fitting the candidate models using the same software and estimation procedure, for example, fitting alternative GCMs in nlme, helps avoid these discrepancies (see Tutorial R Code).

Nevertheless, using the same software does not by itself guarantee comparability: Models with different fixed-effects structures should be compared using maximum likelihood rather than restricted maximum likelihood.

If the goal is to characterize the shape of a trend or a time-varying experimental effect, we recommend using the models together to assess which substantive conclusions depend on the modeling of time. For example, agreement across models about an initial decline followed by a plateau provides stronger support for this qualitative pattern than its appearance in only one fitted curve. Such comparisons can guide the development of a parametric model whose parameters have theoretical meaning, as is common in mathematical psychology. A model developed through this exploratory process should subsequently be evaluated using independent data. Balancing the strengths and weaknesses of each model, we propose the following workflow for investigating time-varying experimental effects (Figure 13). Most simple continuous trends can be reasonably approximated with polynomial models. We recommend using lower order polynomials as the default modeling option due to their manageable parameter interpretation, availability of inferential statistics for parameters, as well as points of interest and ease of extension to model individual random trends. Exact parametric models can be specified as MEMs when there is a specific assumption about the shape of the trend. We recommend that they be used only when supported by strong theoretical grounds, as they can not capture deviating shapes and may obscure such shapes, potentially leading to false conclusions.

Because lower order polynomials, especially linear functions, are unable to depict more complex shapes (which are likely to occur in psychophysiological data) and are susceptible to artifacts at the edges of the data range (e.g., "wiggly" trends), we recommend complementing them with GAMMs. If GAMM results are similar to GCM, the polynomial model is likely sufficiently complex. Differences between GCMs and GAMMs at the edges of the data range may indicate artifacts in the GCM. For more complex smooth trends, GAMMs should be preferred over higher order polynomials with many parameters. Note that GAMMs without individual (random) trends can still be used to compare the shape of the spline to the polynomial curve visually, even though inference for the spline is not reliable when random trends are present. RE-EM trees can be used to detect major deviations from proposed trends caused by abrupt level changes.
