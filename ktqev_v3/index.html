What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Whose deposit is each link? Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Advances in digital technology have made physiological signals such as electrocardiography (ECG) and electrodermal activity (EDA) increasingly accessible, enabling previously covert bodily states to be shared in real time during social exchanges. Recent work, primarily qualitative, suggests that biosignal sharing can modulate social interaction, yet quantitative evidence on its psychophysiological impact remains limited. Here, we investigated whether biosignal sharing during social interaction, and in particular the reciprocity of such information exchange, modulates psychophysiological responses. We present data from 18 sex-matched dyads (N = 36) of strangers, whose ECG and EDA were recorded while they engaged in nonverbal face-to-face interaction. Participants shared their heart rate and skin conductance levels (displayed on a monitor) either unidirectionally, bidirectionally, or not at all. We examined the effects of biosignal sharing on indices of autonomic nervous system activity (including RMSSD, skin conductance level, and the number and amplitude of skin conductance responses) and related these measures to psychometric assessments of empathic traits, social interaction anxiety, perceived scrutiny, and interpersonal closeness. Bidirectional biosignal sharing significantly reduced both the skin conductance level and the number of skin conductance responses compared to both face-toface interaction without sharing and unidirectional sharing. Moreover, greater reductions in sympathetic activation during bidirectional sharing were associated with larger increases in perceived interpersonal closeness. Correlational analyses further indicated that the perceived scrutiny was linked to heightened arousal and reduced closeness. Our findings advance the understanding of the psychophysiological impact of biosignal sharing, with direct implications for the design of social computing systems and computer-mediated interaction.

1. Introduction

Human social interaction relies not only on what people say or do, but also on subtle physiological responses that signal emotional and cognitive states. Mind and body are deeply intertwined and mutually influence one another (Cacioppo et al., 2007; Critchley, 2009; Hugdahl, 1995). Mental states are reflected in physiological signals, while bodily physiology shapes emotional and cognitive processes (Critchley and Harrison, 2013; Engelen et al., 2023; Garfinkel et al., 2017). The autonomic nervous system (ANS) plays a central role in this interplay by coordinating bodily functions that maintain internal balance and support adaptive responses to environmental demands (Critchley, 2009; Porges, 2009; Thayer and Lane, 2000). Physiological measures such as electrocardiography (ECG) and electrodermal activity (EDA) provide non-invasive windows into these dynamics, reflecting the regulation of the parasympathetic and sympathetic branches of the ANS (Boucsein, 2012; Dawson et al., 2017; Levinson and Hajcak, 2020; Shaffer and Ginsberg, 2017; Thayer and Lane, 2000). Together, these biosignals offer insights not only into intra-individual bodily states (ECG: Arakaki et al., 2023; Balzarotti et al., 2017; Gullett et al., 2023; EDA: Boucsein, 2012; Levinson and Hajcak, 2020) but also into how psychophysiological processes are shaped by, and informative for, social interaction (Candini et al., 2021; Deits-Lebehn et al., 2023; Helminen et al., 2011; Jauniaux et al., 2020; Mauersberger et al., 2022; Myllyneva and Hietanen, 2015; Palumbo et al., 2017; Petrocchi and Cheli, 2019; Prochazkova et al., 2022; Quintana et al., 2012; Sariñana-González et al., 2017; Thayer and Lane, 2000), including technologically mediated exchanges (Ekman et al., 2012; Järvelä et al., 2014; Perugia et al., 2017; Torres et al., 2024).

In everyday social interactions, we rely on overt cues, such as blushing, tone of voice, or body language, to infer others' emotional and mental states. If made accessible, covert bodily signals may also carry valuable information about these states and shape social interaction. Recent research has therefore moved from treating biosignals merely as internal markers of psychophysiological states to exploring how externalizing them can provide socially meaningful cues (Chanel and Mühl, 2015; Feijt et al., 2023; Moge et al., 2022; Stepanova et al., 2022). Biosignal sharing refers to the practice of making covert physiological signals (e.g., ECG, EDA) overtly available to others. While such information has traditionally been used intrapersonally for self-regulation (e.g., biofeedback), current work in affective computing and human-computer interaction (HCI) highlights the interpersonal potential of these signals in shaping social interaction (Chanel and Mühl, 2015; Janssen, 2012; Moge et al., 2022). Comprehensive reviews em-phasize that biosignal sharing may support communication, increase awareness of others' emotions, and foster social connection, while also underscoring the importance of design choices (including how and to whom this information is disclosed) in shaping these effects (Feijt et al., 2023; Stepanova et al., 2022).

A growing body of qualitative and design-oriented studies illustrates how biosignal sharing can shape social interaction across several domains. First, shared biosignals can influence how people interpret affect and emotion. For example, heart-rate visualizations in chat or mobile interfaces have encouraged partners to better recognize each other's feelings and promoted empathy and attentiveness (Hassib et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019). Even imagined scenarios involving shared heart-rate information have been shown to influence judgments of trust and cooperation (Merrill and Cheshire, 2017). Similarly, color-changing garments linked to skin conductance prompted friends to validate emotions, signal engagement, or reflect more deeply on their affective states (Howell et al., 2016). Second, biosignals can cue presence, intimacy, and connectedness. Romantic couples reported greater closeness when sharing their heart rates (Slovák et al., 2012a (Slovák et al., , 2012b)), and workplace employees described a stronger sense of human contact when heart rate was displayed during collaboration (Wikström et al., 2021). Along similar lines, sharing breathing patterns through virtual reality installations or vibrotactile displays enhanced feelings of connection with others and with nature (El Ali et al., 2023; Stepanova et al., 2020). In addition, biosignal sharing has been explored as a means to support collaboration and performance. In problem-solving tasks, providing multimodal biofeedback of heart rate, electrodermal activity, and respiration reduced stress and workload while improving clarity of communication (Tan et al., 2014). Taken together, these studies suggest that biosignals can function as socially meaningful cues and that they have value in supporting joint action and computer-mediated interactions.

Alongside these promising findings, research also highlights that the effects of biosignal sharing are often ambiguous and context-dependent. Access to heart-rate information, for example, can lead to divergent social evaluations depending on situational framing: elevated heart rate may be interpreted as anxiety during interpersonal conflict, whereas a lack of elevation may be perceived as indifference e.g. when arriving late to a meeting (Merrill and Cheshire, 2016). People may also learn to attribute meaning to artificial physiological signals, creating new but potentially misleading social mappings (Merrill and Cheshire, 2017). Similar ambiguities have been reported for socially shared EDA, where participants struggled to interpret feedback consistently and employed it in divergent ways (Howell et al., 2016).

More broadly, willingness to share biosignals depends strongly on context. Participants tend to feel more comfortable sharing physiological signals in playful or positive settings, such as gaming, than in professional or adversarial ones (Buschek et al., 2018; Hassib et al., 2017; Slovák et al., 2012b). In workplace studies, heart-rate sharing increased feelings of human contact but also raised concerns about intimacy, professionalism, and work efficiency (Wikström et al., 2021). These concerns often center on privacy and impression management: since physiological signals are involuntary and difficult to control, sharing them can feel like a loss of autonomy over what is revealed about one's internal states and how one is perceived by others (Feijt et al., 2023; Slovák et al., 2012b).

Importantly, design features of biosignal-sharing systems appear to shape how such concerns are experienced. For instance, the degree of reciprocity in the exchange may modulate both perceived risks and potential benefits. Bidirectional sharing, in which interaction partners mutually disclose their biosignals, has been suggested to promote intimacy and trust while mitigating privacy concerns, whereas unidirectional setups may heighten feelings of exposure and social evaluation (Feijt et al., 2023). Despite the centrality of reciprocity in many proposed applications of biosignal sharing, its role in shaping psychophysiological and interpersonal outcomes has received limited systematic investigation.

Overall, biosignals hold strong social meaning, and sharing them during social interaction has shown broad potential to shape subjective experiences and interpersonal dynamics. Yet, despite this growing body of work, most existing studies remain qualitative or design-oriented (Feijt et al., 2023; Stepanova et al., 2022). Consequently, there is a clear need for quantitative, controlled investigations to determine how biosignal sharing influences both social behavior and psychophysiological states.

We addressed three central questions: (1) Does biosignal sharing during social interaction influence individuals' psychophysiological states? (2) Does the reciprocity of information exchange modulate these effects? (3) How do individual psychological traits shape these responses? To this end, we conducted an experiment in which pairs of strangers engaged in face-to-face interactions while their electrocardiography (ECG) and electrodermal activity (EDA) were simultaneously recorded. We implemented a setup that displayed participants' ECG, heart rate (HR), and EDA in real time on a monitor visible to their partner. The study design included three levels of sharing: (1) Face-to-face (no sharing), (2) Unidirectional sharing (one participant shares while the other observes), and (3) Bidirectional sharing (mutual simultaneous sharing). We examined the effects of these conditions on indices of autonomic activity and their relationship to in-dividual differences in empathy and social anxiety, and to changes in perceived interpersonal closeness.

2. Materials and methods

>Materials and methods

2.1. Participants

Thirty-six participants (18 dyads of strangers matched by sex assigned at birth; 9 female pairs and 9 male pairs) participated in the study. Two dyads were excluded from further analysis due to technical issues during the recording session. Since no previous quantitative research had examined the physiological effects of biosignal sharing in social interaction, we conducted a power analysis based on the presumably smaller contrast of interest in our study, that is, for a paired-samples t-test between the two levels of biosignal sharing. We targeted a medium-to-large effect size (d = 0.50) with a significance threshold of 0.05. The result indicated that 34 participants would provide an 80% chance of detecting the effect of interest.

The participants were mainly students or researchers in the Helsinki area, aged 18-30 years (females: mean (M) age ± standard deviation (SD): 24.75 ± 3.72 years; males: 24.69 ± 3.39 years). Eligibility criteria included a good understanding of spoken English, no diagnosed psychiatric, neurological, or cardiovascular disorders, and no use of drugs or treatments affecting the central nervous system (CNS) or cardiac activity. Participants were also provided with a set of behavioral recommendations to follow 2 to 3 hours before the experiment to optimize data quality (Quintana and Heathers, 2014). These recommendations included avoiding nicotine, caffeine, and vigorous activity, as well as staying hydrated and sated while avoiding overeating and excessive water consumption.

All participants provided written, informed consent prior to the study. As compensation, they received a lunch voucher with an approximate value of €13. The experimental sessions were conducted at the Aalto Behavioral Laboratory (ABL) at Aalto University. The study was approved by the Aalto University Research Ethics Committee (reference: D/1339/03.04/2023).

2.2. Experimental design and setup

The experiment consisted of four face-to-face conditions differing in the level and reciprocity of biosignal sharing: (i) face-to-face interaction without biosignal sharing (F2F), (ii) unidirectional biosignal sharing in which one participant shared their biosignals while being observed (Sharing; S), (iii) the complementary role in which the participant observed the partner's biosignals without sharing (Observing; O), and (iv) bidirectional biosignal sharing (BS), in which both participants shared and observed each other's biosignals simultaneously (see Figure 1). Each experimental condition lasted 5 min and was preceded by a baseline period during which participants watched an emotionally neutral video (Jennings et al., 1992), which lasted approximately 4 min and featured relaxing scenes of fish and coral reefs. The order of the experimental conditions was counterbalanced across dyads to control for order effects.

Participants sat about 1 m apart, facing each other. A monitor was placed behind each participant and aligned such that participants could shift their gaze between their partner's face and the monitor positioned next to their partner without moving their heads. Participants could not see the content displayed on their own monitor while facing their partner.

At the beginning of each condition, participants received written instructions displayed on the monitor positioned in front of them (adjacent to their partner). Depending on the condition, the monitors either displayed biosignal information or remained black. During biosignal sharing, the relevant monitor displayed filtered ECG and skin conductance level (SCL) traces, along with numerical values for heart rate and SCL. During the baseline periods, the participants turned around to watch the neutral video on their own monitors.

During biosignal-sharing conditions, physiological signals were streamed in real time using Lab Streaming Layer (Kothe et al., 2025) and visualized on the monitor via a custom Python interface implemented with PyQtGraph. The incoming ECG and EDA signals were continuously buffered and displayed as scrolling traces over a 10-s rolling window. For visualization purposes, signals were low-pass filtered using a zero-phase 4th-order Butterworth filter with a 20-Hz cutoff frequency. Heart rate (HR) was estimated online from the ECG signal using automated R-peak detection (two-average detector, implemented in the pyecg-detectors Python package; Porr et al., 2024) and calculated as the reciprocal of the mean interbeat (R-R) interval within a 10-s sliding window, expressed in beats per minute. The displayed skin conductance value reflected the mean skin conductance level computed over a short sliding window (0.5 s). Numerical HR (beats per minute) and SCL (µS) values were updated approximately every 500 ms. This online processing was implemented solely for real-time feedback; all physiological analyses reported in Results were conducted offline using separately preprocessed data (see Section 2.6). For details on the physiological data acquisition, see Section 2.5.

We used PsychoPy2 (v.2023 .1.3, Peirce et al., 2019) to implement the experiment. 2.3. Questionnaires and self-reports

We used several questionnaires and self-report items to assess participants' experience of biosignal sharing as well as relevant psychological traits. Participants completed the 28-item revised Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI; Davis, 1980), which measures four empathy-related constructs: perspective taking (PT), empathic concern (EC), personal distress (PD), and fantasy (FS). As the fantasy scale was not relevant to the present research questions, it was excluded from further analyses. Participants also completed the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale (SIAS; Mattick and Clarke, 1998), a 20-item measure assessing anxiety during social interactions and in situations involving observation or scrutiny. Perceived interpersonal closeness within each dyad was assessed using the Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) scale (Aron et al., 1992), administered both before and after the experiment.

In addition to these established instruments, we developed a General Experiment Questionnaire (GEQ) to capture beliefs, perceptions, and behaviors specific to the experimental context. The GEQ included items assessing familiarity with biosignals (e.g., principles, analysis, and interpretation of ECG and EDA), confidence in recognizing biosignal features, and beliefs regarding the relationship between physiological signals and mental states. Two versions were administered (see Supplementary Material): Version A (GEQ-A), completed prior to the experiment, assessed baseline familiarity, beliefs, and concerns; Version B (GEQ-B), completed after the experiment, probed changes related to the sharing and observation of biosignals and included items assessing subjective experiences during the interaction (e.g., gaze direction, attention allocation, and perceptions of the partner's attention). Additional items measured affective responses, discomfort, and concerns related to biosignal sharing. From GEQ-B, items 9-11 were combined into a composite score reflecting efforts to divert attention from face-to-face interaction in order to manage discomfort or feelings of overwhelm (Cronbach's alpha = 0.840). GEQ-A items and GEQ-B items 1-11 were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Strongly Disagree) to 6 (Strongly Agree), whereas GEQ-B items 12-15 were rated on a 7-point scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 6 (All the time). Table 1 provides an overview of all questionnaires, subscales, and items considered for correlation analyses (see Section 2.7).

2.4. Experimental procedure

Upon arrival at the laboratory, participants met for the first time and were informed about the experimental procedure. After providing written informed consent, they were guided to the experimental room and seated in designated positions facing each other (see Section 2.2). The experimenter introduced the biosignal acquisition devices and prepared the physiological measurement equipment, including ECG and EDA electrodes and the respiration belt, as detailed in Section 2.5.

Participants then completed the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale (SIAS), the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), the Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) scale, and the General Experiment Questionnaire Version A (GEQ-A). During this period, electrode contacts and physiological signals were allowed to stabilize. The experimenter continuously monitored signal quality and adjusted electrode or belt placement when necessary.

Participants subsequently received oral instructions describing the experimental setup and task. They were instructed to look at each other or at their partner's biosignals if available, and to behave naturally and relax while refraining from any overt form of interaction (verbal or gestural). They were also asked to minimize large movements, particularly of the arms and shoulders, and to avoid laughter, as these behaviors could introduce artifacts into the physiological recordings.

Participants then proceeded to the experimental conditions. Condition-specific instructions were displayed on the relevant monitor prior to each block. Before each experimental condition, participants watched a neutral baseline video. Although breaks were permitted if needed, no dyad requested a break during the experiment.

Throughout the session, the experimenter continuously monitored the biosignal quality and, if necessary due to persistent artifacts in the recordings, paused the experiment to adjust the acquisition devices. After the experiment, participants completed a second set of questionnaires: the IOS scale and the version B of the General Questionnaire. During electrode removal, participants were debriefed and informally asked about their experience.

The entire session lasted approximately 80 minutes, including around 40 minutes for preparation, electrode placement, and questionnaires, and approximately 40 minutes for the experimental conditions.

2.5. Acquisition of physiological data

During the experiment, we collected participants' ECG, EDA, and respiration signals using the BITalino PsychoBIT bundle (PLUX Wireless Biosignals S.A., Lisbon, Portugal) at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. ECG was acquired using three electrodes placed on both clavicles and the left iliac crest, following an Einthoven Lead II configuration. EDA electrodes were placed on the distal phalanges of the left index and middle fingers, and a respiration belt was positioned around the ribcage between the 8 th and 10 th ribs. Electrode contacts were allowed to stabilize for approximately 15 min before recording. Sensor placement and acquisition procedures followed manufacturer recommendations and relevant literature (Boucsein, 2012; Prochazkova et al., 2022; van Dooren et al., 2012). Respiration was recorded but not analyzed in the present study.

Physiological data acquisition was performed using the OpenSignals software package (v2.2.1; PLUX Biosignals, Lisbon, Portugal). Lab Streaming Layer (Kothe et al., 2025) was used to stream physiological data from OpenSignals alongside experimental triggers from PsychoPy. These triggers were used to segment the experimental conditions in the recorded data. During the biosignalsharing conditions, the real-time physiological stream was also used to display ECG, HR, EDA, and SCL to the participants. All physiological signals and triggers were recorded using LabRecorder (v1.16.4; Kothe et al., 2025).

2.6. Analysis of physiological features

To examine participants' physiological states, we analyzed signal features reflecting parasympathetic and sympathetic activity. From ECG recordings, we computed the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) of R-R intervals, an index of parasympathetic regulation. From EDA recordings, we extracted the skin conductance level (SCL), the number of skin conductance responses (SCRs), and their mean amplitude, which index sympathetic activation (Boucsein, 2012; Pham et al., 2021). Feature extraction was performed using NeuroKit2 (Makowski et al., 2021) and custom Python scripts. Signal features were computed over the full five-minute duration of each experimental condition. Baseline values were calculated for each condition using the final three minutes of the preceding neutral video baseline.

2.6.1. ECG

To compute the RMSSD, the raw ECG signal was preprocessed and R peaks were detected to derive interbeat intervals (IBIs). Preprocessing included highpass filtering at 0.5 Hz (5th-order Butterworth filter) to remove slow drifts, and notch filtering at 50 Hz to attenuate power-line interference.

The R peaks were detected using a custom algorithm based on a slidingwindow approach designed to identify high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) segments. The algorithm compared signal power between two windows: a 60ms window capturing candidate R peaks and a 500-ms window estimating the surrounding noise. A moving root-mean-square (RMS) standard deviation was computed, and regions exhibiting higher power values in the 60-ms window were flagged as potential peak segments. Within each segment, the point of maximum amplitude was identified as an R peak. A minimum inter-peak interval of 200 ms was enforced to exclude physiologically implausible detections. This approach proved robust in moderately noisy recordings. Heavily noisy segments were manually removed after visual inspection.

Concatenation of clean signal segments occasionally introduced spurious R-R intervals at segment boundaries. To address this, IBIs shorter than 300 ms or longer than 1300 ms were removed based on physiological plausibility and visual inspection. Additional outlier IBIs were identified and excluded using a threshold of 2.5 times the median absolute deviation (MAD), computed separately for each condition (Leys et al., 2013). The RMSSD was then computed from the remaining IBIs for each experimental condition.

2.6.2. EDA

The EDA signal was pre-processed by applying a 4th-order Butterworth lowpass filter with a cutoff frequency of 3 Hz and visually inspected for artifacts. The signal was then decomposed into phasic and tonic components. Portions of the signal fluctuating above 0.05 Hz were categorized as phasic EDA, representing skin conductance responses (SCRs), while portions fluctuating below 0.05 Hz were categorized as tonic EDA, or skin conductance level (SCL). From the phasic component, we extracted the number of SCRs (EDA peaks) and their amplitude using the NeuroKit method; that is, local maxima in the signal were identified as peaks, and only those with amplitudes exceeding 10% of the maximum peak height were retained as SCR occurrences.

2.7. Statistical and correlational analysis

Statistical analyses were performed on the mean values of the physiological metrics of interest: RMSSD, SCL, the number of SCRs, and the mean amplitude of SCRs. All physiological analyses followed a within-subjects design. Metrics were compared across experimental conditions: Face-to-Face (F2F), Observing (O), Sharing (S), and Bidirectional Sharing (BS). The unidirectional condition was split according to participant role (observing vs. sharing). Physiological features were also correlated with psychometric questionnaire scores. All statistical tests used an alpha level of 0.05, unless otherwise specified.

ANOVAs were performed using the Pingouin package (Vallat, 2018, v0.5.4), permutation t-tests with MNE Python (Gramfort et al., 2013), and Spearman correlation tests with SciPy. False discovery rate (FDR) corrections were applied using the StatsModels package. All permutation tests were two-tailed and based on 5000 permutations. Figures were created using Seaborn (Waskom, 2021) and ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016).

2.7.1. Baseline reliability and experimental effects relative to the baseline

We first assessed the reliability of the baseline periods by comparing the mean values of each physiological metric across the four baseline video-watching segments. Mean values were submitted to a one-way repeated measures ANOVA with Greenhouse-Geisser correction, followed by pairwise permutation t-tests.

We then examined whether the experimental conditions induced changes in autonomic activity relative to their respective baselines. For each condition, mean physiological metric values were compared to those of the immediately preceding baseline period using permutation t-tests.

2.7.2. Effect of biosignal sharing

To investigate the effects of biosignal sharing, physiological metrics were baseline-corrected by subtracting the mean value of the baseline period preceding each experimental condition. Baseline-corrected values were then submitted to separate one-way repeated-measures ANOVAs with Greenhouse-Geisser correction for each metric, followed by pairwise permutation t-tests.

2.7.3. Experiment-related changes on self-report measures

We evaluated experiment-induced changes in participants' perceptions and comfort levels with respect to the sharing and interpretation of physiological signals. To this end, we compared responses from the General Experiment Questionnaire collected before (GEQ-A) and after (GEQ-B) the experiment. Specifically, we examined changes in: belief in the link between physiological signals and internal states (1B-5A), confidence in inferring emotions from physiological signals (2B-6A), discomfort with sharing physiological signals (4B-7A), and concern about exposing internal states (6B-8A).

We also compared IOS scores collected before and after the experiment to assess changes in perceived interpersonal closeness. All comparisons were performed using permutation t-tests.

2.7.4. Correlation analysis

To examine associations between physiological states and psychological traits, questionnaire scores and self-report items (summarized in Table 1) were correlated with physiological features. For each biosignal-sharing condition (Bidirectional Sharing, Observing, and Sharing), baseline-corrected physiological metrics were further normalized by subtracting the corresponding baseline-corrected values from the Face-to-Face condition. These normalized values were then correlated with questionnaire scores and self-report items using Spearman's rank correlations. For each metric and condition, 25 correlations were tested, and pvalues were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. These 25 items were also correlated with each other, applying the same false discovery rate correction procedure.

3. Results

>Results

3.1. Baseline reliability and experimental effects relative to the baseline

The four baseline periods did not show any significant differences for any of the physiological metrics examined (see Supplementary Material). Furthermore, when comparing the experimental conditions to their respective baselines, both SCL and the number of SCRs exhibited experimental effects (see Fig. 2, left). Specifically, SCL increased significantly relative to baseline in all conditions except bidirectional sharing (Fig. 2A, left), while all experimental conditions showed a significant increase in SCRs compared to their corresponding baselines, indicating a general increase in sympathetic arousal during social interaction (Fig. 2C, left). Detailed statistical results are provided in the Supplementary Material.

3.2. Effect of biosignal sharing

The baseline-corrected physiological metric values across experimental conditions are shown in Figure 2. Repeated-measures ANOVAs revealed a significant effect of condition on skin conductance level (SCL), F(2.82, 87.34) = 4.52, p = .006, η² G = .078. No significant omnibus effects were observed for the other physiological metrics (see Supplementary Material).

Follow-up pairwise permutation t-tests indicated significantly lower baselinecorrected SCL during bidirectional sharing (BS) (M BS = 0.10 ± 1.44 µS) compared with face-to-face interaction without biosignal sharing (F2F) (M F2F = 1.73 ± 2.29 µS, t(31) = -3.77, p < .001), unidirectional Observing (O) (M O = 0.96 ± 2.28 µS, t(31) = -1.81, p = .041), and unidirectional Sharing (S) (M S = 0.93 ± 1.91 µS, t(31) = -2.20, p = .034). No other pairwise SCL comparisons were significant.

Given our a-priori interest in reciprocity effects, and the fact that SCRs showed consistent experimental modulation relative to baseline (see Section 3.1), we conducted planned pairwise permutation t-tests focusing on the number of SCRs. These analyses yielded a significantly lower number of SCRs during bidirectional sharing (BS) (M BS = 6.94 ± 8.11) compared to face-to-face interaction without biosignal sharing (F2F) (M F2F = 11.16±11.75, t(31) = -2.31, p = .029), as well as compared to the unidirectional Sharing role (S) (M S = 10.69 ± 12.76, t(31) = -2.22, p = .017). No significant differences were observed between BS and unidirectional Observing (O).

3.3. Experiment-related changes on self-report measures

Participants reported a significant increase in perceived interpersonal closeness (IOS) with their experiment partner after completing the experiment (M pre = 0.219 ± 0.410, M post = 0.969 ± 0.740, t(31) = 6.313, p < .001). No other questionnaire items showed significant differences before and after the experiment.

3.4. Correlations

Significant correlations are summarized in Figure 3 and explained below in detail.

3.4.1. Sympathetic activation is associated with interpersonal closeness and perceived scrutiny

We observed two significant correlations involving face-to-face-normalized changes in the number of SCRs across the biosignal sharing conditions (Fig. 3A-B). All remaining correlations between the physiological metrics and questionnaire measures were non-significant after FDR correction (p FDR > .05). First, the change in the number of SCRs during the bidirectional sharing (BS) condition (relative to F2F) was negatively correlated with experiment-induced changes in IOS ratings (r S = -0.497, p FDR = .047) (Fig. 3A). In other words, a stronger reduction in sympathetic activation during BS was associated with a greater increase in the perceived closeness after the experiment. In addition, the number of SCRs during the BS condition (relative to F2F) was positively associated with subjective ratings of how often participants perceived their partner to be looking at their biosignals (r S = 0.544, p FDR = .039) (Fig. 3B).

3.4.2. Perceived scrutiny is associated with reduced interpersonal closeness

We found that participants' perception of how much their partner observed their biosignals was positively correlated with how much they reported observing their partner's signals (r S = 0.632, p FDR = .004), and negatively correlated with increases in IOS scores (i.e., perceived interpersonal closeness) after the experiment (r S = -0.662, p FDR = .001) (see Fig. 3C-D).

In addition, discomfort about sharing one's biosignals due to exposure concerns (r S = 0.745, p FDR < .001), as well as the concern of making the partner uncomfortable when observing their biosignals (r S = 0.541, p FDR = .026), were both positively correlated with the discomfort experienced when the partner observed one's biosignals during the experiment (see Fig. 3E-F).

4. Discussion

Our findings highlight that sharing physiological signals during social interaction modulates sympathetic arousal and can influence perceived interpersonal closeness. Specifically, bidirectional biosignal sharing (BS) was associated with reduced skin conductance level (SCL) compared with face-to-face interaction without sharing (F2F) and both unidirectional sharing roles. Similarly, the number of skin conductance responses (SCRs) was significantly reduced during bidirectional biosignal sharing (BS) compared with both face-to-face interaction without sharing (F2F) and the unidirectional sharing role (S). Moreover, this reduction in arousal was functionally meaningful: greater reductions in SCRs during bidirectional sharing (relative to F2F) were associated with larger increases in perceived interpersonal closeness. These results extend prior qualitative work by providing quantitative physiological evidence that the reciprocity of biosignal sharing is an important determinant of its psychophysiological impact.

4.1. Audience effects and reciprocity in biosignal sharing

The presence or perceived presence of an observer typically elicits heightened sympathetic arousal, self-awareness, and impression-management processes (Bond, 1982; Cottrell et al., 1968; Hamilton and Lind, 2016; Yoshie et al., 2016; Zajonc, 1965). These effects are shaped by social comparison and they arise from efforts to appear competent or avoid disapproval, as well as by fluctuations in cognitive capacity due to the attentional conflict induced by the presence of others (Aiello and Douthitt, 2001; Guerin, 1993). Such audience effects likely account for the general elevation in sympathetic activation observed across all interactive conditions in our study, relative to the baseline video-watching periods where mutual observation was not feasible.

Research on biosignal sharing suggests that making covert physiological information externally available may further amplify these audience effects. Since physiological signals are involuntary and difficult to control, sharing them can feel like losing autonomy over what is revealed about one's internal states (Feijt et al., 2023; Slovák et al., 2012b). In this context, unidirectional sharing configurations are often perceived as intrusive, potentially reinforcing audience effects. Conversely, reciprocal sharing may mitigate such concerns, as mutual exposure restores a sense of fairness and shared vulnerability (Feijt et al., 2023). This framework aligns with our main finding of reduced sympathetic arousal during bidirectional exchange, and with the observed association between lower sympathetic activation in BS (relative to F2F) and increased perceived interpersonal closeness.

Under this framework, one might expect unidirectional sharing to elicit stronger sympathetic activation than F2F interaction, given the heightened asymmetry of exposure. However, our data did not support this pattern. A plausible explanation is that the F2F condition, despite lacking biosignal sharing, is among the strongest possible evaluative potentials in day-to-day social interactions. Indeed, participants informally reported during debriefing that the F2F condition was perceived as the most stressful and effortful part of the experiment. Thus, the elevated arousal inherent to this social-interaction "baseline" could have created a ceiling effect, limiting the detectability of additional audience effects introduced by unidirectional sharing.

4.2. Attention allocation, exposure, and scrutiny in biosignal sharing

Beyond general audience effects, the reduction in arousal during bidirectional sharing may be influenced by shifts in visual attention. Direct eye contact is a strong social cue that consistently increases sympathetic arousal (Helminen et al., 2011; Myllyneva and Hietanen, 2015). During face-to-face and the unidirectional sharing role, participants lacked alternative visual anchors and were likely to sustain continuous gaze toward each other, maintaining elevated arousal. In contrast, bidirectional sharing introduced an alternative visual element (the biosignal display), which could have intermittently diverted gaze and reduced the duration and frequency of mutual eye contact, thereby decreasing arousal. Crucially, however, this reduction in arousal cannot be attributed solely to the screen acting as a visual distraction. No comparable reduction was found in the observing-only condition, where participants also viewed the display of their partner's biosignals. This observation challenges the purely attentional-diversion account. Rather than attributing the observed effects solely to shifts in visual focus or mutual gaze, our findings suggest that attention-related processes may interact with the social context in which biosignals are shared. However, because attention allocation was not measured objectively, further research is needed to clarify how gaze behavior and attentional deployment contribute to the biosignalsharing effects reported here (see Section 4.3).

Our correlational findings provide further insight into how perceived exposure and observation relate to participants' psychophysiological and subjective experiences during biosignal sharing. Specifically, the number of SCRs during bidirectional sharing (relative to face-to-face interaction) was positively associated with how often participants felt their partner was looking at their biosignals, indicating that higher sympathetic activation co-occurred with a stronger subjective perception of being observed. This association is consistent with prior accounts linking perceived monitoring to heightened self-awareness and evaluative concern, previously described as audience effects (Bond, 1982; Cottrell et al., 1968; Hamilton and Lind, 2016; Yoshie et al., 2016; Zajonc, 1965).

In addition, participants' exposure-related discomfort was positively associated with greater unease when their own biosignals were being viewed. Importantly, this discomfort was paralleled by increased concern about causing discomfort when monitoring a partner's biosignals. This pattern points to a reciprocal awareness of vulnerability, whereby concerns about being watched were mirrored by concerns about scrutinizing others. Along similar lines, participants' perception of having their biosignals observed was positively associated with how much they reported looking at their partner's signals, suggesting mutual monitoring dynamics and attunement to the other person's behavior, rather than a purely unidirectional focus on exposure.

Furthermore, a stronger perceived sense of being observed was associated with smaller increases in perceived interpersonal closeness following the experiment. Taken together, these associations suggest that heightened monitoring and self-focused awareness may co-occur with elevated sympathetic arousal, potentially hindering closeness formation. This pattern aligns with prior work indicating that comfort with physiological transparency depends on perceived reciprocity, control, and contextual safety, rather than on access to physiological information per se (Feijt et al., 2023; Slovák et al., 2012b; Wikström et al., 2021).

4.3. Limitations and future directions

Our results should be interpreted in light of some limitations. First, regarding visual attention processes, our questionnaire items captured participants' subjective perceptions of observing the partner or their biosignals, and of having one's own biosignals observed, but did not objectively quantify visual attention. Consequently, interpretations concerning the specific role of eye contact and attentional diversion (e.g., monitoring the biosignal display) warrant further investigation. Future work should explicitly measure attention allocation using objective methods such as eye tracking, electrooculography, or electrophysiological indices of visual engagement (e.g., SSVEPs). Combining such measures with analytic approaches, such as Granger causality or other directed connectivity estimators, could help elucidate the temporal interplay between attentional shifts and autonomic regulation, and clarify whether moment-to-moment gaze dynamics underlie the physiological effects observed during bidirectional sharing.

Second, the effects of biosignal sharing are likely shaped by the social context and relationship between interacting individuals. Our sample consisted of strangers engaged in a minimal, nonverbal face-to-face interaction, a setting that may heighten social-evaluative awareness. Prior work suggests that the meaning of biosignal sharing varies with relational closeness and situational framing (Feijt et al., 2023; Janssen, 2012; Slovák et al., 2012b; Stepanova et al., 2022). Future studies should therefore examine how these dynamics unfold in more naturalistic interactions and across different social bonds (e.g., friends or romantic partners) and social contexts (e.g., cooperative vs. evaluative).

Third, our implementation displayed minimally pre-processed biosignals to isolate the fundamental effects of sharing. However, interface design can strongly modulate social interpretation (Moge et al., 2022). Future research should systematically vary the modality, aesthetics, and framing of the biosignal display to understand how design choices might further shape psychophysiological responses.

Notably, we did not observe significant experimental or biosignal-sharing effects for the ECG-derived RMSSD metric. Thus, while biosignal sharing modulated sympathetic arousal as indexed by EDA-derived measures, it did not produce detectable changes in parasympathetic activity as reflected by this standard HRV measure. Previous evidence has suggested that HRV during first-time face-to-face encounters is often characterized by an initial drop that remains stable throughout the interaction, showing no statistical variation across different interaction contexts (Rudnicki et al., 2019). Moreover, it has been suggested that standard HRV metrics may lack sufficient temporal resolution to capture the non-stationary cardiac dynamics characteristic of brief social or cognitive tasks (Candia-Rivera et al., 2025). Future studies employing ECG feature estimators with higher time resolution may therefore provide more accurate insights into ongoing autonomic dynamics and the parasympathetic contributions during biosignal sharing.

Finally, while we focused on individual physiological responses, social interaction is inherently dyadic. Interpersonal EDA interdependencies have been linked to shared attention, attunement, and social connection (Palumbo et al., 2017; Prochazkova et al., 2022). Building on this work, future studies should examine how partners' physiological signals covary during biosignal sharing. Investigating how autonomic coupling unfolds across different sharing configurations, and how it relates to objective measures of attentional deployment, may clarify whether biosignal sharing influences physiological interdependencies, and whether these interdependencies contribute to the changes in perceived closeness observed in our study. 21

5. Conclusions

Our study provides quantitative evidence that biosignal sharing influences both autonomic regulation and perceived interpersonal closeness. Specifically, we show that bidirectional biosignal sharing significantly reduces sympathetic arousal relative to both face-to-face interaction without sharing and the unidirectional sharing role. Importantly, this physiological attenuation was socially meaningful: participants exhibiting larger reductions in sympathetic activation also reported greater increases in perceived interpersonal closeness. Our findings further point to an interplay between exposure, perceived scrutiny, and empathyrelated processes in shaping participants' experiences during biosignal sharing. Together, these results highlight reciprocity as a key variable shaping the autonomic and relational impact of biosignal sharing and provide quantitatively grounded insights for the design of social computing systems and computermediated interactions.
