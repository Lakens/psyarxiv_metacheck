Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Background: Parental and adolescent emotion regulation are associated, yet the role of parental reflective functioning (PRF) as a relational pathway linking them remains largely unexamined, particularly among parents of youth with serious mental health challenges.

Methods: Parents (N = 372) of adolescents (ages 9-19) with serious mental health challenges reported on their own emotion regulation within the parentadolescent relationship, PRF, and their adolescent's emotion regulation. Structural equation modelling with bootstrapped indirect effects examined direct and indirect associations between parental emotion regulation (dysregulation, suppression), PRF facets (prementalizing, certainty about mental states, and interest and curiosity in mental states), and adolescent emotion regulation (dysregulation, suppression).

Results: Parental dysregulation was positively associated with adolescent dysregulation and suppression, whereas parental suppression showed no direct associations with adolescent outcomes. All three PRF facets were associated with adolescent emotion regulation: greater prementalizing with greater youth dysregulation; greater certainty with lower youth dysregulation and suppression; and greater interest and curiosity unexpectedly with greater youth dysregulation and suppression. Three indirect associations emerged: parental dysregulation was associated with adolescent dysregulation through higher prementalizing and, separately, through lower certainty about mental states, and parental suppression was associated with lower adolescent suppression through lower interest and curiosity.

Conclusions: Parental dysregulation was associated with adolescent emotion regulation in this clinical sample, relating to adolescent dysregulation through both prementalizing and certainty. Less consistent with prior research, interest and curiosity was associated with poorer adolescent emotion regulation, raising the possibility that this facet relates differently in clinical, adolescent populations than in the community samples of younger children where it has typically been studied. These findings suggest PRF facets may represent differentiated intervention targets, and highlight the need for longitudinal, multi-informant research to establish temporal ordering.

These findings suggest parental dysregulation and suppression may be linked to youth mental health, in part, through relational pathways. However, less is known about which parent-level relational processes may help explain associations between parental and adolescent emotion regulation.

One parental cognitive-affective capacity that may help explain these links is parental reflective functioning (PRF), defined as the parent's ability to hold in mind both their own and their child's mental states within the caregiving relationship (Luyten, Nijssens, et al., 2017). PRF reflects an affectively attuned, relational process through which parents recognize their child as a psychological agent with an internal experience that is distinct from, yet meaningfully connected to, their own (Fonagy et al., 2016). PRF is commonly operationalized as multidimensional, with three facets: prementalizing (non-mentalizing or distorted attributions about the child's mental states), certainty about mental states (perceived confidence in understanding the child's mental states), and interest and curiosity in mental states (active motivation to consider and explore the child's mental states) (Luyten, Mayes, et al., 2017). Prementalizing indexes a breakdown of mentalizing, such that elevations indicate mentalizing impairment. Certainty reflects the parent's stance toward the inherent opacity of mental states. Insufficient certainty may indicate disengagement from the child's mental states, whereas excessive certainty may indicate intrusive overattributing that denies the child's psychological separateness (Luyten, Mayes, et al., 2017; Luyten et al., 2020). Interest and curiosity, in contrast, captures the exploratory openness that most directly embodies the mentalizing stance, and is the facet most consistently theorized as adaptive, although excessive curiosity may likewise become intrusive rather than attuned (Luyten, Mayes, et al., 2017).

Empirical evidence examining PRF with child outcomes suggests that higher prementalizing is indeed associated with greater child regulatory difficulty, whereas higher certainty and interest and curiosity have been associated with better child emotional adjustment, though this evidence derives largely from studies of young children (Álvarez et al., 2022; Ghanbari et al., 2023; Keleynikov et al., 2024; Misailidi et al., 2025; Zreik et al., 2025). These distinctions may operate differently in adolescence, when youths' mental states become increasingly private and autonomous (Herd et al., 2020; Morris et al., 2017). Confident parental claims to know the adolescent's mind may no longer reflect attunement, whereas genuinely curious, non-presumptive interest may help parents understand adolescents' mental states. Despite this, research examining PRF in relation to adolescent emotion regulation remains limited, with existing work focused primarily on community samples or other outcomes, such as adolescent identity development and reflective functioning (Fiore et al., 2025; Park & Song, 2024). PRF may also be especially relevant to parental emotion regulation because reflective functioning is supported by parents' capacity to remain engaged and regulated during emotionally charged caregiving interactions. According to the biobehavioural switch model, heightened emotional arousal shifts parents from controlled, reflective mentalizing toward automatic, non-mentalizing modes of responding (Fonagy & Luyten, 2009; Luyten & Fonagy, 2015). Dysregulation may thus disrupt reflective mentalizing, favouring the automatic, non-mentalizing processing reflected in higher prementalizing. Suppression may operate differently, as an effortful, response-focused strategy; it directs attention inward toward inhibiting one's own emotional display, leaving less available for the outward, exploratory attention to the child's mental states that defines interest and curiosity, and may manifest in reduced parental responsiveness and warmth during interactions (Gross & Cassidy, 2019; Karnilowicz et al., 2019). When the caregiving environment is characterized by elevated relational stress, such as that involving adolescents with serious mental health challenges, heightened parent emotional reactivity and stress may further undermine PRF (Dollberg & Hanetz-Gamliel, 2023; Keleynikov et al., 2024).

Extant research provides emerging support for this approach. Among a community sample of mothers, emotion regulation difficulties and expressive suppression were associated with higher prementalizing, poorer emotional awareness was associated with lower interest and curiosity, and difficulty engaging in goal-directed behaviour was associated with lower certainty about the child's mental states (Schultheis et al., 2019). Moreover, two studies have examined PRF as a potential explanatory process linking parental emotional functioning to child outcomes. In a community sample of parents of school-age children, parent emotion regulation difficulties were associated with child behavioural problems through higher prementalizing, but not through certainty or interest and curiosity (Wang, 2022). In a separate community sample of parents of preschoolers, prementalizing mediated the association between parental depressive symptoms and poorer child emotion regulation, with the direct association no longer significant once prementalizing was included; certainty moderated this association, whereas interest and curiosity did not (Keleynikov et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings suggest that PRF, particularly prementalizing, may be an important parent-level relational process linking parental emotional difficulties with child regulatory and mental health outcomes. Whether this facet-specific patterning holds, however, has not been examined in adolescents or in clinical samples, where these processes may operate differently than in community samples of younger children.

Building on this framework, the present study examined whether PRF operates as a relational link in the association between parental and adolescent emotion regulation in a sample of adolescents with serious mental health challenges. Consistent with relational models of emotion regulation development (Paley & Hajal, 2022; Vernon & Moretti, 2024), we hypothesized that parental dysregulation and suppression would be associated with adolescent emotion regulation. We further hypothesized that prementalizing would be associated with greater adolescent dysregulation and suppression, while certainty and interest and curiosity would be associated with lower adolescent dysregulation and suppression, consistent with evidence from samples of younger children (Álvarez et al., 2022; Ghanbari et al., 2023). We further anticipated that parental dysregulation would be positively associated with prementalizing, reflecting the arousal-driven shift from controlled to automatic mentalizing (Fonagy & Luyten, 2009; Luyten & Fonagy, 2015), and negatively associated with certainty and interest and curiosity. We further hypothesized that parental suppression would be positively associated with prementalizing and associated with decreased certainty and interest and curiosity, reflecting the attentional cost of monitoring one's own emotional display (Gross & Cassidy, 2019). Centrally, we hypothesized that prementalizing would carry indirect associations between parental and adolescent emotion regulation, consistent with its role as the facet through which parental emotional difficulties have been most associated with child outcomes (Keleynikov et al., 2024; Wang, 2022), with indirect effects through certainty and interest and curiosity examined on an exploratory basis.

Method Participants & Procedure

Participants were 372 parents and caregivers of adolescents enrolled at baseline in a longitudinal study evaluating Connect, a 10-session attachment-based group parenting intervention for caregivers of adolescents with significant socialemotional and behavioural difficulties (Moretti & Obsuth, 2009). Parents were predominantly female (80.2%; M age = 46.25, SD = 8.07) and were mostly birth parents (79.9%), followed by kinship caregivers (5.4%), adoptive parents (5.4%), foster parents (5.2%), and step-parents (2.7%). Youth ranged in age from 9 to 19 years (M = 13.69, SD = 2.28), and 62.1% were female. Both parents and youth were primarily Caucasian (70.4% and 73.4%, respectively), with Indigenous, East Asian, and South Asian representation. Half of parents (50.1%) reported financial resources as barely enough or insufficient to meet basic needs. Full demographic information is presented in Appendix A. Parents were referred through mental health agencies, schools, and hospitals, and data were collected between 2022 and 2023. Exclusion criteria included acute mental health concerns (e.g., psychosis, severe depression, suicidality) or low intellectual functioning. Parents provided informed consent and completed an online or paper-based questionnaire battery assessing parental emotion regulation, PRF, and parent-reported youth emotion regulation. All procedures were approved by the Simon Fraser University Office of Research Ethics (REB # 30000990).

Measures

Parental Reflective Functioning. PRF was assessed using the Parental Reflective Functioning Questionnaire -Adolescent Version (PRFQ-A; adapted from Luyten, Mayes, et al., 2017), a self-report measure of parents' capacity to recognize and interpret their adolescent's mental states. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale and load onto three subscales: prementalizing, certainty about mental states, and interest and curiosity in mental states. Prementalizing reflects a nonmentalizing stance characterized by rigid or distorted attributions about the adolescent's mental states, with higher scores indicating greater impairment (e.g., "My child sometimes gets ill to keep me from doing what I want to do"). Certainty reflects the degree of confidence with which parents perceive themselves to understand the adolescent's mental states (e.g., "I always know why my child acts the way he or she does"). Interest and curiosity captures the extent to which parents are motivated to explore and reflect on the adolescent's thoughts and feelings (e.g., "I wonder a lot about what my child is thinking and feeling"). Although certainty and interest and curiosity are sometimes theorized as optimalrange constructs, the empirical evidence for curvilinear associations is mixed, and prior work has consistently modelled these dimensions linearly across samples (Kamza et al., 2024; Luyten, Mayes, et al., 2017; Park & Song, 2024; De Roo et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2022). Consistent with this, PRFQ-A subscales were modelled as linear in the present study. Notably, recent validation work has indicated that the interest and curiosity subscale warrants context-sensitive interpretation in at-risk samples, with very high scores associated with reduced observed maternal sensitivity and poor psychometric differentiation at the upper end of the scale (Egmose et al., 2024; Stuart et al., 2025).

Psychometric research supports the three-factor structure of the PRFQ across community and clinical samples and its convergent validity with interviewbased measures of parental mentalization (Anis et al., 2020; Edler et al., 2023; Kamza et al., 2024; De Roo et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2022). To evaluate the factor structure in the present sample, exploratory structural equation modelling (ESEM) was conducted. The initial 18-item model demonstrated acceptable fit (RMSEA = .057, 90% CI [.047, .068], CFI = .961, TLI = .942, SRMR = .031); however, three items (items 7, 14, and 18) showed factor loadings below the accepted threshold of .30 and were removed, and a residual correlation was added between two conceptually and linguistically similar interest and curiosity items (items 6 and 9; Edler et al., 2023). Following these modifications, model fit was excellent (RMSEA = .047, 90% CI [.032, .061], CFI = .983, TLI = .972, SRMR = .024), with all remaining items loading on their intended factors (λ = .45-.81; see Appendix B). The final analytic solution comprised 15 items. Internal consistency in the present sample was acceptable across subscales (α = .65-.74).

Parental and Adolescent Emotion Regulation.

The 12-item Affect Regulation Checklist (ARC; Moretti, 2003) was used and comprises three factors measuring emotion dysregulation, suppression and reflection. The present study focused on the dysregulation and suppression subscales, which have demonstrated associations with mental health problems (Goulter et al., 2022; Vernon & Moretti, 2024). The dysregulation subscale assesses difficulties regulating distressing emotions (e.g., "I have a hard time controlling my feelings"; "I find it very hard to calm down when upset"), while the suppression subscale captures attempts to avoid or control emotion through distraction or inhibition (e.g., "I keep my feelings to myself"; "I try to do other things to keep my mind off how I feel"). The factor structure, convergent validity, and reliability of this scale have been documented previously (e.g., Goulter et al., 2022; Moretti et al., 2015; Vernon & Moretti, 2024; Santos et al., 2025). The ARC has also been adapted to assess parent emotional regulation as a general trait (ARC-P); parent emotion regulation in their relationship with a specified child (ARC-PR); and parents' rating of their child's emotion regulation (ARC-PY). A youth self-report version has also been developed (ARC-Y). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from not like me/my youth to a lot like me/my youth, with higher scores indicating greater dysregulation and suppression. In the current study, parents completed an adapted version of the ARC-PR, rating their own emotion regulation in their relationship with their child. They also completed the ARC-PY, rating their child's emotion regulation. Cronbach's alpha values were .89 and .77 for parental dysregulation and suppression, and .85 and .72 for youth dysregulation and suppression, respectively.

Analytic Approach

Before model estimation, data were screened for normality and outliers. Univariate normality was evaluated using skewness (±2) and kurtosis (±7) criteria (West et al., 1995). Multivariate outliers were assessed using Mahalanobis and Cook's distances. Missing data were addressed using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) (Enders, 2022), such that all 372 cases contributed to model estimation; variable-level ns reported in Table 1 range from 345 to 359 due to item-level missingness. Hypotheses were tested using a path model within a structural equation modelling (SEM) framework (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). The model examined direct effects as well as whether PRF subscales accounted for associations between parental emotion regulation (dysregulation, suppression) and adolescent emotion regulation (dysregulation, suppression). Youth age was included as a covariate. Indirect associations were evaluated using 10,000 bootstrap resamples, with significance determined by 95% confidence intervals that excluded zero.

Discussion

This study examined direct and indirect associations among parental emotion regulation, PRF, and adolescent emotion regulation among parents of adolescents with serious mental health challenges. Parental dysregulation was indirectly associated with adolescent dysregulation through both higher prementalizing and lower certainty about the adolescent's mental states, whereas parental suppression was indirectly associated with lower adolescent suppression through lower interest and curiosity. Contrary to hypotheses, interest and curiosity was associated with greater, rather than lower, adolescent dysregulation and suppression. Together, these findings suggest that distinct forms of parental regulatory difficulty may relate to adolescent emotion regulation through different facets of parental mentalizing. Extending prior work conducted largely with younger children in community samples, this pattern underscores the importance of conceptualizing both emotion regulation and mentalizing as multidimensional processes within the parent-adolescent relationship (Paley & Hajal, 2022; Vernon & Moretti, 2024).

Among direct associations, parental dysregulation was associated with both greater adolescent dysregulation and suppression, consistent with metaanalytic evidence linking parental emotion dysregulation to child and adolescent regulatory difficulties (Lin et al., 2024; Zimmer-Gembeck et al., 2022). Parental suppression, by contrast, showed no significant direct association with adolescent emotion regulation, suggesting that suppression, as a less outwardly expressed form of regulatory difficulty, may relate to adolescent emotion regulation less directly than dysregulation in this sample (Vernon & Moretti, 2024). PRF facets were also directly associated with adolescent emotion regulation. Prementalizing was related to greater adolescent dysregulation, consistent with previous evidence linking non-mentalizing parental attributions to poorer child regulatory functioning (Fiore et al., 2025; Keleynikov et al., 2024; Misailidi et al., 2025; Zreik et al., 2025). Certainty about mental states was negatively associated with adolescent dysregulation and suppression, consistent with research linking greater certainty to more adaptive child socioemotional functioning (Keleynikov et al., 2024; Madsen et al., 2024). Because certainty in this sample concentrated below the scale midpoint, these associations may reflect movement away from hypomentalizing, rather than a uniform benefit of greater certainty, supporting conceptualizations of certainty as adaptive at moderate levels (Fiore et al., 2025; Madsen et al., 2024).

Contrary to predictions and findings from community samples of parents of younger children (Álvarez et al., 2022; Ghanbari et al., 2023), interest and curiosity was associated with greater adolescent dysregulation and suppression. In this clinical adolescent sample, elevated interest and curiosity may, in some circumstances, reflect intrusive over-attention to mental states rather than the attuned engagement this facet is intended to capture, as has been theorized for excessive curiosity (Luyten, Mayes, et al., 2017). Consistent with this, interest and curiosity was concentrated near the upper end of the scale (M = 5.61), and higher maternal interest and curiosity has been associated with lower observed sensitivity (Egmose et al., 2024). A related possibility concerns what the subscale captures. Items index how much parents report wondering about their adolescent's thoughts and feelings (e.g., "I wonder a lot about what my child is thinking and feeling"), which reflects motivation to think about the adolescent's mind rather than the attunement of that thinking. Among parents of adolescents with serious mental health challenges, frequent wondering may reflect worry or preoccupation rather than open, non-presumptive curiosity. Because adolescents' mental states become increasingly private (Herd et al., 2020; Morris et al., 2017), sustained parental attention to them may also be experienced as intrusive. Alternatively, parents may become more curious as their adolescent's mental states become harder to read during periods of dysregulation or withdrawal, consistent with evidence that parental mentalizing shifts in response to child difficulties (Malcorps et al., 2024). Under this account, curiosity may be a response to adolescent difficulty rather than a contributor to it. The findings for certainty and curiosity also ran opposite to the possibility raised in the introduction that certainty might be less adaptive and curiosity more adaptive in adolescence. Here, greater certainty, which was low on average, was associated with better regulation, and greater curiosity, which was high on average, was associated with poorer regulation.I Parental dysregulation was also directly associated with both greater prementalizing and lower certainty about the adolescent's mental states, and each of these facets, in turn, carried a significant indirect association with adolescent dysregulation. This pattern is consistent with the biobehavioural switch model, in which heightened affective arousal shifts parents from controlled, reflective mentalizing toward more automatic, non-mentalizing modes of processing (Fonagy & Luyten, 2009; Luyten & Fonagy, 2015), and with evidence that parental regulatory difficulties are associated with greater prementalizing and reduced confidence in understanding children's mental states (Schultheis et al., 2019). In caregiving contexts characterized by sustained relational strain, such as those involving adolescents with serious mental health challenges, this switch may be chronically rather than transiently activated (Dollberg & Hanetz-Gamliel, 2023). Accordingly, parents who feel emotionally flooded within the relationship may be more likely to make distorted, non-mentalizing attributions while also feeling less able to make coherent sense of their adolescent's mental states, with both difficulties potentially constraining attuned responses when coregulation is most needed. These findings extend prior work in which prementalizing mediated associations between parental emotional difficulties and child outcomes (Keleynikov et al., 2024; Wang, 2022). Whereas certainty has previously been examined primarily as a moderator of the association between parental distress and child adjustment (Keleynikov et al., 2024), the present findings may identify certainty as an additional relational pathway through which parental emotion regulation associates with adolescent regulation.

Although parental suppression was most strongly associated with prementalizing among the PRF facets, prementalizing was unrelated to adolescent suppression, and the corresponding indirect association was not significant. The only significant indirect association with adolescent suppression instead involved interest and curiosity. Parental suppression was associated with lower interest and curiosity, consistent with conceptualizing suppression as an effortful, response-focused strategy that directs attentional resources inward toward monitoring and inhibiting one's own emotional expression (Gross & Cassidy, 2019; Karnilowicz et al., 2019). However, greater interest and curiosity was also associated with greater adolescent suppression, yielding a negative indirect association whereby higher parental suppression was associated with lower adolescent suppression through lower interest and curiosity. This pattern runs counter to, and therefore does not explain, the positive bivariate association between parent and adolescent suppression. Instead, if elevated interest and curiosity reflected intrusive over-attention, reduced curiosity among more suppressive parents may have corresponded to less intrusive parenting, which could plausibly relate to lower adolescent suppression. Alternatively, adolescents who suppress less may elicit less parental curiosity, or both processes may reflect broader strain within the parent-adolescent relationship. As parental intrusiveness and adolescents' perceptions of parental attention were not assessed, these interpretations remain speculative and future research should continue to explore these associations.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study is not without limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not establish temporal ordering; associations may equally reflect adolescent regulatory difficulties shaping parental regulation and reflective functioning, and longitudinal designs are needed to clarify their direction. All variables were assessed via parent report, raising the possibility that shared method variance inflated associations and limiting the extent to which findings reflect relational processes versus parent perception biases. This concern is particularly relevant for parent-reported adolescent emotion regulation, as parents higher in prementalizing may also perceive and report their adolescents' regulation less accurately. Multi-informant approaches, including adolescent selfreport and observational measures, would strengthen confidence in these findings. Further, whether PRFQ facets, particularly interest and curiosity, function equivalently for parents of adolescents and parents of young children requires further psychometric investigation. Finally, this sample limits generalizability, as it represents a population in which these processes are likely to be most pronounced; it remains unclear whether similar patterns would emerge in community samples or at different developmental stages.

Conclusion

This study's central finding is that parental dysregulation was associated with adolescent dysregulation through both prementalizing and certainty, consistent with a shift toward more automatic, less reflective processing under high arousal. A second, unanticipated finding was that interest and curiosity was associated with greater adolescent dysregulation and suppression, and that a significant indirect path linked parental suppression to lower adolescent suppression through reduced curiosity. These associations raise questions about whether interest and curiosity carries the same adaptive meaning in clinical, adolescent populations as it does in community samples of younger children. If replicated longitudinally, these findings may carry clinical implications: parents presenting with high-arousal dysregulation may benefit most from support in sustaining reflective attributions under stress, and interventions that cultivate parental curiosity may need to attend to whether that curiosity is attuned to the adolescent's experience rather than intrusive. More broadly, this research may help refine which mentalizing processes are targeted in interventions for families navigating relational strain (Georg et al., 2023; Gervinskaitė-Paulaitienė et al., 2024; Moretti et al., 2015; Zayde et al., 2023).

Adolescent capacity to regulate emotional arousal, mood, and physiological states is foundational for social-emotional adjustment, and difficulties in this capacity are implicated in the development and maintenance of psychopathology (Cavicchioli et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024). Although emotion regulation is often conceptualized as an individual capacity, its development is supported relationally through parental coregulation that helps youth interpret, tolerate, and modulate emotional experiences (Hajal & Paley, 2020). During adolescence, this coregulatory process unfolds alongside rapid adolescent social-emotional and neurobiological change, heightened sensitivity to social interactions and emotional experiences, and increasing demands for autonomy (Arslan et al., 2024; Meredith & Silvers, 2024). These changes can lead to increased conflict in the parent-child relationship and place substantial strain on parents' ability to regulate their own emotions within the relationship (Arslan et al., 2024; Meredith & Silvers, 2024). This may be particularly challenging among families of adolescents with serious mental health challenges, where emotional exchanges are more frequent, intense, and taxing (Zimmer-Gembeck et al., 2022). Accordingly, understanding adolescent emotion regulation requires attention not only to adolescent's regulatory capacities, but also to parents' emotion regulation within the caregiving relationship.

Literature has largely characterized parental emotion regulation as a generalized, trait-like disposition, an approach that indexes whether parents regulate emotion effectively in general but not how they regulate within specific caregiving relationships. While this work has been instrumental in establishing robust associations between caregiver emotion regulation and youth socialemotional adjustment across development, this measurement approach may limit insight into the specificity and complexity of the relational process (Fombouchet et al., 2023; Goagoses et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024). Parental emotion regulation is also not a unitary construct. Facets such as dysregulation, affective arousal or expression that disrupts goal-directed tasks, and suppression, inhibition or concealment of emotional expression once emotion has been activated, represent distinct forms of regulatory difficulty (Blader et al., 2025; Gross & Cassidy, 2024; Thompson, 2019). Because dysregulation is more outwardly expressed and suppression is, by definition, less visible, these facets may relate to youth outcomes through different pathways. Dysregulation may be more directly observable within parent-adolescent interactions, whereas suppression may operate less directly, through reduced parental engagement, emotional openness, or availability. Consistent with this distinction, among a sample of youth with serious mental health challenges, parental dysregulation measured within the parent-child relationship was associated with greater youth internalizing and externalizing problems both directly and indirectly through youth attachment.
