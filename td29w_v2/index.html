How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Abstract

Delaying the start of tasks with deadlines is a widespread phenomenon that occurs across multiple domains. While delaying tasks can be maladaptive, here we propose that, in some situations, it results from an optimal time-management strategy. We propose a theoretical model of the strategic delay of the initiation of tasks with deadlines that relies on an unavoidable aspect of any projection into the future, namely, that the time it takes to complete a task is variable. This fundamental assumption distinguishes our theory from alternative theoretical accounts that invoke specific structures of preferences or costs to elucidate the occurrence of (adaptive or maladaptive) task-initiation delays. Our model captures the basic features of strategic delay and predicts a phenomenon that we demonstrate empirically, the 'farther-earlier' effect: decision-makers who live farther to their destinations (e.g., schools or offices) tend to arrive earlier than those who live closer. The same theory explains the widely documented tendency to repeatedly delay the initiation of a task (e.g., drafting a grant application) as the deadline for the completion of the task approaches. Our framework thus puts forth a normative theory in which various manifestations of strategic delay reflect an optimal adaptation to environmental statistics.

Introduction

Delaying the start of a task bound by a deadline is a pervasive behavioural phenomenon that occurs in diverse forms and in multiple domains: when leaving home to reach the office or the airport, booking a flight, or drafting a grant. In all these scenarios, people often delay the initiation of the task until the 'last moment'. Moreover, as deadlines approach, people often reevaluate their choices and end up postponing the initiation of tasks, and thereby often end up completing their tasks prohibitively close to the deadlines (Steel, 2007; Steel et al., 2018).

Delaying task initiation appears as puzzling because it usually grants only a small benefit but might entail a great cost (Ellis & Knaus, 1977; Harriott & Ferrari, 1996; Silver, 1974; Steel, 2007). For this reason, the tendency to delay task initiation has often been associated with procrastination and in turn to maladaptive psychological mechanisms such as avoidance behaviour, self-regulation failure, and motivational deficits. For example, chronic procrastination is typically characterized as a persistent tendency to delay intended actions despite anticipated negative consequences, and is often associated with subjective anxiety and poor self-efficacy (Sirois, 2004; Solomon & Rothblum, 1984; Steel, 2007).

However, not all instances of task-initiation delays reflect maladaptive procrastination. Previous work in economics and psychology has long recognized that delays in carrying out tasks can sometimes be rational-work that appears under the rubrics of "(optimal) time management and planning" and "active" or "strategic delay." Here, delays are viewed as resulting from adaptive strategies that account for a rational cost-benefit trade-off in time management (Chun Chu & Choi, 2005; Corkin et al., 2011; Klingsieck, 2013). George Stigler tersely summarized the corresponding intuition in his famous quip: "If you never miss a plane, you're spending too much time at the airport" (Ellenberg, 2015).

Concretely, strategic delay has been proposed to arise from a trade-off between two costs: in Stigler's example, the cost of arriving at the airport too early and the cost of missing a flight (Ellenberg, 2015). It is then rational to arrive as late as possible, accepting a minimal risk of missing a flight in order to avoid the cost of time wasted at the airport. Other theoretical accounts propose instead that delaying task initiation can be optimal under specific structures of preference or cost that make acting immediately less attractive than acting later. One approach, for example, suggests that decision makers (DMs) delay the initiation of a task because they undervalue its later outcome as a result of temporal discounting; the initiation time then results from successive re-evaluations as the deadline approaches (Durán-Hormazábal & Tirachini, 2016). A related perspective focuses on differential discounting of effort and reward: DMs delay task initiation because they discount their future effort more than their future reward, hence procrastinating on a task appears to reduce effort more than reward (Le Bouc & Pessiglione, 2022). Another effort-related account proposes an asymmetric salience of present and future costs: the immediate cost of executing a task is perceived more vividly than the future cost of executing it under time pressure (Akerlof, 1991). Finally, urgency-based accounts propose that DMs delay task initiation because the pressure to complete a task (e.g., register to a conference) increases as the inverse of the remaining time to the deadline (Alfi et al., 2007), or because the 'urgency' to complete a task reaches a triggering threshold only when a deadline is close enough (Durakiewicz, 2016; Konig & Kleinmann, 2005).

In all these approaches, the rationality of delaying task initiation under a deadline follows from assumptions about structure of preference or costs affecting the DMs, or the time dependence of their cognitive state. These theories largely disregard an important aspect of task statistics that the DM must contend with: namely, that the time required to complete a task is often variable and uncertain, and that this uncertainty can change over time. This simple and general observation is key to understanding various aspects of task delay in a normative framework, in which the DM's decision process is well-adapted to the task statistics.

Here, we propose a rational model of the delay of the initiation of tasks with deadlines. The model does not rely on restrictive assumptions on the form of preference or cost; rather, it predicts delay of task initiation as optimal behaviour in the presence of variability in the duration of tasks. It makes use of a simple idea anticipated by the writer Jean de La Fontaine. In his 1668 fable, Le Lièvre et la Tortue (The Hare and the Tortoise), the author describes how a slow but regular tortoise reaches a goal before a hare-fast but suffering from distractions and disruptions along its path. This fable highlights that temporal disruptions can cause even a fast animal to arrive too late. The question a DM should therefore address is how to avoid being late under temporal uncertainty. The model of strategic delay proposed here is based on two simple ingredients. First, under temporal uncertainty, one ought to opt for a safety margin in planning to reach a goal. Second, the longer the way, the greater the uncertainty. Based on these two ingredients, a normative prescription to minimize costs under uncertainty predicts strategic delay as an optimal adaptation to temporal uncertainty and captures two key behavioural observations.

First, we show that our normative theory makes a counterintuitive prediction, which we call 'farther-earlier' effect: DMs who live farther from a target location (e.g., school or office) should arrive earlier (or delay their trip to a lesser degree) than those who live closer. Alternative theories based on the rational trade-off of the two costs of leaving too early and arriving after the deadline would predict the opposite pattern: that DMs who live farther would incur more delay as a way to save on the cost of leaving exceedingly early. By providing empirical evidence for the 'farther-earlier' effect in a controlled experimental study, we validate the model's prediction.

Second, we show that the theory addresses the recursive nature of delaying task initiation: the observation that, as the deadline approaches, DMs often re-evaluate a decision to initiate a task and tend to postpone initiation repeatedly (Steel, 2007; Steel et al., 2018). This phenomenon, often associated with maladaptive procrastination, emerges in our account as a simple consequence of the fact that, as the deadline approaches, the intrinsic variability of task completion time diminishes. Thus, according to our approach, these puzzles do not constitute 'irrational biases' but rather can reflect optimal behaviour in the presence of uncertainty in the duration of tasks.

In sum, our proposed explanation frames the phenomenon of delaying the initiation of tasks with deadlines as optimal time management in the face of the randomness inherent in human actions. It also unifies disparate observations, some of which cannot be explained simply as a rational trade-off in a deterministic setting on the basis of specific structure of costs.

Statistical description of task-initiation delay

In a deterministic environment, one would set the starting time so as to end the task precisely at the deadline. In reality, however, the duration of any task is variable or uncertain, either because of extraneous factors (e.g., traffic) or internal ones (e.g., tiredness). As a result, the trade-off is statistical: optimal behaviour results from minimizing the total expected cost. Experimental demonstration of the 'farther-earlier' effect

To our knowledge, the 'farther-earlier' effect predicted by our model has never been assessed in a controlled experimental setting. To examine it, we designed an online experiment that mimics the example of Jane and Charles described above. In the experimental task, participants (n=45) had to navigate in a 2D virtual environment composed of a "treasure room" and a "hallway" (Fig. 2). The goal of a participant was to collect as many coins as possible in the treasure room and then reach the exit at the end of the hallway before a deadline (randomly selected in each trial), indicated by a countdown that was always visible. Each trial had a maximum duration of 30 seconds, during which coins spawned in random positions in the treasure room, at a constant rate. The maximum number of coins that could spawn was 60. If participants failed to reach the exit before the deadline, they received a penalty of 100 coins. Participants began with a total of 100 coins.

Repeated task-initiation delays

Having assessed the empirical validity of a prediction of the model -the 'farther-earlier' effect -we now discuss its further implications and its ability to shed light on the observation that when DMs re-evaluate a decision to initiate a task, they tend to postpone its initiation and, as a consequence, tend to initiate and complete the task at hand later than they had initially planned (Steel, 2007).

To model this, we extend our setting by considering two successive processes. In the simple model described above, we considered a single process -the initiation of a task -and we showed that the initiation time is a function of both the mean and the variance of the task's duration. The model did not, however, account for how and when the initiation time was set in the first place. In an extended model, we instead consider two coupled processes: first, the DM sets an initiation time, well before the task is actually initiated (e.g., deciding early in the morning when to leave the house); second, later on the DM initiates the task (e.g., actually leaving home). Discussion

Observations of delays in initiating tasks with deadlines are common but puzzling. Why do people who live closer to their destination tend to arrive later than those who live farther? Why do people repeatedly postpose the start of intended tasks when a deadline approaches? While many instances of delaying tasks are associated with maladaptive procrastination and psychological processes such as poor self-control and anxiety (Sirois, 2004; Solomon & Rothblum, 1984; Steel, 2007), not all cases need reflect dysfunction. Here, we develop a framework in which delaying the initiation of tasks with deadlines emerges as an optimal strategy for managing time subject to uncertainty in the duration of tasks.

Our account connects to previous work in economics and psychology that examined action delays in terms of (optimal) time management and planning. In this context, authors have alluded to 'active' or 'strategic delay' (Chun Chu & Choi, 2005; Corkin et al., 2011; Klingsieck, 2013). In these accounts, strategic delay is conceptualized in terms of a trade-off between the cost of early task initiation and the cost of completing the task too late (Ellenberg, 2015). Unlike these theoretical approaches (discussed below), our proposal does not assume particular or time-dependent forms of preference or cost but instead takes as its central assumption the uncertainty in task duration. Specifically, our approach assumes that people take real-world temporal statistics into account when planning. Our approach also assumes that temporal uncertainty grows with mean duration. This assumption is natural since a longer duration presents more opportunities for disruption. It is also in agreement with empirical observation: various studies in different cities and countries (Australia, Chile, China) have reported a linear relation between the mean travel time and the standard deviation of travel time, in the contexts of walking as well as traveling by car, bus, and subway (Durán-Hormazábal & Tirachini, 2016).

Based on this statistical assumption, our model captures key features of strategic delay. One such feature is the 'farther-earlier' effect: people who live farther from a destination tend to arrive earlier (or procrastinate less) than those who live closer, a trend which is not explained naturally based on forms of preference. We reproduced this trend in a laboratory experiment in which we manipulated the statistics of temporal uncertainty. While -to the best of our knowledge -ours is the first laboratory demonstration of the 'farther-earlier' effect, this effect has also been reported in real-life situations. For example, a 2015 study of transportation dynamics funded by the cities of Lafayette, Orinda, and Moraga found that "People that live closest to the BART stations (in Lafayette and Orinda) are more likely to report not being able to find parking within the BART lots in the last 30 days than are people who live farther away (in Moraga or outside Lamorinda), suggesting that residents living farther away plan ahead and arrive earlier at BART, knowing the parking constraints" (Lamorinda Program Management Committee, 2015). A two stage extension of our model also explains the tendency of people to repeatedly postpone the initiation of a task, as the deadline approaches -a feature often associated with maladaptive procrastination, because each delay invalidates an earlier decision (Steel, 2007; Steel et al., 2018). This extension assumes that the task-initiation time is chosen under uncertainty on both task duration and the time of task initiation itself. Because the latter ban be estimated more precisely as it approaches, the optimal initiation time is postponed, as a revision of an earlier decision. The two-stage model extension does not imply a new form of optimality; it illustrates dynamics in which DMs repeatedly revise earlier decisions as planning uncertainty decreases. The additional prediction of the two-stage model is therefore a form of recursive delay through successive-re-evaluations, even when preferences and cost functions remain unchanged. Here, strategic and repeated delays do not result from exotic forms of preference nor from a maladaptive process, but from optimal adaptation to an uncertain environment.

Relation with other theoretical accounts of task-initiation delaying and procrastination

In addition to the theories of optimal time management and strategic delay (Chun Chu & Choi, 2005; Corkin et al., 2011; Ellenberg, 2015; Klingsieck, 2013), various alternative theoretical accounts explain delaying as the outcome of a time-dependent, task-related cost, aversiveness, or urgency function. In one such proposal, the expected cost of a task varies with time: predicted costs are temporally discounted, with a discount factor that increases as a function of the interval of time separating the time of planning and the initiation of the task (Akerlof, 1991; Durán-Hormazábal & Tirachini, 2016; Y. Loewenstein et al., 2026; O'Donoghue & Rabin, 1999). Time-inconsistent preferences can also lead DMs to avoid unpleasant tasks (G. Loewenstein & Prelec, 1992; Prelec, 1989); for example, they can induce a preference for a more effortful but delayed task over a less effortful but earlier task (Mazur, 1996). Another, related account of procrastination, termed 'temporal decision model,' assumes a temporal discounted 'aversiveness' to future tasks (S. Zhang & Feng, 2020); here, DMs procrastinate because, as the task approaches, aversiveness increases and outweighs the incentive to carry it out. Similarly, 'temporal motivation theory' assumes that the perceived utility of a given task (and hence the motivation to engage in it) increases exponentially as the deadline approaches, and hence procrastination is a result of low motivation when deadlines are too far away (Steel et al., 2018; Steel & König, 2006). Related proposals describe procrastination as a way of avoiding aversive and difficult tasks, under the fear of failure (Eckert et al., 2016; Solomon & Rothblum, 1984). Given that, as the task approaches, its aversive aspects become more prominent, DMs tend to procrastinate again and again. Along similar lines, the emotion-regulation perspective assumes that procrastination depends on the prioritization of short-term over long-term objectives (S. Zhang et al., 2019). Procrastination has also been assigned to a 'lack of urgency' to complete a task when it is not imminent. In this account, the sense of urgency (or time pressure) to initiate a task increases exponentially as the deadline approaches, and it is only when it exceeds a threshold that the DM engages sufficient resources in carrying out the task (Durakiewicz, 2016; Konig & Kleinmann, 2005).

Most of these theories do not provide a rationale for the 'farther-earlier' effect. In formulations involving temporally discounted cost, the prediction would in fact be reversed: individuals residing farther from the destination would value delaying departure more than those residing closer. Symmetrically, in urgency-based formulations, one could argue that individuals living closer to the destination would feel more compelled to depart than those who perceive the deadline as temporally more distant. Models incorporating task aversiveness and its increasing salience as the task approaches could, however, offer a justification for the effect, as individuals who live farther depart earlier because they perceive less aversiveness toward the task.

In contrast to these proposals, our model does not assume specific forms of time-dependent cost, aversiveness, or sense of urgency, but instead makes use of a statistical argument. It is interesting, however, to examine whether the two types of approaches might be more closely related than they appear. While temporal discounting can give rise to a rich phenomenology and can explain various puzzles, its own foundation remains to be fully elucidated. It has been proposed that temporal discounting can be viewed normatively when a DM takes future uncertainty into account (Gabaix & Laibson, 2017). Thus, while a phenomenology of preference modulation might explain puzzling behavioural trends, underlying it might be a correct apprehension of fixed preferences and costs together with environmental statistics by the DM. It is also worth noting that, in contrast to theories emphasizing the role of motivation and emotion on task-initiation delaying and procrastination, our approach views delay as a rational strategy to achieve goals while minimising costs-motivation does not enter the picture.

Yet another account of procrastination, based on ideas from reinforcement learning, has been proposed to explain why a rational agent would delay the initiation of a task at the expense of a subsequent greater expenditure of effort (P. Zhang & Ma, 2019). As in our work, this approach views procrastination as an adaptive process: the DM adapts to the rate of reward provided by the environment, whereas in our model the DM adapts to temporal uncertainty.

Finally, our model is closely related to probabilistic (Bayesian) approaches to perception and action in cognitive and motor neuroscience (Doya et al., 2007; Parr et al., 2022; Pouget et al., 2013). A common assumption is that DMs explicitly represent and exploit uncertainty when making decisions. In particular, one can draw a direct analogy between the temporal effects under scrutiny here and motor (spatial) effects observed in the context of limbs and eye movements (Maloney, 2002; Shadmehr et al., 2010). (Trommershäuser et al., 2005), for example, showed that subjects making rapid reaching movements toward a target optimally compensated for the spatial variability of their own movements, by biasing hand trajectories away from an adjacent penalty region, and adapted this compensation when movement variability was experimentally modulated. Our experimental and theoretical results can be viewed as a temporal counterpart of the near-optimal accounting of uncertainty in decisionmaking.

While it is gratifying that a simple framework such as the one reported on in this paper can explain various empirical observations on task-initiation delaying as optimal adaptation to environmental statistics, obviously such a simple approach is bound to yield only a very partial picture of a rich phenomenology. Furthermore, we do not suggest that all forms of delays under deadlines can be viewed as rational adaptation: DMs may also exhibit sub-optimal procrastination. Various studies have shown that DMs occasionally procrastinate in daily situations, even in situations in which delays lead to harmful consequences. This maladaptive procrastination may arise from individual cognitive, motivational, and personality factors, including poor self-control or anxiety (Sirois, 2004; Solomon & Rothblum, 1984; Steel, 2007), poor perception of the passage of time (Conte & Jacobs, 2003), and excessive reliance on multitasking (DeLonzor, 2013) -mechanisms that fall outside the scope of our model, but that can co-exist with rational adaptation to uncertainty. Here, we have not attempted to advance a unified theory of every type of delay under deadlines but instead asked which ones can be understood as strategic or optimal adaptations to environmental (temporal) statistics.

The model can be extended in various ways. More involved forms of rewards and costs can be included. The numerical parameters entering the model can themselves become functions of psychological variables, such as personality traits. More concretely, the current model considers only the costs associated with starting a task too early and ending it too late, but not other costs such as the effort required to execute the task itself. If an added task cost depends only on the nature of the task and not on its duration, our conclusions remain unchanged. If, however, the task cost depends on the task duration, it can affect the model predictions. Crucially, the effect will depend on whether the task cost increases or decreases with task duration -one can easily imagine examples of both types -and the two cases will lead to different conclusions. In the latter case, we expect qualitatively similar conclusions and a stronger effect. In the former case, an extended model would involve a trade-off between delaying task initiation and compensating subsequently through increased effort, in line with the observation that individuals sometimes complete tasks under time pressure by investing more effort than necessary to meet deadlines (Steel et al., 2018).

A feature of our model is that it focuses on situations in which explicit deadlines are present. However, people also incur delays in situations that do not necessarily involve external deadlines, such as answering e-mails, cleaning the house, donating blood, going to the gym, or scheduling a medical check-up. While at least some of these scenarios are plausibly related to maladaptive forms of procrastination -and influenced by motivational and psychological processes (e.g., anxiety or lack of self-control) beyond the scope of the current model -future extensions of the model may be able to account for at least some of the phenomenology. Indeed, although our equations and simulations assumed explicit deadlines, the model itself does not require the cost function to increase abruptly at a deadline; it is sufficient that the expected cost increases over time, as is the case for some of the examples mentioned above. More generally, the model can be extended to time-sensitive tasks, that is, tasks for which postponement entails an increased cost, regardless of whether this increase is induced by an explicit deadline or through a smooth temporal mechanism. It is also possible that even in the absence of explicit deadlines, DMs often impose internal deadlines on themselves as a way of self-regulation (Ariely & Wertenbroch, 2002). Incorporating self-imposed deadlines would naturally extend the current framework to tasks lacking externally imposed deadlines; in that case, the issue would shift to explaining, on a normative basis, the necessity of internal deadlines.

Experiment: the task

In the experimental task, participants had to navigate in a 2D virtual environment composed of a "treasure room" and a "hallway". The goal of a participant was to collect as many coins as possible in the treasure room and then reach the exit at the end of the hallway before a deadline indicated by a visible countdown. Each trial had a maximum duration of 30 seconds, during which coins spawned in random positions in the treasure room, at a constant rate. The maximum number of coins that could spawn was 60. If participants failed to reach the exit before the deadline, they received a penalty of 100 coins, and lost the coins collected in that trial. Participants began with a total of 100 coins. To reach the exit, participants had to leave the room through a door that would close behind their avatar to prevent them from backtracking, and then to cross a hallway interrupted by a number of doors. To open a door, participants had to bring their avatar in contact with it, and the door would then gradually open over some duration. Once participants reached the exit, they had to wait for the end of the countdown, as a way to prevent them from quitting the experiment any sooner. During this time, they were shown relevant information, such as the number of coins they collected during the trial, the total amount of coins they possessed at that instant, the time at which they left the room, and the time remaining before the deadline.

The main variable of interest we examined in this work is the estimated arrival time (EAT) of the participant avatar to the exit platform. The EAT calculation is based upon the time at which the avatar left the "treasure room" summed with the average time needed to move across the hallway for that trial condition.

Behavioural experiment

The behavioural experiment was divided in two blocks of 24 trials each, one for each experimental condition -Long hallway or Short hallway (see Fig. 2)-mimicking the case of a DM living farther or closer to a target destination, respectively. The order of blocks was randomized across participants. The first four trials in each block were practice trials; they had no effect on the final score and were excluded from the analyses. The Long-and Short-hallway conditions comprised 1 and 4 doors respectively. The time each door needed to open was variable, with a mean of 1.5 seconds and a standard deviation that depended on the number of doors (σ1 Door=0.15s, σ4 Doors =0.6s). This design ensured that the standard deviation of the commuting time through the hallway was proportional to its mean, thus adhering to the assumptions in the model.

Experiment: participants

We recruited 50 participants between the ages of 18 and 40 years old through the online platform Prolific. Participant gave informed consent online prior to participation and we remunerated them according to their game score. After pre-processing stage (see below for exclusion criteria), we included 45 individuals in the analysis. Of the participants, 26 identified as male, 16 as female, 2 as other, and 1 preferred not to say. The mean age was 29, with a standard deviation of 6, and all participants were located in the United States and the United Kingdom. All the procedures were approved by the CNR Ethics committee.
