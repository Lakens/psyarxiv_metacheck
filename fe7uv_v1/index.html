Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Children's environmental exposures are associated with important cognitive outcomes later in life, and several lines of research suggest socioeconomic disadvantage and deprivation are associated with different pace of brain maturation and worse outcomes on common tests of executive functioning. However, less is known about protective environmental features that may support cognitive development. Engagement with potential protective factors, such as extracurricular activities, can serve as enriching environments that provide physical and cognitive stimulation outside of the household.

Here, we leverage longitudinal cognitive computational models (drift diffusion models) to estimate a task-general, computational underpinning of executive functioning, the efficiency of evidence accumulation (EEA), in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study. Next, we used latent growth models to examine associations of participation in, and time spent on, varied extracurricular activities at ages 9 to 10 years with EEA trajectories from ages 9 to 17 years.

Participation in more extracurricular activities and more time spent on activities across categories (social or physical) were associated with higher EEA at baseline, including after adjusting for household socioeconomic status (SES) and neighborhood deprivation. These differences remained stable through development, except for non-social and social physical activities which showed negative associations with EEA trajectories, possibly indicating differences in EEA diminishing with development.

Our findings indicate that participation in extracurricular activities at age 9-10 is positively associated with EEA, a task-general and biologically plausible measure of executive functioning, and underscore the need to consider specific types of activities when examining links to cognition and cognitive development.

Introduction

An individual's early environmental exposures are associated with important outcomes later in life, including physical and mental health (S. Cohen et al., 2010; Evans & Cassells, 2014), educational attainment (G. J. Duncan et al., 1998), and cognition (Bradley & Corwyn, 2002; G. J. Duncan & Magnuson, 2012; Rakesh et al., 2025). Several lines of research suggest socioeconomic disadvantage and deprivation during childhood and adolescence are associated with differences in brain development (Carrick et al., 2026; Marek et al., 2025; Tooley et al., 2021) and lower performance on tasks that index key cognitive functions, such as executive functions (Merz et al., 2019; Noble et al., 2005; Rakesh et al., 2025; Spielberg et al., 2015). Executive functions refer to a range of high-level cognitive processes that are key for goal-directed behavior, and include response inhibition, working memory, task-switching, and planning behaviors (Denckla, 1996; Jurado & Rosselli, 2007). Executive functions develop rapidly during early adolescence and reach adult-like levels at around 15 to 18 years of age (Luna et al., 2004; Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023), and environmental factors are thought to be an important source of individual differences in executive functions (Rakesh et al., 2024 (Rakesh et al., , 2025)). Adolescence is a unique life phase in which neurodevelopmental processes refine, specialize and consolidate maturing brain circuits (Blakemore & Choudhury, 2006; Casey et al., 2008; Sydnor et al., 2026). Importantly, this is a time of environmental receptivity driven by increased plasticity (Larsen & Luna, 2018; Sydnor et al., 2026), when early life stressors, such as those associated with deprivation, can affect the organization and pace of neurodevelopment (Colich et al., 2020; Tooley et al., 2021). For example, a recent study suggests that exposure to threatening environments is associated with accelerated neurodevelopment, while deprivation is associated with delayed brain maturation (Beck et al., 2025). On the other hand, if adolescence is a time of increased sensitivity to negative experiences affecting brain and cognitive development, then it may also be a time of receptivity to positive experiences, offering a window of opportunity.

Cognitive stimulation refers to the availability of enriching environmental inputs that provide a variety of experiences and facilitate learning opportunities, which may have an impact on the ability to engage executive processes. Environments enriched with cognitive stimulation are typically operationalized as educational resources and learning materials at home, but may also include extracurricular activities, academic opportunities, environmental novelty, and a positive school environment (Rakesh et al., 2025; Sydnor et al., 2026; Sydnor & Luna, 2026). These may serve as buffers against the negative impacts of environmental adversity or deprivation, where the latter may be viewed as an absence of cognitive enrichment (McLaughlin et al., 2014; Sheridan & McLaughlin, 2014). Enrichment and household socioeconomic status (SES) are positively associated, as children in higher-SES homes tend to be exposed to more complex environments (Bradley et al., 2001), and research suggests that cognitive enrichment is associated with improved cognition in youth independent of stress exposures (Amso et al., 2019; McLaughlin & Sheridan, 2016; Rosen et al., 2018; Sheridan et al., 2017). Further, cognitive stimulation may mediate the association between SES and executive function, language ability, and academic achievement (Rakesh et al., 2025; Rosen et al., 2020). However, relative to studies on environmental disadvantage, environmental enrichment is understudied in humans (Sydnor & Luna, 2026). Moreover, cognitive stimulation outside the parent-child dyad remains largely unexplored (Rakesh et al., 2024). Studying the role of factors outside the home is important given that as children grow older, they spend less time at home and more time in the broader community. Thus, influences such as school environments and extracurricular activities may provide important sources of cognitive stimulation during childhood and adolescence.

In animal models, environmental enrichment paradigms typically consist of novel objects (e.g., mazes, running wheels) and novel social partners (Tooley et al., 2021). Analogs in humans may be found in extracurricular activities, which provide opportunities for diverse experiences through varied activities, physical exercise, and social connectedness, thereby facilitating environmental complexity. Indeed, engagement in extracurricular activities has been associated with positive cognitive outcomes (Farb & Matjasko, 2012). For example, engagement in physical activity in late childhood and throughout adolescence has been linked to better cognitive performance during adolescence (Ayuso-Moreno et al., 2026; Bidzan-Bluma & Lipowska, 2018; Esteban-Cornejo et al., 2015; Herting & Chu, 2017). Similarly, a recent study showed that participation in both team-based and individual-based sports at 9-10 years old and at a 2-year follow-up was associated with better performance on most cognitive measures both at baseline and over time (Tan et al., 2025). Habitual sports participation and superior physical fitness may be associated with improved cognitive performance due to a higher capacity for effective allocation of neural resources during mentally demanding tasks (Chaddock-Heyman et al., 2014). Even activities involving little physical activity such as musical training (Jamey et al., 2024; Zuk et al., 2014), have been linked to better executive functioning (Linnemann et al., 2016; Rodriguez-Gomez & Talero-Gutiérrez, 2022; Trehub, 2003; van Drunen et al., 2026), possibly by boosting social interactions, reducing stress, and improving emotion regulation.

While these studies provide valuable information about links between potentially enriching experiences and cognitive gains, the cognitive measures that were used are agnostic to the underlying cognitive processes and have inherent limitations. Performance metrics such as accuracy and response time are difficult to interpret, often providing only an indirect, or even confounded, window into the underlying cognitive processes (Miyake et al., 2000). Specifically, raw performance metrics provide information about the outcomes of cognitive processes, rather than the processes themselves (Vermeent et al., 2026; Wagenmakers, 2008). This is partly because they conflate a range of latent processes, such as peripheral processing (e.g., stimulus encoding), prioritizing accuracy over speed, or motivation. Also, response time and response accuracy are often weakly correlated, such that an increase in response time does not necessarily predict increases in accuracy (Domingue et al., 2022). This also presents a reverse inference problem when studying individual differences in response times, as differences in a cognitive function can manifest as differences in response times, but differences in response times do not guarantee differences in cognitive function (White & Kitchen, 2022). Finally, differences in cognitive abilities can manifest differently between individuals and across development. For example, higher cognitive ability can lead to faster response times in one group and higher accuracy in another. These issues can be mitigated by using a common metric that combines both response time and accuracy, while accounting for other sources of behavioral variability.

Theory-driven computational models can be leveraged to study the mechanistic processes underlying observed behavior and can provide richer information about the cognitive functions of interest by emphasizing mechanism rather than aggregate performance. The drift diffusion model (DDM) is a wellestablished model for binary decision-making that integrates both accuracy and response times while accounting for multiple sources of variability on a trial-by-trial level (Ratcliff, 1978; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Ratcliff & Rouder, 1998). The model assumes that, when an individual is presented with two options, responses arise from the noisy accumulation of evidence for one option over the other, toward one of two response thresholds. A response is initiated once the accumulator reaches a response threshold. The model has parameters that represent various sources of variability in responses and response times, such as response caution and the time taken to encode stimuli and execute a response. Importantly, the DDM has been linked to neural dynamics, as the evidence accumulation process has been well characterized at the level of neurophysiological recordings in nonhuman primates and rodents (Gold & Shadlen, 2007; Hanks et al., 2015; Ratcliff et al., 2003; Shadlen & Newsome, 2001; Smith & Ratcliff, 2004), and similar neural signatures have been identified in humans with both electroencephalography (Loughnane et al., 2016; Nunez et al., 2017; O'Connell et al., 2012; Pedersen et al., 2026; Ratcliff et al., 2009) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI; Heekeren et al., 2004; Ho et al., 2009; T. Liu & Pleskac, 2011; Pisauro et al., 2017).

One parameter that has received much attention is the drift rate, which measures the average rate of evidence accumulation and contributes to variability in both responses and response times. Variability in drift rates is driven by an individual's efficiency of information processing and ability to separate relevant information from irrelevant information (Weigard et al., 2026). Individual differences in drift rate have been linked to various domains of higher-order cognition, such as general intelligence (Lerche et al., 2020; Ratcliff et al., 2011; Schmiedek et al., 2007) and executive functions (Frogner et al., 2026; Löffler et al., 2024; Pedersen et al., 2023; Schmiedek et al., 2007; Tomlinson et al., 2025; Weigard et al., 2020; Weigard & Huang-Pollock, 2017; Wiker et al., 2024). Recent studies suggest that drift rates across a variety of tasks and cognitive domains are driven by a shared latent process (Lerche et al., 2020; Schmiedek et al., 2007; Weigard et al., 2021). The efficiency of evidence accumulation (EEA) is a model-based candidate for a trait-like cognitive capacity driving performance across executive function tasks and explains shared variance in drift rates across tasks (Lerche et al., 2020; Löffler et al., 2024; Schubert et al., 2016; Tomlinson et al., 2025; Weigard & Sripada, 2021). Thus, a single drift rate parameter reflects evidence accumulation for a specific task or condition, whereas EEA is a latent variable composed of drift rate estimates across multiple tasks and is thought to index a general ability to effectively gather goal-relevant information to make adaptive decisions regardless of task conditions (Tomlinson et al., 2025; Weigard & Sripada, 2021). This conceptualization of a general ability to process goal-relevant information to deliver optimal responses fits well with broadly accepted definitions of executive processes (Miller & Cohen, 2001), and EEA may be the primary process underlying findings that most variance in executive function performance is explained by a task-general factor rather than task-specific ability (Löffler et al., 2024; Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023). Recent work shows that EEA has strong reliability across tasks and reasonable stability over time, including in adolescents (Tomlinson et al., 2025; Weigard et al., 2021). Further, it has strong and replicable neural correlates, as represented in functional dynamics of brain networks (Weigard et al., 2024). In sum, EEA provides a reliable, parsimonious, and mechanistic measure of task-general executive functioning that facilitates more precise theoretical explanations and stronger links to neurobiology than traditional metrics.

Only a few studies have assessed how EEA relates to environmental factors (Vermeent et al., 2024 (Vermeent et al., , 2025;; Zhang et al., 2026). For example, Vermeent et al. (2024) estimated associations between adversity, EEA, and task-specific drift rates across executive functioning tasks in 9-10 year old participants drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study. They found that higher household threat, but not material deprivation, was associated with lower EEA. Further, when accounting for EEA, neither threat nor deprivation was associated with task-specific drift rates, suggesting that the association between these dimensions of adversity and drift rates is mostly related to task-general processes. The latter finding is in line with other studies suggesting that material deprivation during childhood and parental SES are related to task-general ability rather than specific cognitive skills (Bignardi et al., 2024; Vermeent et al., 2025). However, to our knowledge, no studies have examined how EEA is related to potentially positive environmental experiences outside the home. Also, little is known about how EEA develops over time. Tomlinson et al. (2025) showed that EEA is sensitive to developmental changes, as seen in significant improvements after approximately one year in a sample of 9-17-year-old participants. However, there is a need for studies examining how EEA develops across more than two time points.

To address these gaps in the literature, the current study aimed to examine associations between extracurricular activities and EEA using a large longitudinal sample that spans the transition from childhood to mid-adolescence, drawn from the ABCD Study. We applied task-specific, hierarchical DDMs to behavioral data from five tasks that were administered biennially to estimate drift rates associated with sustained attention, working memory, processing speed, and response inhibition. To derive an estimate of EEA, we applied a longitudinal confirmatory factor analysis with strong measurement invariance and assessed associations with participation in extracurricular activities. Importantly, we aimed to see if differences in extracurricular activity participation preceded cognitive outcomes, by using growth curve models to examine how differences in late childhood, around 9 to 10 years old, were related to both EEA at baseline (i.e. intercept or level) and pace of maturation in EEA (i.e. slope or change). As children growing up in higher-SES homes tend to be exposed to more complex and cognitively stimulating environments, we were interested in seeing whether extracurricular activities as an enriching factor were related to interindividual differences in EEA at baseline and EEA trajectories after adjusting for household and area-level SES. We examined participation in extracurricular activities in two ways. First, we used a count of the activities the participant had endorsed in the past year, with higher values indicating more varied leisure activities and possibly more environmental complexity. Second, we split the activities into four categories along two dimensions, social/non-social and physical/non-physical, and calculated the average number of hours spent on each category each week in the past year, to test if potential links between cognition and extracurriculars were related to varying degrees of physical activity or social connectedness.

Based on previous work (e.g., Farb & Matjasko, 2012; Herting & Chu, 2017; Jamey et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2025), we expected positive associations between participation in extracurricular activities, regardless of category, and EEA at baseline. Also, we hypothesized that greater involvement in extracurricular activities would be associated with more rapid EEA development, and thereby greater cognitive gains. However, we expected these associations to become substantially smaller after adjusting for household and area-level SES.

2.2.2. Socioeconomic status

Socioeconomic information was reported by a parent or guardian. Income-to-needs ratio was calculated by dividing the total combined family income (i.e. median value of the income band) by the 2017 Federal Poverty Guidelines for a household of that size (Table S2). An income-to-needs ratio value of 1 indicates income exactly at the poverty threshold and values greater than and less than 1 reflect being above and below the threshold, respectively (Fig. 1B). Parental education was measured as the highest level of education across caregivers with five levels (1 = Up to high school, no diploma;5 = Graduate school or professional degree). To create a single household SES index, we created a composite score of income-to-needs ratio and parental education. This was done by z-scoring parental education and income-to-needs ratio separately, followed by calculating the average of the two variables. For participants who had missing data for one of the variables, the z-scored value of the other variable was used alone. All participants had parental education available, while 808 participants did not have income data, due to parents or caregivers responding "don't know" or declining to answer.

We included neighborhood deprivation as an index of area-level SES. Neighborhood deprivation was assessed using a composite score from the Area Deprivation Index (ADI). The ADI is the scaled and weighted sum of 17 U.S. census variables, including measures of poverty, education, housing, and employment, with a higher score indicating greater deprivation (Kind et al., 2014).

2.2.3. Cognitive tasks

Tasks were selected based on the following criteria. First, the task had to be administered at baseline and biennially. Second, the task had to have data that could be fit with a DDM. This required trial-level response time and accuracy data, as well as binary responses (e.g., match/non-match, right/left). Third, average response times had to be short, generally under 3 seconds (Lerche et al., 2020). These criteria yielded the five tasks described below, which measure aspects of decision-making and executive functioning by engaging working memory, sustained attention, basic processing speed, and inhibitory control.

2.2.3.1. n-back task

The emotional n-back task (Barch et al., 2013; Casey et al., 2018; A. O. Cohen et al., 2016) engages memory and attention by varying between low (0-back) and higher (2-back) memory demand conditions, in addition to engaging emotional regulation processes by varying the emotional valence of stimuli. The task consists of 160 trials split into two runs that are completed during fMRI. Each run consists of eight blocks, split equally into either 0-back or 2-back task blocks, each with 10 trials. At each trial, participants are tasked with deciding whether the current stimulus matches a target stimulus. In the 0-back condition, the target stimulus is presented at the start of the task block and participants are asked to respond "match"/"target" every time the stimulus is presented, and "no match"/"nontarget" otherwise. In the 2-back condition, participants need to decide whether the current stimulus matches the stimulus presented 2 trials back. In both task conditions, the stimulus and the target match in 20% of trials in a given task block. For the remaining trials, stimuli are either presented for the first time (nonlure trials) or have been presented before (lure trials). In the 0-back condition, the remaining trials consist of 30% lure trials and 50% nonlure trials. In the 2-back condition, they consist of 20% lure trials and 60% nonlure trials. Stimulus category varies across blocks comprising happy, fearful, or neutral faces, or houses. However, stimulus category was not included in the DDM adapted to the n-back task, because it would result in too many parameters for reliable subject-level estimates.

In the task, responses that were slower than 2,000 milliseconds (ms) were reported as missing. We removed trials with response times faster than 200 ms and included data from participants with a recorded response on at least 80 of the total 160 trials to ensure sufficient measures of performance. Datasets with task accuracy below 60% across all trials were excluded. These criteria were based on our previous studies where we reported good model fit for the n-back DDM (Frogner et al., 2026; Pedersen et al., 2023). However, we increased the minimum amount of trials from 50 to 80 as we previously found that some participants had to be excluded after fitting the DDM due to lacking specific trial types (Frogner et al., 2026). Before data cleaning, n-back data were available for 9,553 unique participants, of whom 8,975 remained after cleaning (Table 1).

2.2.3.2. Stop signal task

The visual stop signal task (SST; Casey et al., 2018; Logan, 1994) engages aspects of decision making such as response inhibition, impulsivity, and conflict monitoring. Participants are instructed to respond quickly and accurately when shown a go stimulus, but to cancel their response if the go stimulus is followed by a stop stimulus after a variable delay. The task consists of 360 trials split into two runs completed during fMRI, where thirty trials (16.67%) in each run are stop trials. Each trial begins with the presentation of a go stimulus, either a leftward or rightward pointing arrow. Participants are instructed to indicate the direction of the arrow with their dominant hand via a two-button response panel. On go trials, the go stimulus is presented for 1,000 ms or until a response is given. On stop trials, the go stimulus is presented for the duration of the stop-signal delay (SSD), which is a variable interval between the go stimulus and stop stimulus, or until a response is made. During these trials, the leftward or rightward arrow is followed by an upward-pointing arrow presented for 300 ms, indicating that participants should withhold their response. To ensure that there are approximately 50% successful and 50% unsuccessful inhibition trials for stop trials, the SSD varies depending on task performance. The initial SSD is 50 ms and following an unsuccessful inhibition trial, the task is made easier by reducing the SSD by 50 ms on the next stop trial. After a successful inhibition trial, the task is made more difficult by increasing the SSD by 50 ms on the next stop trial.

In the present study, only data from the go trials were included. We removed trials with responses faster than 200 ms. Datasets with task accuracy below 60% on go trials or fewer than 100 trials with a recorded response were excluded to ensure compliance. Before data cleaning, SST data were available for 9,593 unique participants, of whom 9,315 remained after cleaning (Table 1).

2.2.3.3. Flanker task

The flanker task is part of the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery (Bleck et al., 2013; Gershon et al., 2013; Hodes et al., 2013) and is a variant of the Eriksen Flanker task (Eriksen & Eriksen, 1974). The task engages cognitive control and attention by testing the participant's ability to modulate responding under congruent versus incongruent stimulus contexts (Zelazo et al., 2014). Participants are instructed to indicate the left-right orientation of a centrally presented stimulus, while inhibiting attention to the potentially incongruent stimuli that surround the central stimulus on each side (i.e. flankers). On each trial, participants are presented with five arrows, with four arrows serving as flankers. After four practice trials, participants complete 20 trials, of which 12 were congruent (flankers pointed in the same direction as the central stimulus) and eight were incongruent (flankers pointed in the opposite direction as the central stimulus). For the 2-year and 4-year follow-ups, the ABCD Study team developed a version of the Flanker task that could be administered at remote assessments presented in the Inquisit system from Millisecond. While the Millisecond Flanker was designed to mimic the NIH Toolbox Flanker as closely as possible, there could still be version-specific differences in performance. Therefore, we only used Flanker data that were administered as part of the NIH Toolbox.

We excluded datasets with accuracy below 60% and datasets that had fewer than 15 recorded responses. Also, we excluded trials with response times that were faster than 250 ms or slower than 4 seconds. Before data cleaning, Flanker data were available for 10640 unique participants, of whom 10622 remained after cleaning (Table 1).

2.2.3.4. Pattern Comparison Processing Speed

The Pattern Comparison Processing Speed (PTTCP) task is a part of the NIH Toolbox (Bleck et al., 2013; Gershon et al., 2013; Hodes et al., 2013) and is a measure of visual processing speed (Carlozzi et al., 2015). Participants are presented with two images at each trial and are instructed to respond whether the two images are the same or different. Images can differ in three dimensions: color, adding/taking something away, or one versus many (containing more or less of a particular item). Participants complete up to 130 trials within 90 seconds; on average, participants completed 48 trials. Datasets with task accuracy below 60% or less than 15 trials with a recorded response were excluded. Also, we removed trials with response times that were faster than 1 second and response times that were slower than 10 seconds. We set these criteria because they resolved issues related to DDM estimation, in line with a previous study applying the DDM to this task (Vermeent et al., 2024). Before data cleaning, PTTCP data were available for 10,638 unique participants, of whom 10,621 remained after cleaning (Table 1).

2.4. Drift diffusion modeling

To extract task-specific drift rates, we fit DDMs to trial-level choice and response time data from each task. The DDM has four main parameters.

The average rate of evidence accumulation is represented by the drift rate parameter 𝑣. Higher drift rate values are associated with faster and more accurate decisions. The accumulation process begins at the starting point 𝑧, where values closer to the lower or upper boundary indicate biased decision making. The amount of evidence required before a response is made is determined by the distance between the response boundaries, as represented by the boundary separation parameter 𝑎. Boundary separation captures the speed-accuracy trade-off, or response caution. Lastly, the DDM accounts for non-decision processes such as motor execution and stimulus encoding with the non-decision time parameter 𝑛𝑑𝑡.

For the Flanker, SST, and PTTCP DDMs, we estimated the drift rate, non-decision time, and boundary separation as free parameters. The response bias 𝑧 was fixed at 0.5, as we assumed that participants would not be biased toward a particular response option. For the Flanker DDM, we specified a model where the drift rate varied depending on whether the task trial was a congruent or incongruent condition, as the average rate of evidence accumulation might vary depending on task difficulty. However, we found that many of the models did not converge, likely due to the low number of incongruent trials and few errors. Therefore, a single drift rate was estimated for each subject and group for the Flanker DDM as well.

We adapted the DDM to the n-back task using a model specification similar to that in our previous studies, in which we found good model performance in terms of fit and parameter recovery (Frogner et al., 2026; Pedersen et al., 2023). We coded upper bound responses as target responses and lower bound responses as nontarget responses. We assumed that the drift rate could vary depending on the block type, 0-back vs. 2-back, as they vary in difficulty and working memory load. The 0-back condition imposes minimal working memory demands and has been considered to index sustained attention rather than working memory in recent studies (Kardan et al., 2022 (Kardan et al., , 2025)). Further, we specified that the drift rate could vary depending on the stimulus type, as trials with lure stimuli are likely more challenging than nonlure trials. This resulted in six drift rates for each measurement. We flipped the lure and nonlure drift rates to a common positive direction and calculated a mean drift rate across stimulus types, which resulted in two mean drift rates: 0-back drift rate and 2-back drift rate.

Although the remaining DDM parameters are outside the scope of the present study, we specified that boundary separation and starting point bias parameters could change depending on the block type, reflecting shifts in response style with increasing task difficulty. As previously reported, the boundary separation and starting point parameters show different developmental trajectories for the 0-back and 2-back conditions during adolescence, highlighting the importance of estimating these parameters as free parameters that vary depending on task condition (Frogner et al., 2026).

2.4.1. Model fitting

Each DDM was fit with Bayesian hierarchical estimation with the HDDM package via dockerHDDM (Pan et al., 2025; Wiecki et al., 2013). Due to the large number of measurements, running a single model on all data from a given task would be too time-consuming. To circumvent this issue, we randomly assigned participants along with all their measurements to one of 100 groups and ran Bayesian hierarchical models for each group. On average, each group consisted of 106 participants and 335 datasets. Group compositions varied across DDMs, as participants were randomly assigned to groups separately for each DDM. Each DDM was naïve to the longitudinal nature of the data as measurements from the same participants were not explicitly linked within the model. However, parameter estimation for each measurement from a participant was not completely independent, as all data from a given participant were in the same group with shared hierarchical priors.

Estimation was performed using Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling. Each model was fit using four chains, with 2,000 samples each, where 1,000 samples were discarded as burn-in samples. Sampling performance was assessed using the Gelman-Rubin statistic (Gelman & Rubin, 1992). Rhat values >= 1.1 were considered to represent poor convergence. If there were more than five non-converging parameters in a group, we reran the sampling procedure with 5,000 samples, with 4,000 burn-in samples, to obtain stable parameter estimates. If the issue persisted, we increased the samples to 7,500, with 6,500 burn-in samples. For the Flanker DDM, we found that the majority of groups had at least one non-converging parameter, likely due to the low number of trials and having very few error trials. Therefore, we reran all the groups that had at least one non-converging parameter with 5,000/4,000 samples/burn-in. If a participant had a non-converging parameter after these iterative steps of increasing the number of samples, we excluded all parameter estimates for that participant's measurement. In brief, all subject-level drift rates converged for the Flanker task and PTTCP. Six subjectlevel drift rate estimates did not converge in the SST. For the n-back DDM, one participant had multiple non-converging parameters and was therefore excluded, whereas all other parameters converged. For an overview of non-converging parameters, see Table S3. For an overview of the distribution of DDM parameters, see Figs. S1-S2.

2.4.2. Posterior predictive checks and parameter recovery

To assess model fit, we performed a posterior predictive check, which entails simulating data from the fitted models and comparing them to the observed data. Overall, model fit was good, and predicted choice and response times across conditions were close to the observed data, as shown in the posterior predictive quantile probability plots (Fig. 2). We also split the observed and predicted data by time point to confirm that model fit was adequate across development, and fit was good at each time point (Figs. S3-S6).

To test parameter identifiability, we conducted parameter recovery analyses. Specifically, we tested whether fitting the DDMs to data simulated from our parameter estimates recovered similar parameter estimates. Parameter recovery analyses were done on simulated datasets from 10 randomly sampled groups, for each task-specific DDM. All parameters showed good recovery (Figs. S7-S8). The recovery for drift rate parameters were as follows: SST drift rate (𝜌 = 0.96), Flanker drift rate (𝜌 = 0.86), PTTCP drift rate (𝜌 = 0.87), 0-back drift rates (lure: 𝜌 = 0.85, nonlure: 𝜌 = 0.84, target: 𝜌 = 0.81), and 2-back drift rates (lure: 𝜌 = 0.85, nonlure: 𝜌 = 0.79, target: 𝜌 = 0.85).

2.5. Efficiency of Evidence Accumulation

To extract EEA, a task-general latent factor that captures the shared variance across task-specific drift rates, we conducted a longitudinal confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). To identify an appropriate CFA model, we ran tests of measurement invariance across time (i.e. longitudinal invariance). This formally tests whether our construct of interest is on the same metric at each measurement (Widaman et al., 2010). If longitudinal invariance is not established, then changes in the latent factor may reflect measurement error rather than true developmental change in the construct and may not carry the same meaning or scale across time points and individuals (Hawes et al., 2025; Y. Liu et al., 2017). To evaluate longitudinal invariance, we specified CFA models with increasingly stringent requirements: (i) a configural invariance model that tests whether the same general pattern of factor loadings holds across time; (ii) a weak (metric) invariance model in which each indicator variable, or task-specific drift rate, has the same factor loading across time; and (iii) a strong (scalar) invariance model that sets both factor loadings and intercepts to be equal over time (Widaman et al., 2010). If the model fit does not deteriorate considerably from one model to the next, then the level of invariance is considered established.

We referred to the following widely used thresholds for evaluating model fit: comparative fit index (CFI) > 0.95, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.06, and standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR) < 0.08 (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002; Hu & Bentler, 1999; Perry et al., 2015). If these cut-off criteria were met, the model was considered to have a good fit to the observed data.

Next, to evaluate if a change in model fit was a considerable deterioration, the following three criterion cut-offs were deemed unacceptable: a decrease in CFI value ≥ 0.01, an increase in RMSEA value ≥ 0.01, an increase in SRMR ≥ 0.03 when comparing the configural and weak invariance model, or an increase in SRMR ≥ 0.01 when comparing the weak and strong invariance models (Chen, 2007; Cheung & Rensvold, 2002; Millsap & Cham, 2012).

Models were estimated in lavaan (version 0.7-2) in R version 4.4.2. (Rosseel, 2012; Rosseel et al., 2026) with robust maximum likelihood. At each timepoint, a single latent factor was indicated by the five task-specific drift rates (Fig. 3). Residuals of the same indicator were allowed to covary across measurements. Missing data were handled with full information maximum likelihood (FIML), using age at each timepoint and sex as auxiliary variables. FIML is considered an appropriate approach for handling missing data in structural equation models, as it shows less bias than other techniques, such as imputation (Enders & Bandalos, 2001).

The configural model showed good model fit (CFI = 0.986, RMSEA = 0.038, SRMR = 0.025), suggesting that the same factor structure held at each measurement. Next, the differences in model fit were acceptable for the configural vs. weak invariance models (ΔCFI = -0.003, ΔRMSEA = 0.002, ΔSRMR = 0.008) and weak vs. strong invariance models (ΔCFI = -0.007, ΔRMSEA = 0.006, ΔSRMR = 0.003). Strong longitudinal invariance was therefore supported, and the strong invariance CFA model was retained for subsequent analyses and used to estimate the latent factor referred to as EEA. Table S4 shows each task-specific drift rate's factor loading, both unstandardized (constrained to be equal across time) and standardized (which varied with the freely estimated factor and residual variances). See Fig. S9 for a visual overview of the CFA model. The average standardized loading across time is shown in Fig. 3B.

To estimate the reliability of EEA across the four measurement timepoints, we used single-measure consistency intraclass correlation, ICC(3,1), which accounts for fixed effects across time and is unbiased by sample size (Koo & Li, 2016; Shrout & Fleiss, 1979). Following previous studies (Enkavi et al., 2019; Schurr et al., 2024), we used a nonparametric bootstrap approach with 1,000 samples. We calculated the ICC only on the basis of participants with all four EEA estimates (n = 5,372), as the ICC cannot be calculated with missing data. These analyses showed moderate reliability (ICC(3,1) = 0.554, 95% CI = [0.539, 0.568]). The EEA was calculated by including the drift rates that were estimated for each task, subject, and measurement in a longitudinal CFA. The EEA represents a latent factor 𝜂 that explains shared variance between the drift rates at each measurement, thereby capturing each subject's task-general efficiency in gathering goal-relevant evidence. 2.6. Latent growth curve modeling

Latent growth models were used to identify average trajectories of EEA development and to test for interindividual variability in these trajectories. Latent growth models capture development by treating model parameters (i.e. intercept and slope) as latent variables and using factor loadings to indicate the association of each parameter with the observed timepoints (T. E. Duncan & Duncan, 2009; Grimm et al., 2011). We fit separate latent growth models to EEA using latent basis slopes to capture change.

With the latent basis slope, we fixed factor loadings at the first timepoint to 0, the fourth timepoint to 1, and the factor loading at the second and third timepoint is freely estimated. Thus, the latent basis slope gives us information about the pace of EEA maturation without specifying its expected shape (McNeish, 2020), while the intercept captures each individual's characteristic level at baseline. To establish whether it was appropriate to model EEA development with a latent basis slope, we compared model fit between a latent basis growth model and a linear growth model using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Next, we examined how participation in varied activities and time spent on social/non-social and physical/non-physical activities at baseline were associated with individual differences in the intercept and slope of EEA development. These two models were fit in two steps: an unadjusted model without household or area-level SES and an adjusted model with household and area-level SES at baseline included as a time-invariant covariate. This approach was taken to test the robustness of the primary associations to SES-related factors. Household SES was operationalized as a composite of household income-to-needs ratio and highest level of education across caregivers. Each model included age at baseline to control for potential confounding effects of age-related differences at each measurement.

Latent growth models were fit using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012; Rosseel et al., 2026) with robust maximum likelihood estimation. FIML was used to estimate the missing data in the models. Across models, we checked whether model fit was good (CFI > 0.97, SRMR <0.05, RMSEA <0.05) or acceptable (CFI = 0.95 to 0.97, SRMR = 0.05 to 0.1, RMSEA = 0.05 to 0.08) (Schermelleh-Engel et al., 2003). We characterized standardized parameter estimates below 0.1 as very small, between 0.1 and 0.2 as small, between 0.2 and 0.3 as medium, and over 0.3 as large effect sizes (Funder & Ozer, 2019; Gignac & Szodorai, 2016). We corrected p-values for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), applying correction separately for the unadjusted and adjusted models.

As sensitivity analyses, we reran the latent growth models with one participant from each family to account for non-independence among siblings, preferentially selecting those who had more measurements and randomly otherwise (excluded: n = 1,888), and excluding assessments from participants who had suffered mild traumatic brain injury in the year before the measurement (excluded: n datasets = 206). In a separate stage of sensitivity analyses, we tested whether the primary results were independent of sex. Specifically, we assessed whether associations between the intercepts, slopes and covariates varied depending on sex by running multigroup latent growth models with sex as the grouping variable. Regression paths were freely estimated for each group, and we used Wald tests to test for sex differences in regression paths. See Supplementary Tables S5-S9 for all sensitivity analysis results.

3. Results

To examine the average and individual-level EEA development, we fit latent growth models to longitudinal EEA estimates. The latent basis model showed mostly good fit (CFI = 0.995, RMSEA = 0.053, SRMR = 0.020), in which RMSEA indicated acceptable rather than good fit, and outperformed the linear growth model (CFI = 0.962, RMSEA = 0.110, SRMR = 0.044) with lower AIC and BIC values (ΔAIC = 344.09, ΔBIC = 329.9). On average, participants showed a significant increase in EEA with an estimated 40% of the total change occurring between baseline and the 2-year follow-up and 75% of total change occurring by the 4-year follow-up (Table 2, Fig. 4). With a constant rate of change, we would expect 33% and 67% of the total change to have occurred by the 2-year and 4-year follow-ups, respectively. This suggests that EEA changes most rapidly in early adolescence. There were significant individual differences in EEA baseline levels and developmental pace, as shown by significant variances in both intercept and slope.

There was also a weak correlation between intercept and slope (r = 0.12, SE = 0.04, p = 0.002), suggesting that participants with higher baseline EEA show more change in EEA from baseline to the 6-year follow-up. As shown in Fig. 4, the standard deviation band widens slightly, suggesting that individual differences in EEA increase over time. 4. Discussion

In a large longitudinal sample spanning late childhood to mid-adolescence, we tested the associations between engagement in extracurricular activities, as a potential marker of exposure to an enriched environment, and EEA, a computational underpinning of higher-order cognitive functioning that captures shared variance in task-specific evidence accumulation processes. We found that participants who were exposed to a variety of extracurriculars or spent more time on extracurricular activities, regardless of whether it was physical or social, had higher EEA at baseline. These associations were still significant when adjusting for household SES and neighborhood deprivation, suggesting extracurriculars as a positive environment for cognitive stimulation outside the household. Although the effect sizes were very small after adjustment, they may still be meaningful at the population level, as small effects can accumulate (Funder & Ozer, 2019). The differences in EEA related to participation in varied extracurriculars remained stable across development as well, as there was no significant association with change in EEA after adjusting for SES. There was also a very small positive association between non-social non-physical activities (e.g., music training) and slopes, suggesting that participants that spent more time on such activities in late childhood also show increasing EEA during adolescence, although this association was no longer significant after adjusting for SES. To our surprise, we found very small negative associations with slopes for participants who spent more time on physical activities, regardless of social context, which may indicate that the differences in EEA between participants with low and high levels of physical activity hours level out as they develop. The use of multiple DDMs to examine developmental differences in cognitive processing moves beyond conventional task performance metrics, as the models decompose trial-level behavior into meaningful processes. Thus, the present approach may provide a more informative understanding of adolescent cognition and environmental covariates.

Consistent with our hypotheses, we found positive associations between participation in extracurriculars, both at the level of the absolute number of activities participated in during the past year and time spent on categories of activities, and differences in EEA at baseline. These findings are in line with previous studies that find positive associations of both physical and non-physical activities with executive functioning (Ayuso-Moreno et al., 2026; Bidzan-Bluma & Lipowska, 2018; Herting & Chu, 2017; Jamey et al., 2024; Rodriguez-Gomez & Talero-Gutiérrez, 2022). Previous studies have often focused on associations between specific types of activities and specific cognitive functions, for example, regular exercise and inhibitory control (Herting & Chu, 2017; Lee et al., 2014). It is therefore interesting to find that task-general executive functioning, as indexed by EEA, is positively related to participation in extracurricular activities in the past year regardless of whether it is physical or social, even when adjusting for SES. These results are consistent with the idea that extracurriculars provide opportunities for cognitive stimulation outside the household and classroom, and, in turn, such environmental enrichment may affect task-general cognitive efficiency. For some of the tested variables, the model implied that these differences persisted into mid-adolescence, such as in the case of the number activities, which may be a marker of experiencing more complexity in environmental inputs. While the neurodevelopmental pathways are unknown, environmental variability may enhance and prolong windows of brain plasticity, thereby promoting a longer trajectory of functional network segregation and more efficient cortical networks later in life (Sydnor et al., 2026; Tooley et al., 2021). Only a few studies have investigated the neural basis of EEA (Weigard & Sripada, 2021), however, one study found that greater EEA from a single task, and thereby likely higher trait EEA, was related to greater structural and functional connectivity in the frontoparietal network in adults (Brosnan et al., 2020). Weigard and Sripada (2021) propose that EEA is influenced by an array of biological and contextual factors, such as brain network properties, and may serve as a watershed node, a prominent driver of downstream effects such as differences in complex executive tasks, simple perceptual choices, and inhibitory control. From this perspective, enriched environments may influence brain development and thereby EEA, which in turn may have downstream consequences across cognitive domains. However, it is important to note that our results are associative and do not elucidate the direction of tested effects. For example, in the present study, while it is plausible that extracurriculars contribute to cognitive growth, it is equally plausible that youth with higher executive functioning pursue, even indirectly, cognitively stimulating environments. Also, both of these dynamics are likely to unfold in a complex process across development.

To our surprise, we found indications of negative associations between time spent on physical activities and EEA development in the SES-adjusted model. This is in contrast to the fairly consistent positive associations observed between physical activity and cognitive functioning (Ayuso-Moreno et al., 2026; Bidzan-Bluma & Lipowska, 2018; Erickson et al., 2019; Esteban-Cornejo et al., 2015; Haverkamp et al., 2020; Herting & Chu, 2017). However, a recent study in the ABCD sample found that the duration of physical activities was not associated with improvements in various domains such as inhibitory control and information processing speed, and negatively associated with reading and language ability and verbal intellect at the 2-year follow-up (Zheng et al., 2026). As Zheng et al. (2026) note, the deviation from previous literature may be related to the types of physical activities, as many studies examine controlled activities such as walking or training in classrooms, rather than the diverse range of physical activities that adolescents may engage in. There are also studies that implicate less favorable links between cognition and physical activity. For example, Esteban-Cornejo et al. (2015) found that adolescents who maintained moderate levels of physical activity tended to show higher cognitive performance, whereas high levels of physical activity were associated with lower cognitive performance compared to low activity participants. Also, these differences in EEA may not be related to physical activity itself, but rather to factors related to the time commitment of such activities, which may come at the cost of less after-school supervision or negative effects of scheduling demands (Farb & Matjasko, 2012). Further, spending more time on both social and non-social physical activities was associated with higher EEA at baseline, and the negative association with slopes may be related to EEA differences evening out in mid-adolescence, rather than less favorable cognitive development. As shown by the sex-stratified analyses (Table S9), the negative associations with slopes were only present in males, and there were significant sex differences in the association between time spent on social physical activities and EEA slopes. Even in the unadjusted models, males showed a negative association with EEA slopes whereas females showed a significant positive association. Such sex differences may be related to differences in developmental timing, such as pubertal maturation, and sensitivity to environmental exposures (Blakemore et al., 2010; Blakemore & Choudhury, 2006; Colom & Lynn, 2004). We note that the associations were very small, and we only considered participation at baseline. In particular, the present study did not consider changes in extracurriculars or other lifestyle factors that are likely to have occurred within the six years of development. Future research using this sample would benefit from examining longitudinal, co-occurring changes in physical activities and cognition, as well as accounting for other factors that may play a role, such as pubertal development, stress, sleep, and parental expectations.

A strength of the present study is the use of EEA, a novel, task-general measure of executive functioning formally defined in computational models. To our knowledge, the present study provides the longest time span of longitudinal EEA, with four assessments over approximately six years in a developmental sample. The latent growth model showed non-linear changes in EEA, with the most change occurring during early adolescence. This is in line with previous studies showing rapid nonlinear executive functioning development in adolescence, both with conventional performance metrics (Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023) and with task-specific drift rates from the n-back task (Frogner et al., 2026). Also, EEA showed reasonable developmental stability (ICC(3,1) = 0.554). This is comparable to some (Schubert et al., 2016) and lower than other (Lerche et al., 2020) estimates of test-retest reliability for task-specific drift rates. Remarkably, it is very close to the estimate from a similar study that examined EEA in participants aged 9-17 years with two assessments approximately one year apart and found a test-retest reliability of 0.53 (Tomlinson et al., 2025). This stability is notable given that adolescence, and especially early adolescence, is a developmental period with rapid cognitive development and substantial individual differences in developmental timing and tempo (Blakemore & Choudhury, 2006; Frogner et al., 2026; Kolb et al., 2012; Larsen & Luna, 2018; Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023). Further, our hierarchical modeling approach is likely to underestimate the developmental change taking place as it did not account for repeated measurements or age. This compresses between-and within-subject variation through hierarchical shrinkage toward the population mean (Efron & Morris, 1977; Katahira, 2016). Therefore, the estimates of change are likely conservative. Although it was too computationally intensive for a sample of the present size, a joint estimation approach would likely improve reliability and yield estimates closer to true cognitive change (Brown et al., 2020; Sullivan-Toole et al., 2022; Waltmann et al., 2022). Estimating longitudinal EEA with a hierarchical joint modeling approach conditioned on age or other covariates of interest (i.e. computational growth modeling) holds promise for future studies that wish to precisely model how behavioral processes develop (Haynes et al., 2026). Future research could compare these approaches; ours likely represents a conservative estimate of both developmental change and reliability.

Although it was not one of our main aims, we note that household SES was associated with both initial differences and developmental changes in EEA, whereas neighborhood deprivation was associated only with initial differences. Higher parental income and education are consistently associated with better cognitive performance in children, likely mediated by cognitive stimulation through caregiver involvement in learning, access to developmentally appropriate learning materials and toys, varied experiences, and a complex linguistic environment (Hackman et al., 2010; Hackman & Farah, 2009; Rakesh et al., 2024; Rosen et al., 2019). Similarly, a recent study found a negative association between cumulative material hardship in childhood and drift rates during adolescence, above and beyond childhood experiences of violence exposure and social deprivation (Zhang et al., 2026). Also, neighborhood deprivation, measured with the ADI, has been linked to lower executive function performance in children and adolescents (Kalb et al., 2023). Households facing material hardship and neighborhood deprivation may face greater challenges in providing cognitively stimulating environments, highlighting the importance of external arenas for cognitive growth. Links between household and neighborhood SES and EEA may also be driven by SES-dependent sleep deprivation and stress (Marek et al., 2026). On the other hand, our SES-related results may instead reflect normative adaptation to more adverse conditions, that is, cognitive styles adapted to more unpredictable or resource-scarce environments rather than impaired executive functioning as measured with conventional cognitive tasks (DeJoseph et al., 2024). The hidden talents framework emphasizes that individuals living in low SES conditions develop stress-adapted skills that are specialized toward local environmental conditions (Ellis et al., 2023; Frankenhuis & de Weerth, 2013; Torrance, 1968). Such skills may go undetected on static tests with ecologically irrelevant content, as opposed to assessments across settings (Frankenhuis & de Weerth, 2013). These different accounts highlight important avenues for future research, which would benefit from using ecologically relevant assessments in real-world settings. Computational models, such as the DDM, may also serve as valuable tools for providing more accurate, informative, and reliable assessments of cognitive abilities (Vermeent et al., 2026).

The present study has limitations. First, our computational modeling and statistical analyses followed a multi-stage approach. By separately modeling drift rates, EEA, and growth trajectories with covariates, we lose valuable information such as uncertainty tied to estimates. The drift rate point estimates, and the EEA estimates, are treated as true parameter values in downstream statistical analyses. To circumvent this issue, a joint modeling approach should be used, although this would have been too computationally demanding for the present study. Second, the SST DDM included only go trials and did not model stop trials, because of known design issues related to stop trials and incorrect calculation of accuracies in the SST used in the ABCD Study (Bissett et al., 2021). An alternative would have been to use a recently developed DDM that incorporates evidence accumulation for go choices on stop trials as a direct function of the stop-signal delay length, which is specialized for the design of the ABCD SST (Weigard et al., 2023). By incorporating this specialized DDM, we could have obtained EEA estimates that better captured inhibitory control processes. Third, performance on all tasks was generally very high, with few error trials. This was especially pronounced in the Flanker task, which also had very few trials in total. This led to challenges in identifying stable parameter estimates for some models, although we note that qualitative model fit was good, as seen in the posterior predictive plots, and parameters were highly recoverable. Lastly, there was little variance in the categorized activities, especially for time spent on social non-physical activities, and all of the categorized activity variables showed substantial positive skewness. We tried to reduce the leverage of the highest scores on the results by 𝑙𝑜𝑔(𝑥 + 1) transforming the variables. As reported in Table S5, the transformed and untransformed predictors yielded somewhat different results. This should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

Overall, the present study elucidates interindividual variability in EEA trajectories, a computational underpinning of task-general executive functioning, and provides insight into links with environmental experiences outside of the household. We find that participation in a variety of extracurriculars, which may be indicative of increased environmental complexity, and spending more time on extracurricular activities, across categories, are associated with higher EEA at baseline, including after adjusting for household SES and neighborhood deprivation. Most of these differences in EEA remained stable across development. Although effect sizes were small, the present study underscores extracurriculars as potentially enriching environments associated with higher executive functioning. executive function.
