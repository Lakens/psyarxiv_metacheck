Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction Measurement-Based Care

Measurement-based care (MBC), also known as routine-outcome monitoring (ROM) or feedback-informed treatment, involves (a) routinely collecting patient data on symptoms, outcomes, or processes, (b) providing feedback to the therapist, (c) reviewing this information with the patient, and (d) collaboratively adjusting the treatment plan as needed (Lutz et al., 2022). Systematic reviews have demonstrated modest, incremental improvements in treatment outcomes (g ≈ 0.15), with larger effects when clinical support tools are implemented in cases of insufficient treatment response (g ≈ 0.36-0.53) (Barkham et al., 2023; de Jong et al., 2021).

Recent technical innovations are shaping new developments in MBC. While data are typically collected at each consultation, intensive longitudinal data approaches, such as ambulatory assessment, experience sampling, and ecological momentary assessment, are increasingly employed (Myin-Germeys et al., 2024). These enable even deeper integration of data into cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) treatments, for example by improving case conceptualization (Scholten et al., 2022) or supporting self-monitoring or therapeutic homework assignments through interactive feedback systems (Bringmann et al., 2025). Traditionally, MBC has relied on comparison between individual patient data and a reference group for interpretation. By incorporating single-case experimental designs, in which an individual provides baseline data as reference and the start of an intervention is randomized, changes can be attributed more confidently to the intervention itself rather than to external factors such as seasonal variation or spontaneous recovery (Vlaeyen et al., 2020). In clinical practice, the aim is less to isolate causal variables than to apply the logic of such designs to identify and monitor maladaptive mechanisms and treatment targets, and to test whether these change in response to treatment, thereby guiding treatment planning and improving therapeutic change for an individual patient (Kazdin, 2019).

Barriers to Implementation

Despite the benefits of MBC, its implementation in routine practice faces significant barriers (Barkham et al., 2023; Deane et al., 2024). For example, in a survey of 159 providers across three U.S. youth mental health care services (Jensen-Doss et al., 2025), MBC measures were administered to only 40-60% of patients on average. Related practices, such as reviewing scores and graphs, discussing results in supervision, providing feedback to patients, and using results to inform treatment planning, were reported at even lower rates, falling in the 1-39% range. Implementation frameworks emphasize the importance of understanding the factors that influence adoption at multiple levels, including patient and provider characteristics, organizational context, and the broader healthcare system in which mental health care is delivered (Damschroder et al., 2022; Scholten et al., 2025).

To date, MBC has primarily been studied in Western healthcare systems within outpatient or counseling settings (de Jong et al., 2021). In these contexts, a recent systematic review of 23 quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods studies found that patients and providers value MBC for enhancing communication, improving insight, and supporting more structured data-based treatment planning (Dey et al., 2025). Yet, the review also identified several concerns that might hinder uptake, including limited ability to capture clinical complexity, potential reporting biases, negative effects on the therapeutic alliance, time constraints, insufficient training, unclear guidance for interpretation, and issues related to data use and privacy. Although intensive longitudinal data may address some concerns, for example, by providing a more accurate, real-time representation of a patient's experience in daily life, some challenges such as insufficient training and unclear guidance for data interpretation may persist or even intensify, as suggested by qualitative findings (De Thurah et al., 2025; Weermeijer et al., 2023).

Given these barriers, it is recommended to employ implementation strategies when introducing MBC into a specific setting (Lewis et al., 2019; Whitmyre et al., 2024). Recommended strategies include training providers, incorporating stakeholder feedback, using measurement feedback systems, and considering potential disparities in access to and engagement with MBC when planning implementation efforts (Whitmyre et al., 2024). As a critical first step, feasibility and pilot studies can assess contextual and environmental factors to inform the development and refinement of an implementation strategy tailored to a specific setting (Pearson et al., 2020).

Study Aims

This feasibility study had two aims. The first aim was to evaluate the feasibility of an ESMbased SCED infrastructure for MBC in an outpatient university setting. Feasibility was assessed through iterative rounds of testing, with findings from each round informing subsequent refinements of the infrastructure in accordance with the principles of agile research (Wilson et al., 2018). The second aim was to identify and explore perceived barriers to the implementation of the infrastructure and to use these insights to inform the refinement subsequent implementation strategies.

The study was conducted as part of the preparation for the broader implementation of the infrastructure in a subsequent pilot phase (Scholten et al., 2024). In addition to iterative optimization, stakeholder engagement constituted a second key implementation principle, with stakeholders being actively involved throughout the development process to ensure that the infrastructure addressed their needs (Chinsen et al., 2025; Manafò et al., 2018). Details of the stakeholder engagement are reported elsewhere (Herzog et al., submitted). Beyond this specific implementation context, the study contributes to emerging research on barriers to MBC implementation, particularly recent innovations leveraging intensive longitudinal data (De Thurah et al., 2025; Dey et al., 2025; Soyster et al., 2023; Weermeijer et al., 2023).

Method

Study Design

This feasibility study employed a single-site, non-randomized design. The primary focus was to implement and evaluate the feasibility of the infrastructure with adult patients and therapists.

Quantitative feasibility outcomes were collected at three time points (post-baseline, post-diagnostic, and approximately six weeks after intervention start) complemented by qualitative semi-structured interviews across two successive cohorts. Key changes between cohorts included the introduction of individualized items and clearer variable labels to improve visualization interpretability. Ultimately, the procedures were deemed appropriate for a one-year pilot study. An additional iteration with adolescents, caregivers, and therapists assessed the infrastructure's applicability across age groups.

Key changes included adapting the ESM survey to adolescents' needs. The entire implementation process was ethically approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Department of Psychology at the RPTU University Kaiserslautern-Landau (#LEK-535) and outlined in a previously published study protocol (Scholten et al., 2024). A detailed analysis plan has been preregistered in the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/8k5cj). Guidelines for implementation feasibility studies were followed (Pearson et al., 2020).

Treatment Setting

The study was conducted at the outpatient clinic of the Department of Psychology at the RPTU University Kaiserslautern-Landau, which has a treatment unit for adults and a unit for children and adolescents. The adult unit has approximately 250 new admissions per year. A wide range of diagnoses are treated, with the three main diagnoses being depression, anxiety, and adjustment disorders. The unit for children and adolescents also has approximately 250 new admissions per year, with the three main diagnoses being anxiety, depression, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. In the youth unit, caregivers are regularly involved in treatment (usually every fourth session on average), depending on the age of patients. Both units train students after their master's degree to become licensed psychotherapists with a focus on CBT. Trainees receive theoretical input and regular supervision from licensed psychotherapists. Both units are also regularly involved in recruiting and treating patients for clinical trials.

Common diagnostic procedures in both units include an intake interview by a licensed psychotherapist and the administration of a structured clinical interview. General and disorder-specific questionnaires are administered at intake, during the diagnostic phase, every 10 therapy sessions, at the end of treatment and at the 6-month follow-up. In the unit for adults, the Symptom Checklist 90 (SCL-90; Franke, 2014) is used as indicator for general mental health problems. The unit for children and adolescents uses the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL 6-18R; Döpfner et al., 2014), the Youth Self Report (YSR 11-18R; Döpfner et al., 2014) and, for research purposes, the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ, self-and parent-report; Goodman, 1997). The ESM-based SCED infrastructure is integrated into this existing routine diagnostic process.

Recruitment

Patients and caregivers were recruited from the outpatient waiting list. Only patients and caregivers who agreed to be contacted for research studies were called by research assistants. Upon starting therapy, the therapist was also contacted. During the telephone call, individuals were informed of the purpose and procedure of the feasibility study. Written informed consent was obtained before participation. Individuals could decide independently whether to participate in the study.

Inclusion Criteria and Sample Size Considerations

As we plan to apply the ESM-based SCED infrastructure to all outpatients in the future, we did not formulate specific inclusion criteria, but only excluded people with severe mental health problems, such as acute psychosis or suicidal tendencies. Adolescents could participate at the age of 12 and above. In line with previous studies (Bartels et al., 2022), we aimed to recruit 6-12 patient-therapist dyads per cohort, a sample size considered sufficient to provide estimates with adequate precision. This sample size is also consistent with recommendations for interview studies (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022).

The ESM-based SCED Infrastructure

The ESM-based SCED infrastructure is designed to complement traditional diagnostic methods with additional ESM surveys. Beyond information on symptoms and functioning, this approach is intended to provide additional day-to-day insight into treatment changes while empowering patients, supporting self-management, promoting goal-directed assessment and shared decision-making (Myin-Germeys et al., 2024), fostering therapist self-reflection in line with the scientist-practitioner model (Kazdin, 2019), and generating longitudinal data for broader research (Wright & Zimmermann, 2019).

ESM Survey and Protocol

As emotion regulation is considered to be an important transdiagnostic mechanism (Bjureberg, 2026; Palmieri et al., 2022), an ESM survey was developed to assess different aspects of emotion regulation (Kolb-Grunder et al., submitted). We selected this standardized set of measures to simplify implementation and support automatization. Based on stakeholder input, an additional interview guide informed the formulation of problem-and goal-focused idiographic ESM measures for each patient in the second adult cohort (Galden et al., in preparation). The ESM protocol was developed collaboratively between the research team and patient and therapist stakeholders, based on systematic guidelines for methodological decision-making in ESM studies (Janssens et al., 2018). The ESM survey was administered at the end of the day, with semi-random beeps scheduled during a personalized evening time frame. Participants could also use additional event-based surveys to report on relevant situations using a home button which triggers a measurement burst with five follow-up surveys within the next hour to monitor mood. The m-Path software was chosen for its user-friendly ESM data collection and its flexible visualization of patient responses (Mestdagh et al., 2023).

SCED Procedures

The research team initially proposed the SCED procedure based on the Risk of Bias in N-of-1 Trials (RoBiNT) scale, a scale for assessing the methodological rigor of SCED studies (Tate et al., 2013).

Research assistants supported the onboarding phase by helping participants log into m-Path, introducing the ESM survey using handouts, and presenting sample dashboards to enhance compliance. Within the SCED framework, ESM surveys were administered for two weeks following the first appointment (Phase A: baseline), during diagnostic sessions (Phase B: diagnostics), and during initial treatment sessions (Phase C: intervention). After each SCED phase, research assistants created individualized dashboards (the equivalent of mood bars in m-Path) that were discussed with patients, caregivers, and therapists. Our intention was for participants to actively use m-Path, for example, to discuss critical situations, reflect on coping patterns, and monitor therapeutic change goals.

Feasibility Outcomes Measures

Quantitative feasibility outcomes measures were administered via SoSci Survey (Leiner, 2024).

Therapists completed feasibility outcome assessments for each patient separately, as their perceptions of feasibility could vary across patients. To obtain a balanced assessment, participants who had discontinued diagnostic procedures were also encouraged to provide ratings of their experience. In the child and adolescent unit, acceptability outcomes were introduced only from the diagnostic phase onward, as detailed below, to minimize questionnaire burden during the initial phase. Approximately six weeks after the start of the intervention phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted to explore perceived barriers qualitatively. ESM data was used to gain further insight into adherence and compliance.

Quantitative Measures

Usability

System Usability Scale (SUS).

An adapted version of the System Usability Scale (SUS) was used to assess perceived usability of the ESM-based SCED infrastructure. The SUS is a 10-item questionnaire designed to evaluate system usability from the end-user perspective (Brooke, 1996) and has also been applied to clinical interventions (Lyon et al., 2020). Participants completed two adapted versions: one assessing the usability of the tool m-Path as an app (e.g., 'I think I would like to use this tool frequently') and one assessing the dashboard visualizing ESM data (e.g., 'I think I would like to use this dashboard frequently'). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to5 = strongly agree), yielding total scores from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better usability. Internal consistency as indicated by Cronbach's alpha was good (SUS Tool: α = .83, SUS Dashboard: α = .89). SUS scores were interpreted following prior research: ≤55 indicates not acceptable and ≥87.5 excellent usability (Bangor et al., 2008). We aimed for neutral (>55) to good values (>70).

User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ).

The UEQ assessed usability and user experience across 26 semantic differentials (e.g., 'complicated' versus 'easy') rated on a 7-point scale (-3 to +3) (Laugwitz et al., 2008). Internal consistency as indicated by Cronbach's alpha across UEQ subscales ranged from poor to excellent (Attractiveness: α = .91, Stimulation: α = .87, Perspicuity: α = .82, Novelty: α = .78, Efficiency: α = .67, and Dependability: α = .54). Scores were benchmarked against existing product datasets using the UEQ analysis tool (Schrepp et al., 2017; https://www.ueq-online.org/). Ranging from -3 (most negative evaluation) to +3 (most positive evaluation), values between -0.8 and 0.8 indicate neutral evaluations, >0.8 positive, and <-0.8 negative. We aim for neutral to positive scores.

Acceptability

Adoption of Information Technology Innovation (AITI).

Three subscales of the adapted AITI measure (Moore & Benbasat, 1991) were used: Relative Advantage (e.g. 'Using the tool improves the quality of the treatment'), Compatibility (e.g., 'Using the tool is completely compatible with my current situation'), and Ease of Use (e.g., 'Overall, I believe that the tool is easy to use'). Internal consistency as indicated by Cronbach's alpha ranged from good to excellent (Relative Advantage: α = .92, Compatibility: α = .81, Ease of Use: α = .75). The 11 items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = extremely disagree to7 = extremely agree). Mean scores >4.5 were considered indicative of favorable evaluations.

Self-developed Items.

Participants rated acceptability ('The tool/dashboard is acceptable for clinical practice') and utility ('The tool/dashboard is helpful for clinical practice') on 4 self-generated items using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to5 = strongly agree). Mean scores >3.5 were considered indicative of sufficient acceptability and appropriateness (Schleider et al., 2021).

Adherence and Compliance

Beyond retention rates in the daily ESM assessments, adherence to SCED procedures and compliance rates were examined. Adherence was defined as completing at least three daily surveys in each of the three SCED phases, a threshold derived from the RoBiNT Scale, which assigns 0 points when fewer than three data points are available (Tate et al., 2013). Adherence rates exceeding 70% were considered satisfactory for adult participants. For youth, rates above 50% were deemed acceptable.

ESM compliance rates were calculated per SCED phase among adherent participants, with rates >70% considered sufficient (Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). After each ESM survey, patients also rated burden (0 = neutral to10 = disturbing) and perceived helpfulness (0 = neutral to10 = helpful).

Qualitative Interviews

While quantitative data can identify usability problems, it provides limited insight into their nature (Lyon et al., 2020). To address this, research assistants conducted audio-recorded semistructured interviews. The interview guide covered onboarding (e.g., 'How can we improve the onboarding process?'), use of the m-Path app (e.g., 'How did you get on with the m-Path app?'), feasibility of the ESM protocol (e.g., 'Would you change anything about the survey, including the frequency of assessment?'), potential negative effects (e.g., 'Did you experience any negative side effects? How could negative side effects be reduced?'), dashboard clarity (e.g., 'Did you use the dashboard? What would you like to change about the dashboard?), and clinical utility (e.g., 'Was the app part of the treatment/supervision? What could be changed to maximize its utility?').

Data Analysis

A flow chart was used to illustrate participant progression through the study. Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized for patients, caregivers and therapists. Quantitative indicators of usability, acceptability, adherence, and compliance, derived from questionnaires and ESM data, were descriptively computed and either related to benchmark data or evaluated against predefined criteria (see Supplemental Material S1) (Mellor et al., 2023). Data across measurement points were categorized using a grading scheme, with Grade A indicating that criteria were met at all groups and time points, Grade B indicating partial fulfillment, and Grade C indicating no fulfillment.

The qualitative interview data were analyzed using structuring content analysis (Kuckartz & Rädiker, 2022). This pragmatic, systematically interpretive approach was used to summarize, organize, and interpret qualitative data based on a coding framework (Kuckartz & Rädiker, 2022). It was applied here to systematically identify perceived barriers and inform implementation strategies. An inductive approach was used to capture the user experience. The analysis followed several steps. First, the research question was defined ('What barriers do patients (including caregivers) and therapists experience when going through the ESM-based SCED procedures?'). Second, interviews were automatically transcribed using MAXQDA's AI-supported transcription (VERBI Software GmbH, 2025) and checked for accuracy. Third, the first author (LS) reviewed the first cohort and generated initial coding ideas. Fourth, this preliminary system was applied to data from subsequent cohorts by a research assistant trained by the first author, with notes on required refinements. Based on these notes, the initial category system was collaboratively refined through discussion. Fifth, the final category system was agreed upon through consensus and applied to the full dataset by the research assistant. Thirty percent of the interviews were double-coded by the first author, allowing for the assessment of interrater agreement. AI-assisted English paraphrases of the German transcripts were used to illustrate category descriptions. The authors reviewed these paraphrases and take full responsibility for their accuracy and fidelity to the original German content. Finally, category frequencies were analyzed separately for patients (including caregivers) and therapists to explore differences in perceived barriers and inform more targeted implementation strategies.

Both coders were White women with a background in clinical psychology and prior involvement in the implementation project. The research assistant was a master's student in clinical psychology, while the first author was a postdoctoral clinical researcher and licensed CBT psychotherapist. Throughout the coding process, they met regularly to discuss discrepancies, clarify coding decisions, and align their interpretation of the coding scheme. Given their prior involvement in the project, they also reflected on whether their perspectives might have led them to perceive implementation barriers less critically.

Participant Flow and Adherence

In the adult unit, patients were screened across two recruitment periods (May-June and November-December 2024). Of 20 enrolled patients, four discontinued (80% retention; M = 34.4 yrs, SD = 8.9, range = 23-56). Reasons included difficulties integrating assessments into daily life (n = 1), therapist non-participation (n = 1), and unspecified reasons (n = 2). Eleven patients met the adherence criterion (55%), below the predefined threshold. Of 12 invited therapists, four declined, mainly due to time constraints. Affected patients remained eligible and received support from a research assistant.

The remaining patients were treated by eight therapists (M = 31.4 yrs, SD = 8.4, range = 25-50).

In the child and adolescent unit, patients were also screened across two recruitment periods (November-December 2024 and May-June 2025). Of eight enrolled patients, four discontinued (50% retention; M = 14.3 yrs, SD = 1.9, range = 12-16). Reasons included therapist non-participation (n = 1) and unspecified reasons (n = 3). Four participants met the adherence criterion (50%), reaching the predefined threshold. Of 11 enrolled caregivers, four discontinued (63.6% retention rate; M = 43.2 yrs, SD = 7.3, range = 33-54). Reasons included a perceived mismatch between the tool and the respective problem and therapist non-participation (n = 1), as well as unspecified reasons (n = 3). Four caregivers met the adherence criterion (36.4%), below the predefined threshold. Of 14 invited therapists, five declined, mainly due to time constraints. The remaining participants were treated by nine therapists (M = 32.3 yrs, SD = 6.5, range = 27-46).

Usability

Both SUS Tool and SUS Dashboard scores exceeded the predefined usability threshold (> 55 on a 0-100 scale), with observed means ranging from 68.50 to 84.50. Similarly, all UEQ subscale scores were above the a priori acceptability criterion (> -0.8 on a -3 to +3 scale), with mean values ranging from 0.49 to 2.32. Relative to benchmark data, scores aggregated across participants and assessment time points were consistently above average. Overall, these findings indicate good usability of the ESMbased SCED infrastructure.

Acceptability

The AITI subscales exceeded the predefined criterion (> 4.5 on a 1-7 scale), with mean scores ranging from 4.63 to 5.93 across groups. However, some confidence intervals included values below this threshold. Phase-specific analyses indicated that the criterion was generally met in Phase A but decreased over time, mainly in the first cohort in the adult unit. This pattern is illustrated for the AITI subscale Relative Advantage (Figure 1). A similar pattern was observed for the self-developed acceptability and utility items (M = 3.57-4.40; threshold > 3.5 on a 1-5 scale), with confidence intervals occasionally falling below the threshold. Overall, acceptability criteria were met on average but not consistently across groups and phases.

Compliance

Mean compliance exceeded the predefined criterion (70%) for adult patients (83.77%) and caregivers (82.74%), but not for youth patients (58.33%). However, compliance generally declined over time across patient groups (see S3-S4). Daily assessments were rated as minimally burdensome (M = 1.73-3.14). Yet, perceived usefulness was modest (M = 3.63-3.70).

Discussion

Aiming to prepare for broader implementation, the present study examined the feasibility of an ESM-based SCED infrastructure for MBC and explored perceived barriers. Overall, usability and acceptability were good and comparable across adult and child/adolescent units, supporting implementation in both settings. However, acceptability was not consistent across all time points and showed a tendency to decline over the course of treatment, particularly in the first adult cohort. While adherence across SCED phases was below the predefined criterion for adult patients and caregivers, compliance among adherent patients and caregivers met the criterion, except for the adolescent group. Perceived burden rated at the end of each ESM survey was low, whereas perceived helpfulness was limited. Qualitative data provided by interviews revealed distinct barriers from a patient and therapist perspective: Patients and caregivers primarily reported barriers to responding, with difficulties in reflecting on personal experiences most frequently mentioned, followed by response burden and challenges of integrating responses into daily life. Therapists, in contrast, mainly reported barriers to treatment integration, most frequently limited resources, as well as limited utility for guiding change and uncertainty in interpretation.

Patient-specific Barriers

Consistent with broader critiques from a patient perspective that standard MBC instruments inadequately capture clinical complexity and subjective experiences (Dey et al., 2025), patients and caregivers in the present feasibility trial most frequently reported difficulties in reflecting personal experiences as a key barrier, when using a standardized ESM survey and schedule. An interesting observation was that, after introducing individualized items and clearer variable labels in the second adult cohort, acceptability seemed to have slightly improved. This suggests that individualizing ESM assessments may increase motivation and clinical relevance, consistent with recommendations for greater personalization from patients and clinicians in previous studies on the use of ESM in clinical practice (Bos et al., 2019; Piot et al., 2022; Weermeijer et al., 2023). Beyond individualized items (Lloyd et al., 2019), adaptive ESM platforms such as m-Path further support personalization by offering a selflearning function that allows patients to add response options, which are then automatically incorporated into subsequent assessments (Mestdagh et al., 2023). Responses to open-ended question can furthermore contextualize quantitative results, thereby facilitating their interpretation (Bringmann et al., 2025). Accordingly, our subsequent implementation study will incorporate these personalization features wherever feasible.

In line with previous studies on the use of ESM in clinical practice (Bos et al., 2019 (Bos et al., , 2020;; Piot et al., 2022), additional barriers reported from a patient perspective mainly included response burden and challenges of responding in everyday life. While some limitations may be easily addressed, for example by extending the response time window, patient feedback in interviews suggested that burden may also be outweighed by increasing perceived benefit, for instance through integrating collected data into therapy sessions to promote insight and awareness, but also to discuss and derive concrete action steps together with the therapist for dealing with burdensome everyday situations. In contrast, completing the ESM survey alone was unsurprisingly perceived as of limited usefulness.

Therapist-specific Barriers

From a therapist perspective, the main barrier to treatment integration was a lack of resources, including time, workload, technical infrastructure, and potential interference with more important treatment goals, consistent with reported barriers to MBC in general (Dey et al., 2025) and ESM in particular (Piot et al., 2022; Soyster et al., 2023; Weermeijer et al., 2023). Several of these issues can again be easily addressed, for example through automated summaries and visualizations that can be implemented using m-Path (Mestdagh et al., 2023). Thereby, a recent experimental study found that simpler visualizations of intensive longitudinal data, such as annotated time series, were preferred over network visualizations, mainly because they were perceived as easier to interpret and providing more contextual information (Razum et al., 2026). Thus, to reduce therapist burden and facilitate treatment integration, we complemented automated time series and other visualizations (e.g., word clouds and phase comparisons) with therapeutic questions as headings and brief descriptions to enhance their clinical meaningfulness for use in the subsequent implementation trial.

Despite this relatively simple automated solution, a key challenge remains to ensure that the ESMbased SCED infrastructure is not experienced as an additional administrative burden but as a tool that meaningfully supports the therapeutic process. In this regard, our findings also underscore the importance of training therapists in the use of such ESM-based SCED systems, in line with previous studies (Dey et al., 2025; Piot et al., 2022; Soyster et al., 2023; Weermeijer et al., 2023). Therapists play a pivotal role in ensuring that MBC systems generate not only large amounts of data but also clinically meaningful information that is regularly reviewed and integrated into the therapeutic process (Herzog et al., 2023; Jensen-Doss et al., 2025; Piccirillo et al., 2026). Accordingly, our future implementation trials will place particular emphasis on therapist engagement: Beyond mandatory introductory training, we offer additional free supervision and technical support, modest financial compensation for the additional workload, and assistance with digitizing existing worksheets for integration into the predefined ESM-based SCED procedure. Especially through personal exchange with therapists during training and supervision, we see an opportunity to address individual concerns and barriers while drawing on their hands-on experience to explore patient-specific applications. Beyond monitoring treatment progress, ESM-based SCED may offer broader opportunities for MBC. For example, these tools could support different CBT modules through feedback systems, facilitate self-monitoring, and enable the evaluation of homework assignments (Bringmann et al., 2025). Another application could be the integration of just-in-time interventions (Hsu et al., 2025). In combination with methodological considerations inherent to SCED (Kazdin, 2019; Vlaeyen et al., 2020), these individual components have the potential to be systematically visualized and analyzed together with the patient, thereby strengthening collaborative case conceptualization and supporting transfer to everyday life. We hope that these implementation strategies will not only facilitate therapist uptake but also indirectly promote sustained patient engagement throughout treatment.

Limitations and Constraints on Generality

Several limitations merit attention. First, a key limitation is that, although feasibility was assessed in both an adult and a child/adolescent unit, the adult patient sample was relatively young (range 23-56) and thus do not capture barriers that may be experienced by older adults. Only three participants were born outside Germany. Thus, potential language-related or other culture-related barriers could not be examined. Similarly, the spectrum of mental health conditions was not fully represented. For the planned implementation trial, these aspects can be systematically addressed to examine whether and how the ESM-based SCED infrastructure needs to be adapted for broader and more heterogeneous clinical populations. Second, because the perspectives of individuals who discontinued the diagnostic procedure are particularly relevant for understanding barriers, we retained their data in the quantitative analyses and invited them to participate in the interviews. Nevertheless, only three of twelve individuals agreed to be interviewed. In addition, the perspectives of therapists who declined participation at the beginning, despite patients being willing to participate, are lacking. Third, as the ESM-based SCED infrastructure was new to all participants, research assistants supported each patient-therapist dyad in implementation and technical issues. However, this sometimes led to role ambiguity regarding whether the system was part of the study or routine therapy, including uncertainty about how results should be integrated into treatment, which may partly explain lower adherence rates. Fourth, as both units serve as training clinics, the therapists included in this study were relatively early in their professional career and may not fully represent therapists in routine clinical care. Future feasibility and implementation studies in non-university settings and other health care systems are therefore essential to study and facilitate broader implementation of MBC.

Conclusions

Overall, ESM-based SCED implementation appears feasible, but maintaining perceived acceptability and utility throughout treatment remains essential. Two key levers emerged that might support implementation, with potential relevance beyond our particular context. For patients, daily assessments may be more acceptable when they are personally meaningful, flexible, and clearly linked to the therapeutic process, helping to balance response burden with perceived benefit. For therapists, beyond initial training in using the ESM-based SCED infrastructure, implementation efforts may benefit from addressing limited resources and enabling ongoing exchange to discuss patient-specific applications. A central challenge in implementation appears ensuring that the ESM-based SCED infrastructure is perceived not as an additional administrative burden but as a tool that meaningfully supports the therapeutic process.
