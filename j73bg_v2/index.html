What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Youth use social media as a main source for news, political, and societal information, raising urgent questions about the content of such news-like videos, such as the presence of hate speech. Characterizing their actual exposure to news-like content and hate speech requires innovative methods that are not dependent on subjective recall or synthetic accounts. Here, youth (16-25 years old) donated their TikTok feed histories spanning October 2023 till May 2026, resulting in ~6.7M public videos for our analyses. For each video, we extracted the transcripts, descriptions, hashtags, and viewing durations. We then used largescale automated content classification with large language models (Llama 3.1-70B) to identify videos about news, politics, and major societal issues, such as climate change, migration, and war. We find that nearly all (98%) of the news-like content did not come from accounts that youth followed, suggesting that these were mostly algorithmic recommendations. The prevalence of news-like videos peaked during important political events such as elections. Only 13% originated from established news organizations present on TikTok. Instead, most of these videos came from niche content creators. These niche creators were more likely to produce hate speech videos in news-like content compared to other creators. Hate speech videos most often mentioned specific politicians, political parties, and minority groups. We cross checked the identified hate speech videoIDs with the EU transparency database and found that none were removed by the platform, suggesting inefficient content moderation. Our methods offer a scalable approach to directly measure youths' exposure to social media content. This provides a foundation for investigating how digital information environments shape societal perceptions and attitudes during adolescence and emerging adulthood.

[div-01]

As digital natives, youth acquire news primarily on social media, such as TikTok 1 , and indicate that they rely on digital platforms as their main source for news about politics and societal issues (i.e., news-like content) 2 . As social media platforms often lack the institutional oversight common in traditional media, this has resulted in global concerns regarding the quality of their news consumption, especially given online misinformation 3 , the circulation of hateful or extreme content 4 , and inauthentic activity, for example from bots 5 . Exposure to such content is not without consequence. For instance, in December 2024, the Romanian election was annulled due to evidence of foreign interference and manipulation campaigns on TikTok designed to mobilize youth to support a relatively unknown candidate 6 . More generally, a large synthesis of causal and correlational evidence finds consistent negative relationships between social media use and institutional trust, and positive relationships between social media use and polarization 7 . In such studies, self-reported screen time and content exposure remain the dominant method for studying the downstream effects of social media on society. However, characterizing youths' content exposure on social media requires more detailed methods and studying different platforms 8, 9 . For example, most prior research on social media content focused on platforms like X and Facebook 10 , whereas youth spend most of their time on short-form video platforms, such as TikTok. Moreover, even on the same platform, the content that youth watch is likely very different from the content that adults encounter. Currently, we do not know from which types of accounts youth primarily receive information about news, politics, and societal issues, and whether this is from traditional news institutions or more niche creators. It is also unknown which narratives they are systematically exposed to. In this paper, we address this important knowledge gap in youths' online information consumption about such news-like topics by studying their content exposure on TikTok using donated data in combination with natural language processing methods.

As youth, between ages 15-24 11 , grow into participating adults in a democratic society, they start to show an interest in politics and form a political identity 12, 13 . During this process, political socialization by parents plays a pivotal role 14 , but over time youth also rely more on information from their peers and other adults 15 , including influencers 16 . For example, research during COVID-19 has shown that influencers and the opinions they voice can have an impact on public opinion and adolescents' health behaviors, in this case amplifying anti-vaccine sentiments and promoting non-compliance with national regulations 17, 18.

Political content by influencers has also been shown to influence voting decisions, particularly for younger age groups, as indicated by surveys after the 2021 German elections 19 . Online campaigning is also believed to engage even the notoriously difficult-to-reach groups of youth who are not necessarily interested in politics and use social media primarily as a form of entertainment 19.

While social media may be a fruitful platform to stimulate civic engagement 20 , attention-based algorithms have also been shown to push content that is higher in arousal and emotionality 21, 22 . Youth may therefore consume news about politics and society through accounts that expose them to more extreme, shocking, or even hateful narratives. Exposure to hateful content can normalize or stimulate antagonism against specific groups or people 23 . Moreover, widespread exposure to extreme opinions can shift online social norms and create the impression that extreme opinions are more prevalent than they are 21, 24 . For example, exposure to hateful content against women is shown to stimulate the normalization of misogyny amongst young boys 25 . Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis on cross-sectional studies showed that selfreported exposure to extreme online content about real-world events is associated with violent radicalization of youth 26.

Given these concerns and possibly harmful consequences, there is an urgent need for the characterization of youths' consumption of news-like content on social media, specifically their exposure to hate speech. This means that we need to move beyond current dominant methods of self-reports as these rely heavily on the users' subjective recall and are limited by generational or age differences in the meaning of concepts such as "news" or "politics", which complicate the interpretation of the answers 27 . Some studies additionally ask youth to provide a small list of creators that they like most 1 . However, this method does not capture algorithmic recommendations. Another alternative approach is simulating youths' online behavior with mock accounts. Although analyzing the resulting data can identify algorithmic biases 28, 29 , their findings depend on the accuracy of the simulation, and small differences can substantially change content recommendations, making this method unviable to reveal what most young people see online 30 . In summary, we have virtually no accurate understanding of the content youth are exposed to. Here we overcome these limitations by analyzing TikTok data donations, donated by Dutch youth between ages 16 and 25. For each public video on youths' feeds, we extracted the transcripts, video descriptions, hashtags, and viewing durations. We then leveraged the recent advances in natural language processing to classify news-like content and hateful language therein as a method to characterize exposure in youth 9.

2.0 Results

In total, n = 147 Dutch youth (97 women, 2 of non-specified gender) between ages 16 and 25 (M = 20.40, SD = 2.03) donated their TikTok feed history. Most videos dated between October 2023 and May 2026 (see SI Appendix), which after extracting transcripts from all public videos on the feeds, resulted in ~6.7 million analyzed videos (M = 45445.68, SD = 39671.12).

The topic "news, politics, and societal issues" (i.e., news-like content) covered news, commentaries, and discussions related to political systems, such as governance, political actors, ideology, or elections, together with discussions on major societal issues such as climate change, migration, crime, war, or justice.

Celebrity or sports news was not included (see SI Appendix for the full prompt and definition). To identify these videos, we used few-shot classification with the Llama 3.1-70B base model, 4-bit quantization (see SI Appendix for the full performance details and computing environment). The model choice allowed us to work on Leiden University's local computing cluster without sharing the data with external parties, a prerequisite given the potential sensitivity of the data, and key to ensure replicability of the results. Fewshot classification was supplemented with a small set of hand-coded examples to the language model with the aim of improving classification performance on similar text. Classification choices and ratings were computed directly from the raw logits, minimizing output variability, and ensuring consistent results. Each classification task was validated on human-labeled validation sets to optimize the prompts and tested afterwards on separate randomly sampled test sets, resulting in high classification performance (F1topic = 0.80, Krippendorff's α topic = 0.79, ROC AUCtopic = 0.99).

2.1 Youths' Exposure to News-like Content

Most participants reported using social media as their primary source for news (76.74%). The prevalence of videos about news, politics, and societal issues on their feeds was incidental and showed strong individual differences (Median 5.34%, IQR = 3.56%, range = [1.01%, 21.39%]) (Figure 1a). This means that youth saw a median of approximately 126 videos about such news-like topics per month (IQR = 174.91, range = [0.46, 1035.98]) (Figure 1b). Nearly all of this news-like content came from accounts that they did not follow (Med = 98.40%, IQR = 5.88%, range = [37.42%, 100.00%] V = 3.00, p < .001) (Figure 1c), suggesting that most new-like content reached their feeds through algorithmic recommendations. If a participant watched a video for less than 3 seconds, we marked the video as not watched, based on Meta's guidelines for 'Reels' on Instagram. A multilevel survival analysis of the remaining watched videos showed that news-like videos were viewed for 1.19% longer than other videos, irrespective of their topic (HR = 0.981, 95% CI = [0.977, 0.985], z = -8.68, p < .001, σrandom intercept = 0.43).

Even though the overall effect is small, this is interesting as youth report that they mainly use social media for entertainment 31 . The finding was robust when correcting for video duration (HR = 0.988, 95% CI = [0.984, 0.993], z = -5.37, p < .001, σrandom intercept = 0.41) and may imply that youth are interested in newslike content, regardless of the typically longer average video durations in this topic. To understand what type of news-like creators post these news-like videos, we defined three types of news accounts on the platform: legacy accounts, alternative accounts, and niche accounts. Legacy accounts are established news creators with a known institution and produce content on traditional media platforms, such as TV, radio, or newspapers (e.g. CNN or the Dutch NOS). Alternative accounts were defined as popular social media news accounts, identified by retrieving the top 500 most watched nonlegacy news creators from the donated data and manually checking whether they indeed primarily produced news-like content (e.g., the Dutch geenstijl or the international channel dylan.page). In contrast, accounts were categorized as niche if they were less prevalent within our sample or if their content primarily covered videos about other non-news-like topics (see SI Appendix for the channel classifications). Examples of niche accounts are anonymous accounts that primarily post news-like content with AI-generated elements, or vloggers who sporadically comment on news, politics, or societal issues. Niche accounts had a lower follower count (Med followers = 21400), compared to alternative accounts (Med followers = 189300).

Strikingly, we found that niche accounts were by far most prevalent on youths' feeds, spanning on average 63.85% of their news-like content (SD = 12.79%, range = [29.30%, 100.00%], Figure 1d). Legacy news accounts in young adults' news feeds covered 13.28% on average (SD = 9.79%, range = [0.00%, 43.00%]), while alternative news accounts covered 22.83% (SD = 8.52%, range = [0.00%, 57.55%]).

2.2 The Prevalence of Hate in Youths' News-related Content

Next, we investigated hateful language use to identify to what extent youth were exposed to hate in news-like content. We used the LLM to classify intolerant and impolite language in news-like videos on a continuous 1-7 scale (RMSEIntolerance(1-7) = 0.58, Krippendorff's αintolerance = 0.62, RMSEImpoliteness(1-7) = 0.89, Krippendorff's αimpoliteness = 0.66). Intolerance was defined as discrimination against groups or against people based on their group membership, whereas impoliteness was defined as rude and vulgar language against individuals, regardless of their group membership 32, 33 . We operationalized hate speech as high intolerance and/or high impoliteness (>5 on the scale from 1-7), and tuned the prompt and few-shot examples to only classify videos as high on either scale whenever this language showed clear aspects of directed aggression, hostility, dehumanization, or hate (see SI Appendix for the prompts). As a result, the hate speech we focused on was generally rich in extreme group exclusion, direct calls to violence, or repeated verbal aggression (see SI Appendix for example transcripts). Videos that contained hate speech covered a median of 2.21% of youths' news-like content (IQR = 1.90%, range = [0.00%, 12.50%], ntotal videos = 11245) (Figure 2a). This means that youth received a median of approximately 3 hateful videos in their feed per month (IQR = 4.86, range = [0.00, 60.88]) (Figure 2b). Only 5.44% of participants had never come across hateful news. Nearly all the hateful content in youths' feeds came from accounts that they did not follow (Med = 100%, IQR = 3.94% range = [57.14%, 100.00%], V = 0.00, p < .001) (Figure 2c) and was therefore likely algorithmically recommended to them. Youth did not seem to scroll past hateful news faster or slower, compared to nonhateful news, as we found no evidence for viewing duration differences between them (HR = 1.006, 95% CI = [0.982, 1.03], z = 0.49, p = 0.62, σrandom intercept = 0.31) (Figure 2d). Niche accounts also posted more hateful content compared to legacy accounts (Mdiff = 0.82, 95% CI = [0.78, 0.86], p < .001) and alternative accounts (Mdiff = 0.65, 95% CI = [0.61, 0.69], p < .001; full model (F(2, 414) = 1377.85, p < .001). On average, 82.29% (SD = 16.83%) of all hateful videos originated from niche accounts, 0.34% (SD = 0.84%) from legacy, and 17.37% (SD = 16.79%) from alternative accounts (Figure 3a). 2.4 News and Hate Exposure is Tied to Major National and Global Events

News-related content reflects local and global current events, and could influence the course of important societal events, such as elections 6, 19 . It is therefore essential to study social media content longitudinally alongside the societal context as the correlation between social media use and psychological factors could dynamically vary over time 34 . We conducted a time series analysis of the feed histories spanning from November 2023 to January 2026. As expected, their exposure to news-like content peaked around important societal events (All p < .01), such as elections (Figure 4a). The clearest prevalence peaks were detected specifically during the 2023 and 2025 national elections in the Netherlands, as well as during the 2024 presidential election in the U.S. (see SI Appendix for the peak statistics). The prevalence of hate speech was generally noisier across time (Figure 4b). However, exposure to hate speech was significantly increased around the 2024 U.S. elections (p < .001; see SI Appendix for the peak statistics).

2.5 Platform Content Moderation

According to TikTok's trust and safety policies, protective action is taken against hate speech and hateful behavior on their platform. The European Union's transparency database contains videoIDs of content removed by the platform itself and includes a statement of reasons, including hate speech (https://transparency.dsa.ec.europa.eu/). To estimate the success of content moderation on the platform, we took two steps; First, for all donated videos that contained hate, we cross-checked the videoIDs with the EU's transparency database to check if they had been removed by the platform in the time between when we downloaded the data and our analyses. Second, the data donations also contained videoIDs that were removed or otherwise inaccessible due to, for example, being private videos. We also cross-checked those videoIDs with the transparency database as their presence in both datasets could indicate successful moderation of harmful content before we were able to download the data. For both methods we found no matches between the transparency database and the donated videoIDs, indicating that none of the hateful videos on youths' feeds were removed by the platform in the time between youths' exposure to those videos and the content analysis. Even though a complete estimate of the success of content moderation is impossible since we could not analyze videos that never entered youths' feeds, it is still striking that all of the identified hateful videos were not removed in the meantime. This emphasizes the major challenge that social media platforms face when identifying potential hateful videos given the sheer amount of daily content uploaded, amplified by the increased use of generative AI.

3.0 Discussion

This study aimed to characterize youths' exposure to news, politics, and societal issues on their TikTok feeds. We approached this by applying automated content classification with large language models to the transcripts of TikTok videos that were extracted from the feed histories donated by youth aged 16-25 years old. This resulted in a total of 6.7 million examined public videos. We found that nearly all content about this news-like topic did not come from accounts followed by users, suggesting that these were likely algorithmic recommendations. Most of the news-like videos came from niche creators. These niche accounts were more likely to produce hate speech videos compared to established news organizations or popular news content creators. Hate speech videos most often mentioned specific politicians, political parties, and minority groups. The prevalence of news-like videos peaked during important political events such as elections and coalition plans. Peaks in hate speech prevalence were less clearly tied to these events.

By cross-checking our data with the EU transparency database, we found that none of the hate speech videos on youths' feeds were removed by the platform.

Social media has largely replaced traditional media as a primary source of news for most youth and young adults, including for political and societal information 1, 35 . We found that most of this type of content reached youth through algorithmic recommendations instead of through their followed accounts. It is therefore crucial to investigate the news-like content that youth are really exposed to, instead of primarily focusing on specific popular content creators. Our data donations and automated classification approach offer a promising method to understand this content exposure and offer an opportunity to investigate the potential impact on youths' developing societal views. In addition to feed history investigation, viewing duration analyses can also be indicative of the content that youth pay attention to. In our study, this revealed that news-like videos were watched for somewhat longer compared to other videos, suggesting that youth do pay relatively more attention to news-like videos. Moreover, the prevalence of news-like content showed large individual differences, which may indicate that some youths actively seek this content on social media, or that they use social media as an expression of civic engagement and political or societal interest 36, 37.

Niche content creators were more likely to post hate speech videos compared to other content creators. Although the frequency of exposure to hate speech was generally modest, almost all youth in our study encountered hate speech content in news-like videos. We found no evidence for viewing duration differences between hateful and non-hateful news-like content, suggesting that the hate speech videos did not necessarily differ in their successfulness to capture attention. Importantly, regular exposure to hateful rhetoric against politicians or societal groups could have effects on the stability of democratic societies. For instance, a systematic review found that repeated exposure to online hate speech was associated with increased prejudice and that it can undermine safety, well-being, and political participation 4 . Frequent exposure and normalization of hate speech could erode the processes that protect against it, such as empathy and tolerant group norms 23 , which could increase affective polarization and undermine the functioning of democratic institutions 38, 39 . For youth, whose political views are still developing, hate speech towards politicians and political parties may have a normalizing effect and diminish trust in politicians. In prior work, it was shown that young teenagers who encounter more angry comments below news videos on social media do also perceive higher affective polarization in society, highlighting the need to monitor and mitigate exposure 40.

We found that none of the hate speech videos in our data were removed by the platform since having been viewed by our sample, including the videos spread during election times. This suggests insufficient content moderation, or differences in hate speech label agreement between the platform and our approach. Content moderation is, however, a major challenge for platforms. Human moderators are expensive and are only able to examine vastly less than the total volume of content that is uploaded daily 41.

Some videos may be copied and uploaded with a new video identifier which further contributes to the complexity. Automated moderation algorithms may also be challenged by the speed at which human language evolves online 42 . Advanced multimodal models can use contextual information and apply a supplied moderation policy, although demographic and lexical biases may persist 43 . The challenge of content moderation likely also increases as generative AI models can produce harmful content at scale, requiring larger and faster efforts and computational resources to detect harmful content 44.

The large amount of data (6.7M videos) in our study allowed for investigating exposure to newslike videos and hate speech across several years. A limitation, however, is that we could not access data from private accounts, and it is therefore possible that we missed some news-like content. Nevertheless, most private videos likely consist of content made by friends and are therefore less likely to be news-like.

In addition, some context may be inferred only from multimodal data. For example, videos without text but with derogatory song lyrics may have been missed. Donated social media data analyses may improve in the near future as multimodal data processing continues to evolve 43 . For these reasons, our findings should be interpreted as lower limits rather than absolute numbers.

Our methods and findings have clear implications for future studies and testing interventions. First, our results show that exposure to news-like topics dynamically fluctuate over time and showed clear peaks during political events. A single measurement point is therefore not representative for exposure to specific content. Longitudinal measures are needed to monitor exposure and to investigate the impact of social media content on beliefs, attitudes, and wellbeing. Prior research found that the correlation between social media use and wellbeing increased during political unrest, which further emphasizes the need for longitudinal measures and for taking the societal context into account 34, 46 . More specifically, our data donation methods could be combined with longitudinal surveys and measures during political and societal events to understand the impact on youths' political attitudes and how these factors interact with the political context over time. Second, the data donation approach can offer ways to test interventions. For example, the viewing duration data offer an ecologically valid way to test the effectiveness of educational interventions aimed at critical ignoring 47 . Finally, approaches aiming to depolarize feeds by unfollowing or reranking polarizing accounts, such as 48 , may only be fruitful for a very specific group of young individuals, as we found that most news-like content and hate speech reached youth through algorithmic recommendations instead of followed accounts.

Taken together, our findings show that understanding youths' digital news environments requires attention to the content that reaches their feeds, including from creators they do not follow. Across millions of videos, news-like content varied with political events, differed substantially between individuals, and exposed almost all participants to some hateful news-like videos. Although our findings do not establish effects on societal views, political attitudes or wellbeing, they characterize the everyday information environments in which such effects may emerge. Combining data donations with longitudinal surveys and intervention research can help establish when these exposures matter and which potential interventions are effective. Supporting youths' informed participation in democratic life requires attention both to their skills in navigating these environments and to the platform systems that shape what they encounter.

4.0 Methods

Participants (n = 147; age range 16-25) were recruited on Prolific, through TikTok advertisements, and Leiden University's research recruitment portal (SONA). Ethics approval was granted by the Leiden University Psychology Research Ethics Committee (CEP): 2023-11-30-I. MaV1-5120. Deterministic LLM classifications were obtained using the ChoiceLLM package 49 . The test and validation sets for the LLM few-shot classification tasks were labeled by the researchers and students from the Psychology MSc program at Leiden University. Disagreements between coders on a given label for a transcript were settled by members of the research group before running the classification on the test set (see SI Appendix for the classification performance and test set descriptives). To test viewing time differences, we used a multilevel Cox proportional hazards model with video viewing time as the dependent variable, topic as the independent variable, and participant random intercepts. A robustness analysis was done with a second model that also included video duration. The event was operationalized as scrolling to the next video whenever viewing time was not higher than the video duration. To investigate exposure to news-like content on TikTok over time and during key political moments, a cluster permutation t-test was used to group points within an analysis window on similar t-values and test the difference between cluster distributions with the permutation one-sample t-test. The ratios used in the t-test were baseline-corrected using the mean news ratios and mean hate ratios, calculated over the two-week period preceding each analysis window to give an accurate approximation of normal content circulation within a given time of the year. Next, we identified legacy, alternative, and niche news accounts based on the registered account name of the video creator for each classified news-like video (see SI Appendix). A multi-level logistic model with participant-level random intercepts was run to test whether the probability of encountering a hate video (0-1) between other news videos from a given account was lower for legacy and alternative accounts, compared to niche accounts. The groups and politicians in the hate speech videos were identified using a dictionary classification method (see the project's GitHub for all words and categories included). Finally, to estimate the state of content moderation on TikTok, we cross-checked the videoIDs in our SQLite database with the videoIDs from the EU's transparency database 50 . This was done by using the transparency database Python package 51 , which allows for the retrieval of datasets of removed content for a given platform. Since the transparency database was too large to retrieve all data from at once, we sequentially looped through it and compared the platform_uids in the transparency database with the platform_uids in our database. As a final step, we performed this same procedure for the videoIDs that we could not retrieve data for due to the video being removed or in some other way unavailable at the time of scraping the donated URLs.
