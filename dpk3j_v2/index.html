To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

A Source-Specific Approach to Internalised Sexual Prejudice: Scale Development and Initial Validation

Sexual prejudice, the negative attitudes towards people on the basis of their sexual orientation (Herek, 2000; Herek & McLemore, 2013), can shape both how sexual minority people are treated and how they evaluate their own sexuality. Minority stress theory identifies prejudice, discrimination, and rejection as sources of additional stress associated with a minoritised sexual or gender identity, with consequences for psychological wellbeing (Brooks, 1981; Meyer, 1995; Testa et al., 2015). One process through which this social devaluation can become personally consequential is internalised sexual prejudice (ISP): the incorporation of negative attitudes towards minority sexualities into a person's evaluations of their own sexual identity (Herek, 2009). Although ISP is experienced within the individual, its foundations are social. This distinction is central to minority stress theory, which locates disparities in psychological wellbeing in the stigma and disadvantage associated with a minoritised sexual identity, rather than in that identity itself (Frost & Meyer, 2023; Meyer, 2003; Nguyen et al., 2025).

The psychological significance of ISP has motivated substantial research into its correlates, development, and potential modification. In their systematic review of longitudinal and intervention studies, Nguyen et al. (2024) examined ISP as a phenomenon that can develop and change over time within people's social and psychological environments. This perspective places the social context of internalisation at the centre of efforts to understand it. Yet a potentially important feature of that context is often absent from its assessment: whose negative evaluation is being internalised? Sexual minority people may encounter sexuality-based prejudice from straight people or from other LGBTQA+ people, as demonstrated in research on anti-bisexual prejudice (Anderson & Maugeri, 2024; Manalastas et al., 2024) and intraminority stress (Dellers et al., 2026; Pachankis et al., 2020).

The present research examines whether distinguishing internalising responses associated with prejudice from straight people and other LGBTQA+ people provides a useful extension to the conceptualisation and measurement of ISP.

Conceptualising and Measuring Internalised Sexual Prejudice

The internalisation of sexual prejudice has been studied under several overlapping terms, including internalised homophobia, internalised homonegativity, internalised heterosexism, and internalised sexual stigma. Although definitions and measures vary across these traditions (Szymanski et al., 2008a (Szymanski et al., , 2008b)), their common concern is how negative social evaluations of minority sexuality become incorporated into people's evaluations of their own sexuality. Crucially, recognising prejudice is not the same as internalising it. Herek et al. (2009) distinguish internalised stigma from experiences of discriminatory treatment and expectations of such treatment. Sexual minority people may recognise that others devalue their sexuality while rejecting those attitudes and maintaining a positive sexual identity. The defining feature of ISP is the adoption of that devaluation in relation to the self, rather than awareness of its existence in the social environment.

Existing measures assess ISP through negative feelings about one's sexuality (Mayfield, 2001), difficulties accepting one's sexual identity and associated social discomfort (Lingiardi et al., 2012), and a wish to be heterosexual (Herek et al., 2009). For example, the internalised homonegativity subscale of the Lesbian, Gay, and Bisexual Identity Scale (Mohr & Kendra, 2011) includes three items assessing whether respondents would prefer to be straight. These measures capture negative evaluations of one's own sexuality but do not assess whether respondents associate these feelings with prejudice from straight people, other

LGBTQ+ people, or both. Consequently, the resulting scores provide information about the extent of ISP while leaving its source unspecified. This reflects a distinction between how ISP is theorised and the information retained in its measurement. Minority stress and sexual stigma theories explicitly locate internalisation within a stigmatising social environment (Herek & McLemore, 2013; Meyer, 2003; Meyer, 2010), whereas measures such as the LGBIS assess the resulting self-evaluation without specifying its source (Herek et al., 2009; Mohr & Kendra, 2011). A source-unspecified assessment is useful when the research question concerns overall ISP, but it is less informative when the question concerns whether people associate their negative sexualidentity evaluations with different interpersonal or social contexts. For example, two people with similar overall ISP scores might report different internalising responses to rejection from straight and other LGBTQ+ people. Measuring these responses separately would allow researchers to examine variation that an overall score cannot directly represent.

Why the Source of Prejudice May Matter

Research on anti-bisexual prejudice provides a clear precedent for distinguishing the sources of sexual prejudice. Brewster and Moradi (2010) developed the Anti-Bisexual Experiences Scale, to assess perceived prejudicial treatment from heterosexual people separately from treatment by lesbian and gay people. Respondents evaluate parallel experiences involving assumptions of sexual orientation instability, sexual irresponsibility, and interpersonal hostility under each source frame. This approach allows researchers to examine whether similar forms of prejudice are experienced differently depending on who expresses them. Subsequent work has also examined source-specific binegativity in relation to internalised binegativity (Arriaga & Parent, 2019). In that research, however, the source distinction concerns the prejudice experienced, but not the internalisation of prejudice. This leaves open whether the negative self-evaluations associated with those experiences can themselves be meaningfully distinguished by source.

There are theoretical reasons to expect the source of prejudice to matter. LGBTQA+ communities can provide opportunities for belonging, recognition, and affirmation of identities that are devalued elsewhere. In qualitative research with LGBQ adults, Cavarra et al. (2025) found that participants described support from similar others as facilitating selfacceptance and pride, while judgement and exclusion within these communities could undermine identity affirmation. Building on these findings, we propose that rejection from other LGBTQA+ people may challenge expectations of acceptance from those who might otherwise provide support and understanding. Such rejection may therefore concern both the negative evaluation itself and a threatened source of belonging and affirmation. This provides a reason to examine whether people associate different internalising responses with prejudice from straight and other LGBTQA+ people.

The Present Research: Overview and Predictions

The present research examines whether distinguishing the social sources associated with internalisation provides a useful extension to the conceptualisation and measurement of ISP. We define source-specific ISP as negative attitudes and feelings concerning one's sexual identity that individuals associate with sexuality-based prejudice from a specified social source. This approach extends the source differentiation used in research on experienced prejudice (Brewster & Moradi, 2010) to the assessment of internalisation. To our knowledge, existing measures have not assessed equivalent internalising responses separately in relation to prejudice from straight people and other LGBTQA+ people.

To address this gap, we developed the Source-Specific Internalised Sexual Prejudice Scale (SS-ISP). Respondents evaluate the same items under two stems describing sexualitybased prejudice from straight people and other LGBTQA+ people, respectively. This parallel format holds the content of the responses constant while varying the specified source, allowing us to examine whether people distinguish their internalising responses by source.

The measure therefore assesses the connections people report between prejudice from particular others and their evaluations of their own sexuality.

The research programme adopts best-practice guidelines for psychometric scale development (Boateng et al., 2018) and comprises four studies examining the content, structure, measurement invariance, initial validity, and temporal stability of the SS-ISP.

Study 1 evaluated the relevance of candidate items through ratings from researchers familiar with discrimination and LGBTQA+ research. Study 2 explored the factor structure of the retained items in a sample of gay men and examined the internal consistency of the resulting dimensions. Study 3 evaluated this structure in an independent, broader LGBTQA+ sample.

It also examined measurement invariance across cisgender gay men, cisgender lesbian women, cisgender bisexual men, and cisgender bisexual women, alongside exploratory comparisons of their source-specific scores. Study 3 further examined associations with an adapted version of the LGBIS internalised homonegativity subscale (Mohr & Kendra, 2011) and concurrent psychological distress. We expected positive associations with the LGBIS because both measures assess negative evaluations of one's sexuality, and with distress because of the role of ISP within minority stress theory (Meyer, 2003). We also examined whether the two source-specific dimensions jointly accounted for additional variance in distress beyond the LGBIS, providing an initial assessment of incremental criterion-related validity. Study 4 examined test-retest reliability and temporal stability. Participants from Study 3 were re-invited to complete the SS-ISP approximately 12 months after their initial assessment. This follow-up assesses whether individuals' relative standing on each sourcespecific dimension was maintained over time. Because ISP may change alongside people's experiences and social circumstances, stability over this interval provides evidence about the persistence of the measured responses without implying that individual scores necessarily remain unchanged.

We formed the following predictions:  H1: Content Validity (Study 1). The candidate items would be rated as relevant to ISP when presented in relation to prejudice from both straight people and other LGBTQA+ people.

 H2: Source-Specific Factor Structure (Studies 2 and 3).

Responses to the retained items would show a positively correlated two-factor structure corresponding to straight-source and LGBTQA+-source ISP in Study 2, and this structure would demonstrate acceptable fit in an independent sample in Study 3.

 H3: Convergent Validity (Study 3).

Both source-specific ISP dimensions would be positively associated with scores on the adapted LGBIS internalised homonegativity subscale.

 H4: Concurrent Validity (Study 3). Both source-specific ISP dimensions would be positively associated with concurrent psychological distress.

 H5: Incremental Validity (Study 3).

The two source-specific ISP dimensions would jointly account for additional variance in concurrent psychological distress beyond that accounted for by the adapted LGBIS internalised homonegativity subscale.

 H6: Temporal Stability (Study 4).

Scores on each source-specific ISP dimension would be positively associated across the 12-month interval.

Measurement invariance and between-group differences were examined as additional research aims, without specifying a directional ordering of group means.

Study 1: Item Development and Content Validity

Study 1 developed and evaluated items assessing negative attitudes and feelings about one's sexual identity in response to prejudice from straight people and other LGBTQA+ people. Consistent with H1, we expected the candidate items to be judged relevant to ISP under both source frames. A central consideration was retaining parallel item content across sources, allowing subsequent studies to examine source differentiation without introducing differences in the items assessed.

Method

Participants

Researchers were recruited through an email distributed to the BLINDED FOR REVIEW networking group. Eligibility required self-reported familiarity with both discrimination and LGBTQA+ research. Respondents rated their familiarity with research on "discrimination, prejudice, rejection, stigma, and/or harassment experiences" and "LGBTQA+ identity and/or sexuality" on scales ranging from 1 (not at all familiar) to 7 (very familiar). A rating of at least 5 on both items was required for inclusion.

Fifteen researchers participated, all of whom met these eligibility criteria. Familiarity was high for both discrimination research (M = 6.27, SD = 0.85) and LGBTQA+ research (M = 6.20, SD = 0.83). Participants included master's students (6.7%), doctoral candidates (26.7%), early-career academics (26.7%), mid-career academics (26.7%), and late-career academics (13.3%).

Item Development and Procedure

The research team developed an initial pool of eight candidate items, each presented under two source frames (16 item presentations), informed by minority stress theory (Brooks, 1981; Meyer, 2003), reviews of internalised stigma and its measurement (Nguyen et al., 2024; Szymanski et al., 2008b), and existing ISP measures (Herek et al., 2009; Mayfield, 2001; Mohr & Kendra, 2011). These sources guided the development of items assessing negative attitudes and feelings concerning one's own sexuality. Items were revised through discussion within the team, with attention to their applicability across sexual minority identities and their relevance under both source frames.

Following ethical approval from [BLINDED FOR REVIEW], participants read the study information and provided informed consent. They then rated each candidate item twice, once under each of the following stems: "Sometimes straight people dislike me because I'm a [X]. When this happens …" and "Sometimes other LGBTQA+ people dislike me because I'm a [X]. When this happens …". The placeholder [X] represented a sexual minority identity (e.g., gay man). Candidate responses included "It makes me dislike being [X]" (see Table 1).

Participants assessed each item's relevance to the internalisation of negative attitudes towards sexual minority individuals. Responses ranged from 1 (item is not at all relevant to ISP) to 4 (item is very relevant to ISP). Presenting the same items under both stems allowed their relevance to be evaluated separately for each source.

Analytic Approach

Content validity ratios (CVRs) were calculated for each item under each source frame, adapting Lawshe's (1975) procedure to participants' relevance ratings. Ratings of 3 or 4 were classified as endorsements of relevance. CVRs were calculated as (nᵣ -N/2)/(N/2), where nᵣ was the number of participants endorsing an item as relevant and N was the total number of raters.

Following the critical values reported by Ayre and Scally (2014), a CVR of .60 was required for a panel of 15 raters. Thus, at least 12 participants (80%) needed to endorse an item as relevant for it to meet the criterion. Item retention also considered whether the same response could be retained under both source frames.

Results

As shown in Table 1, six of the eight candidate items met the content validity criterion under both source frames, with CVRs ranging from .60 to 1.00. Two items did not meet the criterion consistently. "I feel negative about myself" fell below the threshold under both the straight and LGBTQA+ stems (both CVRs = .47). "My identity as a [X] feels less valid" met the criterion under the LGBTQA+ stem (CVR = .73), but not the straight stem (CVR = .33).

Both items were removed from each source frame, preserving parallel content across the two assessments. This yielded six retained items presented under each stem, producing a 12-item measure for subsequent evaluation. The findings provided partial support for H1: six of the eight candidate responses met the relevance criterion under both source frames.

Study 2: Factor Structure and Internal Consistency

Study 2 examined the factor structure and internal consistency of the items retained in Study 1 in a sample of gay men. Consistent with H2, we expected responses to show two related factors corresponding to straight-source and LGBTQA+-source ISP. Exploratory factor analysis allowed us to examine whether this organisation emerged when the 12 items were analysed together.

Method

Participants

Materials and Procedure

Following ethical approval from [BLINDED FOR REVIEW], participants read the study information and provided informed consent. They completed demographic questions and a broader survey containing measures relevant to other research questions. The present study reports the SS-ISP items.

Participants were informed that they would respond to the same six items twice, considering prejudice from straight people and other LGBTQA+ people separately. Responses ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Following survey completion, participants were thanked and remunerated through Prolific.

Analytic Approach

Analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29. Item distributions were examined for skewness and univariate outliers. Univariate outliers were defined as scores more than three standard deviations above or below the item mean; none were identified.

Because most items were positively skewed, exploratory factor analyses were conducted using both the original items and log₁₀-transformed items to assess whether skewness materially affected the solution. As the analyses yielded no meaningful differences, results are reported for the original items.

The 12 items were analysed together using principal axis factoring with direct oblimin rotation, allowing the factors to correlate. The number of factors retained was informed by the scree plot and parallel analysis, which compared observed eigenvalues with those generated from simulated data matching the sample size and number of items. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha ().

Results

The items were positively intercorrelated (Table 3). The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin coefficient (.93) and Bartlett's test of sphericity, χ²(66) = 2927.40, p < .001, supported the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Inspection of the scree plot suggested a two-factor solution. Parallel analysis supported retaining two factors: the first and second observed eigenvalues exceeded their simulated counterparts (7.61 versus 1.43 and 1.57 versus 1.32, respectively), whereas the third did not (0.54 versus 1.23).

The six items presented under the straight-source stem loaded on the first factor, with loadings ranging from .72 to .95. The six items presented under the LGBTQA+-source stem loaded on the second factor, with loadings ranging from .77 to .92. Both dimensions demonstrated high internal consistency (both αs = .94) and were positively correlated, r = .68, p < .001.

These findings supported the Study 2 component of H2: responses to the same six items were organised according to whether participants considered prejudice from straight people or other LGBTQA+ people. The correlation between the dimensions indicated substantial overlap, consistent with their shared focus on negative evaluations of one's sexuality.

Study 3: Factor Structure Replication and Initial Validity

Study 3 examined whether the source-specific structure identified in Study 2 replicated in an independent sample representing a broader range of sexual and gender identities. Consistent with H2, we expected the two-factor model to demonstrate acceptable fit. We also examined measurement invariance and explored differences in source-specific ISP across cisgender gay men, cisgender lesbian women, cisgender bisexual men, and cisgender bisexual women. Initial validity was assessed through associations with an established ISP measure and psychological distress. We expected both source-specific dimensions to correlate positively with the LGBIS internalised homonegativity subscale (H3) and concurrent psychological distress (H4). We further expected the two dimensions to jointly account for additional variance in distress beyond the LGBIS-ISP subscale (H5).

Method

Participants

LGBTQA+ adults were recruited through Prolific, with no restrictions on country of residence. As in Study 2, recruitment was guided by a target of 20 participants per item (Kline, 2016). We recruited beyond this minimum to support analyses within sexual identity groups. A total of 1,159 participants commenced the survey. Exclusions comprised pilot responses (n = 11), duplicate responses (n = 9), ineligible participants, including cisgender heterosexual respondents (n = 18), failed attention checks (n = 110), implausible response patterns, including straight-lining or inauthentic responding (n = 3), and those with large proportions of incomplete responses (n = 26). The final sample comprised 982 LGBTQA+ adults.

Participants were aged 18-77 years (M = 37.33, SD = 12.11). Most identified as cisgender women (45.8%) or cisgender men (42.8%), with 11.4% identifying as transgender or gender diverse. The most frequently reported sexual identities were bisexual (41.2%), gay (26.1%), and lesbian (24.0%), with other sexual identities comprising 8.7% of the sample.

Most participants reported no innate variations in sex characteristics (93.7%). Materials and Procedure

Following ethical approval from [BLINDED FOR REVIEW], participants read the study information and provided informed consent. They completed demographic questions and questionnaires addressing the present study and other research questions. The measures relevant to the present study were the SS-ISP, the LGBIS internalised homonegativity subscale, and the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale. Participants were thanked and remunerated through Prolific following survey completion.

Source-Specific Internalised Sexual Prejudice.

Participants completed the 12-item SS-ISP using the instructions, source stems, and response options described in Studies 1 and 2. In this study, the sexual-identity wording was tailored to each participant's reported sexual orientation, such as bisexual or gay. Items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The six items associated with each source were averaged separately, with higher scores indicating greater straight-source or LGBTQA+-source ISP.

Internalised Homonegativity. The three-item internalised homonegativity subscale of the LGBIS (Mohr & Kendra, 2011) provided an established comparator. Item wording was adapted to refer to sexual minority identities more broadly, consistent with its use in previous research (e.g., Saunders et al., 2025). An example item was "If it were possible, I would choose to be straight". Responses ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree) and were averaged, with higher scores indicating greater internalised sexual prejudice. Internal consistency was high (α = .90).

Psychological Distress. Psychological distress was assessed using the six-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6; Kessler et al., 2002). Participants reported how frequently they had experienced symptoms of distress during the preceding 30 days, including feeling hopeless. Responses ranged from 1 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time) and were averaged, with higher scores indicating greater psychological distress. Internal consistency was high (α = .92).

Analytic Approach

Following data screening, confirmatory factor analysis was conducted using the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). The two-factor model identified in Study 2 was specified using robust maximum likelihood estimation, with each item assigned to its corresponding source-specific factor and the two factors allowed to correlate. Model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root-meansquare error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardised root-mean-square residual (SRMR). CFI and TLI values of at least .95 and SRMR values below .08 were used as benchmarks for good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999), alongside RMSEA values below .08 as an indicator of acceptable approximation error (Kline, 2016). These indices were considered together when evaluating support for the hypothesised structure. Internal consistency was assessed separately for each source-specific dimension using Cronbach's alpha, with values of .70, .80, and .90 used as conventional benchmarks for acceptable, good, and excellent internal consistency, respectively (Cronbach, 1990).

Measurement invariance was examined across cisgender gay men, cisgender lesbian women, cisgender bisexual men, and cisgender bisexual women. Other groups were not included because their sample sizes were insufficient for separate evaluation. The model was first examined within each group, followed by increasingly constrained multigroup models assessing configural invariance (the same factor structure), metric invariance (equal factor loadings), and scalar invariance (equal factor loadings and item intercepts). A decrease in CFI of no more than .01 between successive models was interpreted as supporting the additional equality constraints (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002). Support for scalar invariance provided a basis for comparing group means. Exploratory group comparisons were conducted separately for each source-specific score using one-way analyses of variance, with significant omnibus tests followed by Tukey's post hoc comparisons.

Convergent and concurrent validity were examined using Pearson's correlations.

Positive associations between each SS-ISP dimension and LGBIS internalised homonegativity were interpreted as evidence of convergent validity. Positive associations between each dimension and concurrent psychological distress were interpreted as evidence of concurrent criterion-related validity. The correlation between the two SS-ISP dimensions was also examined to characterise their association.

Incremental criterion-related validity was examined using hierarchical linear regression, with psychological distress as the outcome variable. LGBIS internalised homonegativity was entered at Step 1, and both SS-ISP dimensions were added at Step 2. The regression used listwise deletion, excluding nine participants with missing data on one or more model variables (analytic N = 973). A statistically significant increase in explained variance (ΔR²), evaluated using the F-change test, was interpreted as evidence that the SS-ISP dimensions jointly contributed information about concurrent distress beyond that captured by the LGBIS. The magnitude of this increase indicated the extent of their additional contribution. Regression coefficients in the final model were examined to describe the unique association of each source-specific dimension with distress after accounting for the other predictors.

Analytic Approach

Temporal stability was examined in multiple ways following recommendations from Boateng et al. (2018). We first conducted internal consistency estimates for both SS-ISP subscales using just the second time-point data. Then, we explored mean-level differences in SS-ISP subscale scores across both time-points using repeated-measures t-tests, as well as associations between these subscales across time to determine the strength of their relationship. Finally, we explored the Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICCs) of these subscales across time-points to understand their test re-test reliability. ICCs were calculated using two-way mixed-effects models with absolute agreement and single-measure estimates, and were interpreted as indicating poor (ICCs < 0.50), moderate (ICCs = 0.50 to 0.75), good (ICCs = 0.75 to 0.90), or excellent (ICCs > 0.90) test re-test reliability according to Koo and Li (2016). Each of these assessments also report 95% confidence intervals to understand the degree of certainty within the estimate and were interpreted collectively to determine the SS-ISPs temporal stability. There were no missing data within the current study.

General Discussion

Although internalised sexual prejudice (ISP) is experienced within the individual, its foundations are social. The present research examined whether retaining information about the social source associated with internalisation provides a useful extension to its measurement. Across four studies, the findings provided initial support for the Source-Specific Internalised Sexual Prejudice Scale (SS-ISP), which assesses equivalent internalising responses in relation to prejudice from straight people and other LGBTQA+ people. Six parallel items met the content relevance criterion under both source frames, and exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses supported two related dimensions organised by source. Study 3 supported measurement invariance across four sexual-identity and gender groups and provided initial evidence of convergent, concurrent, and incremental validity.

Group differences emerged for LGBTQA+-source ISP, and an exploratory comparison indicated that this dimension had a stronger adjusted association with psychological distress than straight-source ISP. Study 4 provided evidence of 12-month temporal stability, indicating that individual differences in both source-specific dimensions persisted over time.

Together, these findings suggest that retaining source information captures meaningful variation in how people associate social devaluation with their evaluations of their own sexuality.

Implications for Understanding and Measuring Internalised Sexual Prejudice

Minority stress and sexual stigma theories locate internalisation within a social environment that devalues minority sexualities (Brooks, 1981; Herek et al., 2009; Meyer, 2003). Established measures assess aspects of this process through negative feelings about sexuality, difficulties accepting a sexual-minority identity, and preferences for heterosexuality (Lingiardi et al., 2012; Mohr & Kendra, 2011; Padilla et al., 2025; Szymanski et al., 2008b). More recent measurement work has extended this coverage to bisexual-specific and culturally situated dimensions, including monosexism and family shame (Wei & Israel, 2023), and gay men and lesbian women have also shown implicit preferences for heterosexual targets, consistent with internalised sexuality-based devaluation (Anderson et al., 2024, Experiment 3). These assessments provide information about the evaluations people hold, but generally leave unspecified whose prejudice respondents associate with them. The SS-ISP addresses this omission by assessing equivalent internalising responses separately in relation to prejudice from straight people and other LGBTQA+ people.

The parallel item format is central to this contribution. Each source frame presented the same six responses, allowing source differentiation to be examined without introducing different item content across the two dimensions. In Study 2, responses were organised into factors corresponding to prejudice from straight people and other LGBTQA+ people. This structure replicated in Study 3, among a sample that included a broader range of sexual and gender identities. Although the dimensions were strongly associated, indicating considerable overlap in participants' internalising responses, their separation supports distinguishing the social contexts that respondents associate with negative evaluations of their sexuality. The findings do not establish that the sources generate independent psychological processes or exhaust the contexts in which internalisation occurs. Rather, the SS-ISP complements sourceunspecified measures when researchers seek to retain information about these contexts.

Research on anti-bisexual prejudice distinguishes negative treatment from heterosexual and lesbian/gay people (Brewster & Moradi, 2010), while intraminority stress research demonstrates the psychological relevance of pressures within LGBTQA+ communities (Pachankis et al., 2020). More recent research similarly links marginalisation within gay communities to sexual-minority men's mental health beyond heterosexist discrimination (Dellers et al., 2026). Together, this research highlights the relevance of prejudice both within and beyond LGBTQA+ communities. The SS-ISP extends this attention to social source into the assessment of internalising responses, rather than experiences of rejection alone.

The group comparisons further illustrated differences in source-specific ISP. Across the four cisgender groups examined, no statistically significant differences were detected in straight-source ISP, whereas differences emerged for LGBTQA+-source ISP. Bisexual women reported higher LGBTQA+-source ISP than lesbian women and gay men, while bisexual men reported higher scores than lesbian women. However, bisexual men did not differ significantly from gay men, who also reported higher scores than lesbian women. The pattern therefore does not indicate a uniform difference between bisexual and monosexual participants. Nevertheless, the findings concerning bisexual participants can be considered alongside evidence of prejudice and bi-erasure within broader sexual-minority communities, including challenges to the legitimacy of bisexuality and bisexual people's belonging (Anderson & Maugeri, 2024; McCole et al., 2025). The present findings extend this literature by identifying group differences in the negative feelings about sexuality that respondents associate with within-community rejection. Support for scalar invariance strengthens the interpretation of these comparisons by supporting comparable measurement across the four groups. This evidence was limited to cisgender gay men, lesbian women, bisexual men, and bisexual women. It does not establish equivalent measurement across all populations included within the LGBTQA+ umbrella, nor does it imply that prejudice from other LGBTQA+ people has the same meaning for everyone. Shared membership of a broad identity category can coexist with differences in recognition, belonging, and acceptance. Both SS-ISP dimensions were positively associated with LGBIS internalised homonegativity, providing evidence of convergence with an established measure of negative evaluations of one's sexuality (Mohr & Kendra, 2011). These correlations were moderate, suggesting that the measures capture overlapping but nonidentical aspects of ISP. This pattern may reflect differences in both content and framing: the LGBIS subscale emphasises a preference for heterosexuality, whereas the SS-ISP assesses a broader range of negative feelings and self-evaluations that respondents associate with rejection from specified sources.

Both source-specific dimensions were also associated with concurrent psychological distress. These findings are consistent with the position of ISP within minority stress theory (Brooks, 1981; Meyer, 2003), although the cross-sectional design does not establish the direction of these associations. Internalising responses may contribute to distress, distress may influence how people experience and report these responses, or both may be associated with other experiences and circumstances. The incremental validity findings further indicated that the SS-ISP captured information relevant to distress beyond that provided by the conventional measure. Adding the SS-ISP dimensions increased the variance explained in concurrent distress from approximately 3% to 13%. Both SS-ISP dimensions retained positive associations with distress in the final model, whereas the association with LGBIS internalised homonegativity was no longer statistically significant.

The exploratory comparison provided further, albeit preliminary, evidence of the potential significance of distinguishing sources. LGBTQA+-source ISP showed a stronger association with concurrent distress than straight-source ISP after accounting for their overlap and conventional ISP. Because the two dimensions assess the same responses on the same scale, this finding suggests that their reported social context may be relevant to understanding differences in their associations with wellbeing. In particular, it draws attention to negative evaluations of one's sexuality linked to rejection from people within a potential identity community. Although exploratory and requiring replication, the finding highlights a distinction that source-unspecified measures cannot directly assess. One possible explanation concerns the relational significance of rejection within a potential identity community. Other LGBTQA+ people may provide recognition and affirmation of identities that are devalued elsewhere, making their rejection consequential for both self-evaluation and expectations of belonging. This interpretation is consistent with accounts of community support and exclusion described by Cavarra et al. (2025). However, neither expectations of acceptance nor threatened belonging were directly assessed in the present studies. The exploratory finding provides an initial basis for investigating these possibilities, with replication needed before drawing broader conclusions about differences between sources.

Temporal Stability

Study 4 provided evidence of moderate test-retest reliability for both source-specific dimensions over 12 months, with positive correlations across assessments and no statistically significant mean-level differences. The 12-month interval provides a context for interpreting temporal stability. ISP can develop and change alongside social and psychological experiences, as reflected in longitudinal and intervention research (Nguyen et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2025). Consequently, correspondence between assessments reflects the persistence of individual differences across a period in which genuine change is also possible.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations qualify the conclusions. First, the SS-ISP assesses respondents' reported connections between rejection and their evaluations of their own sexuality. It does not independently establish the historical origin of those evaluations or demonstrate that a particular source caused their internalisation. The conditional wording may capture relatively immediate or context-dependent responses to rejection alongside more enduring negative self-attitudes. Further research could examine how these source-linked responses relate to persistent ISP and how they vary across everyday encounters.

Second, the source categories are broad. Straight people and other LGBTQA+ people encompass different relationships, identities, and social positions. Rejection from a close friend, partner, family member, or unfamiliar person may carry different meanings even within the same source category. Future research could adapt the parallel format to distinguish more specific sources, such as straight men and women, or cisgender and transgender lesbian, gay, and bisexual people. Examining these distinctions would respond to calls for research that attends to differences within broad LGBTQA+ categories rather than assuming shared experiences across subpopulations (Hässler et al., 2024). Such adaptations could clarify which interpersonal contexts are associated with internalising responses, although their interpretation and measurement properties would require evaluation. The current categories may also overlap, because heterosexuality and membership of the LGBTQA+ umbrella are not mutually exclusive. They should therefore be understood as the source frames presented to respondents, rather than exhaustive or mutually exclusive classifications.

The measure also does not separately assess how often respondents encounter rejection from each source. A higher score may reflect differences in exposure, the perceived significance of particular experiences, or the strength of the internalising response. Future research that assesses both source-specific rejection and internalisation could help distinguish these possibilities. This would be particularly useful for understanding group differences and identifying whether changes in social experiences precede changes in source-specific ISP.

The samples also impose limitations on generalisability. Studies 2 and 3 recruited participants through Prolific, with Study 2 restricted to gay men and Study 3 predominantly comprising White participants residing in the United States. Study 4 involved a subset of Study 3 participants who completed the follow-up assessment, raising the possibility of selective attrition. Further validation in more diverse samples is therefore warranted.

Finally, the present studies provide initial rather than exhaustive validity evidence.

Content evaluation relied on researcher ratings, and cognitive interviews and broader community input could clarify how respondents understand the stems and items. Validation against additional outcomes and related constructs could further establish the boundaries between source-specific ISP, rejection sensitivity, identity-related distress, and other responses to prejudice. Such work would help identify when retaining source information provides the greatest benefit for understanding sexual minority people's experiences.

Conclusion

The present research introduces an approach to ISP measurement that retains information about whose prejudice respondents associate with negative evaluations of their own sexuality. The findings support two related source-specific dimensions, identify variation across sexual identity groups, and provide initial evidence of their relevance to psychological distress beyond a conventional ISP measure. Assessing these responses separately offers a means of examining internalisation with greater attention to its reported social context, while recognising that experiencing prejudice and incorporating its devaluation into the self remain distinct processes.
