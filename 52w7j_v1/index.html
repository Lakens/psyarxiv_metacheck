To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 16 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Full text

Abstract

Learning good and bad associations and updating them amidst change facilitates adaptive behavior. Differences in sensitivity to salient, dynamic information from the environment may influence how learning unfolds. Cross-species research suggests that adolescents, relative to younger and older individuals, may exhibit more persistent learned negative and positive associations. However, direct within-person comparisons of valenced learning processes are scarce, and the mechanisms underlying developmental differences in the persistence of learned associations remain unclear. In adults, the persistence of negative associations has been linked to individual differences in how people use experience to infer the structure of their environment. Developmental changes in these same latent-structure inference processes may underpin greater persistence of valenced associations during adolescence. To test this, participants ages 10-25 years completed a Pavlovian learning task in which distinct cues predicted gain, loss, or neither, followed by extinction, during which outcomes ceased. Conditioned responses to both positive and negative associations peaked and took longer to extinguish in late adolescence, near age 19. Negative associations were similarly robustly learned in young adults and weakly persisted in a new extinction context in early adolescence. While a shared latent-structure inference process was observed across valences, age-related differences in the tendency to attribute changes in reinforcement contingencies to multiple latent states mirrored the persistence of valenced associations in adolescence. These findings suggest that while both positive and negative outcomes shape dynamic learning across age, valenced learned associations more strongly influence behavior and cognition during adolescence, as individuals learn to independently navigate a changing world.

Introduction

Adaptive decisions rely on learning positive and negative associations with environmental stimuli and updating them given new evidence. Links between stimuli and positive or negative outcomes can vary such that, for example, a stimulus no longer predicts a gain or loss. When this occurs, individuals must determine whether new information should modify previously learned information or instead signal that the state of the environment has changed (Gershman & Niv, 2012). These learning demands -and how they are shaped by the positive or negative valence of an outcome -may be particularly impacted during adolescence, a period of both heightened sensitivity to salient experiences and exposure to varied environments (Casey et al., 2019; Somerville et al., 2009). As adolescents gain independence and encounter a widening range of consequential outcomes, increased sensitivity may prioritize learning from both positive and negative events over mundane events (Hartley & Somerville, 2015). How the cognitive processes that support learning from dynamic environments differ by valence and whether they change across development is unclear.

As individuals track the state of the world around them, the motivational significance of cues can fluctuate. Pavlovian acquisition and extinction provide a tractable framework for studying how valenced associations can change (Pavlov, 1927), often used to model learning processes implicated in psychopathologies like anxiety and addiction. During acquisition, a valenced association is learned via repeated pairings of a neutral cue and an inherently positive (appetitive) or, more commonly, negative (aversive) outcome. During subsequent extinction, the cue is repeatedly presented in the absence of its expected outcome, and conditioned responses typically decrease. However, conditioned responses can persist or later reemerge, indicating that acquisition and extinction may be retained as competing memories (Bouton, 2002; Dunsmoor et al., 2015). Dynamic Pavlovian learning therefore poses a representational inference problem: when a reliable environmental contingency changes, an individual may revise the original association or infer that new observations reflect a different state of the environment.

The latent cause framework can account for the cognitive underpinnings of Pavlovian learning dynamics more completely than traditional models of associative learning. Whereas traditional models represent a single, directly modifiable cue-outcome association (Rescorla & Wagner, 1972), latent cause models formalize hidden causes, or states, that capture statistical regularities in the environment (Courville et al., 2006; Gershman & Niv, 2012). When an expected outcome no longer occurs, a learner may attribute the change to the original cause and modify the original association. Alternatively, the learner may state-split to infer a new extinction cause, allowing the original reinforced and extinction associations to coexist. Preserving distinct associations can be adaptive when environmental states recur, but it may allow outdated or maladaptive responses to persist beyond environmental changes. In contrast, updating the original association may promote adjustment to new contingencies at the cost of losing information that may become relevant again in the future. This representational tradeoff may therefore shape adaptive flexibility in dynamically changing environments and the persistence of maladaptive associations. In adults, individual differences in state splitting lead to differences in learning dynamics and predict the return of conditioned fear (Gershman & Hartley, 2015). Maintaining distinct representations of previously learned information may support adaptive learning in adolescence, a period when individuals must increasingly use past experiences to guide decisions under changing circumstances (Murty et al., 2016). Whether distinct representations similarly support learning from aversive and appetitive outcomes, and how they emerge with age remains unclear.

Prior cross-species research suggests that the cognitive representations that support learning from valenced information change from childhood to adulthood. Adolescence is an evolutionarily conserved period of development defined by postnatal days in rodents and often as the second decade of life in humans (Sawyer et al., 2018). In juvenile rodents, extinction can durably modify an aversive association. In older animals, acquired aversive associations persist into extinction, with adolescent rodents requiring more training to extinguish (Baker & Richardson, 2015; Gogolla et al., 2009; Pattwell et al., 2012). Human studies likewise show age-related variation in aversive acquisition and extinction, though the direction and adolescent specificity of these effects differ across tasks, response measures, and testing conditions (Ganella et al., 2018; Waters et al., 2017; Widegren et al., 2025). Developmental work on appetitive extinction is comparatively scarce and comes largely from rodents (Brenhouse & Andersen, 2008; Meyer & Bucci, 2016). Developmental work outside Pavlovian learning suggests that adolescents may be especially likely to interpret unexpected outcomes as signaling a change in environmental state (Jepma et al., 2020). Together, these findings raise the possibility that age-related differences in the persistence of learned associations may reflect changes in the cognitive representations underpinning dynamic learning over development.

Whether representations of learning are shared across valence and if they vary over development remains unclear. Prior findings on learning from valenced outcomes over development are mixed. Some studies that include positive and negative outcomes show that adolescents learn more from reward than punishment (Heffer et al., 2024; Palminteri et al., 2016), while others have found that adolescents weigh negative outcomes more heavily than adults (Rosenbaum et al., 2022). These studies embed positive and negative outcomes in instrumental learning tasks, where the two valences are combined in a single value signal that guides choice (Nussenbaum et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2021). Thus, mixed findings may emerge from varying task demands (Pauli et al., 2023) that might not dissociate developmental differences in learning processes from higher-order cognitive processes, such as action selection or cognitive control (Boddez et al., 2013; Master et al., 2020). Studies that isolate appetitive and aversive learning processes largely comprise separate literatures. Reward learning studies are typically instrumental and report both linear and nonlinear age patterns in striatal reactivity and prediction-error signaling across adolescence (Braams et al., 2015; Cohen et al., 2010; Galván, 2013; Hauser et al., 2015; Silverman et al., 2015). Aversive learning studies are often Pavlovian and emphasize age differences in fear extinction and the later return of conditioned responses (Ganella et al., 2018; Pattwell et al., 2012). Though separate studies have assessed aversive and appetitive Pavlovian learning over development in rodents (Baker & Richardson, 2015; Brenhouse & Andersen, 2008; Meyer & Bucci, 2016; Zbukvic et al., 2017), it is unclear whether appetitive and aversive extinction follow distinct developmental trajectories. Examining both valences within the same task can allow for assessments of valence-specific differences in conditioned responding and the underlying latent structure with which changing contingencies are represented.

The present study investigated how appetitive and aversive associations are learned and extinguished in the same individuals aged 10 to 25 years-old. We hypothesized that conditioned responses to both appetitive and aversive cues would vary nonlinearly with age, with the strongest and most persistent associations during adolescence. Age-related differences in valenced learning could reflect a valence-general shift from one-state learning to state-splitting across adolescence. Our within-person design allowed us to compare state-splitting tendencies to gains and losses across age and assess whether latent cause representations were valence-specific or shared across valence. We predicted that evidence for representing acquisition and extinction as distinct latent causes would increase across adolescence, consistent with greater preservation of previously reinforced associations when contingencies changed. Participants completed a gamified Pavlovian task where separate cues predicted gaining a coin, losing a coin, or receiving a neutral outcome, followed by extinction across two task contexts (Fig. 1). We first characterized continuous age-related patterns in implicit and explicit conditioned responses to appetitive and aversive cues (conditioned stimuli; CSs). We then extended the latent cause framework to represent appetitive and aversive outcomes simultaneously and tested whether valenced learning shared a causal structure.

We show that, within the same individuals, learned expectations of gains and losses were greatest in late adolescence and more persistent when no longer reinforced. Aversive associations were similarly strongly acquired later into young adulthood. A parallel late-adolescent age pattern was observed in the tendency to attribute changes in valenced contingencies to multiple latent causes, which increased across adolescence, peaking in late adolescence. These findings demonstrate a shared representational process across valences and identify late adolescence as a period of heightened sensitivity to and greater retention of valenced associations.

Results

We analyzed data from 90 participants ages 10-25 who completed a gamified Pavlovian learning task with appetitive, aversive, and neutral cues (Fig. 1). During an acquisition phase, participants first learned that different cues (robots; CSs) predicted gaining a coin (CS+pos), losing a coin (CS+neg), or no change (CS-) in coins and reported their expected outcome via expectancy ratings on every trial. During extinction, gains and losses ceased, allowing us to examine how previously learned expectations were maintained as contingencies changed. A second extinction phase occurred in a new task context. Participants subsequently completed a memory test for objects encountered during learning (see Supplement for episodic memory methods and results). Associative learning is often assessed through behavioral measures, like ratings of outcome expectancy, or autonomic indices, such as skin conductance. These measures can provide complementary explicit and cue-elicited implicit arousal measures of learning (Kitt et al., 2023; Shechner et al., 2014). We used expectancy ratings to characterize age-related differences in appetitive and aversive learning, skin conductance responses (SCRs) to validate the valence manipulation, and latent cause modeling to test how participants represented changes in the underlying structure of the environment.

Conditioned responding varied nonlinearly with age, valence, and learning phase

We investigated how expectancy ratings during acquisition and extinction varied with stimulus valence (CS+pos, CS+neg, CS-), learning phase (Acquisition, Extinction 1, Extinction 2), and age using mixed-effects models (Fig. 2A). There were significant main effects of CS (χ 2 (2) = 312.41, p < 0.001) and quadratic age (χ 2 (1) = 4.55, p = 0.033), indicating that expectancy ratings differed by stimulus type and age. These effects were qualified by significant two-way CS × phase (χ 2 (4) = 677.10, p < 0.001), CS × linear age (χ 2 (2) = 32.16, p < 0.001), and CS × quadratic age (χ 2 (2) = 22.68, p < 0.001) interactions. These effects were further qualified by significant three-way interactions between CS × phase × linear age (χ 2 (4) = 102.89, p < 0.001) and CS × phase × quadratic age (χ 2 (4) = 35.43, p < 0.001), indicating age differences in cue differentiation changed nonlinearly across the task. There was no main effect of task phase or phase × age interaction (χ 2 < 2.95 and p > 0.229; Table S1). This omnibus model indicates that Pavlovian acquisition and extinction learning differ by valence and age. Ratings for appetitive and aversive cues diverged during acquisition and converged toward neutral across extinction, but the degree to which participants differentiated the cues varied nonlinearly with age.

We next assessed physiological evidence for arousal responses to cues linked to appetitive and aversive outcomes across age. We fit a mixed-effects model with the same structure as the omnibus expectancy rating model (Table S2). There were significant main effects of phase of learning (χ 2 (2) = 79.22, p < 0.001) and of linear age (χ 2 (1) = 4.41, p = 0.036); arousal was generally higher during Acquisition relative to either extinction phase and in younger relative to older individuals. We also observed a main effect of CS (χ 2 (2) = 15.76, p < 0.001) such that SCRs were numerically highest for CS+pos, followed by CS+neg, and finally unreinforced CS-. There was no interaction of CS and phase (p = 0.53), suggesting a similar pattern of SCRs in each phase. Post hoc comparisons confirmed that arousal to both CS+s were significantly elevated relative to the CS-during Acquisition (CS+neg vs. CS-: difference in estimated marginal means = 0.027, t(9636) = 2.52, p = 0.012; CS+pos vs. CS-: difference in estimated marginal means = 0.043, t(9636) = 4.09, p < 0.001), whereas neither CS+ significantly differed from the CS-during Extinction 1 and Extinction 2 (all p > 0.10; Fig. 2B). This pattern of SCRs indicates that participants demonstrated heightened arousal responses to both valenced CS+s during Acquisition and that these arousal responses were extinguished. To assess whether the age-related differences identified in the omnibus models reflected different learning patterns across trial types within each phase, we fit mixed-effect models for each phase that included trial number in addition to trial type and age. Across age, participants showed different overall levels of conditioned responding within phases but limited reliable differences in age-related responses across trials within phases (see Supplement). During Acquisition only, we observed a main effect of trial number on SCRs (χ 2 (1) = 58.77, p < 0.001), suggesting rapid habituation to learned reinforced cues. Given these findings and that physiological responses to secondary reinforcers, like win/loss outcomes, tend to be less robust than responses to primary reinforcers (Delgado et al., 2006), we restricted all subsequent analyses to behavioral expectancy ratings.

Responses to valenced cues were strongest in late adolescence

To unpack the significant three-way interaction from the mixed-effect models indicating nonlinear age-related patterns of conditioned responding, we fit a generalized additive mixed model (GAMM; Wood, 2017) with a separate age smooth for each CS × phase combination (Table S5; see Supplement for trial-wise GAMMs). Generalized additive models make no a priori assumption about the shape of the age function, allowing for greater precision in characterizing nonlinear age patterns. We examined two features of each fitted age smooth to identify the specific ages during which changes in learning occurred. First, we identified the inflection point, defined here as the age at which the fitted age curve changed most steeply. Second, we examined the first derivative of each age smooth to identify age windows in which the curve showed statistically significant increases or decreases. Reporting both quantities is useful because the inflection point summarizes the location of the strongest curvature, whereas the derivative windows show the age ranges over which conditioned responses changed reliably (Heller et al., 2026; Simmonds et al., 2017).

During Acquisition, age effects were significant for CS+pos (edf = 3.59, F = 32.89, p < 0.001) and CS+neg (edf = 3.67, F = 40.48, p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Appetitive responses increased from approximately 11.9 to 18.8y, peaked at 19.3y (95% interval = 18.7, 21.1), and then decreased from approximately 20.4 to 25.6y. Aversive responses became more negative from approximately 12.5 to 18.9y, peaked at 18.7y (95% interval = 18.0, 19.6), and then attenuated from approximately 20.8 to 22.7y before leveling off in early adulthood. The fitted CS+pos age curve became more positive from younger ages to late teens and then less positive at older ages. The CS+neg curve similarly showed that ratings became more negative through late teens and then less negative in older participants. These findings indicate that while acquisition of both appetitive and aversive associations peaked in late adolescence, aversive responses were similarly elevated into young adulthood.

During Extinction 1, age effects were again significant for CS+neg (edf = 3.23, F = 14.23, p < 0.001) and CS+pos (edf = 3.19, F = 6.95, p < 0.001; Fig. 3B). The fitted smooths again indicated that expectancy ratings had the steepest slope in the late teen years relative to younger and older ages. Direct comparison of age functions across phases indicated that this age pattern was attenuated from Acquisition to Extinction 1 for both CS+s (CS+pos difference smooth: edf = 2.41, F = 8.81, p < 0.001; CS+neg difference smooth: edf = 1.00, F = 20.75, p < 0.001). Derivative analysis showed that during Extinction 1, appetitive responses became more positive from approximately 13.5 to 18.2y, peaked at 19.4y (95% interval = 18.0, 22.2), and decreased from 20.7 to 25.5y. Aversive responses became more negative from approximately 12.5 to 18.9y, peaked at 19.3y (95% interval = 18.4, 22.2), and then became less negative from 20.8 to 22.7y. These results indicate that late adolescents also retained valenced associations to the greatest extent during initial extinction, when valenced outcomes ceased.

By Extinction 2, the age effect remained significant for CS+neg (edf = 2.55, F = 2.78, p = 0.040), but was no longer reliable for CS+pos (p = 0.733; Fig. 3C). The only significant derivative interval occurred for the aversive association, where aversive ratings became more negative between 10.2 to 14.7y, peaking at 15.0y (95% interval = 13.6, 22.3). Appetitive associations did not statistically change across any age range in Extinction 2. This suggests that while aversive associations were weakly maintained in teens and young adults, appetitive associations were extinguished.

Ratings for CS-trials did not significantly vary with age in any phase (all p > 0.37; Fig. 3). Taken together, both appetitive and aversive associations peak around 19y across phases. However, after this peak, the age patterns differed by valence. Responses to appetitive associations declined continuously into young adulthood in both acquisition and initial extinction. Aversive associations, in contrast, were similarly strong in young adults during acquisition and showed some persistence across extinction in early adolescence. This valenced-based difference in learning patterns may reflect an adaptive bias towards learning and retaining negative information.

Distinct latent cause formation emerges during adolescence

We next tested whether the observed late-adolescent peak in valenced learning and extinction indicated age-related differences in the cognitive representations formed during learning. In the latent cause model, the tendency to infer new states is governed by a concentration parameter , where higher values α increase the likelihood of attributing an observation to a new state and = 0 restricts the model to a single α state. Because the standard latent cause model represents a single outcome (US), agnostic to valence, we extended the model to represent two USs simultaneously (see Methods). The unvalenced model variant jointly estimated a single latent cause structure across both outcome types and the valence-dependent model variant estimated a separate state-splitting tendency for each valence.

Model validation supported the use of models that account for both USs across all ages. The original single-US model fit to all trials and the absolute value of expectancy ratings showed poor recovery of simulated compared to true values (r = 0.19) and was not retained for primary inference. The two α α model variants showed strong recovery (unvalenced model r = 0.94; valence-dependent model r neg = α 0.94, r pos = 0.88). Both model variants fit the observed data better than permuted data (both p < 0.001; Fig. S4). Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) favored the unvalenced model, which estimated a single α parameter across both positive and negative contingencies (mean BIC = 220.0) over the valence-dependent model, which estimated separate appetitive ( ) and aversive ( ) concentration α 𝑝𝑜𝑠 α 𝑛𝑒𝑔 parameters (mean BIC = 224.7). The mean within-person difference in BIC was 4.7 in favor of the unvalenced model. Thus, participants' ratings were described better by a shared latent cause structure than by separate causal representations for the two valences. This result indicates that a shared representational process may support learning from both types of outcomes across age.

For each participant, a log Bayes factor (logBF) compared how well trial-by-trial expectancy ratings were explained by a model that permitted multiple underlying states versus a model restricted to a single state. Larger positive logBF values indicated stronger evidence for state-splitting (see Methods). We found no evidence that state-splitting differed by valence (see Supplement 2.3), so we summarized state-splitting using the best-fitting unvalenced model. To confirm that the model output captured behavioral differences, we divided participants into two groups based on their logBF (high and low; median split; Fig. 4A) and compared learning curves across modeled trials for CS+pos and CS+neg stimuli. Simple linear slopes revealed significantly flatter curves in the lower logBF group for both the aversive (low logBF mean gradient = -0.26 ± 0.9; high logBF mean gradient = -0.37 ± 0.9; p = 0.020) and appetitive trials (low logBF mean gradient = 0.14 ± 1.1; high logBF mean gradient = 0.48 ± 1.1; p = 0.001; Wilcoxon rank-sum tests). Thus, consistent with prior work (Gershman & Hartley, 2015), those with a lower tendency to state split showed slower learning.

We characterized the shape of logBF with a GAM and found that the age smooth was significantly nonlinear (edf = 2.55, F = 7.42, p < 0.001; Table S9; see Table S8 for regression with polynomial age terms). LogBF increased significantly from approximately 12.9 to 18.6y, with an inflection point at 19.7y (95% CI = 14.9, 22.9; Fig. 4B). Evidence for state-splitting increased across adolescence and peaked in late adolescence. Notably, the adolescent peak in the tendency to state-split coincided with the peak ages in expectancy ratings during Pavlovian learning and extinction. These parallel age patterns suggest that the preservation of learned associations during late adolescence may be driven by an increased tendency to infer distinct environmental states.

Renewal measures do not vary with age or valence

We next tested whether extinguished responses returned, or were renewed, in a novel context at the beginning of Extinction 2. For each reinforced cue, renewal was quantified as the change in conditioned response from the final block of Extinction 1 to the first block of Extinction 2 relative to the CS-. There was no significant effect of age, valence, or their interaction on renewal (all p > 0.26; Table S10). Thus, the magnitude of context-related change in expectancy ratings did not significantly differ across age or between aversive and appetitive cues.

Discussion

The present study showed that learned responses to both positive and negative cues were strongest in late-adolescents. Expectancy ratings for both valenced cues varied nonlinearly with age, with the largest magnitude responses near age 19 during both acquisition and initial extinction. Although appetitive and aversive conditioned responses shared a late-adolescent peak, their age patterns diverged. While appetitive expectations declined into young adulthood, aversive expectations remained elevated in early adolescents following extinction. Latent cause modeling revealed a shared latent state inference process across valences and a parallel nonlinear age pattern in evidence for separating acquisition and extinction into different underlying states. Taken together, these findings suggest that similar cognitive representations support dynamic learning from both positive and negative outcomes and identify late adolescence as an age window demonstrating heightened valenced expectations and differentiation of changing environmental states.

We found amplified and persistent outcome expectations across valence in late adolescence. Prior findings demonstrated that adolescents require more experience than younger or older individuals to reduce aversive conditioned responding (Baker & Richardson, 2015; Ganella et al., 2018; Pattwell et al., 2012; Waters et al., 2017). We show that this pattern extends to reward learning within the same individuals. Unlike prior findings, largely from rodent work, age-related differences emerged in acquisition. This difference may be due to the use of implicit behavioral metrics in prior work versus explicit ratings in the present study (McCallum et al., 2026). Responses to appetitive cues were strongest around the same age as responses to aversive cues and also remained elevated into early extinction. In line with prior cross-species work suggesting enhanced sensitivity to motivational cues during adolescence (Doremus-Fitzwater & Spear, 2016), we find that the strongest expectations emerged for both gain-and loss-predictive cues under partial reinforcement in adolescence. The present nonlinear age-related patterns in valenced learning may reflect hierarchical brain maturation, in which subcortical systems implicated in processing salient inputs develop in childhood and adolescence before top-down connections with cortical regions that regulate learned associations develop in late adolescence and young adulthood (Casey et al., 2019). Our findings, together with evidence of hierarchical brain development into young adulthood, fit a growing view that adolescence encompasses the late teenage years and early twenties (Cohen et al., 2016; Steinberg et al., 2018). Late adolescence may be a time of amplified associative learning from valenced inputs rather than the selective enhancement of appetitive or aversive learning.

After the shared late-adolescent peak for both valences, conditioned responses to aversive and appetitive cues diverged with age. Appetitive expectations declined in young adulthood during both acquisition and initial extinction and were relatively flat across age during late extinction. Aversive expectations were similarly elevated in both adolescence and young adulthood during acquisition, remained elevated in adolescence during initial extinction, and weakly persisted in early adolescence during late extinction in a novel context. It is possible that valenced expectations were sustained due to greater learning during acquisition. However, although we see the strongest aversive acquisition in late adolescence, we see the strongest maintenance of aversive association during late extinction in early adolescence. This suggests that the strength of acquisition does not fully account for the persistence of aversive associations. The durability of aversive expectations may ultimately reflect the asymmetric cost of misprediction, as failing to anticipate costly outcomes have more dire consequences than failing to anticipate reward (Öhman & Mineka, 2001; Vaish et al., 2008). As adolescents gain independence, accurately anticipating adverse events becomes increasingly consequential. The greater developmental persistence of aversive expectations may therefore reflect an adaptive bias toward retaining information about potential harm.

The age-related peak in conditioned responding coincided with a peak in the tendency to represent acquisition and extinction with distinct latent causes across both valenced outcomes. This finding suggests that beyond age-related differences in the strength of valenced expectations, learners also show differences in how their experiences are cognitively represented. As structured inference becomes increasingly central to learning over development (Nussenbaum & Hartley, 2019), the tendency to state-split aligns with a developmental progression in which learners increasingly build and use structured representations of a task rather than direct stimulus-outcome associations (e.g., Decker et al., 2016; Hauser et al., 2015; Nussenbaum et al., 2022 Nussenbaum et al., , 2026;; Cohen et al., 2020). Because the propensity to state-split increased throughout adolescence and peaked in late adolescence, the way learners carve experience into distinct causes offers a computational account of why valenced expectations are most strongly retained during this age range. When a learner attributes the change in extinction to a new cause rather than updating the original association, the learner preserves the acquisition memory rather than overwriting it, and they continue to express the original preserved cause until enough evidence accumulates to favor the new cause (Gershman et al., 2010; Harhen & Bornstein, 2023). Responding therefore remains elevated early in extinction around the age when the tendency to split states is greatest. This suggests that persistent responding might not be a failure to extinguish but instead a consequence of learning to infer that acquisition and extinction reflect distinct environmental states. These findings position the representational structure of learning as a candidate mechanism through which affect-related learning changes from childhood to adulthood.

The current study has several limitations worth noting. Renewal did not vary reliably across age or valence. This may be due to limited demands on retrieval. Extinction 2 followed only a short (7-minute) delay, and the context shift altered the virtual setting while leaving cues, response format, and task demands unchanged. This within-session context shift may not have provided sufficiently distinct retrieval cues to elicit differential renewal of appetitive or aversive associations across age. Furthermore, developmental work in rodents found no fear renewal after a brief 10-minute interval but observed renewal following a 24-hour delay (Kim & Richardson, 2009), and developmental differences in motivated memory may depend on consolidation processes (Cohen et al., 2019 (Cohen et al., , 2022)). Future studies should combine more distinct context manipulation with delayed renewal tests across multiple sessions. Additionally, the cross-sectional design established nonlinear associations between age and learning but cannot determine whether individuals show corresponding within-person changes across development. Longitudinal studies will be necessary to establish whether the tendency to infer multiple underlying causes truly emerges during adolescence.

The present findings have implications for understanding how extinction-based interventions operate across development, when disorders involving maladaptive learning from positive and negative outcomes often emerge (Goldway et al., 2023). Exposure-related therapeutic approaches reduce maladaptive responses to valenced outcomes by presenting the cue (e.g., feared or rewarding stimulus) without the anticipated outcome and have been used to treat anxiety and substance use behaviors (Kaplan et al., 2010). If changes in extinction are assigned to a new latent cause, treatment may establish a strong competing association without fully revisiting the original maladaptive association. Despite its well-established efficacy, there is evidence that exposure therapy may be less effective in adolescence, and emotional associations often return after therapeutic intervention in adolescents and adults (Drysdale et al., 2013; Liberman et al., 2005). The limited efficacy of extinction-based interventions in adolescence may be related to changes in latent cause inference. At the same time, the more persistent age pattern for aversive expectations suggests that the conditions needed to modify appetitive and aversive associations may not be identical. While the present study does not directly assess clinical symptoms or treatment response, we identify a cognitive process through which age and learning context may influence the durability of extinction-based interventions (Berwian et al., 2025).

Our findings characterize differences in dynamic learning of positive and negative associations within the same individuals and using a computational framework of how individuals cognitively represent their environment across development. Age-related changes in the acquisition and persistence of motivational cues may support adaptive learning in a dynamic world and may also shape vulnerability to salient associations that outlast the circumstances that formed them.

Methods

This study was preregistered on Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/UA684).

Deviations from the preregistered plan and exploratory analyses are noted. Participants 90 healthy participants ages 10-25 years old (M age = 17.88, SD = 4.57; 42 males) were included in the final sample. A target sample size of 90 was selected based on sample sizes from related research on conditioning and extinction learning across development (Shechner et al., 2014; Pattwell et al., 2012; Waters et al., 2017; Ganella et al., 2018). Of the participants included in analyses, 36.7% identified as Caucasian/White, 25.6% as African American, 15.6% as Asian, 1.1% as Native American, 16.7% as multiple races, 1.1% as Other, and 2.2% preferred not to answer. All experimental procedures were approved by Emory University's Institutional Review Board. Parents and adults gave written consent and minors provided written assent prior to participation in the study. All participants received a $30 gift card upon visit completion for their participation. Data from 15 additional participants were excluded from all analyses for meeting one or more of the following exclusion criteria: 1) neither CS-US contingency from acquisition was correctly identified in the post-task questionnaire (12 participants: four 10-13 years, three 14-17 years, one 18-21 years, three 22-25 years), 2) completed fewer than 50% of expectancy ratings during the learning task (one participant), 3) experienced technical difficulties (one participant), or 4) disclosed a diagnosis after participation (one participant). The first criterion diverged from the pre-registered exclusion criterion that participants must identify one contingency correctly. Because extinction can only be interpreted for associations that were learned, we refined the post-task contingency criterion to retain participants who correctly identified at least one reinforced CS-US association and exclude those who identified neither. An additional 15 participants were excluded from physiological data analyses (n = 75, M age = 17.87, SD = 4.51; Fig. S1-2) due to non-responsive data, as indicated by a breath test (11 participants), or technical difficulties (four participants).

Task Design

Participants completed an aversive and appetitive Pavlovian conditioning task adapted from a previous aversive extinction learning paradigm tested in adults (Fig. 1, Norbury et al., 2021), followed by a surprise recognition and source memory test for objects shown in the learning task (see Supplement). Both experimental tasks were programmed in PsychoPy Version 2022.2.5 (Peirce et al., 2019).

Learning Task. Participants were told that they were stuck on a spaceship in space and needed to collect coins to return to Earth. Three different robots (conditioned stimuli; CSs) carried their coins through the spaceship. They were told that the floor of the spaceship was messy, so robots must move around objects scattered on the floor. One of the robots sometimes tripped over the objects on the floor and might have dropped the participant's coins (CS+neg), one of the robots sometimes picked up coins that the other robot dropped (CS+pos), and one of the robots always kept the participant's coins safe (CS-). On each trial, participants were presented with a robot and instructed to rate on a sliding scale how much they expected to lose a coin (-100), keep their coins (0), or gain a coin (100). After each CS presentation, participants made their rating and were then presented with the outcome of that trial. Each trial consisted of a 3s CS presentation, during which participants made their expectancy rating, followed by a 3s outcome, where participants lost a coin, kept their coins, or gained a coin, followed by a 7 ± 2s inter-trial interval. To ensure participants understood how to make the ratings, participants practiced making untimed and timed expectancy ratings with the slider for all possible trial outcomes (lose coin, keep coin, gain coin) prior to task initiation. Importantly, they were told that their ratings did not impact the outcomes, but they may be used to help future space travelers, to ensure that participants did not attribute trial outcomes to their actions.

The learning task was split into three trial phases: Acquisition (Fig. 1A), Extinction 1, and Extinction 2 (Fig. 1B). During acquisition, one robot was reinforced with an appetitive US (gaining a coin; CS+pos), and another robot was reinforced with an aversive US (losing a coin; CS+neg); the third robot was not paired with a gain or loss outcome (CS-). Acquisition consisted of 48 trials (3 blocks × 16 trials; each 6 CS+pos, 6 CS+neg, 4 CS-trials). CSs were partially reinforced (P(US|CS) = ½), such that the CS+pos and CS+neg robots were paired with their respective appetitive or aversive outcome 50% of the time. CS-US contingencies were counterbalanced for each participant from six different possible robot-outcome combinations. Trial orders were also pseudorandomized such that no more than three of the same CS was presented consecutively, and the first presentation of CS+pos and CS+neg was always reinforced.

During Extinction 1, all CSs that were previously reinforced with a US were unpaired with their associated outcome; all robots kept the participant's coins safe. Extinction 1 consisted of 48 trials (4 blocks × 12 trials; each 4 CS+pos, 4 CS+neg, 4 CS-trials), and the transition from Acquisition was unsignaled. After Extinction 1, participants were instructed that they had made it through the spaceship and needed to complete the second leg of their journey on a new spaceship. Before they transferred to the second spaceship, participants viewed a 7-minute clip of a neutral animated YouTube video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e3YuEMOoL3w) played at 50% speed to increase the temporal delay between extinction phases. After the video, participants began the second part of their journey, Extinction 2, on a new spaceship, in a different virtual environment. Here, all CSs remained unreinforced with a gain or loss outcome. Extinction 2 consisted of 36 trials (3 blocks × 12 trials; each 4 CS+pos, 4 CS+neg, 4 CStrials) and which CS was presented on the first trial of Extinction 2 was evenly distributed across participants.

Trial-by-trial US expectancy ratings served as an explicit measure of conditioned responses. These continuous ratings represented how participants acquired and extinguished CS-US associations. SCR was also collected throughout the task as a physiological index of learning. CSs were blue, purple, and orange robots, and spaceship background images were generated using royalty free vector art from the Pixabay database (https://pixabay.com/). 198 unique child-friendly object images were selected from Harvard's Konkle Lab object image database, and 132 images were randomly included in the learning task (Konkle et al., 2010). The white backgrounds were manually removed from all objects, and image sizes were standardized to 126 × 126 pixels in the conditioning task and 315 × 315 pixels in the memory test (see Supplement). Following both experimental tasks, participants were instructed to match the CS with their associated outcome from Acquisition in a post-task questionnaire.

Physiological Data Acquisition.

Electrodermal activity was collected throughout the Pavlovian learning task to measure arousal, an indirect measure of implicit learning. Electrodermal activity (EDA) was measured via two shielded Ag-AgCl electrodes filled with 0.5% NaCl isotonic electrolyte gel (EL507; BIOPAC Systems, Inc.) attached to the hypothenar and thenar eminence of the nondominant palm. EDA signal was amplified with a DC amplifier (model EDA100C) with a gain factor of 5 μS/V, a lowpass filter of 10 Hz, digitalized at 2000 Hz using an MP-150 BIOPAC system. Prior to task initialization, participants were instructed to take a deep breath and hold it for 3s. Participants whose SCR did not increase in response to the breath holding task were considered non-responders and excluded from SCR analyses (Atlas et al., 2022). SCR data were processed in AcqKnowledge (BIOPAC Systems, Inc.) and filtered with a 250-Hz low-pass FIR filter and smoothed with a three-sample Gaussian kernel to reduce noise (Johnson et al., 2023). After downsampling to 250Hz, SCRs were semi-automatically scored in Autonomate (Green et al., 2014) as the largest deflection during the 0.8-3.8s window after stimulus onset for each trial, with a minimum rise time of 0.7s and a maximum rise time of 5s after response onset. Two independent raters then visually inspected the data and excluded trials showing excessive motion or other data distortions. SCR amplitudes less than 0.02 μS were considered non-responses and scored as 0, and all data were square root transformed (Lykken & Venables, 1971).

Modeling simultaneous appetitive and aversive reinforcement.

The standard latent cause model is designed around a single unconditioned stimulus and therefore does not simultaneously accommodate opposing valence outcomes within a single representational framework. Because our task presents both appetitive and aversive outcomes within the same learning session, we extended the model in two ways to reflect the structure of valenced learning. We compared these model variants with the original latent cause models fit to all trials, irrespective of valence.

First, we implemented a two-US model variant in which both outcomes are presented in the model as simultaneous features on every trial. The feature vector on each trial is a binary indicator of any CS-US co-occurrence. This allows a single latent cause to jointly predict the presence of one US and the absence of another. Renewal

To assess the return of conditioned responses in a novel environment in the absence of reinforcement, we compared participants' expectancy ratings for each CS between the end of Extinction 1 and the beginning of Extinction 2. Specifically, renewal was calculated for each CS+ by subtracting the mean response relative to CS-trials during the last 8-trial block of Extinction 1 from the mean responses during the first 8-trial block of Extinction 2. Although physiological renewal was preregistered as a primary analysis, we report it in the Supplement because SCR was summarized at the phase level for primary analyses given limited expected trial-level sensitivity to the task outcomes.

Statistical Approach and Tests

All statistical analyses were carried out in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). For all repeated measures (i.e., expectancy ratings, skin conductance, valenced-logBF) we fit linear mixed-effects models using the lme4 R package (Bates et al., 2015). We initially fit the maximal linear mixed-effects model, including a random intercept per participant and random slopes for all within-person fixed effects and their interactions (Barr et al., 2013). If the maximal model failed to converge, we incrementally removed random slopes to determine the most complex random effects structure supported by the data. Linear models were used for between-subjects analyses, including valence-collapsed logBF model. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons were computed using the emmeans package (Lenth et al., 2026) with Holm correction for multiple comparisons. For SCR, pairwise comparisons of CS SCRs were computed within each task phase, relative to the CS-as a reference condition to validate that participants learned and extinguished the associations.

Age-related differences. Age was treated as a continuous variable in all analyses. For all models with age-related hypotheses, we compared models with orthogonalized linear and quadratic age predictors using a likelihood ratio chi-squared test and retained the better-fitting model. Orthogonal polynomials of age were created using the poly function. Mixed-effects model analyses often favored models that included higher-order age predictors, suggesting complex non-linear age patterns. Thus, to further characterize these nonlinear developmental differences observed from the mixed-effects models, we fit generalized additive models (GAM, generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) for repeated measures) using the mgcv package in R (Wood, 2017). Unlike polynomial age terms often used to characterize age changes, GAMs make no a priori assumption about the shape of the age function, allowing for data-driven estimation of age-related differences in learning and extinction. The omnibus GAMMs predicted expectancy ratings from CS, phase of learning, and smooth age terms specific to each CS-by-phase combination, with random intercepts for participants: gam(rating ~ CS * phase + s(age, by = CS * phase, k = 5) + s(subject, bs = re), method = REML).

We constrained the basis dimension to k = 5 (default upper limit is k = 10) to impose a stricter penalty on complexity to avoid overfitting while still allowing for nonlinear flexibility. Models were fit using restricted error maximum likelihood (REML). To examine within-phase age-related differences, we additionally fit phase-specific GAMs modeling age, within-phase trial, and their interaction separately by CS using a tensor-product interaction term: s(age, by = CS) + s(trial, by = CS) + ti(age, trial, by = CS). We applied this same approach to characterize nonlinear age-related differences in latent cause formation. For age patterns that significantly differ from a horizontal line (p < 0.05), we report effective degrees of freedom (edf), which represents whether the pattern is linear or nonlinear (edf = 1 or edf > 1, respectively).

Identifying age periods of change and inflection points.

To localize age-related transitions in the fitted smooths derived from GAMMs, we followed a derivative based approach (Calabro et al., 2020; Heller et al., 2026; Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023), implemented using the gratia package in R (Simpson, 2024). For each smooth, we computed the first derivative via central finite differencing at 0.1-year intervals across the sample's age range. Age ranges in which the 95% confidence interval of the first derivative did not include zero were considered a period of statistically significant age-related change. Here, inflection points are quantified as the extrema of the second derivative (i.e., the ages at which the rate of change in the slope was maximal in absolute value). To quantify uncertainty around each inflection point, we drew 1,000 samples from the posterior distribution of the fitted smooth, calculated the second-derivative extremum location for each posterior draw, and report the 95% highest density interval (HDI) of these locations.

Age-related differences in learning within each phase of the task

To assess whether the age-related differences identified in the omnibus models reflected different learning patterns across trial types within each phase, we fit separate mixed effect models of expectancy ratings (Table S3) and SCR (Table S4) for each phase that included trial in addition to trial type and age.

For expectancy ratings, during Acquisition, ratings differed across CS (χ 2 (2) = 170.56, p < 0.001), and the change in ratings across trials varied with CS (CS × trial: χ 2 (2) = 13.74, p = 0.001) such that CS+pos ratings became more positive, CS+neg ratings became more negative, and CS-ratings remained near zero. The age pattern was also different across CS (CS × linear age: (χ 2 (2) = 19.09, p < 0.001; CS × quadratic age: χ 2 (2) = 11.42, p = 0.003). During Extinction 1, ratings differed across CS (χ 2 (2) = 69.78, p < 0.001), and the trial-wise trajectory again depended on CS (CS × trial: χ 2 (2) = 156.09, p < 0.001) such that responses to both CS+s moved toward zero, whereas CS-ratings remained comparatively stable.

There was also a CS × trial × linear age interaction (χ 2 (2) = 8.57, p = 0.014), reflecting greater trial-wise extinction of aversive associations across age. A similar overall pattern was observed during Extinction 2, with a main effect of CS (χ 2 (2) = 17.42, p < 0.001), and a CS × trial interaction (χ 2 (2) = 30.42, p < 0.001), as responses to both reinforced cues continued to approach neutral. Age × trial interactions were not significant in any phase, and no corresponding CS × age × trial interactions were observed in Acquisition or Extinction 2 (all p > 0.052).

For SCR, responses during Acquisition differed across CS (χ 2 (2) = 12.08, p = 0.002) and decreased across trials (χ 2 (1) = 58.77, p < 0.001). During Extinction 1, no effects involving CS, trial, or age were observed (all p > 0.055). During Extinction 2, SCR varied linearly with age (χ2 = 6.06, p = 0.014), but neither trial-wise changes nor their interactions with CS or age were significant (all p > 0.056).

S2.1 Supplemental model implementation

Theoretical motivation

The latent cause model (Gershman et al., 2010; Gershman & Niv, 2012) formalizes the intuition that learners do not encode associations directly between cues and outcomes but instead infer hidden states, or latent causes, that are jointly responsible for generating the cues and outcomes observed on a given trial. In this framework, extinction is construed as the inference that a new latent cause has begun generating outcomes, leaving the original acquisition cause intact but dormant. The concentration parameter governs, at a trial level, how readily the learner posits a new cause in response to evidence α that the current observations are poorly explained by existing causes.

In the context of the present task, a participant who enters extinction and finds that the previously-aversive CS+neg robot no longer loses coins may update their belief about the existing CS+neg-associated cause (behavior of low learner) or instead infer that something about the current α context has shifted and that a new cause is now generating outcomes (behavior of high learner). The α former yields gradual within-cause extinction learning whereas the latter preserves the CS-US belief from acquisition, which can be re-expressed in a new context (Extinction 2) as renewal. The concentration parameter therefore operationalizes the cognitive distinction between updating existing representations and constructing new ones.
