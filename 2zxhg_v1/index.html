To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate). This validation was made with an earlier, looser pattern; the pattern now requires “significant” or “significance” to follow the trigger word closely (#695).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. Introduction

As Dahl (2020) argues, democratic systems thrive when citizens are informed about political processes and actively participate in civic life, ensuring that decisions serve the broader public good rather than narrow partisan interests. In recent decades, scholars have documented a gradual erosion of democratic norms across many Western nations. This decline is reflected in reduced trust in democratic institutions, the rise of populist leaders, and deepening political polarization (Hyde, 2020). These developments prompt an important question: why some individuals lose faith in democracy, and through what psychological mechanisms does this occur?

We examine a central psychological construct in this study: national narcissism, also known as collective narcissism (Marchlewska et al., 2022). This refers to an exaggerated belief in the nation's superiority, sustained by a need for recognition from others (Golec de Zavala et al., 2009).

Research has shown that individuals high in national narcissism often reject pluralistic values (Golec de Zavala & Keenan, 2023), display greater openness to conspiracy narratives (Michalski et al., 2023a), and endorse discriminatory attitudes toward marginalized groups within the nation (Szczepańska et al., 2022). They may also support hierarchical arrangements that perpetuate inequality (Golec de Zavala, 2024). Such defensively oriented identities are frequently reinforced by populist rhetoric portraying the nation as morally exceptional yet constantly under threat from both internal and external sources (Marchlewska et al., 2017).

Prior research has linked national narcissism to reduced support for democratic governance (Marchlewska et al., 2022). More recent studies suggest that insufficient knowledge of democratic norms and institutional structures may contribute to the development of this defensive identity (Michalski et al., 2023a). We argue that individuals lacking "rules of the game" knowledge are more likely to adopt a narcissistic form of national identity. This, in turn, can diminish democratic commitment, strengthen preferences for authoritarian leadership, and increase receptivity to conspiracy narratives. Limited understanding of democratic principles may leave citizens vulnerable to rhetoric portraying the national ingroup as undervalued and under constant threatdiscourse that can, over time, cultivate anti-democratic attitudes.

Political Knowledge and its Dimensions

Numerous studies have shown that political knowledge is central to sustaining democratic life, fostering civic engagement, encouraging solidarity with fellow citizens, and reducing support for autocratic or populist governance (e.g., Cho, 2014; Delli Carpini & Keeter, 1996; Delli Carpini, 2005; Galston, 2001). Politically informed citizens are more likely to recognize threats to democratic governance, critically assess populist rhetoric, and reject oversimplified narratives that scapegoat outgroups or promote authoritarian solutions to complex problems (Galston, 2001). But what exactly constitutes political knowledge, and how should we conceptualize it in the context of democratic functioning?

Studies indicate that political knowledge is not a unidimensional construct as it has different sources (Barabas et al., 2014). One dimension, often cultivated through formal education, involves understanding the stable principles and structures of politics -what is commonly referred to as "rules of the game" knowledge. Another, more fluid dimension, is "current political knowledge", which reflects awareness of ongoing events and political actors, acquired through engagement with news and public affairs (Michalski et al., 2023a; 2023b). While such awareness can produce "vigilant citizens" (Norris, 2011), it may also encourage perceptions of politics as an arena of constant conflict, potentially undermining confidence in the system's effectiveness. We argue that rules of the game knowledge (encompassing democratic principles, institutional functions, and system structure) plays a particularly important role in fostering and sustaining democratic support (Cho, 2014; Delli Carpini & Keeter, 1996). Prior research shows that this form of knowledge promotes unconventional participation by clarifying how citizens can influence politics (Michalski et al., 2023b) and serves as a protective factor against national narcissism and climate change conspiracy beliefs (Michalski et al., 2023a). In our framework, it is treated as a civic resource that equips individuals to navigate institutional complexity, interpret political processes, and act effectively within a democratic system. Moreover, it may help inoculate citizens against populist rhetoric that reduces complex realities to emotionally charged divisions between "the people" and "the elites" as well as against other anti-democratic narratives.

Political Knowledge, National Narcissism and Anti-Democratic Sentiments

Studies consistently show that citizens with a strong understanding of democratic systems (reflected in high rules of the game knowledge) tend to hold political attitudes that strengthen democratic governance. For example, they are generally less polarized and less likely to support candidates who promote anti-democratic positions (Eroglu et al., 2025). They also display greater tolerance for political diversity (e.g., Delli Carpini & Keeter, 1996; Galston, 2001; Hall, 2018) and report fewer negative health effects from political stress (Smith, 2022). Likewise, individuals with substantial political knowledge are less inclined to restrict civil liberties when confronted with threats (Carriere et al., 2019). A common thread in these findings is psychological security (Sniderman, 1975), which rules of the game knowledge can enhance in two ways: by making political processes more understandable, thereby reducing uncertainty (Galston, 2001), and by increasing political efficacy through clearer pathways for exerting influence on political decisionmakers (Michalski et al., 2023b; Reichert, 2016). This security fosters respect for differing viewpoints and helps guard against authoritarian impulses (Sniderman, 1975; Marchlewska et al., 2019). Conversely, when psychological security is lacking, individuals may resist compromise, view political diversity negatively, and challenge democratic norms intended to accommodate a range of perspectives (Peffley & Rohrschneider, 2003).

When psychological security is lacking, national identification can take forms that undermine democratic support. Research shows that the character of national identity significantly shapes citizens' commitment to democracy (Erhardt et al., 2021; Marchlewska et al., 2022). This connection is rooted in psychological processes, with low security, such as diminished personal control, emerging as a key factor in how individuals relate to their nation (Cichocka et al., 2018; Marchlewska et al., 2020; Cichocka & Cisłak, 2020; Golec de Zavala et al., 2019; Marchlewska et al., 2017). National narcissism is understood as a defensive form of ingroup identity arising from unmet individual and collective needs (Cichocka, 2016). For example, Marchlewska et al. (2018) found that inducing perceptions of long-term disadvantage for the national ingroup raised levels of national narcissism. In their study, this disadvantage was framed as the belief that outsiders had systematically diminished the nation's influence and sovereignty. Elevated national narcissism subsequently predicted greater support for populist policies. In situations of perceived threat, national narcissism often serves as a compensatory response, sought through the perceived grandiosity of the national group (Golec de Zavala et al., 2009; Marchlewska et al., 2017). This inflated self-image makes individuals more receptive to leaders who claim they can restore the group's status and act as its sole defenders. As a result, national narcissism is consistently linked to support for populist, anti-democratic, or authoritarian leaders who promise simple solutions to complex problems (Golec de Zavala & Keenan, 2021).

In other words, individuals high in national narcissism often see democratic processes (with their emphasis on debate, disagreement, compromise, and gradual change) as ineffective and, more importantly, as threatening to their group's fragile self-image (Golec de Zavala & Keenan, 2021). This defensiveness extends to intolerance toward groups perceived as challenging the ingroup's narrative or status, such as minorities or rival political parties (e.g., Golec de Zavala et al., 2009 , 2016). Because these outgroups are viewed as critics or potential threats to the ingroup's perceived superiority, collective narcissists tend to avoid the dialogue and negotiation that are central to pluralistic societies, thereby undermining their support for democratic norms.

Low democratic support among national narcissists should be particularly acute when evaluating the functioning of democracy within their own country. This is, in part, because democratic systems, with their emphasis on compromise, pluralism, and the accommodation of diverse interests, are inherently ill-equipped to satisfy the profound and often insatiable psychological needs that fuel collective narcissism -such as the need for constant external validation of the ingroup's superiority and unquestioned special status (Golec de Zavala et al., 2009). When their nation's democracy fails to deliver on these unrealistic demands, collective narcissists are likely to perceive it not merely as underperforming, but as fundamentally flawed.

This perceived inability of their democracy to cater to these deep-seated, identity-protective needs can then lead them to generalize this frustration, resulting in a low evaluation of democracy as a viable or effective governing system overall, further justifying their preference for leaders and systems that promise to fulfill those narcissistic cravings.

The psychological insecurity underpinning national collective narcissism also manifests in a heightened susceptibility to conspiracy beliefs (Biddlestone et al., 2022; Cichocka et al., 2016).

These narratives offer a means to protect a threatened and idealized ingroup image by attributing negative events to the actions of malevolent external forces, rather than confronting internal shortcomings (van Prooijen & Douglas, 2018). Given that political knowledge is argued to foster psychological security (Sniderman, 1975), which in turn can buffer against national narcissism (Marchlewska et al., 2022 , Michalski et al., 2023a), it is plausible that greater political knowledge may indirectly reduce the endorsement of conspiracy beliefs. This would occur by diminishing the reliance on national narcissism as a compensatory mechanism for underlying insecurities, thereby lessening the need for conspiratorial explanations to maintain a grandiose ingroup view. Supporting this mechanism, Michalski et al. (2023a) found that greater rules of the game knowledge reduced climate change conspiracy beliefs by mitigating national narcissism. In our research, we examine anti-democratic sentiments (i.e., lower support for democracy, endorsement of dictatorship, and conspiracy beliefs) by focusing on knowledge about the rules of the game. We assume that lower levels of such knowledge will be positively associated with national narcissism, which, in turn, will predict stronger endorsement of anti-democratic sentiments.

Overview of the Current Research

The present research has two primary objectives. First, it aims to investigate the relationships between dimensions of political knowledge and anti-democratic sentiments (i.e., lower support for democracy, dictatorship endorsement, conspiracy beliefs). This research, following the works of Michalski and colleagues (2023a, 2023b; also see Delli Carpini and Keeter, 1996), distinguishes and incorporates the rules of the game dimension of political knowledge, referring to an understanding of democratic principles and institutional functions and current political knowledge consisting of facts related to recent political events and prominent political actors. Furthermore, we include a direct measure of dictatorship endorsement recognizing that dissatisfaction with democracy does not invariably translate to support for authoritarian regimes (Schedler & Sarsfield, 2007; Wuttke et al., 2020).

Second, this research seeks to investigate the psychological mechanisms underlying these relationships. We propose that limited rules of the game knowledge may leave individuals without a clear sense of how democracy works, and that without the psychological security such understanding affords, national narcissism may become a more accessible form of compensation for frustrated psychological needs (Cichocka, 2016; Golec de Zavala et al., 2019). Because rules of the game knowledge and national narcissism each appear to predict lower levels of the other over time (Michalski et al., 2023a), we specify this ordering on theoretical grounds rather than as a causal test. To further elucidate these mechanisms, in Study 2 we incorporate citizens' evaluation of their own country (i.e., Poland) as a democratic state, linking abstract democratic attitudes to evaluations of a specific national political system. Individuals scoring high on national narcissism should therefore evaluate democracy in their own country less favorably, to the extent that its institutions are perceived as failing to affirm the ingroup's inflated image and this dissatisfaction should in turn extend to support for democracy in general (Marchlewska et al., 2022). We attribute this evaluation to these unmet needs rather than to informed appraisal of institutional performance alone.

To this end, we conducted two studies with the two facets of political knowledge (i.e., knowledge about the rules of the game vs. current political knowledge) as independent variables and support for democracy, dictatorship endorsement and conspiracy beliefs as dependent variables.

In all studies, we controlled for national identification to observe the unique effects of national narcissism accounting for its overlap with national identification. In Study 1, we predicted that higher knowledge about the rules of the game (vs. of current events and actors) would be associated with higher support for democracy (H1), lower dictatorship endorsement (H2) and lower conspiracy beliefs (H3). We further hypothesized that these associations would be explained by lower national narcissism (H1a, H2a, and H3a, respectively). Study 2 aimed to replicate and extend these findings by proposing a serial indirect effects model. Specifically, we predicted that higher knowledge about the rules of the game would be associated with lower national narcissism, which in turn would be associated with higher perceptions of the country's system as democratic. We hypothesized that this indirect pathway will be associated with higher support for democracy (H4), lower endorsement of dictatorship (H5), and lower belief in conspiracies (H6). Analytic Strategy

To examine the unadjusted associations between the variables of interest, we started with performing basic bivariate analyses. Next, since knowledge dimensions share common antecedents and tend to be highly correlated (e.g. Delli Carpini & Keeter, 1996; Johann, 2012), we examined the discriminant validity of two political knowledge dimensions we investigated. The last step involved estimating a structural equation model which involved two types of political knowledge as predictors, national narcissism (when controlling for national identification) as an intervening variable and support for democracy, dictatorship endorsement and conspiracy beliefs as dependent variables (see Fig. 1). Additionally, we estimated the model controlling for demography and political conservatism -the results are presented in Supplementary Materials. The model was estimated using the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012) with the WLSMV estimator, which is suitable for categorical (binary and ordinal) indicators. Model identification was achieved by fixing the variance of latent variables to 1. We allowed the residuals of two reverse-coded items from the support for democracy scale to covary to account for shared method variance associated with reverse wording. The dataset contained no missing data, as participants were required to respond to all items. Indirect effects were estimated using bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples and biascorrected 95% confidence intervals.

Discriminant Validity of Two Dimensions of Political Knowledge

First, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with the WLSMV estimator to test whether the two dimensions of political knowledge represent correlated yet distinguishable factors.

Identification was achieved by fixing the latent variables' variances to 1. The results supported the two-factor structure: the model with two correlated dimensions fit the data acceptably (χ²(43) = 105.19, RMSEA = .045, CFI = .973, sRMR = .064) and fit significantly better than a one-factor model (χ²(44) = 151.29, RMSEA = .059, CFI = .953, sRMR = .075, Δχ²(1) = 28.36, p < .001) 4.

Second, we inspected the 95% confidence interval of the correlation between the latent factors (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). Its upper bound did not exceed unity (UL = .876), which suggests that this criterion is met. However, the more stringent benchmark proposed by Rönkkö and Cho (2022), namely, that the upper bound should fall below .80, yields a different picture: by this standard, the factor correlation in Study 1 indicates a marginal discriminant validity problem.

Third, we assessed discriminant validity using the Fornell-Larcker (AVE-SV) criterion (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The average variance extracted for both dimensions (rules of the game = .42; current political knowledge = .51) was lower than their shared variance (SV = .65), indicating that this criterion was not met.

Finally, given the known limitations of the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which has a high falsepositive rate in detecting discriminant-validity problems (Rönkkö & Cho, 2022), we computed HTMT2 (Roemer et al., 2021). Its value fell below the commonly accepted threshold of .85 (HTMT2 = .809).

Taken together, these results indicate that the two dimensions are highly correlated and that the evidence does not decisively establish their empirical distinctness, consistent with prior observations that dimensions of political knowledge tend to be strongly correlated (Delli Carpini & Keeter, 1996). A high correlation, however, need not imply theoretical redundancy: general dispositions toward acquiring knowledge coexist with substantial differentiation across content domains (Ackerman, 1996), suggesting that the shared variance of the two dimensions reflects a general propensity to be knowledgeable, while what distinguishes them lies in their unique variance. Our analyses therefore focus on the unique variance of each dimension, where contentspecific relations should be more clearly located. We treat the two-dimensional distinction as theoretically motivated and interpret the dimension-specific findings with appropriate caution.

Discussion

Study 1 aimed to examine how knowledge about the rules of the game relates to antidemocratic sentiments. The findings supported our predictions. In line with our central hypothesis, knowledge about the rules of the game was indirectly associated with support for democracy (H1), dictatorship endorsement (H2), and conspiracy beliefs (H3), with these indirect associations operating through lower national narcissism (H1a, H2a, and H3a respectively). These results highlight that understanding democratic principles, more so than awareness of current events, is linked to pro-democratic orientations, largely through its association with lower national narcissism.

At the same time, an opposite pattern emerged for current political knowledge when controlling for knowledge about the rules of the game. Greater awareness of current political events and political actors net of knowledge about democratic principles was associated with higher national narcissism. In turn, current political knowledge was indirectly associated with lower support for democracy and with higher dictatorship endorsement and conspiracy beliefs through its association with higher national narcissism. This pattern suggests that the relationship between political knowledge and anti-democratic attitudes may depend not only on how much individuals know, but also on the type of political knowledge they possess.

In Study 2, we aimed to replicate these findings and, additionally, extended our model by testing a serial indirect effects pathway. Specifically, we investigated whether perceptions of the country's political system as democratic would function as a key link between national narcissism and its negative outcomes. We predicted that higher national narcissism would be associated with perception of Poland as less democratic (i.e., government is less receptive to citizens' opinions, civil rights are not respected etc.) given its links with limited understanding of democratic norms and outcomes misaligned with national narcissists' desires (i.e., protection of inflated group image). This negative perception, in turn, was expected to be associated with less democratic regime preferences and greater endorsement of conspiracy beliefs.

As in Study 1, we began by examining the unadjusted associations between the variables of interest. Discussion

Study 2 aimed to replicate and extend the findings of Study 1. A key extension was the introduction of a new variable: the perception of Poland as a democratic state, which captures citizens' subjective assessment of how well their country's political system adheres to democratic principles. The results largely replicated the core findings from Study 1: knowledge about the rules of the game was again associated with more favorable democratic attitudes via lower national narcissism which confirmed our hypotheses.

Consistent with our primary hypotheses and Study 1, knowledge about the rules of the game was negatively associated with national narcissism, which in turn was indirectly associated with higher support for democracy, lower dictatorship endorsement, and lower conspiracy beliefs.

Furthermore, serial indirect effects analyses indicated that knowledge about the rules of the game was associated with all three dependent variables sequentially through its association with national narcissism and subsequently through its association with the perception of Poland as a democratic state. This suggests that national narcissists, fueled by a lack of knowledge about the rules of the game, report lower evaluations of their own country's democratic system. This is likely because it is unable to fulfill their extremely high expectations related to idealized and grandiose ingroup image.

Furthermore, results of serial indirect effects analyses provided support for the hypothesized theoretical model. Taken together, these findings suggest that lower levels of this knowledge are associated with stronger narcissistic identity, which is in turn linked to more negative evaluations of the democratic functioning of one's own country, and ultimately to greater rejection of democracy as a system and stronger endorsement of conspiracy beliefs.

Study 2 also replicated findings related to negative associations of current political knowledge, net of knowledge about the rules of the game, with anti-democratic sentiments via national narcissism. However, results also indicated a reversed, yet weaker, relationships with the dependent variables via national identification. Taken together, these dual pathways suggest that current political knowledge is neither straightforwardly beneficial nor harmful for democratic attitudes, but its function depends on the psychological channel through which it operates. When current political awareness heightens national narcissism, it may serve as a vehicle for antidemocratic sentiment, particularly when the political discourse is dominated by conflict or threatframing narratives. Yet its weaker, positive indirect association via national identification suggests a civic potential: staying informed may also reinforce a sense of shared belonging that is potentially beneficial for democracy.

General Discussion

The contemporary erosion of democratic norms has spurred urgent questions about the psychological factors that lead citizens to turn away from democracy. The present research sought to address this issue by exploring the protective role of political knowledge against anti-democratic sentiments. We proposed that this relationship is not straightforward but is instead driven by a specific psychological mechanism: the mitigation of national narcissism. We found that knowledge about the rules of the game, but not current political knowledge, had the potential to reduce psychological defensiveness in the form of national narcissism, a compensatory mechanism for coping with psychological shortcomings (Cichocka et al., 2018; Cichocka & Cisłak, 2020; Golec de Zavala et al., 2019; Marchlewska et al., 2020).

Across two studies conducted in Poland, our findings consistently supported this theoretical model. We demonstrated that knowledge about the rules of the game, rather than mere awareness of current political events, was negatively related to anti-democratic sentiments. Study 1 established that this type of knowledge was associated with lower national narcissism, which in turn predicted greater support for democracy, lower endorsement of dictatorship and lower conspiracy beliefs.

Study 2 replicated these core findings and added a contextual-related variable (i.e., evaluation of political system in democratic terms). The results showed that individuals with greater rules of the game knowledge were less prone to identify with their nation in narcissistic fashion, and therefore more likely to perceive their own country's political system as democratic, which was associated with weaker anti-democratic sentiments. Together, these studies suggest that it is the deep, structural knowledge of democratic processes (i.e., what are democratic norms and principles and why they are crucial for this type of government) that serves as a crucial psychological buffer, protecting citizens from the allure of national narcissism and its anti-democratic consequences.

Relations between political knowledge and anti-democratic sentiments are, however, more nuanced. Specifically, current political knowledge showed the opposite relationship with national narcissism than knowledge about the rules of the game after controlling for their shared variance.

Statistically, this reversal reflects a suppression effect -although both dimensions were negatively correlated with national narcissism at the zero-order level, the unique component of current political knowledge was positively associated with it. This pattern replicated across both studies with coefficients of comparable magnitude (β = .38 and β = .31), and collinearity diagnostics were unproblematic in both (see footnotes 5 and 11), which suggests a stable estimate rather than one destabilized by the high correlation between the two dimensions. It indicates that the shared and unique portions of current political knowledge relate to national narcissism in opposite ways. One possible interpretation of the opposing associations is that national narcissism may be linked not to political awareness per se, but to a specific configuration of political knowledge: relatively high awareness of current political events combined with limited understanding of democratic institutions. In such cases, individuals may be attentive to political conflict and perceived threats while lacking the institutional framework needed to interpret these dynamics as normal features of democratic systems. This imbalance may foster perceptions of instability or threat, which align with the defensive worldview characteristic of national narcissism (Cichocka, 2016; Cichocka et al., 2016). This pattern resonates with Norris's (2011) notion of the "critical citizen" -someone highly attentive to political affairs yet dissatisfied with how democratic institutions perform. While Norris (2011) originally framed such dissatisfaction as potentially constructive for democracy, our findings suggest a more troubling possibility: when political attentiveness is not grounded in an understanding of democratic principles, it may reinforce grievance rather than engagement. Without a grasp of why democratic systems are inherently slow, contested, and compromise-driven, politically aware individuals may read the normal friction of democratic governance as evidence of failure or elite manipulation -narratives that national narcissism readily amplifies. This also has broader implications for civic education efforts: increasing political awareness without simultaneously deepening citizens' understanding of democratic norms and institutions may not only fail to protect against anti-democratic sentiments, but could, under certain conditions, contribute to them. This is not to say, however, that current political knowledge is without democratic value. A deep understanding of democratic principles may anchor citizens' commitment to the system, while vigilant monitoring of the political landscape is essential for critically evaluating political actors and identifying those who may pose a threat to citizens' self-interests. Indeed, citizens with up-to-date political knowledge are better equipped to assess how well political elites represent their preferences and interests -a core component of democratic legitimacy (Dalton, 2018). While this type of knowledge helps translate individual interests into political choices and actions, it is knowledge about the rules of the game that orients those choices within a democratic frameworkensuring that personal goals are pursued in ways that respect pluralism, the rule of law, and institutional checks and balances. This distinction helps clarify why, in our findings, it was knowledge about the rules of the game, not current political knowledge, that consistently buffered against anti-democratic sentiments.

The interpretation of these divergent relations, however, must be considered in light of the strong correlation between the two dimensions. Knowledge across broad domains tends to be positively correlated, as its acquisition draws on shared antecedents such as general cognitive ability (Ackerman, 1996). Political knowledge is no different in this respect. As a form of knowledge, its dimensions rest on the same general foundations, while also drawing on more specific antecedents such as political interest and opportunities to acquire political information (Delli Carpini & Keeter, 1996). The variance shared by the two types of political knowledge therefore largely reflects a general tendency to be politically knowledgeable, rather than what is distinctive about either type.

For this reason, our analyses focus on the unique variance of each dimension, which, we argue, better captures the content specific to a given type of knowledge and its distinct relations with other variables. This focus on unique variance is what allows the opposing associations of the two dimensions with national narcissism to surface. Their divergence, in turn, indicates that the two forms of political knowledge occupy different regions of the nomological network (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). It also has a broader implication, because treating political knowledge as a unidimensional construct may obscure the distinct, and sometimes opposing, relations that its components bear to external variables -in our case, national narcissism.

This relationship is further complicated considering the nature of national narcissism. Our model proposes that rules of the game knowledge fosters psychological security (Sniderman, 1975), thereby diminishing the need for the defensive compensation that defines national narcissism (Cichocka, 2016). However, the relationship may be bidirectional, creating a self-reinforcing cycle.

In fact, studies by Michalski and colleagues (2023a) found not only that knowledge about the rules of the game may attenuate national narcissism, but also that individuals high in national narcissism were less likely to acquire knowledge about the rules of the game over time. This suggests that national narcissism not only stems from a knowledge deficit but also actively obstructs the learning process, making it difficult to reach narcissistic individuals with civic education. Adding to this complexity, research by Górska and colleagues (2023) showed that national narcissism and antidemocratic sentiments (i.e., conspiracy beliefs and right-wing authoritarianism) also mutually reinforce each other over time. The results of our studies, thus, emphasize the role of knowledge about the rules of the game as a potential antidote capable of interrupting this self-perpetuating, democracy-eroding cycle. A key challenge for future research is to explore how effective civic education should be designed to circumvent the defensive mechanisms that prevent learning among national narcissists.

Furthermore, our research extends the theoretical understanding of how national narcissism translates into anti-democratic sentiment by introducing a key intervening construct: the perception of one's own country as a high-functioning democracy. Results of Study 2 indicated that lack of knowledge about the rules of the game was related to higher national narcissism and in turn predicted a more negative evaluation of Poland's democracy. This dissatisfaction is not arbitrary. It stems from the inherent inability of a pluralistic, compromise-based democratic system to provide what national narcissists psychologically crave: constant external validation of the nation's greatness and the soothing of their deep-seated insecurities (Cichocka et al., 2018; Golec de Zavala & Keenan, 2021). By devaluing their own nation's democratic processes as flawed and incapable of delivering this recognition, individuals high in national narcissism may more easily justify their turn towards authoritarian alternatives that promise to restore the nation's "true" greatness. This finding illustrates why citizens who lack knowledge about the rules of the game and adopt a narcissistic identity find anti-democratic sentiments so appealing.

Limitations and Future Directions

The present research has several limitations that offer valuable directions for future investigation. First, the cross-sectional nature of our data precludes causal claims. For instance, an experimental design could test whether an intervention aimed at increasing knowledge about the rules of the game leads to a subsequent decrease in national narcissism and anti-democratic attitudes.

A further limitation concerns the dimensionality of political knowledge. The two facets were strongly correlated, and the discriminant validity evidence was mixed, supportive in Study 2 but only marginal in Study 1, so we cannot claim with certainty that they are fully separable constructs.

Importantly, this does not undermine our finding that the unique parts of the two dimensions relate to national narcissism in different, and even opposite, ways -this pattern was robust and replicated across studies. What remains less certain is how these unique parts should be interpreted. Because the two dimensions overlap so heavily, the labeling of their unique components rests on the theoretical interpretation we develop in this paper, rather than on the discriminant validity evidence alone.

Related to this, we do not claim that two dimensions exhaust the construct of political knowledge, which is regarded as multifaceted and unlikely to be fully captured by a limited set of indicators (Barabas et al., 2014; Johann, 2012). Building on the work of Barabas et al. (2014), we focused on two dimensions: a structural one, concerning the relatively stable rules and institutions that define how the political system works, and a temporal one, concerning the events and actors that occupy that system at a given moment. The role of the past, however, was omitted. Knowledge of political history, particularly of systemic transformations such as Poland's transition from authoritarian rule to democracy, provides an interpretive lens for evaluating present political developments and may be no less consequential for democratic attitudes than knowledge about the rules of the game. It is also a theoretically interesting case, since such knowledge could relate to national narcissism in either direction by supporting an appreciation of how democratic institutions have been won and lost, or instead furnishing narratives of national grievance and lost greatness (see e.g., Główczewski et al., 2022; Klar & Bilewicz, 2017). Clarifying how additional dimensions such as this one fit within the structure of political knowledge, and how each relates to defensive national identity, is an important task for future work.

While our findings provide support for the proposed psychological mechanisms, some elements of our model were inferred rather than directly measured. We theorized knowledge about the rules of the game enhances psychological security (in contrast to current political knowledge), which in turn reduces national narcissism, but we did not include a direct measure of individuallevel psychological security in our studies. Future research should explicitly measure this construct to provide more direct evidence for its mediating role.

Another limitation was related to the measurement of conspiracy beliefs. We used a general measure to capture overall susceptibility to conspiracy thinking (Brotherton et al., 2013), in line with our aim to treat it as a broader indicator of anti-democratic sentiment. However, recent work suggests that different types of conspiracy beliefs may serve distinct functions (Nera, 2024; Sutton et al., 2024), especially in the context of research on collective narcissism (e.g., Karakula et al., 2025 Karakula et al., , 2026)). Our measure included non-political content, which did not allow us to distinguish between such forms. Future research should use more specific measures aligned with the research context.

Finally, our studies were conducted exclusively in Poland which limits the generalizability of our findings to other political environments, such as countries with longer democratic traditions (e.g., the United States or the United Kingdom). Cross-cultural replications are essential to establish the broader validity of our model and to understand how the interplay between political knowledge, national narcissism, and pro-democratic sentiments varies across different societal contexts.

Overall, our studies extend previous research by pointing to psychological mechanisms that may help account for why individuals with greater knowledge about the rules of the game tend to be less likely to endorse anti-democratic sentiments. Our findings suggest that a deeper understanding of democratic principles may be linked to lower levels of national narcissism, which in turn appears to relate to more favorable evaluations of democracy in one's own country and stronger democratic attitudes.

However, important questions remain open. At what stage of life can such educational interventions be most effective? In what contexts can these methods of democratic education be implemented? Can high-quality civic education meaningfully counteract the appeal of antidemocratic beliefs? These questions call for further empirical investigation.
