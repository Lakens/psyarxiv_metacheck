Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 13 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Our study examined how modulating agency through free choice and retrospective cues: temporal delay and spatial congruency, could influence memory for words. Intentional binding (IB) was used as the implicit measure of Sense of Agency (SoA). In Experiment 1, participants pressed a key to display words at varying delays (200 ms, 450 ms, and 700 ms), and spatial congruency of word positions, followed by a surprise recognition memory test. Results indicated that SoA was influenced by time delay but not by spatial congruency, while memory remained unaffected. Contrary to the existing literature of IB, the 200 ms delay showed no binding effect, warranting further investigation into factors affecting intentional binding in short time windows, especially with spatial incongruity in visual modality. Experiment 2 was similar to Experiment 1, with one addition: participants responded yes/no to whether the word appeared in their intended location, exploring how engagement with spatial cues of word appearance influences the SoA.

Results for the time delay on SoA were similar, and spatial congruency this time showed a significant effect on SoA. However, recognition memory showed no sensitivity to either factor. These findings suggest that while temporal proximity between action and effect enhances the SoA, spatial congruency matters when participants were asked to reflect upon it. In contrast, recognition memory remains unaffected by these factors, indicating either a dissociation between agency computation and memory encoding processes or competition for mental resources required for agency perception and encoding.

Introduction

The sense of agency (SoA) is understood as the experience of initiating and controlling one's actions and their effects, which is central to voluntary action and well being (Haggard, 2017; Frith, 2005). SoA is believed to emerge from a comparison between internally generated motor predictions and actual sensory feedback (Chambon at al., 2014; Blakemore & Frith, 2003), with discrepancies reducing SoA and coherence enhancing it (Sato & Yasuda, 2005).

Contemporary models suggest SoA arises from a combination of prospective cues, such as action preparation, free choice; and retrospective cues, like temporal contiguity and spatial congruency (Barlas & Obhi, 2013; Moore & Fletcher, 2012).

Recent studies have demonstrated that SoA may not simply accompany action but actively enhance memory encoding. A growing body of evidence suggests that when participants perceive greater control over actions or outcomes, they show better memory performance (Ruiz et al., 2023; Murty et al., 2019; Voss et al., 2011a Voss et al., , 2011b)). Recognition memory improved when participants had the opportunity to choose and act upon the stimulus presentation, with a simple key press. (Markant et al., 2016; Markant et al., 2014; Chiu & Egner, 2015; Cloutier & Macrae, 2008). Furthermore, the SoA-memory link has been attributed to several mechanisms, including the self-reference effect (Kim & Johnson, 2012) and the enactment effect with deeper associative or spatial encoding (Engelkamp, 1986; Roberts et al., 2022). However, to accurately attribute the change in memory to SoA, a better operationalization needs to be established with proper incorporation of cues. Very few studies have attempted to modulate the levels of SoA through multiple cues besides free choice and then assess the subsequent effect on memory (Hon & Yeo, 2021; Schreiner et al., 2025; Tsuji & Imaizumi, 2022).

In a recent key study, Hon and Yeo (2021) found that participants remembered stimuli more accurately when SoA was high, achieved through short delays and spatially congruent action-effects. However, subsequent work has yielded mixed findings. Tsuji and Imaizumi (2022) failed to replicate these memory benefits using a remember/know task. Schreiner et al. (2025) used the paradigm of Hon and Yeo (2021) and manipulated SoA through action-effect congruency and predictability (random vs. blocked trials). They found that congruency and predictability independently influenced the sense of agency (SoA), with congruent and predictable effects leading to higher SoA. Congruency also provided a slight memory boost, while predictability did not enhance memory despite increasing SoA. In all these studies, researchers have been able to manipulate the Sense of Agency (SoA) using a similar paradigm.

However, they have only examined the two extremes of SoA manipulation: either low or high.

Therefore, eliciting distinct experiential shift of agency, but eroding the subtlety with which SoA usually is processed through brain mechanisms. So, by including moderate manipulations to cues that elicit different SoA experience, we may correctly attribute any changes in memory, if occurring, to not explicit rather implicit change of agency experience. Further, the small and occasional memory effects that have been found cannot be directly attributed to SoA due to the bias associated with the explicit measurement of SoA.

Previous studies that have tried to assess the impact of SoA on memory have used explicit measures to identify the feeling of agency associated with key press choices (Hon & Yeo, 2021; Tsuji & Imaizumi, 2022; Schreiner et al., 2025). We wanted to use intentional binding (IB) as an implicit and indirect measure of SoA, wherein IB refers to the subjective temporal compression between a voluntary action and its sensory outcome, such that the interval between them is perceived as shorter than it objectively is (Haggard et al., 2002; Moore & Obhi, 2012). Stronger temporal compression is generally interpreted as reflecting a stronger implicit sense of agency. Unlike explicit agency judgments, which require participants to consciously evaluate their level of control, IB provides an indirect behavioral index of agency based on temporal perception. This distinction is important because explicit ratings may be influenced by demand characteristics, post-hoc reasoning, or metacognitive interpretation, whereas IB is thought to capture lower-level sensorimotor and predictive processes involved in action-outcome integration. By employing an implicit measure of agency, the present study sought to reduce reliance on explicit self-report and examine whether agency-related processes influence memory under conditions that minimize strategic introspection. While IB has proven robust across a range of paradigms (Imaizumi & Tanno, 2019; Engbert et al., 2007; Haggard et al., 2002), it has not been employed in the previous SoA-Memory studies.

In the current study, we aimed to investigate how the modulation of the SoA over movement of words through temporal delay and spatial congruency could influence the recognition memory associated with it. Given previous findings, we expected greater SoA at shorter delays and for spatially congruent outcomes (Imaizumi & Tanno, 2019; Moore & Obhi, 2012), and we hypothesized that SoA-enhancing conditions might also facilitate memory encoding.

Experiment 1

Our experimental paradigm was adapted from prior SoA research (Hon & Yeo, 2021), in which participants chose between two response options (up or down arrow keys) to make the word appear in the upper or lower position of the computer screen. The timing of the word's appearance following the action was manipulated through two variables: temporal delay (200 ms, 450 ms, 700 ms) and spatial congruency (the alignment or misalignment between the direction of the keypress and the word's appearance). These manipulations were designed to modulate the SoA. Trials with low delay and congruent conditions were expected to produce a stronger sense of agency, reflecting a strong intentional binding effect. This approach was grounded in previous research highlighting the significance of delay and congruency as key factors influencing the SoA (Moore & Haggard, 2010; Hon & Yeo, 2021). Participants were asked to estimate the time interval between their action (the key press) and the subsequent outcome (the word's appearance) using a time interval estimation task, to assess the intentional binding effect as an implicit measure of SoA. Memory for the words was evaluated in a subsequent surprise memory recognition task, once all the words had been presented.

Method Design

The study utilized a within-subject design with two variables: Temporal Delay (200ms, 450ms, 700ms) and Spatial Congruity (Congruent, Incongruent). Each word was randomly assigned to one of the six conditions, ensuring equal distribution. The variables measured were participants' time estimation accuracy during the Time Interval Estimation Task (IB Task) and their recognition accuracy during the Memory Task. SoA was inferred from intentional binding, where more negative time estimates indicate stronger agency, while memory performance was assessed through recognition accuracy. Participants shall estimate the temporal interval between their keypress and the appearance of the word stimulus. SoA (measured through Intentional binding) has been operationalized as the reported time estimation error, which shall be calculated as: Estimated Interval -Actual Interval. Negative values would indicate temporal underestimation (i.e., perceived temporal compression between action and outcome), which would be interpreted as stronger intentional binding and thus stronger implicit sense of agency. Conversely, more positive values reflected weaker temporal compression and reduced binding.

Participants

Participants were prescreened to ensure normal or corrected-to-normal vision. An a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009) for detecting 2x3 Repeated Measures ANOVA, given a moderate effect size f = 0.25, with 80% power, and an alpha value of 0.05, yielded a desired sample size of 19 participants (8 females and 11 males), age ranging from 18-31 years.

Participants were selected from the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research Bhopal, through a Google form that was circulated in the through mail and social media groups.

Apparatus

The experiment was conducted on a 24-inch monitor with a resolution of 1920x1080 pixels, positioned at a viewing distance of 60 cm, with word appearing up/down at a visual angle of 8.14 degree. The tasks were designed using PsychoPy 2024.2.1. Responses were collected via a standard keyboard, and visual stimuli were presented on a dark background for optimal visibility. JASP 0.19.13.0 was used for statistical analysis.

Materials

The word list used in this experiment comprised 180 words, generated from the MRC Psycholinguistics Database (Coltheart, 1981). Of these, 120 words were used in the Agency Task, while the remaining 60 words served as novel foils for the surprise recognition memory test. The 120 words for the Agency Task were divided into six lists, which were randomly assigned to the experimental conditions. Ten words from each condition were subsequently included in the recognition memory test, intermixed with the 60 novel foils, resulting in a total of 120 trials in the Memory Task. Words from different conditions in the memory test were matched for concreteness, familiarity, concreteness, imaginability and word length.

Procedure

The experiment consisted of two main phases: the Agency Task, implicitly measured through intentional binding, through temporal reproduction task (IB Task); and the Memory Task, recognition based memory task conducted sequentially in a single session.

Participants were first introduced to the study through on-screen instructions that explained the procedure. They were informed that they would be required to press the UP/DOWN key to make a word appear on the screen and estimate the time it took for that word to appear after a keypress. Each participant was given 20 practice trials, to get familiar with the interval estimation task (IB task) and how to report it on the Time scale. Feedback for the estimated time was provided so that they may develop a sense of milliseconds duration, and better estimate the time in the experiment phase, where no feedback had to be provided. During the practice trials, only the Agency Task was administered, that too with 3-letter nonsense syllables.

Agency Task

The first phase began with participants viewing a fixation cross ("+") displayed at the center of the screen for 700 milliseconds (See Figure 1). This was followed by the presentation of the letter "O" at the center of the screen, as a Ready Screen that would remain there until further input is made. Participants were instructed to press either the "Up" or "Down" arrow key to indicate the position where they would want the word to appear.

After the keypress, there was a temporal delay of either 200 ms, 450 ms, or 700 ms before a word appeared on the screen. The position of the word was either congruent or incongruent with the direction indicated by the keypress. Each word was presented for 500 ms, followed by the Interval Estimation Scale (IB task) that remained there till input was given.

Participants had to estimate the time interval between their keypress and the word's appearance. A horizontal scale ranging from 0 to 1000 milliseconds was displayed on the screen, and participants used the mouse to click on their estimated time. This was repeated for 120 trials, with random combinations of temporal delays and spatial positioning, using words from six predefined lists without any feedback about their time estimation throughout the task.

Memory Task

Following the Agency Task, participants took a surprise recognition test. They were shown 120 words: 60 old (previously seen, 10 from each of the 6 conditions) and 60 new (novel), to classify as "Old" or "New." The words appeared one at a time, and participants responded by pressing the "M" key for "Old" or the "Z" key for "New," without any feedback for correct or incorrect responses.

Results

The descriptive results for time perception revealed a counterintuitive trend: at the shortest delay of 200 ms, participants produced positive time estimates (Congruent: Mean = 106.18 ms; Incongruent: Mean = 93.98 ms), indicating an absence of intentional binding. As delays increased, estimates became increasingly negative, with the strongest binding occurring at 700 ms (Congruent: Mean = -185.69 ms; Incongruent: Mean = -194.33 ms). This trend contradicts the hypothesis that shorter delays would elicit stronger SoA, and instead suggests that minimal delay may affect intentional binding with the word's encoding.

In Experiment 1, recognition memory was analyzed using a 2×3 repeated-measures ANOVA, with Congruency (Congruent, Incongruent) and Time Delay (200 ms, 450 ms, 700 ms) as within-subjects factors (Fig. 3). Descriptive statistics showed that memory scores were relatively stable across conditions and displayed no consistent pattern in relation to either factor. At 200 ms, congruent trials had a mean memory score of 0.442 (SD = 0.232), and incongruent trials 0.468 (SD = 0.254). At 450 ms, scores were 0.468 (SD = 0.258) for congruent and 0.479 (SD = 0.255) for incongruent trials. At 700 ms, means were 0.484 (SD = 0.246) for congruent and 0.479 (SD = 0.244) for incongruent trials. These minimal differences suggest no systematic advantage for congruent outcomes or particular time delays.

The ANOVA confirmed this interpretation. There was no main effect of Congruency, F(1,18) = 0.178, p = .678, η²p = 0.010; no main effect of Time Delay, F(2,36) = 0.268, p = .766, η²p = 0.015; and no interaction, F(2,36) = 0.105, p = .901, η²p = 0.006. Thus, even though intentional binding varied substantially across delays in this experiment, it did not yield any measurable benefit or cost to memory encoding.

The ANOVA confirmed the absence of reliable effects: there was neither main effect of Time Delay, F(2,36) = 0.451, p = .640, η²p = 0.009; nor main effect of Congruency, F(1,18) = 0.080, p = .780, η²p ≈ 0.001; and no Time Delay × Congruency interaction, F(2,36) = 0.879, p = .424, η²p = 0.020. This pattern indicates that despite the modulations observed in SoA through time estimation, memory recognition was unaffected by either spatial congruency or temporal proximity of action-outcome pairs. These findings replicate the results from Experiment 1 and reinforce the conclusion that subjective agency experiences, even when enhanced through reflective attention, do not translate into improved memory performance.

To quantify evidence for the null hypothesis, a Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with Time Delay and Congruency as within-subject factors. Model comparison favored the null model over all alternative models (P(M|data) = .681). Inclusion Bayes factors indicated evidence against the inclusion of Time Delay (BFincl = 0.134), Congruency (BFincl = 0.285), and the Time Delay × Congruency interaction (BFincl = 0.257). According to conventional interpretation guidelines, these values provide moderate evidence against including each effect. Thus, converging with the frequentist analysis, the Bayesian analysis supported the conclusion that recognition memory was not reliably influenced by Time Delay, Congruency, or their interaction in Experiment 2.

General Discussion

Across two experiments, we explored how temporal and spatial features of action-effect mappings influence the implicit sense of agency (SoA) and recognition memory. In Experiment 1, temporal delay and spatial congruency were manipulated, and participants estimated the timing of word appearances as a proxy for SoA (measured through intentional binding, IB), followed by a recognition memory test. The results revealed a significant main effect of delay on IB, with longer delays (450 ms and 700 ms) producing stronger binding, contrary to the established expectation that shorter delays elicit stronger SoA. Spatial congruency did not significantly affect IB in this experiment. Recognition memory remained statistically unaffected across all conditions. In Experiment 2, we introduced a spatial verification task wherein participants were explicitly asked to indicate whether the word appeared at their intended location. This reflective engagement brought spatial congruency into focus. As a result, spatial congruency now showed a significant main effect on IB. Yet, even with enhanced SoA under these conditions, recognition memory again showed no reliable modulation. Importantly, Bayesian analyses conducted for both experiments converged with the frequentist results. Across Experiments 1 and 2, inclusion Bayes factors consistently provided moderate evidence against the inclusion of Time Delay, Congruency, and their interaction in models predicting recognition memory. Thus, the absence of memory effects cannot be interpreted merely as a failure to reject the null hypothesis. Rather, the data provide affirmative evidence that the manipulations which successfully altered agency-related experience did not reliably influence subsequent recognition memory under the present task conditions.

The sense of agency, as measured by intentional binding (IB), arises from the integration of internal and external cues (Moore & Haggard, 2008; Moore & Fletcher, 2012; Moore et al., 2009; Wolpe et al., 2013). In Experiment 1, the retrospective cue of spatial congruity had little influence on IB because participants perhaps focused mainly on estimating the temporal delay between their action and the word presentation, and not the position. As a result, spatial congruency had no significant effect. In Experiment 2, the addition of a spatial verification task made the spatial cue more relevant, resulting in a clear effect on IB. This suggests that while retrospective cues are available post-action, they only shape agency when they are cognitively engaged. This aligns with the view that IB depends not just on action and outcome association but on perceived causality and predictability. As Buehner and Humphreys (2009) argued, intentional action alone is insufficient; binding occurs when the action is seen as causing the outcome. In our study, presenting congruent and incongruent outcomes within a single block likely disrupted this causal perception in Experiment 1. IB only emerged when spatial congruency became salient in Experiment 2, reinforcing the idea that perceived causal linkage is key to the experience of agency.

The emergence of a congruency effect in Experiment 2 may also be interpreted through more recent accounts of agency that emphasize goal-related and postdictive evaluation processes.

Traditional comparator models propose that agency arises when predicted sensory consequences match actual outcomes. However, recent work suggests that agency judgments may also depend on the degree to which observed outcomes align with an intended or goal-relevant state, even when detailed motor predictions are unavailable or unreliable. In the present study, participants in Experiment 1 were required only to estimate the temporal interval between action and outcome, making the spatial location of the word relatively incidental. In Experiment 2, the addition of the spatial verification task transformed location from a peripheral stimulus feature into an explicitly evaluated outcome dimension. Participants were now required to compare the intended location with the observed location on every trial. This interpretation is consistent with recent findings showing that action-effect congruency can enhance agency even under conditions where outcome properties are not fully predictable, suggesting an important contribution of postdictive evaluation processes (Schreiner et al., 2025). Likewise, Hon and Teo (2025) argued that the cognitive salience of agency-related cues plays a critical role in determining whether agency manipulations exert downstream cognitive effects. The present findings extend this idea by demonstrating that increasing the salience and task relevance of spatial congruency strengthened its influence on intentional binding. Importantly, however, this increase in agency did not translate into improved recognition memory, suggesting that making agency cues more salient may be sufficient to alter agency experience without necessarily enhancing memory encoding.

The absence of intentional binding at the shortest 200 ms delay in both experiments stands in contrast to traditional understanding (Haggard, 2002; Moore and Obhi 2012), which suggest that shorter action-effect delays strengthen agency perception. However, both theoretical and empirical work increasingly challenge this assumption as well. Kong et al. (2024) further substantiate this account by demonstrating that action intention is neither necessary nor sufficient for binding to occur. In their study, no binding emerged at 150 ms intervals, even under intentional conditions, reinforcing the idea that extremely short delays may induce a perceptual ceiling, where the action-effect relationship is processed as automatic, leaving no perceptual gap to compress. In a study by Nolden et al. (2012) controlled for the temporal predictability of passive keypresses using the method of constant stimuli and found that binding was present at 600-ms intervals but not at 250-ms intervals. The observed delay-dependent increase in IB, with strongest binding at 700 ms, also aligns with findings from Buehner (2015), who report that longer intervals support more reliable temporal estimation under certain conditions. Notably, their findings mirror ours: binding emerged only at longer intervals (450 ms, 750 ms) and failed to appear at the shortest interval. Humphreys and Buehner (2009), in their contrasting findings compared to Haggard et al. (2002), emphasized that binding is not a universal phenomenon, it heavily depends on the integration of predictive, contextual, and perceptual factors. If the actioneffect pairing lacks causal coherence, binding may decrease or even reverse.

In our case, the use of semantically rich visual word stimuli likely disrupted early causal processing. Visual stimuli, particularly those requiring semantic decoding, demand longer perceptual access than auditory tones typically used in IB paradigms (Ruess et al., 2018). The lack of binding at the 200 ms delay may also be attributed to limitations in visual temporal resolution. As shown by Stetson et al. (2006), rapid visual presentations can distort or delay temporal judgments, while Engbert et al. (2007) demonstrated that visual masking and saccades reduce the fidelity of event perception. In our paradigm, the early appearance of semantically rich words, especially in spatially incongruent positions, may have precluded accurate perceptual registration, thereby weakening the conditions necessary for temporal compression and the experience of agency. Kong et al. (2024) also observed that visual outcome modalities consistently produce weaker binding than auditory ones, and semantic content exacerbates this gap by introducing cognitive load. In our study, this was especially apparent given the wordbased outcome and the added burden of spatial verification.

The use of intentional binding as an implicit measure of SoA may inherently tax cognitive resources that are also critical for memory encoding. As highlighted in the temporal cognition literature, accurate time perception relies on attentional allocation, working memory, and perceptual processing (Matthews & Meck, 2016). Tasks that require participants to continuously monitor and estimate action-effect intervals may compete with, or even overshadow, the encoding of concurrent stimuli. Specifically, attention directed toward temporal estimation can narrow the scope of perceptual processing (Block & Zakay, 1997), leading to less elaborate encoding of incidental events such as the visual words in our paradigm. This resourcesharing constraint suggests that the very process of measuring SoA through IB may attenuate any potential SoA-memory link, not because the link is absent, but because the measurement method intrudes on the encoding stage.

This raises concerns about using IB as an implicit measure of SoA in complex contexts, such as auditory or visual tasks with load or task complexity involving semantic or multi-modal interaction. The time perception task does not allow a confound-free measurement of SoA when examining the cognitive effects of agency. Additionally, the cognitive mechanisms involved in temporal contraction between action and outcome could be engaged by other task demands, given the complex environment humans live in. Therefore, other subtle methods are needed to determine how the brain maintains a sense of agency during complex, demanding tasks.

Interestingly, recognition memory remained unaffected across all delay and congruency conditions. This finding diverges from Hon and Yeo (2021), who reported enhanced memory for outcomes associated with stronger agency experiences, and from Schreiner et al. (2025), who observed superior recognition for action-congruent compared with action-incongruent outcomes.

However, Schreiner et al. also reported a partial dissociation between factors influencing agency and those influencing recognition memory, leading them to question whether agency experience itself directly mediates memory enhancement. In contrast, our findings align more closely with Tsuji and Imaizumi (2022), who replicated agency manipulations but failed to observe corresponding improvements in recognition memory. Taken together, the emerging literature suggests that the relationship between agency and memory is less robust and more contextdependent than initially assumed. This is notable given prior findings suggesting that SoA can enhance memory encoding through self-referential processing and attentional amplification (Voss et al., 2011; Kim & Johnson, 2012; Murthy et al., 2019).

The temporal cognition research highlights a bidirectional interaction between timing and memory encoding that is relevant to our null SoA-memory findings. Dividing attention between a timing task and a concurrent memory task impairs recall (Hicks et al., 1976), and while encoding more events can lengthen perceived duration, this effect depends on attentional capacity (Block & Zakay, 2008). Thus, performing an IB task during word presentation may have diverted resources away from deep encoding, attenuating any SoA-related memory benefit.

Measuring SoA with IB in such complex, semantically loaded paradigms risks conflating agency-specific processes with general perceptual-attentional load, underscoring the need for complementary or alternative implicit measures in future work (Block & Gruber, 2014).

Another factor may be the difference between implicit and explicit measures of agency.

Much of the prior literature linking SoA and memory relies on explicit agency ratings (Hon & Yeo, 2021; Ruiz et al., 2023). In contrast, IB reflects low-level sensorimotor integration, which may not engage the cognitive and motivational systems necessary for memory enhancement, as in case of the self-referential processing thought to enhance memory encoding (Kim & Johnson, 2012). The current findings thus support a partial dissociation between agency experience and memory performance. It is also possible that different memory processes are differentially sensitive to agency manipulations. For example, Tsuji and Imaizumi (2022) distinguished between recollection and familiarity and found no reliable enhancement of either process despite successful agency manipulations. Future work may therefore benefit from examining whether agency preferentially influences specific components of recognition memory rather than overall recognition performance alone.

The present findings may also be viewed in relation to broader questions concerning conscious and automatic influences on memory. Jacoby's work demonstrated that memory performance often reflects the combined contribution of consciously controlled and more automatic processes, and that these influences can emerge even when remembering is not the individual's explicit objective (Jacoby, 1991; Jacoby et al., 1993; Debner & Jacoby, 1994). In the current experiments, participants were primarily engaged in making actions and evaluating their consequences rather than intentionally memorizing stimuli. Consequently, any memory benefit associated with agency would have reflected incidental influences of action-outcome processing on later recognition. Despite reliable changes in agency-related experience, recognition memory remained largely unaffected. This suggests that variations in implicit agency experience may not be sufficient, by themselves, to alter memory performance under incidental encoding conditions.

In summary, our findings clarify several important aspects of SoA and its cognitive consequences. First, intentional binding is sensitive to delay and spatial congruency but only under conditions that support causal inference, predictability, and reflective evaluation. Second, the absence of binding at short delays in visual word paradigms suggests strong modality and processing constraints, consistent with recent critiques of the IB paradigm (Kong et al., 2024; Moore & Obhi, 2012; Buehner, 2015). Third, while SoA can be enhanced through temporal and spatial structuring of outcomes, this does not guarantee improved memory, highlighting either a disconnect between implicit agency experience and memory encoding, or some methodological issues in measurement and task design. These findings support a more nuanced understanding of agency as a multi-component construct, with distinct neural and cognitive correlates depending on how it is measured and the context in which it arises.
