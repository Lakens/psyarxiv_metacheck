Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

This study aimed to evaluate the structure of the Polish version of the Scale of Body Connection (SBC) and whether bodily dissociation mediates the link between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and alexithymia. An online sample (N = 271, M = 23.49 years) completed the 10-item ACE scale, the Perth Alexithymia Questionnaire Short Form (PAQ-S), and the SBC. A total of 209 participants (77.12%) reported having experienced at least one ACE in their childhood, and as many as 57 (21.03%) reported at least four ACEs.

Regarding SBC, results showed Cronbach's alpha coefficients of .84 for Body Awareness (BA) and .77 for bodily dissociation (BD). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed inadequate fit for the original two-factor model (robust CFI = .71). Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) supported a four-factor structure, where following factors have been distinguished: BA1 (Body Awareness), BA2 (Noticing Negative States), BD1 (Difficulties in Processing Emotions), and BD2 (Detachment from the Body). Because BD1 demonstrated insufficient discriminant validity from alexithymia, only BD2 was retained as a mediator. The mediation analysis revealed a significant indirect association between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and alexithymia through bodily detachment (β = .07, 95% CI [.02, .13], p = .008). ACE exposure explained 3.0% of the variance in bodily detachment (R² = .030), while ACE exposure and bodily detachment together explained 16.6% of the variance in alexithymia (R² = .17). These findings provide preliminary support for a four-factor structure of the SBC in a Polish sample and suggest that bodily detachment may play an important role in the association between childhood adversity and difficulties in emotional awareness and processing. Future research should examine the stability and replicability of the proposed factor structure, investigate the indirect association in independent samples, and identify additional factors contributing to bodily detachment and alexithymia.

Introduction

According to the somatic marker hypothesis (Damasio et al., 1996), physical bodily signals, or "gut feelings," are essential for emotional awareness and effective decision-making. Somatic markers (e.g., stomachache, sweaty palms, increased heart rate) that appear in a particular situation remind us of emotions we felt in similar occurrences in the past, guiding our behavior accordingly. This mechanism relies on interoception, which is the nervous system's ability to perceive and process internal physical states, including the ability to accurately detect body signals and integrate them with emotional states (Fani et al., 2024; Khalsa et al., 2018).

Research shows that disruptions to interoception can significantly impair psychological well-being and daily functioning (e.g., Bertsch et al., 2024; Brewer et al., 2016; Price & Thompson, 2007; Schmitz et al., 2021 Schmitz et al., , 2023;; Van der Kolk, 2006).

To operationalize interoceptive engagement in clinical and mind-body research, Price and Thompson (2007) introduced the Scale of Body Connection (SBC), which distinguishes two dimensions of bodily connection: body awareness (BA) and bodily dissociation (BD). Bodily dissociation occurs when a person avoids, disconnects from, or ignores physical sensations, which leads to the feeling of separation from one's body. It also manifests as difficulty with identifying emotions and expressing them. In contrast, body awareness is the ability to pay attention to bodily sensations and to identify them as responses to various stimuli. It means being aware of both inner physiological sensations and overall state at a given moment, such as noticing one's shoulders are tense, realizing one's heart is racing, becoming aware of being tired, and sensing a headache.

Bodily dissociation overlaps conceptually with alexithymia, a construct defined as difficulties with the identification, expression, and description of one's feelings (Sifneos, 1973). Brewer et al. (2016) described alexithymia as characterized by "a general failure of interoception," meaning that individuals with its symptoms struggle to interpret a wide range of bodily cues. The authors suggest that those individuals feel the internal signals but cannot accurately identify their meaning, struggling to decide whether a bodily signal means something purely physical or rather emotional. For example, they could misinterpret a hunger-related stomachache as anxiety (Brewer et al., 2016; Van der Kolk, 2006). In contrast, bodily dissociation is characterized by the sheer avoidance of internal experience (Price & Thompson, 2007). It is more about an experiential "disconnection" rather than just a failure to label a sensation. However, what the two constructs have in common is the struggle with identification and expression of emotion, and that both may be coping mechanisms to deal with difficult or overwhelming experiences (Elzinga et al., 2002). Few studies have explored the link between bodily dissociation and alexithymia, but one recent study (Casale et al., 2023) reported a positive association between the two constructs.

Such interoceptive disruptions frequently stem from traumatic experiences. A history of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)-including abuse, neglect, and hostile home environments (Boullier & Blair, 2018)alters the development of the mind-body connection. Trauma may lead children to develop dissociation as a coping mechanism, which enables them to escape the painful experiences. Research shows that dissociative symptoms are associated with a blunting of autonomic reactivity and a reduction in interoceptive precision (Lemche et al., 2008). Woelk and Garfinkel (2025) claim that detachment from the body and the world, blunted emotional experience, and altered subjective recall, experienced by individuals who suffer from dissociation, may result from changes in interoceptive processing influencing the integration of interoceptive signals into wider cognition.

Consequently, ACEs are consistently associated with higher rates of both BD and alexithymia (e.g., Bertsch et al., 2024; Brewer et al., 2016; Price & Thompson, 2007; Schmitz et al., 2021 Schmitz et al., , 2023;; Van der Kolk, 2006).

Interestingly, body awareness does not reliably differentiate individuals with trauma histories from healthy controls (Bertsch et al., 2024; Price & Thompson, 2007; Schmitz et al., 2021), highlighting the unique clinical significance of bodily dissociation rather than sheer awareness.

While both alexithymia and BD are correlated with overall ACE scores (Salokangas et al., 2024; Schmitz et al., 2023), their relationships with specific ACE types differ. Research on direct comparisons of how bodily dissociation relates to specific instances of trauma, especially emotional abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect, is scarce. Price and Thompson (2007) found that bodily dissociation was strongly connected to physical and sexual trauma, but other childhood traumatic experiences were not included in the analysis. When it comes to alexithymia, evidence generally indicates that it is positively correlated with childhood maltreatment as a whole (Brown et al., 2016; Ditzer et al., 2023; Feyzioğlu et al., 2023; Khan & Jaffee, 2022; Kick et al., 2024; Zlotnick et al., 2001). However, the strongest correlations consistently involve emotional neglect, whether isolated (Brown et al., 2016; Feyzioğlu et al., 2023), paired with physical neglect (Zlotnick et al., 2001), or combined with both physical neglect and emotional abuse (Ditzer et al., 2023; Khan & Jaffee, 2022; Kick et al., 2024).

Critical gaps remain in our understanding of how trauma maps onto psychological mechanisms related to experiencing of the body such as alexithymia and BD. Lloyd et al. (2021) theorized that dissociation may act as a connecting factor between alexithymia and ACEs. Specifically, individuals with trauma histories may learn to dissociate as a coping mechanism to avoid experiencing painful or negative emotions, and therefore they may develop alexithymic traits. Given the consistent findings on the relationship between BD and trauma, the present study aimed to investigate whether disconnecting specifically from the body mediates the relationship between traumatic experiences and alexithymia. This proposal aligns with other research (Schmitz et al., 2021 (Schmitz et al., , 2023)), which has demonstrated that bodily dissociation mediates the link between childhood trauma and emotion dysregulation. Our hypothesis is that BD will at least partially mediate the relationship between ACEs and alexithymia. Because alexithymia is closely associated with emotion regulation difficulties (Krvavac & Jansson, 2021), the proposed mediation model is a feasible extension of this work.

Crucially, testing these pathways requires validated measurement instruments. However, no validated Polish adaptation of an instrument measuring both body awareness and bodily dissociation currently exists.

Therefore, the overarching aim of the present study was to adapt and validate the Polish version of the Scale of Body Connection (SBC), evaluating its internal consistency, factor structure, and construct validity.

Methods

Participants

A total of 271 participants were recruited through the research participation system at the University.

Participants were permitted to participate if they were at least 18 years of age and fluent in Polish. Participation in our study was voluntary, and participants received credits for completing the study.

Procedure

The data were collected from 10 March 2026 to 3 April 2026. The study was administered online using the Gorilla Experiment Builder platform (http://www.gorilla.sc/ Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020). A link to the study was attached to a scientific survey panel. Participants registered through an online research panel, where they provided personal data, including their name, surname, and email address. This information is used solely for study administration purposes, such as enabling communication regarding participation. The researcher has access only to anonymized data identified by user ID, ensuring that participants cannot be personally identified in the dataset.

For participants who are University students, additional information (e.g., student ID) may be collected by the system administrator for the purpose of awarding course credit. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any point without penalty.

After accessing the study through the system, participants reviewed the informed consent form and completed the demographic questionnaire, where they indicated their gender and age. Then, they filled out five self-report measures and an emotional release scale. The order of the questionnaires and tasks was fixed and completion time lasted about 25 minutes.

Measures

Scale of Body Connection

The primary measure evaluated in the present study was the Scale of Body Connection (SBC; Price & Thompson, 2007). It assesses individuals' awareness of bodily sensations and experiences related to their body.

It consists of 20 items measuring body awareness and bodily dissociation. Participants responded using a 5-point Likert scale: 0 "Not at all", 1 "A little bit", 2 "Some of the time", 3 "Most of the time", and 4 "All of the time". It consists of two sub-scales: body awareness (SBC-BA; 12 items) and bodily dissociation (SBC-BD; 8 items). The former measures the ability to experience and identify bodily sensations, as well as respond to them; the latter concentrates on the emotional disconnection from one's own body. For participants who omitted a sexual activity item (n = 3) subscale scores were calculated as the mean across the remaining available items, thereby retaining the full sample (N = 271) across all correlational, regression, and mediation analyses. The translated and authorapproved Polish version of the SBC was used in this study.

emBODY Tool

To assess bodily emotional mapping, participants completed the computer-based emBODY tool, which measures the subjective localization of emotional experience in the body (Nummenmaa et al., 2014). This measure was included to examine the relationship between body awareness as assessed by the SBC and patterns of embodied emotional experience. In this paper, the analysis of emBODY data was not included; the data were collected for use in other research projects and fall outside the scope of the current paper.

Perth Alexithymia Questionnaire -Short Form (PAQ-S)

Alexithymia was assessed using the Polish version of the Perth Alexithymia Questionnaire -Short Form (PAQ; Preece et al., 2023; Larionow and colleagues (2023)). It is a 6-item short form scale that evaluates difficulties in identifying and describing emotions. Items (e.g., "When I'm feeling bad, I can't tell whether I'm sad, angry, or scared") are rated on a seven-point Likert scale (from 1 to 7). A score is defined as the sum of all responses. A higher number means higher levels of alexithymia.

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE)

Originally developed by Felitti et al. (1998), the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (Rzeszutek et al., 2024) was used to assess exposure to potentially stressful or traumatic experiences during childhood. This self-report instrument consists of 10 dichotomous (Yes/No) items covering three broad domains of childhood adversities: abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction. Participants were instructed to indicate whether they had experienced each adverse event before the age of 18. Responses are coded as either 0 (No) or 1 (Yes), and the cumulative ACE score was calculated by summing affirmative responses, resulting in total scores varying from 0 to 10, where higher scores indicate greater exposure to adverse childhood experiences.

Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS)

Respondents' current emotional states were assessed using the Polish version of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988; Fajkowska and Marszał-Wiśniewski (2009)), a 20-item self-report questionnaire to measure both positive and negative affect. Participants were asked to rate the extent to which they experienced each emotion during the specific time frame using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Very slightly or not at all) to 5 (Extremely). Scores for the Positive Affect (PA) and Negative Affect (NA) subscales were calculated separately by summing responses to the corresponding 10 items, yielding scores ranging from 10 to 50 for each subscale. Higher scores indicated greater levels of positive or negative affect, respectively.

SBC Validation Procedures

Translation and Back-Translation

The original English version of the Scale of Body Connection (SBC) was translated into Polish by two authors of this study and a researcher connected with the project, and a common version was then agreed upon together with a third author of this study. The translated version was then reviewed to ensure linguistic accuracy, conceptual equivalence, and cultural appropriateness of the items. Subsequently, the Polish version was backtranslated into English by an independent professional translator who had no prior access to the original version of the scale. The back-translated version was compared with the original SBC by the research team of this study and discrepancies were discussed and resolved to achieve semantic equivalence. The final version was reviewed and approved by the author -Cynthia Price -before being used in the validation study (see Appendix B).

Mediation Analyses

Mediation was subsequently examined using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) implemented in the seminr package in R (Version 2.5.0; Ray et al., 2021). The specification of the mediator was informed by the factor-analytic results. As more than one candidate factor emerged, discriminant validity from alexithymia was evaluated using the heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT); factors that could not be adequately distinguished from alexithymia were not treated as separate mediators. Factors reflecting body awareness rather than bodily dissociation were not included in the mediation model because they did not correspond to the hypothesized mediating mechanism.

Cumulative ACE exposure was modeled as a single observed predictor. Multi-item constructs were estimated as Mode A composites using their individual questionnaire items (original, or those that emerged from factor analyses). Measurement quality was evaluated using indicator loadings, Cronbach's alpha, rhoA, composite reliability (rhoC), average variance extracted (AVE), and HTMT. The structural model included paths from ACE exposure to the mediator, from the mediator to alexithymia, and a direct path from ACE exposure to alexithymia.

Statistical inference for the structural paths and the indirect effect was based on 5,000 bootstrap resamples with 95% confidence intervals.

Internal Consistency Reliability of the Scale of Body Connection

The internal consistency of the SBC and its two subscales was assessed using Cronbach's alpha. The overall SBC showed an acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha of .73 across all 20 items. The BA subscale demonstrated a good internal consistency, with a Cronbach's alpha of .84 across 12 items. Corrected item-total correlations ranged from .33 to .68, indicating satisfactory contribution of all items to the scale.

Examination of the "alpha if item deleted" statistics showed that removal of any individual item did not meaningfully improve the reliability coefficient (α = .81-.84). The BD subscale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach's alpha of .77 across eight items. Corrected item-total correlations ranged from .31 to .58, indicating satisfactory item discrimination. Cronbach's alpha remained largely unchanged after deleting individual items (α = .72-.76), indicating that each item contributed to the internal consistency of this subscale.

Factor Structure of the Scale of Body Connection

A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to evaluate the original two-factor structure of the Scale of Body Connection, consisting of the BA and BD subscales. Since SBC items were measured on an ordinal Likert scale, the model was estimated using the WLSMV (Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance Adjusted) estimator.

Lack of responses on the items concerning sexual activity (SBC8 and SBC19), indicating that these items were not applicable, were recorded as missing values (NA) prior to the CFA. The final model was estimated using data from 268 participants. Missing data were handled using the default pairwise approach implemented in lavaan for WLSMV estimation. The original two-factor model demonstrated poor fit to the data: χ2 (169) = 651.07, p < .001, robust CFI = .71, robust TLI = .67, robust RMSEA = .12 (90% CI: .11 -.13), and SRMR = .11.

Standardized factor loadings for the BA factor ranged from .37 to .86, whereas for the BD factor they ranged from .23 to .79. Most items loaded significantly on their intended latent factors, however, SBC5 (In uncomfortable situations, my body seems paralyzed, almost numb) demonstrated a particularly weak loading (λ = .23), whereas, SBC14 showed the strongest loading (λ = .86). The latent correlation between BA and BD was weak and negatively correlated (r = -.28), indicating that higher body awareness was associated with lower bodily dissociation.

Based on the largest modification indices, two modifications were evaluated. First, item SBC5 was allowed to load on both BD and BA factors. This resulted in a modest improvement in model fit: χ2 (168) = 548.14, p < .001, robust CFI = .73, robust TLI = .69, robust RMSEA = .11 (90% CI: .105 -.12), and SRMR = .01.

A second modification to the model additionally allowed the residuals of SBC7 and SBC10 to correlate. This further improved model fit: χ2 (167) = 476.90, p < .001, robust CFI = .79, robust TLI = .76, robust RMSEA = .10 (90% CI: .09 -.11), and SRMR = .09. The modified model demonstrated improved fit compared with the original one. Although indices improved, the overall model fit remained below commonly accepted criteria for adequate fit (CFI > .90). Therefore, the original two-factor structure was considered insufficient to satisfactorily represent the observed data in the presented sample.

Exploratory Factor Analysis of the Scale of Body Connection

Since the confirmatory factor analyses indicated that the original two-factor structure did not adequately fit the data, an exploratory factor analysis was conducted, on the unmodified model, to investigate the latent structure of the Polish version of the SBC. Prior to conducting the EFA, sampling adequacy was assessed. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure indicated good sampling adequacy (KMO = .83). Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant as well (χ2 (190) = 1726.08, p < .001), which confirms that the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis.

Parallel analysis based on polychoric correlations suggested retaining four factors (Table 1). Therefore, an EFA using minimum residual (minres) extraction with oblimin oblique rotation. Oblique rotation was selected since the underlying factors were expected to be correlated. As a result, the four-factor solution explained 47.9% of the total variance.

The majority of items originally assigned to BA subscale (SBC1, SBC4, SBC8, SBC9, SBC12, SBC13, SBC14, SBC17, SBC18) clustered primarily on a single factor, which was named BA1 (Body Awareness). In contrast, items representing the original BD subscale were distributed across two correlated clusters: items reflecting difficulties in processing emotions (BD1 items: SBC2, SBC11, SBC16, SBC20) and items that reflect detachment from the body (BD2 items: SBC7, SBC10, SBC19). The factors were thus named BD1 (Difficulties in Processing Emotions) and BD2 (Detachment from the Body). The fourth factor comprised four items from original BA scale and two items from BD scale. With the majority focusing on body awareness, it was agreed to name it BA2 (BA2 items: SBC3, SBC5, SBC6, SBC15, SBC16, SBC17). As all these items related to experiencing negative situations and noticing/managing one's negative emotions, the factor was called Noticing Negative States. Two items (SBC16 and SBC17) demonstrated cross-loadings on more than one factor. An additional exploratory analysis consisting of a two-factor solution was conducted in order to facilitate comparison with the original theoretical model of the SBC. The vast majority of items loaded on their theoretically expected factors, with the exception of SBC5, which exhibited a secondary on the BA factor as well. This finding was consistent with the modification indices obtained in the CFA, which further suggests that this item did not function as a pure indicator of bodily dissociation in the Polish adaptation of the scale.

Mediation Analysis

Discriminant validity and specification of the mediator

Following the exploratory factor analysis, the original bodily dissociation construct was divided into two dimensions: BD1 (Difficulties in Processing Emotions) and BD2 (Detachment from the Body), which were initially considered as potential mediators. Given the conceptual similarities between BD1 items (especially SBC2, SBC11, SBC20) and alexithymia, it was crucial to check whether they are different or essentially the same construct, before including BD1 in the mediation model. The discriminant validity of the BD1 factor (difficulties in emotional processing) and alexithymia was examined in a separate PLS-SEM model in seminr. Although BD1 demonstrated acceptable internal consistency and convergent validity (α = .73, ρA = .80, ρC = .83, AVE = .56), its heterotrait-monotrait ratio with alexithymia was very high (HTMT = .99, p = 0.776), indicating inadequate discriminant validity between the two constructs. Thus, BD1 could not be meaningfully distinguished from alexithymia and was not considered as a separate mediator.

In contrast, the conceptual overlap between BD2 and alexithymia was less apparent. Discriminant validity between BD2 and alexithymia was supported by an HTMT value of .50 (95% CI [.34, .64]), with the upper confidence limit falling below the conservative threshold of .85, indicating that BD2 was sufficiently distinct from alexithymia. Accordingly, the subsequent mediation analysis focused on BD2.

Measurement model

The BD2 composite demonstrated satisfactory measurement properties. Indicator loadings were .815 for SBC7, .892 for SBC10, and .707 for SBC19. Internal consistency was acceptable (α = .73, ρA = .77, ρC = .85), and convergent validity was supported by an AVE of .65. The alexithymia composite also demonstrated satisfactory reliability (α = .82, ρA = .83, ρC = .87) and convergent validity (AVE = .52). PAQ-S item loadings ranged from .626 to .769. Discriminant validity between BD2 and alexithymia was supported (HTMT = .50, p < .001). Structural collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). The VIF values for ACE exposure and BD2 as predictors of alexithymia were approximately 1.03, substantially below the conservative threshold of 3, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Discussion

The current study was designed to achieve two aims: (1) to adapt and psychometrically validate the Polish version of the Scale of Body Connection (SBC); and (2) to test a mediation model proposing that bodily dissociation bridges the gap between cumulative childhood trauma and alexithymia. To contextualize our findings on how early childhood trauma impacts adult functioning, we first examined the prevalence of adversity within our sample. We found that 77.12% of participants reported experiencing at least one ACE -a rate closely mirroring the 76.6% reported by Makaruk et al. (2018). Furthermore, 21.03% (n = 57) of the sample experienced at least four types of ACEs, indicating a tendency for adverse experiences to cluster together among more than one in five children.

Validation

The psychometric evaluation of the Polish version of the SBC provided mixed results regarding its structure in a new cultural context. Body Awareness (BA) subscale showed good internal consistency, (α = .84), while the bodily dissociation (BD) subscale reached an acceptable level (α = .77). The coefficients are similar to the original study by Price and Thompson (2007) and the international multi-sample validation by Price et al. (2017). The latter tested SBC data from eight international samples and generally supported the two-dimensional BA/BD structure. BA reliability ranged from about .72 to .86 and BD from .63 to.81. Although our results demonstrate acceptable internal consistency, structural analyses (CFA) indicated that the original two-factor model did not provide an adequate fit for the Polish data, with a robust CFI = .71.

An interesting, albeit tentative, observation concerned item SBC5 ("In uncomfortable situations, my body seems paralyzed, almost numb"), originally conceptualized as an indicator of bodily dissociation. In the Polish sample, the CFA results indicated a cross-loading between bodily dissociation and Body Awareness in the two-factor unmodified model. One possible explanation is that recognizing and reporting bodily numbness or paralysis may involve some degree of attention to internal bodily sensations.

Despite two model modifications, the fit of the two-factor model provided unsatisfactory results (robust CFI = .79), which certainly contrasts with results of other validation studies (Morganti et al., 2020; Price et al., 2017; Quezada-Berumen et al., 2014). However, the model from the Portuguese study did not exceed the acceptable threshold of .90 (Neves et al., 2017), similarly to ours. The two-factor model therefore appears to be broadly applicable across international samples; however, the present findings do not allow us to attribute the discrepancy specifically to characteristics of the Polish sample. An alternative possibility is that the bodily dissociation component of the SBC is less structurally stable across samples and language adaptations than the Body Awareness component.

This discrepancy led to an exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which revealed a more complex, preliminary four-factor solution. The majority of BA items clustered into a single factor (BA1 -Body Awareness)

The remaining ones (items: SBC3, SBC6, SBC15, and SBC17) as well as two items of the BD scale (items SBC5 and SBC16) loaded together creating a second factor BA2 -Noticing Negative States. Although BA2 was moderately positively associated with BA1, as it is described in further paragraphs, its associations with external measures differed substantially. Although both factors involve awareness of bodily experiences, BA2 may reflect a more context-dependent form of bodily awareness where an individual pays a lot of attention to their negative emotions, uncomfortable situations and ways to deal with them.

The BD items split into two distinct but correlated clusters: BD1 -Difficulties in Processing Emotions and BD2 -Detachment from the Body. This diverse nature of BD suggests that in the Polish population, bodily dissociation may not be a monolithic construct but rather a complex experience involving different layers of emotional and physical disconnection. This discrepancy among BD items already showed up in the international multisample study (Price & Thompson, 2017); in this study, BD items with least consistent factor loadings (items SBC5, SBC7, SBC10, SBC19) concerned a sense of separation from the body. In our sample, the CFA showed that the item SBC5 cross-loaded between original BD and BA in the two-factor model; SBC16 contributed to BA2, while also showing cross-loading in exploratory analysis; and items SBC7, SBC10, and SBC19 formed distinct "bodily detachment" BD2 factor. On the other hand, Price and Thompson's most consistent factor loadings (SBC2, SBC11, SBC20) were associated with emotional disconnection. These items, along with SBC16, comprised our BD1 -Difficulties in Processing Emotions factor. Therefore, the Polish two-factor bodily dissociation may be elucidating a heterogeneity already visible at item level in previous international research.

Moreover, the items with highest loadings in BD1 were most reminiscent of alexithymia (see Appendix C for all indicator loadings).

The EFA revealed a somewhat different pattern of cross-loading, involving items SBC16 ("I try to turn my feelings away from physical discomfort"/) and SBC17 ("When I'm tense, I notice where I feel it in my body"/).

The cross-loading identified for both of those items may also reflect conceptual overlap between the underlying dimensions of body awareness and bodily dissociation.

Item SBC16, originally assigned to BD subscale, loaded onto both BD1 and BA2, which may be attributed to the dual nature of the experience described: purposefully directing one's attention away from physical discomfort requires some initial recognition of unpleasant sensations, while the latter tendency to disengage from those sensations may demonstrate challenges in processing bodily experiences on the emotional level.

On the other hand, item SBC17 that was initially assigned to BA, loaded onto both BA1 and BA2. In order to recognize manifestations in the body, such as tension, it involves both general capacity to attend to one's internal bodily sensations and an awareness of bodily responses associated with negative or unpleasant emotional states. Overall, these findings suggest that certain aspects of bodily experiences may not be that easily attributed to a single dimension, since as demonstrated, they can simultaneously involve the recognition of bodily sensations and the ways in which one attends to them.

The correlations with external measures provided preliminary support for the convergent and divergent validity of the four-factor SBC structure. This analysis showed that BA2 is not just a second part of body awareness -it correlates positively with negative affect and ACEs, but essentially not at all with alexithymia, whereas BA1 correlates positively with positive affect and negatively with alexithymia. This suggests BA2 may be better conceptualized as focusing on negative bodily states, especially given its positive correlation with PANAS-NA. BA2 correlating significantly with higher ACE scores suggests that childhood adversity may be associated not only with bodily dissociation, but also with heightened attention to negative bodily states. The two bodily dissociation factors correlated similarly with external factors. BD1 was strongly associated with alexithymia, suggesting substantial overlap between difficulties in processing bodily-emotional experiences and broader difficulties in identifying and understanding emotions. BD2 was also positively related, but to a much smaller extent. Both BD1 and BD2 were associated with more negative affective states and ACEs. Overall, the correlations among the factors suggest that they are related but not redundant, supporting the interpretation of the preliminary four-factor solution as reflecting partially overlapping but distinct dimensions of body awareness and bodily dissociation.

Mediation analysis

Previous studies consistently reported a significant and positive relationship between ACEs and alexithymia (Brown et al., 2016; Ditzer et al., 2023; Feyzioğlu et al., 2023; Khan & Jaffee, 2022; Kick et al., 2024; Zlotnick et al., 2001), and ACEs and bodily dissociation (Bertsch et al., 2024; Price & Thompson, 2007; Schmitz et al., 2021; Schmitz et al., 2023). This study aimed to combine these three variables into a coherent structure via the mediation analysis. We wanted to examine whether childhood trauma can impact emotion processing (alexithymia) through the mechanism of disconnecting from the body (bodily dissociation). Given the emergence of the four-factor structure of the Scale of Body Connection in our sample, the conceptualization of the mediation model had to be slightly changed. Seeing that bodily dissociation emerged in two constructs: BD1 -Difficulties in Processing Emotions and BD2 -Detachment from the Body, inspection of items comprising them was needed.

As items from BD1 were conceptually very close to those of alexithymia, we checked the discriminant validity using the heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT), which eventually indicated significant overlap between them. Thus, the relationship between bodily dissociation and alexithymia should be further analysed in terms of their inadequate discriminant validity in future research. BD2 factor and alexithymia did not show such overlap, which allowed us to proceed with mediation analysis.

The model involved the relationship between ACEs and alexithymia through BD2. Results showed that ACEs were a significant predictor of the mediator (BD2), but they explained only 3% of its variance, suggesting that the level of bodily detachment is also largely associated with other factors not included in the model. The results of the mediation analysis showed that BD2 -Detachment from the Body mediates the relationship between ACEs and alexithymia. The indirect effect of ACEs on alexithymia through BD2 was significant, whereas the direct association between ACEs and alexithymia was not significant after BD2 was included in the model. ACEs and BD2 together explained 16.6% of variance in alexithymia. Therefore, many factors are likely to contribute to the development and maintenance of bodily detachment, but the comparatively small component associated with childhood adversity may nevertheless be meaningfully related to individual differences in alexithymia.

Our results provide an interesting insight to the proposition by Lloyd et al. (2021) about how dissociation may bridge the gap between trauma and alexithymia. We showed that bodily detachment, as a dissociative experience, plays a role in this relationship. More specifically, repeated disengagement from bodily experience may reduce opportunities to use internal bodily signals as information about emotional states. Over time, this could be associated with greater difficulty identifying and describing emotions. However, with ACEs being associated with BD2 rather weakly, it's necessary to find other factors driving the development of body detachment, and in some cases subsequently alexithymia. Some of the symptoms of depersonalization/derealization disorder (DPDR) are similar to our BD2 construct, especially the ones from the depersonalization (DP) part of the disorder. DSM5 describes depersonalization as "Experiences of unreality, detachment, or being an outside observer with respect to one's thoughts, feelings, sensations, body, or actions (e.g., perceptual alterations, distorted sense of time, unreal or absent self, emotional and/or physical numbing)" (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022, p. 330). Our bodily detachment factor may possibly be an indicator of an underlying depersonalization disorder. One longitudinal study (Lee et al., 2012) examined factors associated with the development of depersonalization syndrome in children in the UK. Surprisingly, they did not find any relationship between adverse experiences and DP. The only significant predictors were teacher-rated anxiety at age 13, adult case-level depression and anxiety. Another study (Arora et al., 2020) proposed that sleep efficiency and number of sleep episodes relate to dissociative experiences in female students with depersonalization disorder. Additionally, a study investigating specifically out-of-body experiences (OBEs) and their predictors, highlighted several psychological and experiential factors potentially associated with the occurrence of out-of-body experiences (OBEs), like absorption, fantasy proneness, dissociative tendencies or hypnotizability (Rabourdin et al., 2026). Therefore, it may be the case that DPDR, anxiety, depression, sleep efficiency or any other of the factors mentioned predict our BD2 better than ACEs, which should be examined in further research. Such research could bring us closer to determine which factors are the most salient in the development of BD2 and alexithymia, facilitating the development of successful prevention/treatment techniques.

Our findings extend the body of research suggesting that bodily dissociation may represent one pathway through which childhood adversity is associated with later difficulties in emotional functioning (Bertsch et al., 2024; Schmitz et al., 2021 Schmitz et al., , 2023)). The findings from Bertsch et al. (2024) and Schmitz et al. (2021 Schmitz et al. ( , 2023) ) suggest that bodily disengagement may constitute a relatively transdiagnostic mechanism linking childhood adversity with disturbances in emotional, self-related and personality functioning. The present study extends this literature by showing that a similar indirect association may also apply to alexithymia, specifically through the component of bodily dissociation that was empirically distinguishable from alexithymia itself.

Clinical Implications

The present findings suggest that bodily detachment may represent a clinically relevant feature in individuals with histories of childhood adversity and difficulties identifying or describing emotions. This may support the inclusion of interventions that aim to increase safe engagement with bodily sensations and improve integration of bodily and emotional experience, particularly in individuals showing pronounced bodily disconnection. Rather than focusing exclusively on verbal identification of emotions, treatment may benefit from also addressing the person's relationship with internal bodily states. However, given the cross-sectional and nonclinical nature of the present study, bodily detachment should be considered a potential treatment target rather than an established causal mechanism, and intervention studies are needed to determine whether reducing bodily detachment leads to improvements in alexithymia.

Limitations

The present study has some limitations. First, the four-factor structure of the Polish SBC was derived from exploratory analyses and should therefore be interpreted as preliminary. Consequently, conclusions based on this structure require caution until it is replicated in an independent sample. Future research should evaluate the proposed four-factor solution using confirmatory factor analysis in a larger and more demographically diverse Polish sample, to determine whether SBC evaluates the same constructs across cohorts. Secondly, just as in the case of the Spanish study (Quezada-Berumen et al., 2014), future research should assess test-retest reliability, which was not examined in the present study. Our analysis also lacked control for such variables as trait dissociation or depersonalization/derealization disorder symptoms. This is an important limitation because bodily detachment may partly overlap with more general tendencies toward dissociative experience.

When it comes to our sample, the overwhelming advantage of females over males, as well as younger over older individuals, limits the generalizability of this study to the general population. Because the present study was cross-sectional, the mediation findings should ultimately be interpreted as evidence of an indirect association rather than a demonstrated causal developmental sequence. Although the observed pattern is consistent with the theoretical pathway ACEs → bodily detachment → alexithymia, the data cannot establish temporal ordering or rule out alternative possibilities, including reciprocal associations between alexithymia and bodily detachment.

The findings therefore provide preliminary support for bodily detachment as a potential mechanism connecting childhood adversity with alexithymia, while longitudinal and prospective research is needed to test the proposed developmental pathway directly.

Lastly, using self-report questionnaires means relying solely on participants' perceptions of themselves and their history, which may differ from the actual state of a participant. Retrospective data on adverse childhood experiences may sometimes differ from what actually happened, due to false memories or the repression of difficult memories.
