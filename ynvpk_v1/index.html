How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Experiencing the Queer Collective Through Time: Exploring Perceptions of Cultural and Historical Continuity Among LGBTQ+ Emerging Adults

Psychologists and philosophers agree that a defining feature of identity is the sense that one's self persists across time and space-a phenomenon referred to as self or personal continuity (Erikson, 1963; McAdams et al., 2006; Pasupathi et al., 2007). Although this is widely accepted among identity theorists, a related but lesser studied phenomenon is collective continuity (Cobb et al., 2025; Haraldsson & McLean, 2021; Sani et al., 2007). Whereas personal continuity refers to sense of temporal persistence in one's self, collective continuity refers to having the sense that one's group-its culture and history-persists through time. Both personal and collective continuity are correlated with positive psychological and social functioning (e.g., Maoulida et al., 2024; Sani et al., 2008). Yet collective continuity may be especially important for people who are marginalized by society (Jetten & Wohl, 2012). Connecting to one's culture and history across time can engender a sense of community, foster pride in one's identity, and provide access to collective wisdom for navigating marginality, in turn, buffering the adverse effects of prejudice and discrimination (e.g., Fish & Syed, 2018; Jones & Neblett, 2016; Parmenter et al., 2020).

Distinguishing Personal and Collective Continuity Personal Continuity

In John Locke's (1690) Essay Concerning Human Understanding, he advanced one of the earliest formulations of personal identity in the Western philosophical tradition. He argued that personal identity is based on the continuity of one's consciousness. Despite profound physical changes in one's body and changes in the circumstances of one's life, if a person can remember an experience as their own, then they are the same person who had that experience.

Locke believed this continuous thread of memory that gets woven across time is both necessary and sufficient for personal identity.

This view has been largely adopted by contemporary psychological scientists, who believe that having a sense of personal continuity is a critical part of healthy or mature identity development (e.g., Erikson, 1963; McAdams et al., 2006; Pasupathi et al., 2007). Having a sense of personal continuity is considered both a basic psychological need and universal identity motive (Becker et al., 2018; Sedikides et al., 2023; Vignoles, 2011) and a central function of autobiographical memory (Bluck & Liao, 2013). Supporting this view, personal continuity has been associated with a wide range of health and well-being indicators (e.g., Adler et al., 2015; Chandler et al., 2003; Pasupathi et al., 2007). This perception of personal continuity is supported in (at least) two ways. First, individuals may identify essential parts of their self that they believe remain constant through time-e.g., personality traits like being sociable, interests in certain hobbies, or enduring concerns like caring for the climate. Second, individuals may view their self as changing in some ways, but make meaning of those changes in such a way that their sense of personal continuity is preserved. Thus, and somewhat paradoxically, the notion of self-sameness does not imply the absence of change, but that such change can be understood through the linkages people create between the key scenes in their life stories (McAdams & McLean, 2013; Pasupathi et al., 2007).

According to Sani et al. (2007), "… although our own self is fluid and ever-changing, the different phases it goes through during its existence are meaningfully interconnected: they are part of a coherent, intelligible story." (p. 1120). Therefore, as people narrate their lives, they forge causal connections or explanatory linkages between sometimes very different events or self-attributes, and these connections provide a sense of personal coherence over time -a quality sometimes referred to as diachronic integration (Habermas & Bluck, 2000; Lilgendahl, 2006; McAdams, 2001; Pasupathi et al., 2007).

Collective Continuity

While early identity theorists emphasized the importance of societal influences on the individual (e.g., Erikson, 1963), many subsequent empirical approaches to identity development have largely focused on intrapsychic processes (e.g., Marcia, 1980; Meeus et al., 1999; Waterman, 1982). Recently, some developmental theorists have returned to Erikson's original emphasis on self and society, pushing the field to recognize the centrality of society and culture in identity development (e.g., Fish & Syed, 2018; Hammack, 2008; McAdams & Pals, 2006; McLean & Syed, 2015; Nelson & Fivush, 2004; Rogers, 2018; Rogers et al., 2021). As Erikson (1959) noted, "The term identity expresses such a mutual relation in that it connotes both a persistent sameness within oneself (self-sameness) and a persistent sharing of some kind of essential character with others. " (p. 190). That is, not only do we have a temporally enduring self-narrative that supports our sense of personal continuity, we also share a story with the people who make up our social group. The concept of collective continuity provides one approach to understanding the role of culture and society in identity development. This approach takes seriously both the "psycho" and the "social" of Erikson's early psychosocial theory and situates identity development at the nexus of meaningful self-society transactions (Rogers, 2018).

Compared to personal continuity, less empirical attention has been paid to collective continuity. Although there has been abundant research on identification with social groups at any given moment in time-see the substantial literature on social identity (e.g., Ellemers, 2012; Hogg, 2006; Tajfel & Turner, 1979)-there has been little focus on identification with those collectives across time. Like personal continuity, despite profound change in the character and circumstances of our collective, there is an unbroken thread that connects one's groupgeneration after generation-extending decades or centuries before one's lifetime. Seeing one's self as a fiber in that thread can provide a sense of collective continuity.

This temporal dimension of our group-based identity was first conceptualized by Sani et al. (2007) in relation to national identities in the Italian, Spanish (Valencian), and Scottish contexts. Sani and colleagues theorized that collective continuity has two dimensions. The first dimension, cultural continuity (sometimes referred to as essentialist continuity), applies when groups are perceived as "having deep, essential cultural traits that have a degree of permanence" across time (p. 1120). This includes group values, beliefs, norms, traditions, customs, habits, inclinations, and so on that are transmitted across generations.

The second dimension, historical continuity (sometimes referred to as narrativist continuity), concerns the perception that "different ages, periods, and events in the history of the group are causally linked to one another, that they form a coherent narrative" (p. 1120). That is, one has the sense that the current situation of their collective in some way descends directly from the events of previous generations as part of an endless, interconnected, and unbroken chain that can be traced across time. Sani et al. (2007) argued that cultural and history continuity each have a unique character and have shown them to be empirically distinct; however, they are also correlated and form the superordinate construct referred to generally as collective continuity. Both forms of continuity are also consistent with research in developmental psychology that assumes older generations (usually parents) as key socializers of knowledge about one's group, especially exemplified in the literature on racial and ethnic socialization (e.g., Umaña-Taylor & Hill, 2020). We will argue later, however, that cultural and historical continuity may be more or less distinct depending on the nature of one's social group, and that some collectives differ substantially from ethnic or national identity groups.

Collective Continuity Supports Positive Functioning

Collective continuity has been operationalized with a self-report scale measuring both cultural and historical continuity (Sani et al., 2007). Correlational research examining collective continuity among ethnic and national identity groups has shown positive associations with group identification, inclusion of ingroup in the self, perceived group entitativity (i.e., the perception that one's group is a cohesive, unified whole), and the presence of outgroup threat (Sani et al., 2007 (Sani et al., , 2008;; Smeekes et al., 2017; Smeekes & Verkuyten, 2015). In terms of psychological functioning, collective continuity is positively correlated with collective self-esteem and social well-being (Sani et al., 2007 (Sani et al., , 2008)), as well as individual well-being (Maoulida et al., 2024).

Experimental research shows that mortality salience inductions lead to increases in perceived collective continuity, which, in turn, enhances group identification (Sani et al., 2009). The intention of this policy, now recognized as cultural genocide, was to sever the thread that connected First Nations youth to their past-to disrupt their sense of collective continuity. This has manifested intergenerationally in devastating ways in terms of the health and well-being of Indigenous communities (similar state-sponsored programs and effects are also found in the United States). In studying this, Chandler and colleagues found that, despite incredible attempts at cultural annihilation, Indigenous youth who reported experiencing a sense of cultural persistence across time were at much lower risk for suicide than those who felt less connection (Chandler et al., 2003; Chandler & Proulx, 2008).

Developing a Sense of Collective Continuity

Group membership is necessary but insufficient for perceived collective continuity. We conceptualize collective continuity as a three-step developmental process. First, a person needs to identify as a member of a collective. For some identities, this is assigned or inherited, such as being born into a particular racial group. For others, this is acquired over time, such as obtaining citizenship in a new country. Second, one needs to see their collective as persisting across time. This is where group and individual differences may become especially pronounced. A key requirement for this temporal component is access to historical knowledge about one's group, and the ability to interact with cultural products that imply continuity, such as national anthems and flags, language, or other commemorative vehicles like monuments and statues. Third, a person needs to connect this perception of collective continuity to their sense of self in some meaningful way. Of relevance here is Ashmore et al.'s (2004) distinction between the "narrative of a particular social category of which [I] am a member" and the "narrative of self as a member of a particular social category" (p. 83). According to Ashmore et al., the first is a relatively depersonalized "group story" and the second is a "collective identity story" in which the self is inextricably embedded within one's perception of group continuity. That is, we can have an abstract, depersonalized sense that we are a member of a temporally persisting group without articulating a sense of self-authorship over that group story. We argue that the sincerest version of collective continuity involves some self-authorship over the group story (see Schiff et al., 2011). We articulate these three components to collective continuity here because operationalization has not always clearly captured them, leaving some gaps between theory and measurement, which we attempted to observe by examining associations between different measures of collective continuity, as well as by including some new ways of capturing the phenomenon: measures focused on stories (see Haraldsson & McLean, 2021).

The Importance of Stories

As narrative psychologists, we are interested in the ways that people come to an understanding of self as connected to a collective that persists over time through processes related to memory and storytelling. A range of literatures speak to this process, such as collective memories (Cordonnier et al., 2021; Hirst et al., 2018), vicarious memories (Thomsen et al., 2025), intergenerational narratives (Merrill & Fivush, 2016), and collected stories (Schiff et al., 2001). Important to all of these frameworks, but more explicit in some than others, is the role of storytelling from elder members of the collective. This is particularly present in models that emphasize families as a key node in a person's narrative ecology who serve the function of transmitting family histories (Merrill & Fivush, 2016; McLean, 2016). Narrative ecologies also include storytelling from other community members, which is especially important when identities are not shared within families (Weststrate et al., 2024). Therefore, story-based intergenerational socialization from in-group members is an important way that collective continuity develops.

Stories are also transmitted at more distal levels of the narrative ecology, including stories told in schools, in the media, and in broader cultural discourses (e.g., Breen et al., 2017).

Therefore, group narratives that provide the basis for collective continuity exist, potentially, at multiple levels of our narrative ecologies, which we can internalize or attach to our sense of self.

Although our assessments (particularly in Study 2) allowed us to observe stories and information transmission at various levels of the ecology, we focus on intergenerational transmission as a particularly potent means of transmitting information as it comes from an ingroup source with a potential relational component, as opposed to disembodied information (e.g., from a book, website, or person with whom one does not have interpersonal contact; see Weststrate et al., 2024), or even from peers who may not have the needed historical perspective (see Haraldsson & McLean, 2021).

Queer(ing) Collective Continuity

In our read of the literature, collective continuity has been undertheorized outside of the context of national and ethnic identity. There are several reasons to suspect that when applied to other groups, key aspects of our understanding of collective continuity may be challenged. Chandler and Proulx (2008) argued that, under ideal circumstances, one's community can, by default, be counted on to ensure younger generations construct a sense of cultural and history continuity. This is likely true for national and ethnic identities when families share these identities (cf., research on transnational adoption; Lee, 2003). However, not all communities live in such ideal circumstances, especially within the context of communities who are marginalized by society. Such marginality can involve the explicit disruption of collective continuity, as we have seen with Indigenous peoples (e.g., Brave Heart, 1998; Fish & Syed, 2018; Freyberg et al., 2008). In extreme cases, cultural ways of being and the transmission of accurate histories have been criminalized or legislated out of existence, therefore making it difficult or even impossible to construct a sense of collective continuity. This logic also applies to LGBTQ+ communities where there are several circumstances that may challenge the intergenerational transmission of culture and history. One of the primary drivers of collective continuity is socialization from older generations within one's ingroup. In most cases, this happens within the family ecology, because families of origin typically share the same identities across generations-identities that are, simply, inherited. Solomon (2012) referred to such identities as vertical-i.e., a sharing up and down of identities across generations, such as ethnic, racial, linguistic, national, and religious identities. This is contrasted with horizontal identities where generations of people within the same family do not share the same identities.

In cases where a person is not born into their ingroup, some type of explicit socialization will need to occur for the transmission of culture and history (Hamilton et al., 2008), and we have argued that intergenerational storytelling between LGBTQ+ elders and youth is a key activity for such transmission (Weststrate, McLean, et al., 2024). However, LGBTQ+ intergenerational contact is rare (Bohan & Russell, 2005; Weststrate, Turner, et al., 2024), as families of origin are likely to lack intergenerational LGBTQ+ identities, and there is lack of space for intergenerational contact (e.g., bars inaccessible to youth, and queer elders unwelcome in schools). Although there are other sources of knowledge, such as school curriculum, museums and commemorative efforts, and media, there are also obstacles to accessing these as well with book bans and "Don't Say Gay" policies in schools (ACLU, 2023; Pen America, 2024-25), the removal of monuments to gay history (e.g., changing the name of US Navy ship named after Harvey Milk; PBS News, June 2025), and media portrayals focused towards the straight consumer (Yaksich, 2005). Although the Internet and social media are promising spaces for education and community connection (Craig & McInroy, 2014; Lucero, 2017), they can also be toxic, particularly for people with multiple oppressed identities, especially along the intersecting axis of race and ethnicity (Hammack et al., 2021), and are often 'not enough' for the deep work needed to develop this sense of collective continuity (see Greyson et al., 2026).

The Current Studies

We argue that a deeper investigation into collective continuity is needed to expand and enrich our conceptualizations by applying the concept to LGBTQ+ communities who have not been included in the literature on the topic, and for whom it might be especially important. We present findings from two exploratory and descriptive studies to probe the concept of "queer collective continuity" with samples of LGBTQ+ emerging adults. We adopted an explanatory sequential mixed method design (e.g., Syed & Westberg, 2025). In the first study, we took a quantitative approach to examining associations among three different measures of queer collective continuity, LGBTQ+ psychosocial functioning and general well-being to assess whether known relationships replicate in this sample, as well as to examine the overlap between operationalizations of our core construct. In the second study, we took a qualitative approach involving in-depth interviews to construct an understanding of how LGBTQ+ emerging adults conceptualize cultural and historical continuity in their own lives, addressing LGBTQ+ emerging adults' perceptions of the "queer collective," moments when they feel and do not feel a sense of queer collective continuity, and their ideas around facilitators of and barriers to continuity.

Our choice to use emerging adults in our sample was informed by two major factors.

First, continuity is a central component of identity development, and identity development is the core developmental task of emerging adulthood (Arnett, 2000; Erikson, 1963). Second, critical identity milestones for both sexual orientation (Bishop et al., 2020) and gender (Pucket et al., 2022) often do not emerge until adolescence or emerging adulthood, respectively. Thus, we wanted to sample LGBTQ+ participants who were more likely to have experienced identityrelevant events and relationships, and who had time to have processed and made some meaning of those experiences, increasing the likelihood of capturing the desired phenomenon.

Study 1

In the first study, we examined associations across measures of collective continuity, and in relation to LGBTQ+ psychosocial functioning and general well-being. No research has examined collective continuity in an LGBTQ+ sample. We operationalized collective continuity in three different ways. First, we adapted the Perceived Collective Continuity Scale (Sani et al., 2008) for use with LGBTQ+ people. This measure assesses the extent to which people see their ingroup as possessing historical and cultural continuity; however, the measure does not assess the extent to which people see themselves as personally connected to those cultures and histories.

Thus, we administered two more measures that we believe tap people's understanding of collective continuity within the context of their own sense of self. We adapted the Inclusion of Other in Self Scale (Aron et al., 1992) to assess whether LGBTQ+ emerging adults included older generations of LGBTQ+ people within their self-schemas. We reasoned that, by including "older generations" of LGBTQ+ people within one's current sense of self, an emerging adult forges a connection to the collective across time. Finally, we asked participants to recount an intergenerational narrative shared with them by an LGBTQ+ person from an older generation.

We then coded these narratives for the presence of self-event and group-event connections (Syed & Nelson, 2021). Such connections, which we describe in more detail below, indicate that the narrator has made a meaningful linkage between the experience of an LGBTQ+ elder and their current sense of self, again indicating some level of collective continuity.

We then examined correlations between these measures, and their correlations with indicators of LGBTQ+ psychosocial functioning and general well-being to answer the following two research questions: 1.

How are different conceptualizations of queer collective continuity related to each other? 2. How are the various conceptualizations of queer collective continuity related to LGBTQ+ psychosocial functioning general and well-being?

Both studies were approved by the IRB (MASKED for review).

Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS).

To measure participants' sense of LGBTQ+ intergenerational self, we adapted the 1-item Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale (Aron et al., 1992). Participants were presented with an array of seven Venn diagrams with two circles labeled "self" and "other," with increasing levels of overlap. In the picture, participants were told that "self" refers to themselves and "other" refers to past generations of LGBTQ+ people. The participants were asked to select the picture that best represents how they feel their identity overlaps with past generations of LGBTQ+ people. This measure specifically targets one way that participants might integrate perceived historical continuity into the self-concept.

Self-and Group-Event Connections.

Participants who reported having an LGBTQ+ elder in their life were asked to think of a time the elder shared something that helped the participant to better understand the LGBTQ+ community and how they fit into it-what we refer to as an intergenerational narrative. The participants were told that the story shared with them could be about a personal experience of the elder, a cultural or historical event important to the

LGBTQ+ community, a story about the values, traditions, or behaviors of the LGBTQ+ community, or anything else that helped the participant to understand the LGBTQ+ community and their place in it. Participants were asked to focus on a specific moment in time when they had a such conversation with an elder and were asked to describe this encounter in detail, being specific about who the elder was, when this happened, what was shared, how they responded, and what it meant to them both then and now. 4The intergenerational narratives were coded for the presence of two types of intergenerational connections (Syed & Nelson, 2021). Self-event connections are defined as, "the relationship between a given experience and one's sense of self constructed within a particular narrative" (Pasupathi et al., 2007). These were coded when the narrator made an explicit connection between the intergenerational narrative and their self-understanding as an LGBTQ+ person. Group-event connections are defined as, "the relationship between a given experience and how one constructs understanding of a group within a particular narrative" (Syed & Nelson, 2021). These were coded when the narrator made an explicit connection between the intergenerational narrative and their understanding of the LGBTQ+ community as a whole. Thus, both self-event and group-event connections provide evidence of how an autobiographical memory (i.e., an experience with an LGBTQ+ elder) directly contributes to a person's sense of personal or collective identity, a core necessity of our definition of collective continuity.

Reliability for narrative coding was established using Syed and Nelson's (2015) guidelines. The first author served as the master coder and a research assistant served as the reliability coder.

Based on one third of the narratives (n = 40), inter-rater reliability was acceptable for both selfevent (Cohen's κ = .76) and group-event connections (Cohen's κ =.74).

Measures of LGBTQ+ Psychosocial Functioning

Positive LGBTQ+ Identity. We administered the identity affirmation (3 items; e.g., "I am glad to be an LGBTQ+ person") and identity centrality (3 items; e.g., "My LGBTQ+ identity is a central part of my overall identity") subscales of the Lesbian, Gay, and Bisexual Identity Scale (α = .88; Mohr & Kendra, 2011). Participants rated each item on a 6-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The subscales were combined to assess overall positive LGBTQ+ identity.

Psychological Sense of LGBTQ+ Community.

To assess the participants' feelings about belonging in the LGBTQ+ community, we used the membership subscale of the Psychological Sense of LGBTQ+ Community Scale (α = .94; Lin & Israel, 2012). Participants rated 3 items from 1 (never) to 5 (always) for how often they feel part of the LGBTQ+ community.

Measures of General Well-Being

Life Satisfaction. We assessed well-being using the 12-item Satisfaction with Life Scale (α = .90; Diener et al., 1985). Participants indicated their agreement with items like "In most ways my life is close to my ideal" on a Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Depression and Anxiety.

We assessed negative mental health with the Depression and Anxiety Stress Scale (α = .93; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995). This 21-item scale asked participants to rate the extent to which they experienced symptoms of depression (e.g., "I couldn't seem to experience any positive feeling at all") and anxiety (e.g., "I felt I was close to panic.") over the past week on a scale from 1 (did not apply to me at all) to 4 (applied to me very much or most of the time).

Results and Discussion

Background Analyses

Of the 309 participants in this study, only 131 (42% of the overall sample) reported knowingly having an LGBTQ+ elder in their life. Of the participants who reported having contact with an LGBTQ+ elder, only 103 (33% of overall sample) provided intergenerational narratives. Within those narratives, surprisingly, only 42 of the participants (40.8%) made any type of intergenerational connection. Of those, 27 (22%) made self-event connections, 27 (22%) made group-event connections, and 12 (11.7%) made both self-and group-event connections in their intergenerational narratives. Given these low frequencies, in a deviation from our preregistration, we created a combined variable for analyses of the presence or absence of any type of intergenerational connection.

Associations Among Three Measures of Queer Collective Continuity

We computed correlations between (1) our LGBTQ+ adaptation of the Perceived Collective Continuity Scale (PCC), for both the overall scale and the cultural and historical continuity subscales; (2) the single-item Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale (IOS); and (3) the presence or absence of self-or group-event connections coded in intergenerational narratives.

Correlational results are presented in Table 2.

Consistent with prior research involving national and ethnic groups, the cultural and historical continuity subscales of the adapted PCC scale were positively correlated. Surprisingly, there were almost no associations between the three different measures of queer collective continuity. There was a small positive correlation between the IOS scale and the historical continuity subscale of the adapted PCC scale, meaning that emerging adults who perceived their sense of self to include older generations of LGBTQ+ people also tended to see LGBTQ+ history as an unbroken chain of interconnected events. We note that the PCC scale does not assess the extent to which people see themselves as personally connected to those cultures and histories.

The perceptions assessed are rather abstract, such as the items, "LGBTQ+ history is a sequence of interconnected events" (historical continuity) and "Shared values, beliefs, and attitudes or

LGBTQ+ people have endurance across time" (cultural continuity). Thus, a person can respond to these items affirmatively without necessarily feeling that their own "collective self" is connected to LGBTQ+ cultures and histories across time.

Overall, these results suggest that, despite conceptual overlap, our different measures of queer collective continuity were tapping empirically distinct constructs. There are several explanations for this. First, the measures themselves use different measurement models-selfreport scale, narrative coding, and single-item array of overlapping circles-so the correlations are not benefitting from shared method variance. Second, it could be that some of the measures are poor indicators of collective continuity in general or queer collective continuity specifically.

Associations Among Queer Collective Continuity and Positive Functioning

Next, we computed correlations between our three indicators of queer collective continuity and LGBTQ+ psychosocial functioning (i.e., positive LGBTQ+ identity, psychological sense of LGBTQ+ community) and general well-being (i.e., life satisfaction, depression and anxiety). We found that the overall PCC, the historical and cultural continuity subscales, and IOS scale were positively associated with both indictors of LGBTQ+ psychosocial functioning but were unassociated with general well-being. Our third measure of queer collective continuity, self-or group-event connections in intergenerational narratives, was unassociated with all the positive functioning measures. These findings partially confirm past research involving national and ethnic groups that has shown that collective continuity is associated with collective self-esteem and social well-being (e.g., Sani et al., 2008), which conceptually parallel our measures of positive LGBTQ+ identity and psychological sense of

LGBTQ+ community. However, our results are distinct from recent research showing associations between collective continuity and general well-being (Maoulida et al., 2024), suggesting that LGBTQ+ communities differ from others in this respect.

Given the concerns with measurement, we interpret the results on positive functioning cautiously. Indeed, our main take-away from Study 1 is that the lack of associations indicates the need for more descriptive research that can deepen our conceptual understanding of collective continuity in LGBTQ+ communities, which we addressed in Study 2.

Study 2

Our findings from Study 1 invited new questions about perceptions of LGBTQ+ culture and history among emerging adults, and how they experience such culture and history across time. Earlier we discussed several ways in which queer collective continuity may differ from other groups. Therefore, we wondered how emerging adults conceptualize LGBTQ+ communities across time, which we hoped would provide a prism through which to expand and refine theorizing about collective continuity and may help to contextualize findings from Study 1. Recognizing this need for deeper understanding, we conducted a qualitative follow-up study with eight participants from the sample in Study 1. To maximize the chance that participants might experience, or be able to describe, collective continuity, we chose participants who reported that they had positive contact, at some point, with LGBTQ+ elders. We did not select participants for specific demographic characteristics or identities for two reasons. First, this study was exploratory in nature and aimed at beginning to describe a construct within the LGBTQ+ population generally. Second, our selection pool for interview participants was limited in several ways: by sample (Prolific does not allow follow up contact with participants recruited through their platform), by experiences (only 42% of our participants had relationships with elders), and by participant interest (providing contact information for follow up was optional). Thus, we note that our main selection criteria was for self-reported positive relationships with elders. This was an intentional decision to take a strengths-based approach to this research, especially in light of the frequent and increasing vilification of intergenerational relationships between LGBTQ+ people. Our guiding research questions were: 1.

How do queer youth define the "queer collective?" 2. How do queer youth experience, or not experience, queer collective continuity?

3. What facilitates and forestalls a sense of queer collective continuity?

Method Participants

In Study 1, college participants were able to provide contact information if they were interested in being contacted about future research participation. Thus, in the Winter of 2022, we invited 10 participants from the college sample who had reported relationships with LGBTQ+ elders in Study 1 to participate in an interview. Of those invited, 8 participants responded and consented to participate. Participants identified as cisgender, transgender, non-binary, and agender, as well as lesbian, queer, bisexual, asexual, pansexual, and demisexual. They also identified as White, Black, Latino/a/x, and Native American. We do not report specific numbers and combinations of demographics to protect participant confidentiality. Participants were compensated with a $25 Amazon gift card for completing the interview.

Interview and Data Collection

Participants viewed and signed the consent form prior to the interview. Qualifying demographic information (i.e., age and LGBTQ+ identity) were confirmed verbally at the start of the interview, as well as confirmation of the participant having an intergenerational relationship with an LGBTQ+ elder. Interviews were recorded and transcripts were generated via Zoom, and then later reviewed and edited for accuracy by research assistants.

Each interview lasted for about one hour. The overall interview structure was inspired by the Life Story Interview (McAdams, 2008) and began by asking participants to imagine their life as a story or novel and to describe the major chapters of their life (as with Study 1, all materials, including the interview protocol, are included on OSF). This approach was used both to warm participants up to the narrative activity and to identify whether/how participants considered their sexual and/or gender identity to be a core part of their life story and understanding of their self.

Questions then departed from the life story interview as participants were asked questions concerning their perceptions of, and experiences with, LGBTQ+ communities. Building from Sani's (2007) original formulations of collective continuity that emphasized history and culture, for the present analyses, we focus on responses to our questions about the participants' general perceptions of LGBTQ+ communities, how LGTBQ+ culture is seen (including values and traditions), as well as questions about LGBTQ+ history, and how participants related to older generations of LGBTQ+ people.

Data Analysis

After interviews were conducted and transcribed, we used reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021) to analyze the data. This approach was desirable because of its flexibility and focus on reflexivity. Because all researchers in our team have close connections to

LGBTQ+ communities, reflexive thematic analysis provided a framework in which our subjectivities could be helpful when making sense of the data, rather than trying to suppress them during the analytic process. To this end, the authors began with an intentional reflexivity exercise, where we discussed our positionalities and how our identities and experiences may influence our analysis. This was done across multiple identity domains to include how our identities might influence interactions between authors during analysis (e.g., power differentials between junior and senior researchers on the team), and potential interactions with and interpretations of interview data (e.g., shared or conflicting life experiences for researchers who shared identities with participants). Especially relevant to this study were our gender and sexual identities. The first author identifies as an agender lesbian, the second author as a gay/queer cisgender man, and the third author as a straight cisgender woman. This unique combination of insider and outsider perspectives was an asset to our interpretive process.

We then moved into the six-phase reflexive thematic analysis process, as outlined by Braun and Clarke (2021). This started with a data familiarization phase, where each author reviewed the transcripts while taking notes on potential themes. Following this, we extensively coded the data. As a group, we discussed our coding of each interview in detail. Two cases were discussed per meeting. After discussing four of the eight cases, we realized the value of this data was not in extracting abstract themes, but in richly and fully describing what was shared with us by participants about individual interview topics. That is, our team found that most of our data was summarizing responses around specific topics (e.g., facilitators and barriers to collective continuity) rather than reflecting broader patterns of shared meaning (see also Braun & Blarke, 2021, p. 341-342). As such, we ended the thematic analysis process at the coding stage and decided to take an inductive, descriptive approach instead (Elo & Kyngäs, 2008).

Results and Discussion

How Do Queer Youth Define the Queer Collective?

In considering how emerging adults define the queer collective, we explicitly asked them questions about their perceptions of LGBTQ+ culture, values, traditions, and history. One of the first things that emerged from participant responses was that the queer collective was difficult for them to define, as almost all expressed some degree of struggle in providing answers. There were various components to this struggle. First, for some participants, this came out as a fear of "getting it wrong." Second, for some, there was a sense of insecurity about their place in the community, about whether they actually belonged, and their answers were tentative as though they were not sure if they were allowed to provide their opinion; this concern with whether someone may be "not LGBTQ+ enough" to have a voice in the community is a documented concern amongst LGBTQ+ emerging adults (Parmenter et al., 2021). In our study, this issue seemed most potent for this who were still exploring their identities or identified in more fluid ways. For example, one participant said, I do have a bit of an imposter syndrome when it comes to my queer identity. Um, I think part of that's due to like the newness like, I'm still, I've barely been out for a year, coming up on a year, and so like it's still getting used to that identity and calling myself bi and calling myself part of the queer community is still taking some getting used to, so there is like kind of a…separation there for me.

Third, many participants described the LGBTQ+ collective as something that changes, grows, and evolves over time, creating a community that is inherently hard to define. One participant compared the amorphous nature of LGBTQ+ communities to a cloud:

I think [the LGBTQ+ community], it's ever-changing. That's kind of my concept of it, is it's not one thing or another. I think it's, and I think in my head it's kinda like a cloud.

Like it has all these different moving parts, and it's not solid, but it's not not solid, so I don't know, it's…it's positive. It's, I think, forward thinking. I think it's moving in the right direction. I don't think it'll ever be like this or that. I think, I think the queer community is too diverse to be blocked in into like a concept.

Almost all participants also expressed uncertainty because, while they felt there were commonalities in experience for all queer people, they also acknowledged that the LGBTQ+ collective is made up of many diverse groups of people who all have their own smaller and more specific histories, traditions, and values. This not only included the subgroups within the community (i.e., those with different sexual and gender identities), but also the varying experiences of LGBTQ+ people of color and queer people with disabilities. As one participant described it: I mean the queer community, um, certain identities under the LGBTQ plus label have, I mean, particular histories of their own and their own, um, I don't know, cultures and events that are significant to their label.

These results beg the question of whether there is even an "essential LGBTQ+ culture" to transmit? While all of these evolutions can be explained through causal linkages in time, making historical continuity somewhat easier to access, the heterogeneity of queer and trans communities present an open question as to whether there is an essential culture or many cultures (see also Parmenter et al., 2020), which has implications for cultural continuity. Perhaps this evolution and rapid change also makes it difficult to place oneself clearly in the collective, with which we saw our participants struggle.

We also note that this question of heterogeneity may be important for other groups. For example, Asian immigrants is a very broad group of people who may have quite different reasons for immigration that create different obstacles to and affordances for collective continuity (e.g., Haraldsson & McLean, 2021). People with disabilities could include a great range of experiences, including those that are present from birth or later acquired (e.g., Adler et al., 2022). Thus, as we move beyond thinking about the collective in terms of race or nationality (also groups with great heterogeneity), these results caution us to question what the 'collective' is for various groups and how that fosters or impedes connection. The qualitative and descriptive approach that we took here is ideal for surfacing some of these issues.

Defining LGBTQ+ Culture

Despite the amorphous and diverse nature of the LGBTQ+ collective, there was some common ground in how participants identified parts of LGBTQ+ culture. Participants noted shared experiences amongst all LGBTQ+ people, regardless of their specific identities. Some of these shared experiences included living outside of heteronormative expectations and explicitly resisting those norms, such as marriage and parenthood. They also described shared experiences of pain and oppression, identity exploration, and moments of queer joy and celebration. One participant summarized their thoughts about the shared queer experience: …coming out to their families, finding their shared family -or their found family rather. Experiences of oppression, experiences the joy of like figuring out who you are and being comfortable in your identity, like there are…shared experiences.

While most participants acknowledged the existence of subgroups and subcultures, they were typically still able to identify some shared cultural experiences that transcended the different labels and identities under the LGBTQ+ umbrella.

Defining LBGTQ+ Values

Participants reported several common values that they observed within the LGBTQ+ collective. First, inclusivity, openness, acceptance, and respect for others were commonly referenced throughout the interviews (see also Parmenter et al., 2020). Love showed up often as a queer value; experiencing and expressing deep love was referred to by one participant as a "keystone" of queer communities. Some participants also mentioned values around activism, camp aesthetics, self-expression, and subversiveness. Activism was described as a value revolving around queer people banding together to protest, fight for LGBTQ+ rights, create safe queer spaces, and generally enact social and political change. Other literature has similarly documented the importance of activism as a core value of LGBTQ+ communities, and how activism for queer rights is often inherently linked to also disrupting other Eurocentric, colonial, racist, and patriarchal systems (Abreu et al., 2023; Sullivan, 2025). For example: …political movements, um and certain historical figures, uh, advocating for queer rights and the, I don't know, the progression of their rights, uh. Like those specific events in history, I feel like those play a large role in like the culture of advocacy, um. Definitely unites a lot of the community together, um, and helps create that like recognition that it's bigger than just one label or one individual. We're all working towards, like, creating a safer space for everybody else. Camp aesthetics have historically been closely linked with LGBTQ+ communities, especially gay men. As a value within the community, camp aesthetics appear in the form of drag, queer films/media, theatrical performances, and even the common "gay" accent, all of which serve as ways for queer people to subvert mainstream ideologies about beauty and being by intentionally exaggerating the characteristics that society chastises them for (e.g., being effeminate). Further, camp as a feature of queer culture facilitates the disruption of many cisheterosexual norms and provides opportunity to turn what mainstream society might consider "failure" into critical sites of creativity, subversion, and hope (Simple, 2022).

…it's more like cultural values, um, which a lot of that's historical. So, like drag culture and kind of camp aesthetics and, like, kind of the value of the subversive.

The value of self-expression reflected something we refer to as queer embodiment and was referenced in different ways among almost all participants. Specifically, participants described the experience of finding ways to look and act queer to "signal" one's LGBTQ+ identity to others. We found that queer embodiment was sometimes described as being used to signal to everyone, queer or not, a sense of LGBTQ+ pride, while other times it was described to signal only to other queer folks a shared identity, like a secret handshake. These types of strategies for authentic, visible expressions of LGBTQ+ pride for both sexual and gender identities have been previously described as a source of queer joy (Flynn et al., 2026), a sentiment reflected by the participants in our study. One participant described how she intentionally changed aspects of her appearance after coming out in order to let others know that she was part of the LGBTQ+ community: …But before [coming out], I definitely dressed to try and impress boys, and now I feel like I dress to try and impress myself and other queer people and girls, which is a lot more fun [laughs]. Um and that, like, really helped me feel more included in the community of -I feel like now, like, I never get mistaken for straight. Or, if I do, it's from a very old person. And like I get it, like people generally, just assume I'm queer at this point, which is so, so affirming. And like, I -your appearance isn't what dictates your sexuality or your labels or part of the community all, but for me that's, just like the external um…Yeah, I guess, validation that like I come across in every way as queer now.

Finally, participants also cited subversiveness as a value of the LGBTQ+ collective. We found this particularly interesting for several reasons. First, valuing subversiveness appeared central to the shared LGBTQ+ experience of existing outside of society's expectations. Second, this value appeared deeply embedded in the shared histories and traditions of the queer collective.

LGBTQ+ folks have a long history of combining resistance and celebration, as can be seen in events such as annual Pride parades. Indeed, many of the other cultural values referenced above are at least in part tied to subversiveness, as well as to the broader culture within the queer community of resisting societal norms.

Defining LBGTQ+ Traditions

Participants struggled to identify traditions that were important for the entire LGBTQ+ collective. Similar to the struggles with defining the collective, this was often described as difficult because subgroups and subcultures are more likely to have their own traditions, rather than those that are shared by the entire group. However, almost every participant identified Pride parades and events as LGBTQ+ traditions, and many also cited drag shows and ballroom culture as important traditions. For example, one participant noted: I know Pride parades are a very big part of the culture, like it's very exciting when pride month comes around and everybody's like, I don't know, connecting with their friends trying to go to the local Pride parades together, like travel together, um, and it's just a very like joyful time. I know I celebrate, like when I was back at home, celebrating with my friends was a very exciting time. Um, and there's also things like drag shows or like ballroom, various events like that. Yeah, they're very tradition, pretty much a part of like tradition in the queer culture, queer community, yeah.

There were also some smaller traditions mentioned, such as a single ear piercing in the right ear to signal sexual preference to other LGBTQ+ people. Overall, endorsement of Pride parades and drag shows as the main shared traditions of LGBTQ+ communities is likely a reflection of the overall values of many LGBTQ+ people; both settings are well-known for embodying many of the queer values participants also cited above, such as subversiveness, self-expression, activism, and camp (e.g., Baker & Kelly, 2016; Egner & Maloney, 2016; McFarland, 2012).

Defining LGBTQ+ History

The responses about shared queer history were complex. Participants identified several historical events that they felt were defining moments for the LGBTQ+ collective, such as Stonewall, the AIDS epidemic, the Lavender Scare, Boston Wives, the passing of marriage equality, and the Pulse nightclub shooting (see also Weststrate & McLean, 2022). However, participants also expressed a lack of access to queer history. Specifically, they described frustration with the lack of stories that are collected and retold, both because of stigma and fear of talking about queer historical events and the lack of access to that information. For example: I'm super unfamiliar with, like, the history of it all. I know some…um. Like a lot of my own experience with my own personal, family relations has led me more away than to the queer community, even though I find myself, consider myself a part of it, and so it's like I, honestly, I can't say much about [it].

I don't know anything about the history. I want to, and that's one of my goals and to educate myself on it um but there's a history there…

[a participant discussing the HIV/AIDS crisis] I was very curious at the time, like you know…cause I had elders, I had people to talk to, but that was all of maybe two or three people, you know, that were around and everyone else is young, younger than that. And I felt that there was so much, I have no idea what the scene was like in this area at all that time, or prior. And these would be the people that would know, you know, but they're not around to tell. And, or even beyond that talking again about shared history, you know each of those people have a history that is no longer present. A history that could be learned from.

Some participants also described the dominant versions of queer history to be heavily white-washed and unequally discussed, such as the erasure of the historical work of transgender people and people of color.

Because most of the queer history I was exposed to was based in the US, and the US is predominantly white, there is a tendency to think of like queer history as like particularly white for me. Although I'm aware that there are, you know, numerous queer figures like um, queer figures of color…I'm blanking on her name right now, um... Marsha Johnson?

Additionally, many participants acknowledged that their knowledge of queer history was often restricted to modern U.S. queer history -they expressed a need to expand popular queer histories to include queer folks from around the world, as well as older queer histories (e.g., ancient Greece and Rome) in order to truly cultivate a sense of the LGBTQ+ collective as having been around since the beginning of time.

Overall, most of our participants had some conceptualization of a shared queer history and what that entailed. However, many also expressed that the ways in which that history is shared and discussed (or not), often skewed queer history to seem most applicable to modernday, white, and cisgender members of the community. This is consistent with research on BIPOC experiences within LGBTQ+ communities, which highlights both the importance of access to the histories of BIPOC queer people in terms of facilitating belongingness and well-being, but also the many barriers to accessing those histories in the dominant representations of LGBTQ+ people and their histories (Parmenter et al., 2024).

How Do Queer Youth Experience, or Not Experience, Queer Collective Continuity?

Answering this research question was the most challenging for us as a research team. As we discussed our interpretations of the transcripts, we realized that much of what we were seeing was in reference only to the collective itself, without the temporal component that our definition of collective continuity necessitates. Specifically, given that we define collective continuity as seeing oneself as part of a group that exists through time, evidence of collective continuity in the interviews needed to incorporate some reference to time. Very few participants did this.

For the few participants who seemed to fully express a sense of collective continuity, this often came in the form of being able to verbally express explicit connections between the past, present, and future of the queer community. For example, some participants described an understanding that prior generations of queer people paved the way to create space in our society for LGBTQ+ people today, including oneself (see also McLean et al., 2025). …historical events like the AIDS crisis, and events like the Stonewall riots like, even though I wasn't there, what happened during those periods of time have influenced, um, like the future rights that came for queer people, um, and helped pave the way for like later advocacy work, and, I don't know, helped establish rights for like me, so I would have an easier time, and like my transition and finding my identity and connecting with other people like me.

These clear connections between events and actions across generations indicate an integration of the self into the historical timeline of the LGBTQ+ collective.

Another indicator of collective continuity was in small uses of language throughout the interview. Specifically, the few participants who seemed to experience collective continuity often used words like, "my," "we," and "our," when describing events and actions of queer people that the participant did not experience firsthand. For example, one participant reported: "I mean, Stonewall was probably like the biggest thing in queer history that has ever happened and…we're still fighting against like police brutality." This is a reflection of including the self in queer history, even though all of our participants were born long after the events being discussed.

What Facilitates a Sense of Queer Collective Continuity?

As with the previous research question, we found that we needed to disentangle responses about what facilitates belonging to the LGBTQ+ collective without reference to history or time, versus what facilitates a true sense of collective continuity. In terms of belonging, participants described spending time with other queer people, engaging in activism, participation in LGBTQ+ traditions (e.g., Pride, drag shows, queer aesthetics), social acceptance, and feeling safe in expressing a queer identity as important aspects of feeling like a part of the larger collective.

In terms of facilitating collective continuity, participation in queer traditions could facilitate collective continuity, but only if it was recognized as being connected to queer history.

For example, feeling a sense of connection and belonging at Pride parades, and understanding that they are rooted in the act of protest, originating from the uprising that occurred at Stonewall could facilitate collective continuity. Another example can be seen in one participant's recollection of self-expression as being possible due to the historical work of their elders: Whenever I'm just like walking down the street holding a girl's hand and I, like, am not afraid, I think that's what it like hits me that I can like show affection to another girl in public and like. Like I, there's always in my mind that like, "This could result in something bad," but, like, I'm not like actually, genuinely afraid. And I think that's when it hits me the most. Like this is not how it used to be. People had to fight to get to this point. And that's not really a specific historical event or anything but I think, just like the progress that's been made of, I don't have to, like, be as afraid, at least in this city, to just like publicly exist. I don't have to hide, and I can come out safely. And I think that is such progress from like 50-60 years ago, and there's so much progress that needs to be made…Progress has been made in history and fights, and the will and strength of historical queer people are what allowed me to get here.

Another facilitator of collective continuity was recognizing shared queer experiences across generations. For example, one participant described the act of coming out as being shared with LGBTQ+ people throughout history: My experience of coming out, right? Was a lot more positive than a lot of stories I've heard, but also not as great as I was hoping it to be, um. And that in itself will let me connect to pretty much every older queer adult ever, right? I don't think most people have a connection that strong, or a singular moment in their life that can relate to so many thousands of people. And so I think that alone creates a much stronger connection.

Other shared experiences included a shared history of pain and oppression, reading queer historical fiction, classroom learning about queer history, seeing current legal proceedings as being connected to past LGBTQ+ rights movements, hearing about queer history from those who lived it, and generally being able to access queer spaces, people, and knowledge.

Additionally, collective continuity could be facilitated by mentorship from an LGBTQ+ elder: I don't think I know anybody older than me that isn't cis. I feel like having a mentor in that way would be very beneficial, but in that way I kind of have become the mentor versus having a mentor. Because like my little cousins are coming out as like transgender and things it's like, "Oh OK, so now I have to be the elder here and like pass on this information that I don't, I never got."

This participant expressed trying to help their family members in navigating shared queer gender identities, despite never having received guidance from an elder themselves. In contrast, another participant described their experiences at a boarding school with a high percentage of queer students and faculty as facilitating their journey into queer elderhood: I also met the first queer young people in my life there. And over the period of…two years? Came to understand myself as queer, not just other people as queer. And then from there, I had two years of being a queer authority in that space. And guiding other students through that experience. And so that was like really, really big for me understanding who I am and what I am.

Here, the participant describes their experiences of being in queer spaces and interacting with queer people as being central to not only learning about their own identity, but learning how to pass that knowledge on to others who need it. Indeed, while much of our research focuses on younger generations connecting to older LGBTQ+ people, being able to also pass on hope to future generations has previously been found to be a potent resilience factor amongst queer people (Parmenter et al., 2026). Thus, young queer people could develop a sense of collective continuity through understanding their history, participating in historically-based traditions, recognizing shared queer experiences, and passing along that knowledge to other queer people.

However, all these things first require access to information and fellow LGBTQ+ community members, which we discuss more below.

What are Obstacles to Queer Collective Continuity?

There were several things that we identified as being barriers to the development of collective continuity, some of which were the natural opposites of those that facilitate it. For example, if access to queer spaces, people, and knowledge can encourage the development of collective continuity, then a lack of access to those things can forestall it. Additionally, certain aspects of the self could also hinder this process. Being earlier in the developmental process of understanding one's identity, such that if one does not truly feel like part of the collective itself, then collective continuity cannot occur. Mental health was also identified as a barrier, such that when more cognitive resources must be devoted to simply functioning in day-to-day life, fewer resources are available for identity exploration. This is similar to discussion of identity conflict and coherence amongst LGBTQ+ people, and how a lack of access to queer community can disrupt identity development processes (Parmenter et al., 2022). Additionally, conflicting intersectional identities and internalized stigma came up several times as barriers. One participant explained the difficulties that came up when their racial and LGBTQ+ identities collided:

It's interesting going between like sides of my family, and going from like the African American part to the white part, and how they respond to my very openly queerness. I, the, it's kind of interesting that, like, the Black side of my family isn't as accepting of me and my queerness and my genderqueerness as the white side. But at the same time, it's like, in the queer community, because of your race or your skin color, you get excluded from like conversations, or you get -there is a lot of like racism in the community as well. So, it's like can't you can't win regardless of where you are.

Another participant described his experience growing up in a conservative religious environment that contributed to conflicting feelings about his own identity: I think I'm on a path, and there's a desire to become like the exact opposite of who I once was -who I was in the Mormon church, who I was as a teenager and like…to be the opposite of that. And so they're all kind of connected…And, as I become more comfortable with my identity and my bisexuality, I'm hoping that will become easier. Um…But right now it's like in this current stage of change and deconstruction and figuring out who I am, everything's kind of connected.

Additionally, general stigma coming from majority groups hindered collective continuity, leading some participants to feel unsafe in engaging with or exploring their identities. This sometimes crossed generations in the form of intergenerational trauma, such that the negative experiences of prior generations frightened or hindered the younger generation from feeling like part of the community.

However, the forestallment of collective continuity does not originate solely from outside the LGBTQ+ collective. Participants referenced conflict between subgroups of the LGBTQ+ community as being particularly problematic, as well as differences and arguments between generations of queer people, such as appropriate vocabulary (e.g., the use of the term "transsexual" to describe someone who identifies as transgender). Broadly, the LGBTQ+ community is well-documented in often reproducing systems of power and oppression within itself, resulting in experiences of racism, biphobia, transphobia, acephobia, and more for multiply marginalized members (e.g., Parmenter et al., 2021). Interestingly, the use of the common narrative that LGBTQ+ youth should be grateful for how much social progress has occurred in recent years also hindered a sense of collective continuity (see also McLean et al., 2025). Participants sometimes alluded to a sense that they could not truly claim queer history and culture as their own because they felt that they had not suffered in the same ways as prior generations. One participant described their perception of the conflicts that occur between groups of queer people as relating to a lack of resources for everyone now that the community has grown so large and diverse: …if you lay down, lay out the letters, you know LGBTQIA+ you know who, in that, where do you cut it? You know, because it's, you know, there's an idea, I think that there are limited resources and limited, you know, limited help…Only some people can get this help. And, or even in other cases, there's an idea that maybe if I put down all of these other people, then I can look better, you know. That I could get more help as a result, you know?

Additionally, the same participant also expressed that the progression of LGBTQ+ rights over the last few decades to have created an environment in which queer people lack motivators to unite (see also Weststrate & McLean, 2010).

Overall, young queer people named many different challenges when engaging in processes that might lead to collective continuity development. These barriers can occur from within the person, within the LGBTQ+ community, or from society at large.

Summary

Overall, a full sense of collective continuity itself was rare among our participants. Most were able to name shared experiences that the majority of LGBTQ+ people can identify with and relate to, and also felt some sense of belonging and comfort within the community, but few extended their identity to include queer people and events across time. A variety of factors from both within and without the LGBTQ+ community seemed to affect whether someone had a sense of collective continuity or not, as well as characteristics and experiences of the person themselves.

General Discussion

The present studies examined collective continuity in LGBTQ+ samples using both quantitative and qualitative methods. In Study 1 we found relatively little association between different measures, which could be due to method variance, different definitions of collective continuity on which the measures were based, or base rate issues due to the lack of experience of collective continuity in our sample, an interpretation that was further supported in Study 2. In terms of well-being, in Study 1 our survey measures of collective continuity were associated with LGBTQ+ psychosocial functioning, but not with general well-being, and our narrative measure was not associated with positive functioning measures, although we had a very low base rate with this measure. Given the concerns with measurement of the construct, we take these results regarding well-being with caution. Below we discuss implications of these results for the study of collective continuity, as well as implications that may be unique to LGBTQ+ communities.

Implications for Conceptual Definitions of Collective Continuity

Perhaps our first set of implications is the necessity of both the personal connection to the group and the understanding of the group as persisting through time. One may have neither, one of, or both of these experiences, and understanding the distinction is likely to be important to promoting a full sense of collective continuity. Our measures appeared limited in capturing both requirements. The PCC may not tap the self-relevant piece. The IOS only implicitly targeted the relation of the self to the group. And coding the intergenerational narrative for personal and group connections targeted both, but base rates were extraordinarily low (from even having an intergenerational story to the frequency of connections), suggesting that for groups with low levels of collective continuity this measure may not be optimal.

In terms of furthering our understanding of the concept, we also underscore that when collective continuity is based on an identity that is not shared by the family of origin, or when there is a lack of access, historical and cultural continuity may be particularly distinct aspects of collective continuity. For example, in the PCC scale, historical continuity, but not cultural continuity, was correlated with the IOS, or feeling like those from an earlier generation are a part of the self. In the PCC, historical continuity answers the question, "Are major LGBTQ+ events meaningfully related to one another across generations?" whereas cultural continuity answers, "Is there an essential LGBTQ+ culture that persists over time?" Having a sense of historical continuity may be due to events that impact all members of a community, such as the passing of anti-LGBTQ+ legislation or activism (e.g., Stonewall), and knowledge of these events may foster this sense of connection to another generation. However, as seen in Study 2, participants discussed the challenge of defining the "collective," so cultural continuity might be quite specific to LGBTQ+ subgroups or subcultures, and therefore experienced on a much more local and differentiated level. For example, lesbians may not feel a strong identification with "LGBTQ+ culture" but a very strong identification with "lesbian culture."

Finally, given these concerns with methods that were uncovered in our analyses, we argue that the next step is likely not measurement development. Indeed, some readers might expect a Study 3, 4, and 5 to develop and validate a better measure. However, we advocate for examining these questions in different groups and with more descriptive and observational data before determining the best ways to operationalize this construct (see Bringmann et al., 2022; McLean, 2024; Scheel et al., 2001). We also strongly urge researchers to consider how that operationalization may need to be adjusted depending on the group with which one is working, to which we now turn.

Implications for Different Kinds of Identities and Development

Prior conceptions of collective continuity have focused on identities that are 'known,' or visible, from birth, such as race, ethnicity, or national origin. However, other identities may not be known or recognized until much later in development, or may have sudden onsets, such as with some disabilities. Our participants were all emerging adults, with many of them having only fully recognized their queer identities within the last few years. Given that identity exploration is the key task of this developmental period (Erikson, 1963), it makes sense that integrating one's own identity into the historical timeline of LGBTQ+ people may be an ongoing process in emerging adulthood, and one that may not have been engaged prior.

Further, if collective continuity is not being socialized "naturally," such as in the family, scaffolding may be needed to even consider collective continuity. That is, without a clear opportunity to consider both the group one belongs to and its history, individuals may not make these links on their own. For example, we have argued that the interview process itself may have served as an intervention to potentially increase a sense of collective continuity after the conclusion of the study, or to seek out resources needed to begin to develop it (Turner et al., 2024; see also Syed et al., 2011). These data raise the possibility of a newer model for understanding collective continuity as a developmental process, with steps that each need to be assessed and understood. For example, individuals must first identify as a part of the collective, and begin to engage with its culture. And we note that assessments that distinguish aspects of culture (e.g., values, traditions) as we did, may be important to fully understanding what the collective is. Next might be the step of understanding the history and temporal aspect of the existence of the collective through time.

Only once these steps have been taken can one begin to see their self as part of the history of this group, including persistence into the future. That is, one must identify and feel connected to the group, see that the group has a history, and then link oneself to that group through time for a full sense of collective continuity. All of our participants described feeling like a part of the

LGBTQ+ collective to varying degrees, and most could identify aspects of the queer collective's history; it was the integration of the two that was so rare.

While beyond the scope of this project to explore empirically, this lack of temporal continuity may also be exacerbated by the current sociocultural environment that younger generations are being raised in, specifically in regard to increasing rates of loneliness over the last several decades (Buecker et al., 2021). Although the exact causes of this increase have not been determined, some studies have suggested that reliance on internet communication and social media for social support may reduce in-person community interactions (e.g., Shklovski et al., 2006) and increase feelings of isolation and loneliness, leading to poorer mental health outcomes (e.g., Roberts et al., 2024). Similar findings have been replicated in several different countries (e.g., Finland, the Netherlands, Canada; Marttila et al., 2020; Roberts et al., 2024; Shannon et al., 2024), which suggests that this may not a problem that is isolated to only one social group or community, but rather a broader generational phenomenon. Future research may consider examining the influence of technology and reduced opportunities for in-person community-building as a barrier to collective continuity development amongst LGBTQ+ populations and other social groups.

Implications for Examining Collective Continuity in the Context of Cultural Change

In contrast from the focus on more 'stable' aspects of identity such as race, ethnicity, and national origin, our results point to the issue of how to understand collective continuity when there is rapid cultural change for the groupfoot_2 . LGBTQ+ communities are rapidly evolving, as in the last 50 years, we've witnessed the sexual and gender minority communities evolve from being called "homosexuals" to "gays and lesbians" to "LGBT" to "LGBTQ+" (Russell et al., 2023). For national identities, flags are important largely unchanging cultural threads through time-a banner that unites people regardless of decade born. Yet in the LGBTQ+ communities, the original Pride Flag has evolved into the Pride Progress Flag with the introduction of new colors representing BIPOC and trans people, as well as even newer intersex-inclusive variation, for example. Thus, our conceptualization and measurement of collective continuity needs to expand to hold the possibility of massive cultural change, which may be a challenge to continuity, as well as create a greater demand for it.

Implications for LGBTQ+ Communities

We designed this study to examine collective continuity in a specific population, and we have learned some lessons that we think apply to the larger study of collective continuity. But there also there are some very clear implications for LGBTQ+ communities specifically, the largest of which is the resounding lack of collective continuity. In our prior research we have documented the desire for LGBTQ+ youth to learn and know their history, for elders to share and transmit that history (MASKED FOR REVIEW), and that knowledge of that history is associated with positive functioning (MASKED FOR REVIEW). And we have also documented some of the obstacles to gaining access to that knowledge, notably the lack of intergenerational connection within the queer community (MASKED FOR REVIEW). For example, it is rare for

LGTBQ+ people of different generations to come together due to (1) lack of intergenerational families of origin or choice, (2) underrepresentation of elders relative to youth given the percentage of the population identifying as such (Herman & Flores, 2025), and the devastation of the AIDS crisis, which prematurely killed an estimated 300,000 sexual minority men, (3) fears that elders may have in approaching LGBTQ+ youth because they have been misrepresented as predatory and dangerous to youth (see Stiegler & Slovin, 2024), (4) lack of common spaces given that bars are age restricted and older adults are often unwelcome at LGBTQ+ youth groups in community centers. This lack of intergenerational contact may be one of the most profound differences in original conceptions of collective continuity, in which scholars assumed intergenerational transmission of knowledge via families.

Thus, given what we know about the importance of seeing oneself as part of a group that persists through time (Chandler et al., 2003; Maoulida et al., 2024; Sani et al., 2007), our results speak to the need to actively foster collective continuity for queer communities. Along with the lack of opportunities for intergenerational connection documented above, in the US, there is also the increasing structural opposition to including LGBTQ+ content, history, or representation in terms of teachers and staff in schools, libraries, and cultural intuitions like museums. Thus, intentional work to provide access at multiple societal levels to facilitate collective continuity in

LGBTQ+ communities is needed, particularly since we know it is associated with such important measures of psychological functioning. We also note that although LGBTQ+ individuals are atrisk at multiple levels of psychological functioning (e.g., Moagi et al., 2021), LGBTQ+ communities have strong capacities for resilience, including a rich oral history tradition, as is often the case for communities whose experiences are marginalized in mainstream public discourses; these are capacities can be nurtured and supported.

It is also possible that one of the main, unifying features of the LGBTQ+ community is simply the shared experience of oppression. Indeed, both our participants and other scholars have noted that, across generations and identities, one of the most cited experiences that LGBTQ+ people share is the struggle associated with resisting prejudice, discrimination, victimization, and internalized stigma (e.g., Hammack et al., 2026; Parmenter et al., 2020), and it has been argued that LGBTQ+ identity is less about belonging to a unified group and more about sharing relationship of marginalization by dominant systems of power (e.g., Sullivan, 2025). However, while still understudied, we believe that these previous studies and our present studies both suggest that there is likely still something more at work in bringing queer people together. This includes factors such as the integral role different LGBTQ+ subgroups have had in advocating for each other in the community (e.g., lesbians caring and advocating for gay men during the AIDS crisis; transgender people advocating for marriage equality and opposing police brutality in gay bars), the shared values that are cited across identities (e.g., acceptance, care, pride), and the experiences of queer joy and support (Parmenter et al., 2020).

Finally, we note that the need to intentionally support and scaffold these relationships may also be the case for other groups, such as transnational adoptees (Lee, 2003), those with disabilities (Adler et al., 2022), and even for youth whose parents immigrated but are unwilling to share origin stories, effectively cutting off intergenerational transmission (see Haraldsson & Mclean, 2021).

Limitations and Conclusion

First, we note the limitations of the age range of our participants. Although this is an ideal sample in terms of examining process of identity exploration, of which collective continuity is a part, there may be different concerns or needs in both younger and older groups. We also note that we did not examine sub-groups or cultures within our sample due to constraints of our sample size, although these issues did arise in the qualitative analyses. We also deviated from our pre-registration in terms of our inability to engage in a replication across the college and online sample due to the lack of participants with queer elders.

Despite these limitations, we argue that the present data represent a major step forward in broadening and deepening our understanding of the complexities and layers of collective continuity, and also reveal the stark lack of it for LGBTQ+ people. In terms of the former, we strongly emphasize the power of and need for more descriptive and exploratory work, even for constructs that been under investigation for some time. The iterative process of science demands that we continue to revisit our conceptual definitions and adequacy of measurement as we learn more about the constructs we study.
