Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Hippocampus supports the retrieval of richly detailed episodic memories from sparse cues. However, an event can be detailed and also lead to generalization. To explain both phenomena, researchers have proposed multiple memory systems or distinct memory traces for specific and general content. In contrast, we argue that a single system can support both specificity and generalization. To do this, we present ATHENA, a formal framework that reinterprets cognitive memory models as recurrent neural networks. We show that many episodic memory models are single-step approximations of dynamic networks with competitive retrieval mechanisms, similar to Modern Hopfield Networks. Notably, under a cognitive interpretation, the intensity of the competitive process is a psychologically-meaningful parameter that shapes generalization in the network. Through simulations, we demonstrate that modulating this parameter during retrieval leads the same traces to produce qualitatively different forms of generalization over the same memory traces. We then extend our analysis to sequential memory, and show how the same competitive retrieval process can be combined with known hippocampal circuits to achieve more sophisticated forms of generalization. These results demonstrate the flexibility of single-system memory retrieval and highlight the interplay of specific and general mnemonic content.

Representations of events that are rapidly acquired and individually retrievable are known as episodic memories, and have been investigated extensively in humans and other species [1, 2, 3]. As our example demonstrates, episodic memory representations are rapidly formed, individually retrievable, and generalizable to new events. But what mechanisms support the retrieval of detailed memories that can also generalize?

To retrieve information from episodic memory, a cue is compared to many stored memory traces to fill in missing details [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Brain research has localized this process to the hippocampus [10, 11], emphasizing the complementary roles of pattern separation and pattern completion [12, 13, 14]. Pattern separation refers to making similar inputs more distinct, allowing them to be individually retrieved, while pattern completion refers to retrieving full event representations from partial cues. Within the hippocampus, the Dentate Gyrus (DG) is thought to make confusable memory representations more distinct, resulting in pattern separation. This result is used by subfield CA3, where dense collateral connections implement an attractor network to complete the event representation [15, 14, 16, 17].

However, recent evidence suggests that hippocampal processing is more nuanced. Despite the importance of pattern separation, the hippocampus also exhibits similarity-based generalization [18, 19], linking events that are adjacent in space or in time [20, 21, 22, 23], that reactivate related memories during retrieval [24, 25, 26], or that statistically co-occur across exposures [27, 28]. Focusing on subfield CA3, electrophysiological recordings show that this area sometimes switches between discrete, all-or-nothing retrieval and graded interpolation between memories [29, 30, 31, 32, 33] that are tightly coupled to behavioral generalization [34].

These findings highlight a fundamental tension: if related events are similar enough to enable generalization, how can the system still recall distinct episodes when they are needed? Some theories propose different subnetworks for recall and generalization [35], while others use dissociable mechanisms that shape network dynamics [36, 37] or form separate memory traces for specific and generalized content [38]. However, multiple-system and multiple-trace solutions raise the question of parsimony: is it possible for a single system to flexibly support both capacities?

Here, we use principles of cognitive modeling to show that a single-system, instance-based memory architecture can support both specific recall and robust cross-episode generalization across several mnemonic and predictive tasks. To do this, we introduce ATHENA, a computational framework that reinterprets classical cognitive models of episodic memory as single-step approximations of recurrent networks. In the first section, we summarize our approach and emphasize the formal connections between memory models in mathematical psychology, computational neuroscience, and machine learning. Then, in the second section, we apply these ideas to more difficult problems involving sequence learning, latent state extraction, and cross-event generalization. Our results show how the common, interdisciplinary core of our theories of memory allows a single architecture to support specific memory and generalization, provides rational justification for the structure of the observed system, and reveals how hippocampus-like networks can use competitive retrieval to flexibly infer latent structure in the extra-hippocampal environment.

1 Cognitive Architectures for Episodic Inference

1.1 Psychological Principles Imply a Network Architecture

Both specific recall and rapid generalization are routinely observed in adult humans, and many cognitive models have been proposed to explain these phenomena [39, 40]. One successful class of models are known as instance models [9] (also called exemplar models [5, 41], or global matching models [7, 39, 42]). Instance models assume that both specificity and generalization can arise from a single computational process, where an individual compares a retrieval cue to many memory traces and uses pattern completion to infer missing information.

Instance models achieve both specific and general memory via the interaction of two key cognitive principles. First, retrieval is assumed to be affected by the precision of a learner's representations [43]. In many notable models, such as the Generalized Context Model (GCM), a precision parameter determines how similar a cue must be to a memory trace for that trace to influence the learner's expectations [5, 41]. Precision is related to the simple discriminability of an item, but can also be influenced by the features used to represent the item, intrinsic noise during encoding, and the learner's expectations about how similar items must be for them to share other properties [44, 43, 45]. In memory models, high precision leads to nearest-neighbor recall, while lower precision allows for retrieval to blend together many similar experiences, enabling simple forms of generalization [46]. However, similarity and precision alone cannot fully explain generalization. For example, after learning to distinguish two types of items across a decision boundary (e.g., 'high' vs. 'low' intensity), learners may be more confident when classifying items far from the boundary, despite them being obviously different from previous examples [46]. This behavior is better explained using relative, or contrastive similarity, where predictions are made by assessing similarity relative to an item's baseline match with other traces [5, 47]. Contrastive similarity allows one to understand that an unfamiliar dog is like other dogs (relative to other animals), while also recognizing its novelty. Many instance models implement contrastive similarity implicitly via the use of scale-invariant response functions (see Supplementary Section S1. A.2 and Supplementary Corollary 1).

Together, precision and contrastive similarity allow a memory system to achieve both specific recall (with high precision) and generalization (with lower precision). These assumptions entail that retrieval functions via a softmax competition process, where a parameter β indicates the level of precision. To see this, start with the contrastive similarity between a cue u and memory traces m. 1.2 General Knowledge from Recurrent Specific Memory

Beyond their role as static mechanisms, the retrieval processes used in cognitive models of memory have also been examined in recurrent, pattern-completion architectures [4, 55]. These approaches parallel formally-similar models studied in neuroscience and machine learning, including associative memory networks and other energy-based models [53, 50, 51, 52, 56], as well as the attention mechanism used in transformers [57]. The correspondence between instance models in computational psychology, transformer attention in machine learning, and associative memory in computational neuroscience represents an impressive interdisciplinary convergence in the theory of memory retrieval, and allows us to translate findings across fields. In particular, increased attention to the theory of associative memory now allows us to return to psychological models with new tools and insights.

In Supplementary Theorem 1, we show that instance models can be implemented with transformer attention, at least approximately and up to proportionality, by deriving item and trace embeddings from the model's psychological assumptions (S1. A-S1. C). Attention is itself a one-step approximation of the Modern Hopfield Network (MHN) architecture, a dynamic associative memory with guaranteed convergence, rapid retrieval, and other useful properties [58, 59, 60]. We show this architecture schematically on the right of Figure 1, labeling each component according to its cognitive analog.

Together, these results entail that any instance model in the ATHENA framework can be implemented as the one-step approximation of a MHN [61] foot_0 . Attractor networks such as MHNs are commonly used as a model of hippocampal subfield CA3, and naturally exhibit specific memory for stored patterns, as long as the memory capacity of the network is not exceeded [61, 14, 63]. When capacity is exceeded, additional attractor states emerge which may not correspond to any stored memory trace, rapidly degrading performance in some architectures [61]. In a landmark paper, Ramsauer et al. [58] showed that the additional attractors that arise in MHNs represent approximate averages over several traces, making them potentially useful for generalization rather than harmful. This result superficially resembles empirical effects discussed in cognitive psychology, such as prototype effects, where the central tendency of a set of items is especially efficacious in memory, even when it was not directly experienced [64]. However, the metastable states reported by Ramsauer et al. occur at averages in the space of neural representations, and there is no guarantee that such averages correspond to meaningful averages in a psychological space of item representations [43, 5].

In this study, we obtained recurrent architectures with psychologically meaningful metastable attractors through a modest alteration of the MHN update. We refer to the augmented models as cognitive attractor networks (CANs; see Methods and S1. C.3), and show an example of typical behavior in Figure 2. In our example, we obtained the CAN generated by the GCM (S2. A.1), and visualized retrieval in three regimes of competition, corresponding to those discussed by Ramsauer et al. [58]. When competition was low (panel a), the system retrieved a single, global representation near the mean of stored patterns. As competition increased (b), meaningful attractor states emerged that generalized across clusters of items. Finally, with strong competition (c), individual traces were retrievable.

Note that the attractors in Figure 2b reflect meaningful psychological states with coherent local generalization. These results show that recurrent instance models implemented as attractor networks can access stable representations of cluster prototypes [64], and support the idea that stable representations of prototypes naturally emerge from a confluence of memory traces, even in the absence of additional mechanisms [5, 4], cf., [48, 65, 35]. Prototype representations are a type of generalized memory, suggesting that such general representations naturally emerge in instance models when implemented in recurrent architectures [4].

In summary, CANs support specific recall (panel c) and meaningful generalization (panels a-b) with the same memory traces in different competitive regimes. Combined with the reasoning in the previous section, these results imply that an instance model capable of modulating the intensity of competition (β) at retrieval could achieve both specific memory and generalization. Modulation of EC, DG, and CA3 during retrieval is empirically known to cause qualitative changes in the resulting retrieval dynamics [66, 67, 68, 12, 69, 70, 71, 72], providing a plausible biological pathway to control this parameter. We discuss this further below. Moreover, in Figure 2, we see that instance models with specific memories naturally achieve stable general representations of clusters of items. Below, we demonstrate that this ability can be surprisingly powerful when applied to more difficult problems. To explore this, we turn to sequential inference tasks, analogous to some of the inferential problems faced by hippocampus. We show that the principles underlying static recall and generalization in CANs enable the same system to achieve flexible and sophisticated inferences with only minor alterations.

2 Two Routes to Sequence Generalization

Events unfold over time, and learners must remember not just static events, but also sequences. Similar to static retrieval, sequence memory also shows both specificity and generalization: hippocampus sometimes replays exact prior experiences and other times allows the organism to extract regularities that unfold over time [21, 62, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77]. These capabilities again raise our central question: how can a single memory system support both specificity and generalization?

In this section, we examine how the CAN retrieval mechanism can flexibly shift between specificity and generalization when applied to sequential data. We consider two extensions of the networks developed above, each capable of learning single-step dependencies between events. Through simulations, we demonstrate that both mechanisms show meaningful generalization, though with important differences and caveats.

2.1 Instance Models for Latent State Inference

Previous research has shown that some instance models from computational psychology implement forms of nonparametric inference to infer the unknown category of a new item based on labeled examples [78, 79]. In S1. C.4, we generalize these results to a broader class of models, and apply them to analogous sequential inference problems.

We first considered an instance model implemented as the hippocampus-like network from Figure 1 and applied to one-step prediction. In this task, the extra-hippocampal environment was assumed to be in one of several latent states, which determined the distribution over input patterns. After a period of simple memory storage, we showed that cuing the network with a new item produces consistent estimates of both the expected next item, and the learner's uncertainty about the latent state of the environment (see Supplementary Theorem 2). These estimates correspond respectively to patterns of activity in the feature layer, analogous to EC, and the memory layer, analogous to CA3. Our results hold under very general regularity assumptions about pre-hippocampal encoding processes, and do not require representations tuned for the task or memory-based inference in general.

A sequential analog of the one-step latent inference task is a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) [80] over discrete latent states (see S1. D). To achieve multi-step sequence retrieval, we considered two augmented architectures, shown schematically in Figure 3b. The Big Loop mechanism corresponds to recurrence in the trisynaptic pathway of the hippocampal complex (EC→DG→CA3→CA1→EC), where the representation in EC at each time serves as a cue for retrieving the next pattern in EC. Alternatively, Local Recurrence corresponds to local, time-asymmetric associative learning in CA3-CA3 connections. Local recurrence between CA3 neurons has previously been considered important to hippocampal sequence learning [74, 81, 82, 83, 84], while big loop recurrence has been used to explain rapid generalization enabled via episodic memory [62].

In our first sequential simulation, we applied the two architectures to data from a HMM with the latent transition structure pictured in Figure 3a. Our formal results suggest that, with sufficient data, both architectures should achieve single-step predictions and extract a distributed representation of latent states of the environment. Moreover, multi-step inference should be possible in the Local Recurrence mechanism, but more difficult to observe in the Big Loop. See S1. D and Supplementary Theorem 3 for details.

In our first set of results (panels d-e), items were presented under idealized conditions with no emission noise (i.e., one-hot state vectors). Both architectures show competence in inferring the one-step latent structure of the environment after 1000 observations. This is despite storing only specific memories (the Big Loop) or storing specific memories and using Hebbian learning for one linear layer of memory-to-memory weights (Local Recurrence; see Methods). Both mechanisms closely approximated the true one-step transition matrix, whether estimated from feature layer or memory layer representations. However, while the Local Recurrence mechanism was able to use this competence to make accurate predictions of future states, the Big Loop's n-step predictions were overly deterministic, with network activity getting stuck within a single community. The observed over-determinism of the Big Loop mechanism may be helpful in more deterministic environments [84], though here it underperforms compared to Local Recurrence. We examine the Big Loop in a deterministic context below (Figure 4), and also consider simpler nondeterministic sequences discussed in studies with human subjects (Figure 5).

We next applied the architectures to data with noisy, high-dimensional emission distributions. In this case, predictions from the feature layer understandably deteriorated, though they retained many qualitative features of the noiseless patterns. Surprisingly, results measured from memory-layer activation were similar to the noiseless case, suggesting that latent state inference is robust to (this form of) noise.

Finally, an important assumption for the asymptotic results in both Supplementary Theorems 2 and 3 is that the effective level of competition β is allowed to slowly increase with the number of memory traces. We hypothesized above that cortical modulation of EC, DG, and CA3 could tune the level of competition for a particular retrieval task. Consistent with this idea, we do not use optimal schedules of competition in our simulations, but instead consider what occurs as values of β are varied over the same number of traces. To examine model performance under reduced competition in this study, we reduced β by a factor of ten. This resulted in visually similar plots for each condition and mechanism, resembling Figure 3f. With lower β, both mechanisms retrieved the steady state of the Markov process, allowing them to predict that more time is spent in some regions of state space (C1 and C2) rather than others (T1 and T2).

In sum, both Big Loop and Local Recurrence allowed instance models to extract regularities about the latent states of a HMM. This enabled the systems to form a model of the environment, stored as a distributed representation over specific memory traces and accessed via recurrent retrieval. Forming a model of the environment is a hallmark of general knowledge, and these results show how forming specific traces can actually facilitate the simultaneous acquisition of such knowledge, either in the same weights (the Big Loop) or in easily acquired weights (Local Recurrence). While the two architectures could be substantially improved, the relative simplicity of such models and the ease with which they acquire general knowledge about latent structure demonstrate that specific memory naturally coexists with and enables generalization. We now turn to simulations to better understand the capabilities of the two sequence learning mechanisms in more realistic tasks.

2.2 Generalization with the Big Loop Mechanism

2.2.1 Replay and Sequence Abstraction

The previous simulation showed that Local Recurrence outperforms the Big Loop mechanism for latent state inference. This was because the Big Loop exhibited an over-deterministic bias, exaggerating the likelihood of probable transitions. We now consider a situation where over-determinism may be useful: replay of previously experienced spatial paths. In the hippocampus, replay plays a key role in memory consolidation and reveals interactions between specificity and generalization [85, 86, 87, 88, 89, 90, 91, 75]. We show that even when applying the Big Loop to a deterministic task such as replay, meaningful forms of generalization still occur in tandem with specific memory.

To examine how recurrent retrieval may enable both specific replay and abstraction, we generated several spatial paths in a simulated environment (see Methods and S2. C). The steps making up each path were assigned spatiotemporal representations, with steps nearby in space or time being more similar. We then stored several paths (rows of Figure 4) into a hippocampus-like network, configured to use the Big Loop mechanism. We cued the model with the first representation of the sequence and considered its behavior under different levels of competition (β).

As seen in the right column of Figure 4, with intense competition, the Big Loop mechanism replayed spatial sequences accurately after a single exposure. Then, as competition was relaxed, performance did not simply degrade, but showed qualitative changes. With lower competition, the retrieved representation became static, and showed activation from units across an entire path. This occurred because the cue was similar to many steps of the stored sequence, and recurrent retrieval rapidly distributed activation across all spatial locations from that path. Under strong within-path similarity (here, due primarily to temporal context [92, 73]), the timepoint-to-timepoint change that previously enabled replay was too weak to escape the shared attractor representing the average of all path representations. In this regime, the locations experienced during the entire event were effectively bound together, creating a stable representation of the whole path.

This result shows how the forms of generalization discussed above allow a model with the same traces to both replay a sequence dynamically and extract regularities as a stable pattern. Moreover, it shows how the Big Loop mechanism can extract regularities from a deterministic sequence, despite the limitations we discussed above.

2.2.2 Rapid Relation Learning and Transitive Inference

The Big Loop mechanism can also achieve forms of generalization when sequences are nondeterministic [62]. To examine this ability, we used a transitive inference task [93, 25, 94], where the same model as above was presented a transitive relation as a set of distinct experiences (e.g., A→B, B→C, ...). After storage, we assessed whether the model could infer the implied relational structure (e.g., A→C) by cuing with each item and allowing activity to propagate through the network.

To extend previous results [62], we considered a variety of transitive relations across four and five items. These are shown as Hasse diagrams in Figure 5, where each edge shows a one-step (A→B) pair. We first stored one experience with each one-step relation into the model. Then, we cued the model with each potential item and observed activation over several steps (right heatmaps; see Methods). Comparing the observed activation to a normative model (center heatmaps of Figure 5; see S2. D.3), we found that the Big Loop mechanism successfully generalized using each relation, with chains of one-step associations able to produce generalization across pairs of items that were not experienced together. This is consistent with previous work showing rapid and meaningful generalization over sequences with relatively few states [62]. More generally, our results with the Big Loop demonstrate how qualitatively different forms of generalization can emerge from the same memory system under different circumstances.

2.3 Incidental Learning with Local Recurrence Enables One-Shot Inference

Finally, recall that the Local Recurrence mechanism outperformed the Big Loop in the many-step prediction task from Figure 3. However, in the first simulation, 1000 steps were stored from a HMM and used to train the memory-to-memory associative matrix. One may worry that the addition of this mechanism makes Local Recurrence unable to achieve the rapid inferences characteristic of instance models.

To further test the architecture, we examined how it performed in a one-shot reward generalization task following a period of incidental learning. We constructed four spatial 'grid world' environments (left panels of Figure 6), where accessible locations formed a graph structure, and 'portals' allowed passage between distant nodes. All states were represented to the model using equally (dis)similar representations, and any competence with the navigation task was a consequence of learning.

In each environment, we first simulated a short, 50-step novelty-based exploration period with no rewards. Example paths with our algorithm can be seen in Figure 8. After exploration, we presented the model a single rewarding experience in the green squares shown in the Figure , and used recurrent retrieval to estimate the value of each state (center panels).

We found that, across environments (a-d), the Local Recurrence mechanism could quickly navigate to the target after a short exploration period and a single rewarding experience. Because exploratory paths in memory consisted of transitions between neighboring locations, recurrent retrieval allowed inferred future locations to spread through the environment step-by-step. This resulted in value assessments that decreased with the distance from the reward (center panels). Then, by repeatedly choosing the next accessible state with the largest expected value, the model could efficiently navigate to the reward (right panels). While similar generalization abilities had been previously observed in the Big Loop mechanism and other circuits [62, 96, 97], these results show that instance storage and local recurrence can also achieve rapid generalization with few exposures and a remarkably simple architecture. They also highlight again the interplay between specific memory (for locations) and general knowledge (multi-step distances), as codependent outcomes of the same system.

2.4 Conclusion

Our results across simulation studies reveal that instance models with minor alterations for sequence learning are adept learners of both specific experiences and general regularities. When competition is allowed to vary flexibly with task demands, both the Big Loop and Local Recurrence mechanisms can extract information about the latent environment over many experiences (with modest competition) while retaining individual event memory (under intense competition). In the context of the ATHENA framework developed in the first Section, these results show how cognitive models can be implemented as recurrent networks, where cognitive assumptions about precision and contrastive similarity lead to single-system, specific memory models that can retrieve useful general knowledge across many tasks.

3 General Discussion

How can a memory system support both specific recall and generalization? While many frameworks resolve this tension using multiple systems of memory or multiple traces for specific and general content [35, 36, 38], these accounts may underestimate both the interplay between specific and general representations and the flexibility of single-system explanations. To examine this possibility, we developed the ATHENA framework, showing that cognitive models of episodic memory can be reinterpreted as recurrent, hippocampus-like networks that achieve both specificity and generalization by modulating competitive dynamics. Our results reveal that a specific memory system can support a wide array of general inferences and aligns with known hippocampal circuits, suggesting that this flexibility may occur in the brain.

While we have argued that a single-system, single-trace account achieves surprising flexibility from a single competition parameter, our results do not rule out multiple-systems approaches to the phenomena that we describe [17, 1]. It could also be that the brain developed many independent memory systems, any number of which may possess the flexibility described above. Instead, we suggest more modestly that general representations naturally emerge in specific memory systems, and that qualitatively different forms of knowledge do not necessarily arise from separate traces or mechanisms.

To derive the cognitively-motivated architectures used in our simulations, we introduced the ATHENA framework, which provides a common mathematical form for many instance models, and builds on the insights of earlier cognitive proposals [4, 5, 9, 78, 46, 62]. We showed that many existing and not-yet-proposed memory models are approximations of recurrent network architectures with known mathematical properties [98, 60, 59, 99]. Recent interest in such models [53, 50, 51, 52] therefore has exciting potential for cognitive theory, as network simulations and formal results may translate to new theoretical mechanisms or empirical predictions. In the other direction, we also discussed how psychological principles can translate to constraints on network architectures. In these ways, theoretical frameworks like ATHENA may enable both 'top-down' transfer from psychological principles to architecture-level constraints, as well as 'bottom-up' influence from neuroscience and machine learning to inform psychological theory.

S1 Formal Framework

The AuToassociative and HEteroassociative Neural Attention (ATHENA) framework is a formal framework for modeling memory retrieval as a cognitive process, where the outputs of the process predict behavioral and physiological measures. The goal of ATHENA is to decompose an inference into functional components that are specific enough to be interpretable in the language of memory retrieval, while being general enough to capture many possible retrieval mechanisms. In line with this goal, many memory-based inference models are special cases of ATHENA, including exemplar models of categorization and global matching models of memory retrieval. While there is substantial disagreement about the mechanisms underlying some memory-based inferences, formal results and simulations with ATHENA are useful because they show that many sensible models will have the properties discussed. Critically for the current paper, this includes the claims made about the role of competition in dynamic memory retrieval in models of instance-based categorization and retrieval from episodic memory.

We first present the generic ATHENA framework and give some useful results (S1. A). Then, after providing general mathematical background for the problems to follow (S1. B), we show how ATHENA models can be implemented as scaled dot-product attention architectures and give rise to a family of dynamic networks with an additional choice of a linking function (S1. C). Additionally, in S1. C.4 we show how a neural system such as hippocampus can implement an ATHENA model to achieve latent state inference, e.g., about the external world. Finally, we examine ATHENA models applied to sequential inference problems, showing that the same properties allow for adept inference in certain Hidden Markov Models (S1. D). These considerations lead us to consider the two architectures used in the main text (the Local Recurrence and Big Loop mechanisms) and show that both are sensible for sequence learning (at least in a particular regime).

S1.A.2 Notable Properties of ATHENA Models

In this section, we outline the properties of ATHENA models mentioned in the main text and useful below. In the most general case, ATHENA models only assume that the input domain U is measurable, the number of memory traces n and output dimensions k are finite, and that memory activation a can be decomposed into an element-wise 'similarity' function s applied between u and each trace, followed by a competition function operating on those scores.

The following properties are important below.

Definition 1 (Properties of ATHENA Models). Let A = (U, M, s, f, V, g) be an ATHENA model.

• Nonzero Competition -A has nonzero competition if a(u) ≠ 0 n for all u ∈ U.

• Positive Competition -A has positive competition if a i (u) > 0 for all u ∈ U and i ∈ {1, . . . , n}.

• Non-negative Competition -A has non-negative competition if a i (u) ≥ 0 for all u ∈ U and i ∈ {1, . . . , n}.

• Scale-Invariant Response Function -A has a scale invariant response function g if, for any scalar c > 0, g(c η) = g(η).

S1.C.4 A Probabilistic Interpretation of Instance Models

Many instance models have been applied to categorization in the psychological literature, and model components are often chosen so that the model performs a type of nonparametric regression [29] or importance sampling [30]. For example, in the GCM, s, f are chosen so that f i [s M (u)] is proportional to a Gaussian or Laplacian kernel, allowing it to perform density estimation for the distribution of items in each category [9, 10, 31]. At the same time, authors have hypothesized that hippocampus performs forms of nonparametric, latent-state inference [42], sometimes implemented in instance models [43]. Authors have also related Modern Hopfield Networks to nonparametric statistics in a more general manner than we discuss [44].
