Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Observers use high-level knowledge to support object recognition, including expectations about object locations. However, these expectations are not uniform: some objects have precise object-location priors, whereas others have more flexible spatial distributions. Across two experiments collected in 2024-2025, we examined how the precision of object-location priors shapes object processing by manipulating spatial certainty and object placement within scenes.

Experiment 1 used an online two-alternative forced-choice recognition task, and Experiment 2 used a gaze-contingent paradigm to examine processing across fixations. Recognition accuracy and sensitivity showed a significant interaction between spatial certainty and placement: highcertainty objects benefited from expected placement and were impaired by unexpected placement, whereas low-certainty objects showed weaker placement effects. In contrast, eye movement measures in Experiment 2 showed broad effects of placement, but no interaction with spatial certainty. These findings suggest contextual prediction in scenes is graded, objectspecific, and temporally distinct across visual processing and recognition.

Public Significance Statement

People do not recognize objects based only on visual input; they also use knowledge about where objects are likely to be found in the world. Across two experiments, we found that this knowledge matters most for objects whose locations are highly predictable. When an object's location is more flexible, and therefore, less predictable, then the recognition of the object is less affected by its location. Interestingly, we found that when we examined processing using eye movements, unexpected placements mattered for all objects. These findings may help explain why misplaced objects differ in how they affect performance and slow visual decisions in everyday tasks only in some cases. For instance, finding a tool in a messy garage versus spotting the stop lights while driving.

How Predictive Spatial Constraints Shape Object Recognition

The apparent ease with which we understand the visual world around us belies the complexity of underlying processes. Current theories of perception suggest that visual processing is fundamentally predictive, with incoming sensory input continuously evaluated against expectations derived from prior knowledge and recent experience (Clark, 2013; Keller & Mrsic-Flogel, 2018; Rao & Ballard, 1999). In scenes, these expectations are not only semantic, but also spatial: observers know where objects are likely to appear. Critically, the precision of these object-location priors varies across objects. Some objects are strongly tied to a particular region of a scene, whereas others can plausibly appear in several locations. The Spatial Certainty Index captures this variability by quantifying the strength or precision of learned object-location regularities (Krzyś et al., 2025). Objects high in spatial certainty have higher spatial precision, belonging to a specific region of a scene (i.e., a chandelier is in the upper region), while objects low in spatial certainty have more distributed spatial distribution. In the present study, we examined how spatial certainty (as reflective of the precision of object-location priors) shapes object recognition processes, and whether this influence emerges during visual processing or later recognition decisions. This question follows directly from predictive-processing accounts, in which prior knowledge constrains perception by weighting incoming sensory evidence against expectations derived from experience (Rao & Ballard, 1999; Peelen et al., 2024). Applied to object recognition in scenes, this raises two possibilities. For an early-integration account, precise spatial priors should influence object processing as visual information is first sampled and integrated across fixations. Under this account, high-certainty objects should show the strongest benefit from expected placement and the greatest cost from unexpected placement in eye movement measures, particularly those sensitive to parafoveal and early foveal processing. For a later evidence-evaluation account, spatial priors may instead exert their strongest influence after initial object information has been sampled, when observers need to evaluate visual evidence and map it onto a decision of the object's identity. Under this evidence-evaluation account, placement and spatial certainty may both affect eye movements, but their interaction should most clearly emerge in behavioural measures, such as accuracy and signal detection sensitivity.

Eye Movements and Parafoveal Processing

Eye movements provide a useful way to distinguish these accounts because object recognition in scenes unfolds across successive fixations. High acuity information is available only near the center of fixation (~2° from the center), so observers must use extrafoveal information and scene-based expectations to guide processing before and during fixation (for review, see Rayner, 2009). A key issue concerns how information sampled across successive fixations is combined into a coherent perceptual representation. Research has shown that information acquired parafoveally (~4°-5° of visual angle from center) on one fixation can be integrated with foveal input in the next (Rayner, 1998; Rayner et al., 1978).

Gaze-contingent paradigms provide direct evidence that visual information acquired before fixation can influence subsequent processing once its been directly fixated. This was first shown in a seminal study where Rayner (1975) introduced the Boundary Paradigm. This involved placing an invisible boundary before a preview word in a sentence and changing the visual information once the boundary is crossed by an eye movement. Subsequent work showed that preview benefits depend on the relation between the preview and the target, as well as the predictability of the target word is within the sentence context can benefit its processing (Schotter, Angele & Rayner, 2012; Veldre & Andrews, 2018).

Early studies also showed that preview benefits also extended to the processing of objects. Using controlled object arrays, Henderson, Pollatsek, and Rayner (1987) showed that object features were extracted parafoveally and benefited subsequent processing (Henderson et al., 1987 (Henderson et al., , 1989;; Pollatsek et al., 1984). In a more recent study, Castelhano and Pereira (2018) demonstrated that parafoveal object processing was modulated by higher-level semantics from the scene context. Other work has shown that scene context and object-location consistency influence object recognition and visual search (Davenport & Potter, 2004; De Graef et al., 1990; Williams & Castelhano, 2019). However, it is still unclear how precise spatial priors alter the initial integration of object information across fixations, or whether they primarily shape later evidence evaluation.

Precision of Spatial Priors

Scene context constrains object processing, but it is not a single source of constraint (e.g., see Võ, Boettcher, & Draschkow, 2019). In addition to knowing whether an object belongs in a scene, observers have expectations about where that object is likely to appear (Biederman et al., 1982; Castelhano & Pereira, 2018; Davenport & Potter, 2004; Võ, 2021). However, these location expectations vary in precision across objects. Krzyś et al. (2025) introduced the Spatial Certainty Index to quantify the precision of spatial priors for different objects. Using a largescale rating study, participants indicated where they expected objects to appear in real-world scenes, with spatial regions defined according to the Surface Guidance model (Pereira & Castelhano, 2019). This model partitions scene layout into upper (e.g., ceilings and upper walls), middle (e.g., tabletops and countertops), and lower (e.g., floors and lower walls) regions, reflecting systematic vertical regularities in object placement. Objects with high spatial certainty are strongly associated with a single region (e.g., clouds in the upper region), whereas objects with low spatial certainty are associated with multiple possible regions (e.g., plants). Importantly, it is not the case that low-certainty objects have no expected location or no spatial associations, rather the spatial associations are more distributed. In predictive processing terms, spatial certainty can be understood as indexing the precision of an object-location prior. In the present experiments, high-certainty objects appeared either in the region where they are typically expected or in a region that violated that expectation; low-certainty objects were included to test whether placement effects depend on the precision of the object-location prior. So, if the precision matters during object processing, high-certainty objects should be especially affected by whether they appear in expected or unexpected locations. The unresolved question is when this effect emerges: during object processing across fixations, or during later evidence evaluation and recognition decisions.

Present Study

The present study tested whether the precision of object-location priors influences object processing across fixations, recognition decisions, or both. Prior work has shown that parafoveal preview benefits extend to object processing in real-world scenes and that these benefits are modulated by the scene context (Castelhano & Pereira, 2018). In parallel, spatial certainty has been shown to influence object processing and visual search efficiency (Krzyś et al., 2025).

What remains unclear is whether the precision of these spatial priors alters the integration of object information across fixations or whether it emerges when observers evaluate visual evidence and make a recognition decision.

Across two experiments, we manipulated spatial certainty and object placement in a fully crossed design. Experiment 1 examined whether spatial certainty constrains recognition decisions when objects appear in expected or unexpected scene regions. Because the response alternatives differed in spatial certainty, this task was designed to test whether participants used spatial priors when evaluating limited visual evidence. Experiment 2 examined whether these spatial priors also influence object processing across fixations by using a gaze-contingent paradigm and eye movement measures.

These experiments tested between two theoretical accounts of the effects of precision of spatial priors on object processing and recognition. The early-integration account predicts a Spatial Certainty × Placement interaction in eye movement measures, with expected placement facilitating and unexpected placement disrupting processing most strongly for high-certainty objects. Critically, this facilitation should be evident not only after fixation of the target object, but also during the fixation immediately preceding target fixation, when parafoveal information is first extracted. The later evidence-evaluation account predicts a different pattern: placement and spatial certainty may each influence eye movements, but their interaction should emerge most clearly in behavioural measures of recognition accuracy and sensitivity.

Method

Participants

We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions (if any), all manipulations, and all measures in the study. A total of 153 participants (84% women, Mage = 20.9 years) were recruited from the Queen's University SONA Participant Pool during January The sample size was determined using a power analysis with G*Power (Faul et al., 2009) , drawing from a partial eta squared (ηp²) of .062 derived from Krzyś et al. (2025), which was based on an interactive effect of Spatial Certainty and Distractor Placement on attentional guidance. This effect size was converted to Cohen's f (f = 0.257). Given the input parameters (f = 0.257, α = .05, power (1-β) = .95), the computed required sample size was 52. However, as we expected an interaction between Spatial Certainty and Placement, we increased the sample size to 104 participants. This decision was based on guidelines for achieving sufficient statistical power for detecting interactions, which typically require larger sample sizes due to the complexity and smaller effect sizes associated with interaction effects (Lakens & Caldwell, 2021). Additionally, given that the experiment was conducted online, we anticipated a high dropout rate (Rodd, 2024; Zhou & Fishbach, 2016). This further justified the decision to recruit a larger sample to maintain sufficient statistical power.

Design

The experiment consisted of a 2 (Spatial Certainty: High or Low) x 2 (Placement: Expected or Unexpected) within-subjects design. The dependent variables were Reaction Time (RT), average Accuracy, and Discriminability (d') on the 2AFC task.

Stimuli & Apparatus

A set of 48 scene images (800 x 600 pixels) collected from the internet were used as stimuli. A series of target objects were photoshopped into each image using Adobe Photoshop CS5 (Adobe Inc., 2019). An example set of image stimuli is shown in Figure 1.

The target objects were selected from the Spatial Certainty Index (Krzyś et al., 2025), which quantifies spatial associations of objects in scenes. As mentioned above, this Index was created by having participants rate objects based on where they would expect to find them in the real world, either in a single region (i.e., boots) or multiple regions (i.e., plants). The regions were defined using the Surface Guidance Model (Castelhano & Krzyś, 2020; Castelhano & Pereira, 2018), which separates scenes into three vertical regions: upper, middle, and lower.

Objects with low spatial certainty belong to multiple regions (i.e., plants), while those with high spatial certainty are objects expected in a single region (i.e., boots).

From the index list of 150 objects, a total of 96 targets were chosen (48 with High-Certainty and 48 with Low-Certainty). Participants were recruited solely through the Queen's University SONA Participant Pool, a closed institutional system restricted to enrolled undergraduate students who have authenticated accounts, and this Experiment was not listed on any other open recruitment platform. Perceptual Discriminability (d').

We also assessed perceptual sensitivity across conditions by analyzing Discriminability (d′), a signal detection measure that reflects participants' ability to distinguish between target and foil objects (Stanislaw & Todorov, 1999).

Specifically, we examined how d′ varied as a function of Spatial Certainty and Placement to determine how these factors influenced overall accuracy.

Because Experiment 1 used a two-alternative forced choice (2AFC) design, discriminability was computed from each participant's proportion correct within each condition using the standard 2AFC conversion 𝑑𝑑 ′ = √2 𝑧𝑧(𝑃𝑃 𝑐𝑐 ) (Stanislaw & Todorov, 1999). Higher d' indexes greater sensitivity to the target/foil decision.

We hypothesized that Spatial Certainty would enhance perceptual sensitivity, particularly for High-Certainty objects in Expected Placements, where stronger spatial associations could facilitate visual integration across fixations (Castelhano & Heaven, 2011; Krzyś et al., 2025). In contrast, we predicted that Discriminability would decrease for High-Certainty objects in Unexpected Placements, as the violation of spatial expectations would increase the likelihood of errors. For Low-Certainty objects, which are more spatially variable by nature, we expected more flexible processing and thus no significant difference in performance between Expected and Unexpected placements (Çelikkol et al., 2023; Schwanenflugel & Lacount, 1988).

Overall, Discriminability was high across all conditions (see Figure 4B). To further analyze the interaction, we conducted a priori contrasts to assess how Placement influenced Discriminability separately for High-and Low-Certainty objects (Krzyś et al., 2025). For High-Certainty objects, Discriminability was significantly greater in Expected compared to Unexpected Placements, b= -0.585, SE= 0.059, t(447)= -9.872, p< .001, corresponding to a large standardized effect (d ≈ -1.14), suggesting that strong spatial associations facilitated object recognition when objects appeared in typical locations.

Conversely, Discriminability was not significantly different for Low Spatial Certainty objects in Expected Placements compared to Unexpected Placements, b= 0.115, SE= 0.059, t(447)= 1.934, p= .054, corresponding to a small, standardized effect (d≈ 0.22). This result suggested that weaker spatial associations did not receive processing benefits from Expected Placements.

Bayesian Model Comparison

Because the theoretical interpretation of Experiment 1 depended on whether Placement and Spatial Certainty interacted, we supplemented the frequentist analyses with Bayesian model comparisons. Bayes factors quantify the relative support for competing models and were useful here because the non-significance in our frequentist analysis does not provide evidence in favour of the null model (Kass & Raftery, 1995; Dienes, 2014; Faulkenberry, 2025; Faulkenberry, Ly, & Wagenmakers, 2020). Thus, for each dependent measure, we compared an additive model containing the main effects of Placement and Spatial Certainty with an interaction model that also included the Placement × Spatial Certainty term. We report Bayes factors such that values greater than 1 favour the model in the numerator. Thus, BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 indicates evidence for the additive model over the interaction model, whereas BF 𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖/𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎 indicates evidence for the interaction model over the additive model. Bayes factors were computed using the BayesFactor package (Morey et al., 2024) in R with the lmBF function.

For the reaction time measure, reaction times were log-transformed before Bayes-factor computation to reduce positive skew and better match the Gaussian assumptions of the model; frequentist mixed-effects models were reported on the original millisecond scale for interpretability. Both candidate models included random intercepts for participants and items.

The additive model was favoured over the interaction model, BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 = 2.80, ±2.34%, providing weak evidence that reaction time was better characterized by separable effects of Placement and Spatial Certainty than by the interaction. This result is consistent with the frequentist analysis, in which Placement and Spatial Certainty independently slowed responses, but did not interact.

For accuracy, the measure is a binary (Bernoulli) outcome and therefore requires a logistic mixed-model framework. However, the lmBF function in the BayesFactor package (Morey et al., 2024) implements Bayes factors for Gaussian linear models and therefore cannot be applied to generalized mixed models with a Bernoulli likelihood. Thus, the bayes factors for accuracy were computed using Bayesian GLMMs with the brms package (Bürkner, 2017), with marginal likelihoods estimated via the bridgesampling package (Gronau et al., 2020) in R. Both candidate models for accuracy included random intercepts for participants and items. Models were fit in brms using weakly informative priors (Hamra et al., 2013) on fixed effects and variance components: normal (0,1) on slopes, student-t (3,0,2.5) on the intercept, and exponential (1) on random-effect standard deviations. Because Bayes factors can be sensitive to prior specifications (Gronau et al., 2020), these priors should be understood as part of the model definition. Sum-to-zero contrasts were applied to categorical predictors prior to fitting.

Convergence, sampling adequacy, and bridge-sampling stability were assessed via standard MCMC diagnostics, including trace plots and posterior summaries, R-hat values, effective sample sizes, and repeated bridge-sampling estimates. For accuracy, the interaction model was strongly favoured over the additive model, BF 𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖/𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎 = 6.94 × 10 16 , ±3.0%, indicating overwhelming evidence that accuracy was better characterized by a Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction than by additive main effects alone.

For discriminability, we used the same model-comparison approach as above, with a comparison of an additive model containing the main effects of Placement and Spatial Certainty with an interaction model that also included the Placement × Spatial Certainty term. Because d′ was computed at the participant × condition level, candidate models included random intercepts for participants only. The interaction model was strongly favoured, BF 𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖/𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎 = 1.61 x 10 13 , ±1.93%, indicating that, like accuracy, discriminability was better characterized by the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction.

Together, the Bayesian analyses support the same pattern as the frequentist results.

Placement and Spatial Certainty contributed largely additively to response speed, whereas their joint influence emerged strongly in recognition accuracy and discriminability. This pattern is consistent with the later evidence-evaluation account, which states that the precision of objectlocation priors most clearly constrained recognition decisions when participants evaluated briefly presented object evidence.

Discussion

Experiment 1 investigated whether the precision of spatial priors, as measured by spatial certainty, affected recognition decisions under limited visual evidence. The 2AFC task was designed to assess recognition decisions when sensory evidence was limited, and the scene's spatial information remained available. Because the foil was semantically consistent with the scene but differed from the target in spatial certainty, participants could not solve the task based on scene semantics alone. Instead, decisions required evaluating the briefly presented object evidence against the spatial plausibility of the response alternatives. For High-Certainty targets, Expected Placement may have supported the target decision, whereas Unexpected Placement may have weakened support for the target and made the Low-Certainty foil relatively more plausible. Conversely, for Low-Certainty targets, performance may have depended partly on whether the High-Certainty foil was spatially plausible or easy to reject. Thus, the Experiment 1 effects are best interpreted as evidence that spatial certainty constrains recognition decisions when limited object evidence must be evaluated against the spatial plausibility of the response alternatives.

We expected participants to recognize High-Certainty objects in Expected Placements faster and more accurately because the placement of these objects aligned with strong objectlocation priors. In contrast, we expected smaller or absent placement effects for Low-Certainty objects because their spatial associations are more flexible and distributed. The results partially supported these predictions. For Reaction Time, we observed significant main effects of both Placement and Spatial Certainty: participants were slower to respond to objects in Unexpected Placements and to Low-Certainty objects overall. However, the interaction was not significant and the Bayesian model comparison provided weak evidence for the additive model over the interaction model. Thus, the RT data do not allow a firm conclusion about whether Placement and Spatial Certainty act additively or interact.

In contrast, both Accuracy and Discriminability (d′) revealed significant Placement × Certainty interactions, suggesting that the influence of spatial placement on recognition was modulated by the object's spatial certainty. Bayesian model comparisons strongly supported this pattern, with the interaction model strongly favoured for both accuracy and discriminability. As predicted, High-Certainty objects were recognized more accurately and with greater sensitivity when they appeared in Expected locations. This pattern is consistent with the idea that precise spatial priors constrain how briefly sampled object evidence is evaluated during recognition decisions.

For Low-Certainty objects, Placement did not significantly affect discriminability.

Numerically, however, performance did not show the same Expected-Placement advantage observed for High-Certainty objects. One possible explanation is that the spatial plausibility of the foil influenced recognition decisions, such that the High-Certainty foil may have been easier to reject when it was spatially implausible.

Thus, the interaction should be interpreted as evidence that spatial certainty did change the diagnostic value of placement during recognition.

Taken together, these findings emphasize the importance of considering object-level spatial certainty when examining the role of context in visual recognition. They also raise important questions about when these effects arise during processing. Accuracy and sensitivity suggest that spatial priors constrain recognition decisions, whereas reaction time suggests that Placement and Certainty contribute independently to processing speed. The Bayesian analysis weakly favoured this dissociation by favouring an additive account for reaction time but interaction models for accuracy and discriminability. As such, Experiment 1 cannot determine whether spatial certainty alters processing as information is sampled across fixations or whether its strongest effects emerge during later evidence evaluation and recognition decision processes.

To address this, Experiment 2 used eye tracking to examine whether spatial certainty influences object processing across fixations. By analyzing eye movement measures, Experiment 2 tested whether the behavioural benefits observed in recognition accuracy and sensitivity also emerged during parafoveal and foveal object processing, or whether they were most clearly seen in later recognition decisions. This allows for a more complete understanding of how spatial expectations guide object recognition in real-world scenes.

Experiment 2

In Experiment 2, we investigated whether spatial object-scene associations influence the integration of visual information across fixations, as object information is sampled parafoveally and then processed after direct fixation. To do so, we used the gaze-contingent paradigm (Castelhano & Pereira, 2018) to limit the availability of visual input across fixations and examine the extent to which top-down spatial expectations influence processing.

If the early-integration account is correct, then precise spatial priors constrain object processing across fixations. In this case, high-certainty objects should show the strongest benefit from expected placement and the greatest cost from unexpected placement in eye movement measures. This effect should be evident in measures reflecting parafoveal and early processing once the object has been directly fixated (such as the pre-target fixation, and the first fixation and gaze duration measures). In contrast, low-certainty objects should show weaker placement effects because their spatial priors are more distributed and so the placement effects should be smaller or absent.

Alternatively, if the later evidence-evaluation account is correct, then placement and spatial certainty may each affect eye movements without necessarily interacting. In this case, the Placement x Spatial Certainty interaction should emerge most strongly in the behavioural measures, such as accuracy and sensitivity, when observers evaluate the available visual evidence and make an explicit recognition decision, as in Experiment 1.

Method

Participants

We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions (if any), all manipulations, and all measures in the study. There were 94 participants recruited from the Drawing from a previous study, Castelhano and Heaven (2011) reported a significant main effect of location consistency on eye movements with an F-value of 26.21, which converts to a large effect size (Cohen's f = 1.32). To be conservative and ensure robustness, we used an effect size of f = 0.4 for our power analysis, reflecting a medium to large effect size commonly seen in eye movement studies related to spatial consistency (e.g., Malcolm & Henderson, 2010).

Using the effect size f = 0.4 and considering the need to detect an ordinal interaction effect, we doubled the sample size based on guidelines from Lakens and Caldwell (2021). Given the input parameters (f = 0.4, α = 0.05, power = 0.95), the computed required sample size was approximately 20 participants. Multiplying by 4 to account for interaction effects (Lakens & Caldwell, 2021) resulted in a total sample size requirement of 80 participants.

Design

The experiment followed a 2 (Placement: Expected or Unexpected) by 2 (Spatial Certainty: High or Low) within-subjects design. All participants were instructed to indicate whether the object named at the beginning of the trial matched the critical object, with a 1:1 ratio of signal-to-noise trials.

Stimuli and Apparatus

The same stimuli from Experiment 1 were used for Experiment 2. Eye movements were tracked using an EyeLink 1000 + (SR Research) sampling at 1000 Hz. The stimuli were displayed on a 21" cathode ray tube (CRT) monitor with a 100 Hz refresh rate at 800 x 600 pixels resolution, subtending 25° x 33° of visual angle. Viewing was binocular, but only the right eye movements were recorded.

Procedure

After obtaining consent, participants were given the task instructions before being calibrated on the eye tracker. Participants sat 60 cm away from the monitor with their heads positioned on a chin rest. After adjustments to the head and chin rest, they were calibrated using a 9-point calibration screen. Calibration checks with a 5-point calibration screen occurred after every trial to ensure that the eye tracking error was low. The experimenter recalibrated the system if any fixation exceeded .5° from the checkpoint.

First, a name cue was shown for 2 seconds, followed by a fixation cross for 500 ms.

Next, the background scene (not containing the critical object) appeared for 450 ms, followed by a red dot. Participants were asked to fixate on the red dot, and upon doing so, the critical object appeared in the parafoveal vision of the dot, 4° away (Castelhano & Pereira, 2018). Once the participant fixated the object region (rectangular boundary of 1° from the centre of the target) for 60 ms, the critical object disappeared (Rayner et al., 2003). Because the object disappeared, the eye movement measures reflect, at least partly processing, after object offset. The participants indicated via button press whether the critical object matched the object shown at the beginning of the trial (by pressing the "a" key) or did not match (by pressing the "l" key). Each participant completed three practice trials and 48 experimental trials. The experiment lasted about 45 minutes.

Data Analysis

In Experiment 2, we examined both eye movement and behavioural performance measures to assess how spatial associations influenced object processing across fixations and subsequent recognition decisions. Eye movement measures provide a temporally sensitive window into object processing as information is sampled parafoveally and after the object is directly fixated. Behavioural measures index how available visual evidence is evaluated when participants make a recognition decision. To characterize object processing over time, four standard measures were computed within Regions of Interest (ROIs) defined as a 2° rectangular border around the target object's outermost edge: Pre-target Fixation, First Fixation Duration (FFD), First Gaze Duration (FGD) and Total Time (TT).

The pre-target fixation was defined as the as the fixation during which the critical object appeared parafoveally. Because the critical object was only available parafoveally during this fixation, Pre-target fixation duration provides an index of processing before direct fixation of the object. This measure was included to test whether spatial certainty influenced object processing at the earliest point in the trial sequence, when object information could be sampled parafoveally but before the object was fixated. For the present framework, the Pre-target fixation is particularly relevant to the early-integration account, which predicts that precise spatial priors should modulate processing before the transition to direct fixation.

FFD reflects the length of the first fixation on the object and is commonly interpreted as reflecting early object processing and initial object parsing after direct fixation (Nuthmann et al., 2010; Rayner, 2009; Williams & Castelhano, 2019). Shorter FFDs index more efficient early processing, whereas longer FFDs suggest increased or more difficult processing. In the present framework, FFD is informative because an early-integration account predicts that the precision of spatial priors should modulate object processing immediately after it is initially fixated.

FGD, defined as the sum of fixation on the ROI during the first entry, reflects continued processing and integration of object information. In the present paradigm, visual information from the object disappeared 60ms after fixation onset. Thus, FGD likely reflects not only processing of the available visual input, but also the continued evaluation of briefly sampled object information in relation to scene context expectations.

TT represents the sum of all fixations on the ROI and provides a more global measure of object processing that includes refixations and later viewing. Longer TT is typically associated with increased difficulty processing object identity or integrating the object with the scene context. TT is not an early measure, but it reflects whether the interaction between placement and spatial certainty emerges during extended object processing before the explicit response. For all eye movement measures, trials more than ±3 standard deviations from each participants' own mean were excluded (~1.78% of data excluded).

As in Experiment 1, behavioural performance was assessed using Accuracy and Discriminability (d′). These measures were central to testing the later evidence-evaluation account, which predicts that the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction should emerge most clearly when participants evaluate the available visual evidence and make a recognition decision.

We also computed the signal detection measures: response bias, Criterion (c) and Bias (log β) because they can help determine whether the behavioural effects reflect sensitivity versus response tendencies. As was needed, when hit or false-alarm rates approached 0 or 1, we applied a loglinear correction by adding 0.5 to the relevant response count and 1 to the corresponding number of signal-present or signal-absent trials (Macmillan & Creelman, 2005; Stanislaw & Todorov, 1999).

All dependent measures were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) implemented in R (R Core Team, 2022) using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Placement and Spatial Certainty were dummy-coded (Expected Placement/ High Certainty coded as 0, Unexpected Placement/Low Certainty coded as 1). Trial-level models included random intercepts for participant and background image, and signal detection measures computed at the participant × condition level included random participant intercepts only. As in Experiment 1, across measures, planned simple-effects contrasts were computed with the emmeans package (Lenth, 2024), testing the effect of Placement within each level of Spatial Certainty. P-values are reported without correction for multiple comparisons. A Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction in eye movement measures would support the early-integration account, whereas separable effects in eye movements accompanied by an interaction in recognition accuracy or sensitivity would support the later evidence-evaluation account. Standardised effect sizes are again reported as d = b/σ, where σ is the residual standard deviation of the corresponding model.

However, this result should not be taken to mean that spatial expectations were unavailable parafoveally. Instead, it suggests that any influence of spatial certainty was not expressed in the timing of the fixation immediately preceding target fixation. Thus, although spatial certainty did not appear to alter pre-saccadic fixation duration, it may still influence processing after direct fixation or during later recognition decisions.

First Fixation Duration.

First Fixation Duration (FFD) reflects early object processing once fixated and is associated with the initial parsing of object information from the scene background. This measure was examined to assess intensity of initial object processing when it is first directly fixated (Castelhano & Pereira, 2018; Rayner, 2009; Williams & Castelhano, 2019).

We found a significant main effect of Placement, b= 26.35, t(2828.07)=2.91, p= .004 (d ≈ 0.15), suggesting that participants fixated longer on objects in Unexpected Placements than those in Expected Placements. However, we did not find a significant main effect of Certainty, b= 13.55, t(2817.96)=1.50, p= 0.133 (d ≈ 0.08), nor a significant interaction between the two, b= -5.23, t(2815.04)= -0.41, p= 0.684 (d ≈ -0.03). This pattern suggests that unexpected placement increased processing time during the first fixation on the object. However, the absence of a Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction provides little support for the early-integration account at this stage: High-Certainty objects did not show a larger Expected Placement benefit or Unexpected Placement cost than Low-Certainty objects. Thus, placement affected early object processing after direct fixation, but this effect did not appear to depend on the precision of the object-location prior.

First Gaze Duration and Total Time. First Gaze Duration (FGD) was defined as the sum of all fixations on the critical object during its initial viewing, and Total Time (TT) was defined as the sum of all fixations on the object across the entire trial. FGD is commonly interpreted as indexing initial object processing and integration of object information, whereas TT reflects more global processing associated with resolving object identity and integrating the object within the broader scene context (Castelhano & Pereira, 2018; Castelhano & Rayner, 2009; Rayner, 2009).

Linear mixed-effects analyses revealed a significant main effect of Placement for both FGD and TT measures, such that fixations on objects in Unexpected Placements were longer than those in Expected Placements (FGD: b = 42.39, SE = 10.97, t(2812.90) = 3.86, p < .001 [d ≈ 0.203]; TT: b = 45.92, SE = 11.97, t(2805.89) = 3.84, p < .001 [d ≈ 0.202]). In contrast, there was no significant main effect of Spatial Certainty for either measure (FGD: b = 18.27, t(2802.54) = 1.67, p = .095 [d ≈ 0.06]; TT: b = 14.56, t(2797.89) = 1.22, p = 0.222 [d ≈ 0.06]), nor were there significant Placement × Certainty interactions (FGD: b = -11.10, t(2805.27) = -0.71, p = 0.476 [d ≈ -0.03]; TT: b = -20.24, t(2796.77) = -1.19, p = 0.234 [FGD d: -0.05, TT d: -0.09]). The consistency of this pattern across FGD and TT suggests that object placement influenced both initial and extended viewing of the target object. However, the absence of Placement × Spatial

Certainty interactions indicate that this placement effect was not detectably modulated by the precision of the object-location prior. This pattern is more consistent with the later evidenceevaluation account than with the early-integration account: spatial placement influenced eye movements broadly, but the specific interaction between placement and spatial certainty was not evident in object-viewing durations.

Summary of Eye Movement Measures.

Across the eye movement measures, we did not observe the predicted Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction in which expected placement selectively benefited objects high in spatial certainty. Pre-target fixation duration showed no reliable effects of placement or spatial certainty, suggesting that these factors did not measurably influence processing before the target object was directly fixated. Once the object was fixated, however, object placement did exert influence on processing measures across levels of spatial certainty. Objects in unexpected locations were viewed longer than objects in expected locations, and critically, this cost was not detectably modulated by the precision of the object-location prior.

Thus, this pattern provides little support for the early-integration account in eye movements.

However, we return to this issue below using Bayesian Factors to compare whether the data best warrants an additive, or an interaction model.

Signal Detection Measures

To further characterize how spatial certainty and object placement influence object recognition, we also examined signal detection measures of discriminability (d′), criterion (c), and response bias (β). Whereas accuracy reflects overall performance, signal detection measures allow for a more precise assessment of whether spatial expectations affect sensitivity to object identity or instead shift the decision threshold participants apply when responding under uncertainty.

Discriminability (d').

Discriminability (d′) was examined to assess how effectively participants could distinguish target objects from non-targets as a function of spatial certainty and object placement. Responses were categorized as hits, misses, correct rejections, or false alarms, from which hit and false alarm rates were computed for each participant and condition.

Discriminability was calculated as the difference between the z-transformed hit rate and false alarm rate, with higher values indicating greater sensitivity to object identity (Stanislaw & Todorov, 1999).

Overall average discriminability was high across conditions (Figure 6A), indicating that participants were generally able to identify objects successfully. This interaction indicates that the effect of placement on recognition sensitivity depended on the precision of the object-location prior.

Planned comparisons confirmed our a priori hypothesis that placement would selectively affect discriminability for objects high in spatial certainty. For High Certainty objects, discriminability was significantly less in the Unexpected than the Expected Placement condition, b = -0.324, SE= 0.08, t(267) = -4.00, p < .001 (d ≈ -0.60). For Low Certainty objects, placement did not significantly affect discriminability, b = 0.137, SE= 0.08, t(267) = 1.692, p = .092 (d ≈ 0.25).

From the perspective of the later evidence-evaluation account, these findings indicate that spatial certainty constrains how object evidence is used during recognition decisions. For objects High in spatial certainty, Expected Placement increased sensitivity, suggesting that precise spatial priors made the available object evidence more diagnostic when the object appeared in its typical scene region. For objects Low in spatial certainty, Placement did not reliably affect sensitivity, consistent with the idea that expected and unexpected locations are less diagnostic when objectlocation priors are more distributed. Thus, the d′ results parallel the accuracy findings: the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction was expressed in recognition sensitivity, even though it was not evident in eye movement measures.

Bayesian Model Comparison

The central theoretical question in Experiment 2 was whether the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction would appear in eye movement measures, as predicted by the earlyintegration account, or whether it would emerge primarily in behavioural recognition measures, as predicted by the later evidence-evaluation account. The frequentist analyses suggested the latter pattern: the interaction was absent in eye movement duration measures but present in accuracy and discriminability (d′). We used the same Bayesian model-comparison approach as in Experiment 1 to quantify evidence for or against the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction.

For each measure, we compared an additive model containing the main effects of Placement and Spatial Certainty with an interaction model that also included the Placement × Spatial Certainty term. As above, we report Bayes factors so that values are greater than 1, with the subscript indicating which model was favoured. Thus, BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 indicates evidence favouring the additive model over the interaction model, where as BF 𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖/𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎 would indicate evidence favouring the interaction model over the additive model (Faulkenberry, 2025).

Eye Measures. Bayes factors were computed using the BayesFactor package (Morey et al., 2024) in R using the lmBF function. For each measure, we compared an additive model containing the main effect of Placement and Spatial Certainty with an interaction containing both main effects and the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction. For the continuous eye movement duration measures (Pre-Target Fixation Duration FFD, FGD, and TT) values were logtransformed prior to Bayes-factor computation to reduce positive skew and better match the Gaussian assumptions of lmBF (frequentist mixed-effects models were fit on the original scale for interpretability; see van Doorn et al., 2023). Both candidate models included random intercepts for participants and items.

Bayesian model comparisons consistently favoured the additive model for all eye movement measures: Pre-Target Fixation Duration (BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 = 16.8 ±1.2%), FFD (BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 = 16.5 ±1.4%), FGD (BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 = 13.3, ±1.6%), and TT (BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 = 9.72, ±1.4%). Thus, across eye movement measures, the data were more consistent with models that did not include the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction.

Behavioural Measures.

Accuracy. For accuracy, Bayes factors were computed using Bayesian GLMMs with a Bernoulli likelihood in brms, with marginal likelihoods estimated using bridge sampling. The Bayes factor strongly favoured the interaction model over the additive model (BF 𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖/𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎 = 330.59 ± 2%), indicating very strong evidence that accuracy was better characterized by a Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction. This result support the later evidence-evaluation account by showing that the interaction absent from the eye movement measures was strongly expressed in behavioural recognition performance.

Signal Detection Measures.

For signal detection measures, Bayes factors were computed using lmBF (Morey et al., 2024). Because SDT measures were computed at the participant × condition level (aggregated across items), candidate models included random intercepts for participants only. Bayes factor comparison provided very strong evidence in favour of the interaction model for discriminability (d′; BF 𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖/𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎 = 339.68 ±1.84%). In contrast, response bias measures were better explained without the interaction: for criterion, the additive model was favoured over the interaction model (BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 = 5.52, ±4.61%), and similarly for log β (BF 𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎𝑎/𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 = 5.89, ±2.22%).

Together, the Bayesian model comparisons show a clear dissociation. The Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction was strongly supported for recognition sensitivity, as reflected in accuracy and d′, but not for eye movement duration measures or response-bias indices. This pattern supports the later evidence-evaluation account by indicating that the behavioural interaction reflects differences in recognition sensitivity and are not reflecting shifts in response policy.

In summary, Experiment 2 revealed a dissociation between eye movement and behavioral measures of object processing. Object placement influenced eye movements broadly, with longer viewing times for objects in unexpected than expected locations, but this effect did not depend on spatial certainty. In contrast, spatial certainty modulated the effect of placement on recognition performance, with the interaction emerging in accuracy and discriminability but not in response bias measures. This pattern provides stronger support for the later evidenceevaluation account than for the early-integration account: the precision of object-location priors was not expressed as a selective high-certainty placement effect in eye movements, but instead shaped how object information was evaluated during recognition decisions.

Discussion

Experiment 2 examined how spatial certainty and object placement jointly shape object processing across fixations and recognition decisions. Building on prior work demonstrating parafoveal preview benefits in scenes, we asked whether the strength of object-scene spatial associations constrains the extraction and integration of visual information, particularly under conditions in which visual input is limited. Within the framework outlined earlier, Experiment 2 tested two possibilities: the early-integration account, in which the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction should appear in eye movement measures, and the later evidence-evaluation account, in which this interaction should emerge most clearly in recognition accuracy and sensitivity.

The results revealed a clear dissociation between eye movement measures and behavioral indices of performance. Across eye movement measures, object placement exerted a strong influence on processing, such that objects appearing in unexpected locations elicited longer fixation durations regardless of their spatial certainty. This finding suggests that spatial congruency broadly affects object processing once the object is fixated, likely reflecting the cost of resolving discrepancies between expected and observed object locations. Critically, however, object placement did not interact with spatial certainty in these measures. Thus, although unexpected placement increased viewing times, this cost did not depend on the precision of the spatial prior.

By contrast, spatial certainty played a central role in behavioural recognition performance. Accuracy and discriminability both revealed Placement × Spatial Certainty interactions, whereas criterion and response bias did not. Objects High in spatial certainty were recognized more accurately and with greater sensitivity when they appeared in Expected locations, suggesting that precise spatial priors helped participants evaluate the available object evidence more effectively when location information supported the target identity. In contrast, objects Low in spatial certainty showed little or no reliable placement benefit, consistent with the idea that Expected and Unexpected locations are less diagnostic when spatial priors are more distributed. Together, these findings indicate that the precision of object-location priors was expressed most clearly when participants evaluated available object evidence during recognition decisions.

The pattern for Low-Certainty objects also highlights an important distinction between the precision and flexibility of spatial priors. Low-certainty objects do not lack spatial expectations; rather, their expected locations are more broadly distributed. As a result, the Expected versus Unexpected Placement distinction may be less diagnostic for these objects. This may explain why placement produced weaker behavioural effects for low-certainty objects than for high-certainty objects. More speculatively, when low-certainty objects appear in unusual locations, observers may rely more heavily on the available object evidence because location provides less decisive constraint. This interpretation is consistent with predictive-processing accounts in which the precision of priors determines how strongly prediction errors are weighted (Seriès & Seitz, 2013), but it should be treated cautiously because the present measures do not directly isolate prediction-error weighting.

Taken together, Experiment 2 provides stronger support for the later evidence-evaluation account than for the early-integration account. Spatial congruency influenced eye movements broadly, but the precision of object-location priors did not modulate this effect during object viewing. Instead, spatial certainty shaped how placement influenced recognition accuracy and sensitivity. These findings suggest that precise spatial priors constrain object recognition primarily when observers evaluate limited visual evidence and make an explicit recognition decision.

General Discussion

The present study examined how the precision of object-location priors shapes object recognition in scenes. Drawing on predictive-processing accounts of vision, we asked whether spatial certainty, which reflects the degree to which an object is reliably associated with a specific scene region, modulates object processing across fixations or instead has its strongest influence when observers evaluate and make recognition decisions. Across two experiments, we examined behavioral indices of decision making and in the second experiment, used eye movements to ask whether the same effects were evident as object information was sampled parafoveally and after direct fixation.

Across both experiments, object placement exerted a strong influence on processing, with objects appearing in unexpected locations incurring consistent costs. However, the influence of spatial certainty was more selective. In Experiment 1, Spatial Certainty interacted with object Placement in behavioral measures of recognition, indicating that strong object-location priors supported recognition when objects appeared in Expected locations. In Experiment 2, eye movement measures showed effects of Placement, but did not show corresponding Placement × Spatial Certainty interactions. In contrast, behavioral measures again showed strong interactions between Spatial Certainty and Placement in accuracy and discriminability. This pattern provides stronger support for the later evidence-evaluation account than for the early-integration account: spatial placement influenced object processing across fixations, but the precision of objectlocation priors was most clearly seen when observers evaluated the available visual evidence for recognition. Bayesian model comparison reinforced this pattern, favouring interaction models for accuracy and discriminability but models without the interaction for reaction time and eye movement measures.

Together, these findings indicate that spatial context does not operate as a uniform facilitative cue. Instead, its predictive power depends on the precision of the object-location prior, and this precision is expressed most clearly when observers evaluate limited object evidence for recognition.

Spatial Certainty as Precision in Predictive Processing

A central contribution of the present study is to show that the predictive power of scene context depends on the precision of the object-location prior. Prior work on scene context has often contrasted expected and unexpected objects or consistent and inconsistent object-scene relations (e.g., Biederman et al., 1982). The present findings suggest that this distinction is incomplete. Objects differ in how precisely their locations are predicted by the scene, and this variability determines how strongly placement constrains recognition.

Within predictive processing frameworks, prior knowledge is not only used to generate predictions about forthcoming sensory input; it also determines how incoming evidence is evaluated relative to those predictions (Friston, 2010; Seriès & Seitz, 2013). From this perspective, spatial certainty can be understood as an index of the precision of an object-location prior that modulates the impact of sensory evidence on object recognition and decision-making.

The behavioral results from both experiments are consistent with this precision-based account. High-Certainty objects were recognized more accurately and with greater discriminability when they appeared in expected locations, suggesting that precise spatial priors increased the diagnostic value of placement during recognition. When these objects appeared in unexpected locations, recognition was impaired, consistent with a cost of evaluating briefly sampled object evidence against a violated spatial expectation.

In contrast, Low-Certainty objects were less consistently constrained by Placement conditions, as expected if their spatial priors are more distributed and therefore provide weaker location-specific evidence. When spatial expectations are diffuse, confirmation or violation carries less informational weight and perceptual decisions may rely more heavily on visual evidence. The pattern for low-certainty objects also highlights that spatial certainty can influence decisions through the relative plausibility of the available alternatives. When a high-certainty foil is spatially implausible, observers may be better able to reject it, increasing support for the lowcertainty target.

This precision-based interpretation also helps explain why Unexpected Placement had asymmetric consequences across object types. For high-certainty objects, unexpected placement created a strong mismatch between the object and its typical location, reducing recognition accuracy and sensitivity. For low-certainty objects, the same placement manipulation was less diagnostic because these objects are plausibly associated with multiple scene regions. Thus, spatial certainty does not simply scale the magnitude of contextual facilitation. Instead, it changes how spatial information is used when observers evaluate ambiguous or briefly available object evidence. This allows perception to adapt flexibly to atypical configurations (Summerfield & de Lange, 2014).

By framing spatial certainty as the precision of an object-location prior, the present findings extend prior work on scene context and object recognition (Bar, 2004; Castelhano & Heaven, 2011; Seriès & Seitz, 2013). Much of this literature has emphasized whether objects are semantically or spatially consistent with a scene. The present results show that spatial consistency is not a uniform constraint: its effect depends on how strongly the object is tied to a particular location. This notion is also seen in the Scene Grammar account of scene organization of both semantic and syntactic placement of objects. Anchor objects (e.g., stove, bathroom vanity) within the scene structure carry precise spatial predictions for smaller associated objects (e.g., frying pan or soap). Scene grammar as a framework offers a mechanism for why objects appear where they do and from there, we can infer differences in the predictability of the object's location (Boettcher, Draschkow, Dienhart & Võ, 2018; Draschkow & Võ, 2017; Võ et al., 2019; Võ, 2021). However, there is an interesting theoretical distinction: scene grammar treats spatial predictability as relational, in that it is derived from an object's position within an anchor-phrase hierarchy, whereas Spatial Certainty treats it as an object-level property that can be derived based on ratings of that object in isolation. Thus, these accounts point to two complementary sources of variation of spatial placement of objects in scenes. The current study further adds that this graded variability determines whether spatial context affects recognition as a broad placement cost or as a precision-dependent influence on behavioural sensitivity.

Object Processing Across Fixations

A central question raised by the present findings concerns when the precision of objectlocation priors affects object recognition. The early-integration account predicted that spatial certainty and placement should interact in eye movement measures if precise spatial priors constrain object processing as information is sampled across fixations. This prediction was not supported. Instead, fixation durations were sensitive to placement, with objects in unexpected locations producing processing costs, but these placement effects did not depend reliably on spatial certainty. Thus, spatial placement influenced object processing across fixations, but the precision of the object-location prior did not strongly affect eye movement measures.

This pattern is broadly consistent with fixation-control models that distinguish the timing of eye movements from the later evaluation of object and scene information. According to the CRISP model (Nuthmann et al., 2010), fixation durations in scene viewing are governed by an autonomous timing mechanism that can be modulated by processing difficulty after fixation onset, rather than being fully determined by anticipatory predictions about upcoming objects.

The strong placement effects observed in the current study are consistent with the idea that unexpected object locations create general integration difficulty once the object is processed.

However, the absence of Placement × Spatial Certainty interactions suggests that the precision of object-location priors did not modulate this integration difficulty at the level of fixation durations.

Importantly, the absence of spatial-certainty interactions in eye movement measures should not be interpreted as evidence that spatial priors never guide attention. For example, Krzyś et al. (2025) showed that spatial certainty can guide attention in visual search, where observers choose where to look among competing regions. In the present task, participants were directed toward a predefined target location, reducing the opportunity for spatial certainty to influence gaze allocation. Thus, the present eye movement measures are best interpreted as indices of object processing once the relevant location had been fixated.

The dissociation of effects of precision on eye movement measures vs. recognition decision has a broader parallel in work distinguishing the grain of information at different points in a processing sequence. This interpretation is consistent with work showing that extrafoveal information and contextual expectations can modulate fixation durations after sufficient processing time has elapsed (Antes, 1974; Rayner, 2009; Reingold & Glaholt, 2014).

Furthermore, in a recent study, Yu, Hanks and Geng (2022) found that initial attentional guidance draws on coarser, more relational target information, while subsequent decisions about whether a fixated object matches the target draws on more precise information. The present eye movement measures index object processing after the target object has been selected, so the mapping onto their two stages is not direct. However, the broader principle still applies, in that later, more deliberate judgments can be more sensitive to precise prior information than the processes that precede them. So, the framework by Yu et al. (2022) offers a way to understand why spatial certainty shaped recognition decisions, but not object-processing eye movement measures.

Across studies, coarser and more precise information appear to influence processing at different temporal windows.

Recognition Decisions Under Spatial Uncertainty

The behavioural findings provide stronger evidence for the later evidence-evaluation account. Across both experiments, Spatial Certainty interacted with Placement in accuracy and discriminability (d′), indicating that the precision of object-location priors influenced how effectively observers identified the object. In contrast, criterion and response bias showed little evidence of corresponding interactions. Thus, Spatial Certainty appears to have affected recognition sensitivity, but does not appear to produce a general shift in the response threshold.

Measures of discriminability indicated that Expected Placement improved recognition for

High-Certainty objects, suggesting that precise object-location priors increased the diagnostic value of the available visual evidence when expectations were confirmed. In contrast, when spatial priors were weak, discriminability was less sensitive to object placement, suggesting that diffuse expectations exert fewer constraints on perceptual inference.

This asymmetry is consistent with predictive processing accounts in which the precision of prior beliefs determines not only perceptual weighting but also the downstream consequences of prediction errors (Friston, 2010; Seriès & Seitz, 2013). Strong priors increase the informational value of both confirmation and violation. When a high-certainty object appeared in its expected location, placement provided useful evidence for the object decision. When it appeared in an unexpected location, the mismatch between the object and its typical location reduced recognition sensitivity. For low-certainty objects, placement was less diagnostic because these objects are plausibly associated with multiple regions. Thus, spatial certainty did not uniformly scale performance; it changed how much weight placement carried during recognition.

Importantly, these recognition effects help reconcile the dissociation observed between eye movement measures and behavioural performance. Eye movement measures were better characterized by models without the Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction, whereas accuracy and discriminability showed that the impact of placement depended on the precision of the object-location prior. This pattern suggests that spatial priors exerted their strongest influence when briefly sampled object information was used to determine object identity under uncertainty.

By incorporating signal detection measures into the analysis of scene perception, the present study shows that spatial knowledge affects not only whether objects are processed more easily, but also how diagnostic the available evidence is for recognition.

Parafoveal Processing and Predictive Scene Perception

A central motivation for the present study was to ask whether spatial priors in scenes operate like predictability effects in reading (Rayner, 1998; Rayner et al., 2003), where parafoveal preview and contextual constraint influence subsequent processing. The present findings suggest a more nuanced conclusion. Spatial placement affected eye movements, indicating that object-location information influenced processing across fixations. However, the precision of object-location priors did not produce the predicted Placement × Spatial Certainty interaction in eye movements. Instead, that interaction emerged most clearly in recognition accuracy and discriminability. This refines prior work showing that parafoveal object processing in scenes is modulated by higher-level scene context (e.g., Castelhano & Pereira, 2018). The present study suggests that not all forms of contextual constraint are expressed in the same way.

Broad violations of expected placement may affect object processing across fixations, whereas the graded precision of an object's spatial prior may be most evident when observers evaluate limited visual evidence for recognition.

At the same time, the present findings leave open an important question about how spatial priors operate across the transition from parafoveal preview to foveal object processing.

Within a predictive-processing framework, parafoveal information may be used to generate an initial prediction about object identity or location, which is then updated once the object is directly fixated. Spatial certainty provides a way to ask whether some object-location predictions are held with greater precision than others. For high-certainty objects, a mismatch between expected placement and foveal input may require greater updating because the prior is more constrained. For low-certainty objects, the same mismatch may be less costly because the prior is broader and can accommodate a wider range of locations. Thus, the key issue for future work is not only whether parafoveal information is extracted, but how strongly it is weighted, maintained, or revised when new foveal evidence becomes available.

This question has clear parallels with the reading literature. In reading, mismatches between parafoveal previews and subsequently fixated words produce processing costs, suggesting that information extracted before fixation is carried forward and revised when it conflicts with foveal input (Rayner et al., 2003). A similar mechanism may operate in scenes, but with an added source of complexity: object-location priors vary in precision. Spatial certainty may therefore determine the cost of resolving inconsistencies between what is expected parafoveally and what is confirmed after fixation, either in terms of object identity, object location, or the relation between the object and the surrounding scene. The present findings suggest that these costs may not always appear as early changes in fixation duration; instead, they may become most evident when observers must use briefly sampled visual information to identify an object.

The present task was not designed to fully isolate this updating process because eye movements were constrained by requiring participants to fixate the target object. This likely reduced the opportunity for spatial priors to influence initial orienting or unconstrained gaze allocation. This distinction may help explain why other studies have observed earlier effects of scene consistency on eye movements. For example, Coco, Nuthmann, and Dimigen (2020) found that scene-consistent objects were fixated sooner and viewed for less time, whereas inconsistent objects produced longer fixation durations and increased viewing times. In contrast, the current paradigm directed gaze toward a predefined target location and limited object availability after fixation onset. Under these conditions, spatial priors may have been less likely to affect where or when the eyes moved and more likely to affect how the available evidence was evaluated for recognition.

Future studies could more directly manipulate the relation between parafoveal and foveal object information. For example, spatial certainty could be crossed with preview validity so that the parafoveal object either matches or conflicts with the object revealed after fixation. This would allow researchers to test whether precise spatial priors affect the incorporation, discounting, or revision of parafoveal information when predictions are violated.

Constraints on Generality

The target population is intended to be adult individuals, as we are interested in those who have experience with the types of indoor environments used in the stimuli (North American) and align with those who contributed to the spatial certainty ratings that define the High and Low Certainty conditions (Krzyś et al., 2025). In this way, we intend the study to examine the precision of the priors we manipulated. The participant sample was drawn from a university undergraduate population, which may have limited the generalizability of the findings to a broader demographic. University students often share similar cognitive and perceptual characteristics, which might not reflect the diversity found in the general population. Therefore, whether the findings are applicable across different age groups, educational backgrounds, and cultural contexts is an empirical question.

Conclusion

In sum, the present study shows that spatial regularities in scenes constrain object recognition in a graded and temporally specific manner. Spatial placement broadly influenced object processing across fixations, but the precision of object-location priors, indexed by spatial certainty, determined whether placement altered recognition accuracy and sensitivity. This pattern provides stronger support for a later evidence-evaluation account than for an earlyintegration account: spatial priors did not uniformly shape eye movement measures, but instead influenced how briefly sampled object information was used to determine object identity. By treating spatial certainty as a measure of the precision of object-location priors, the present study extends predictive accounts of vision to the graded structure of scenes. More broadly, these findings show that scene context is not a uniform source of facilitation; its influence depends on how reliably particular objects are associated with particular locations and on the stage of processing being measured.
