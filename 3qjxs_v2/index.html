To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .17 In the primary model, the estimated change from 1984 to 2025 was -8.3 percentage points, 95% CI [-22.2, 5.6], p = .17 (Table 4).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

For educational material on preventing the misinterpretation of p values, see Improving Your Statistical Inferences.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

For seven decades, studies have asked whether the people who receive psychologists' evaluation reports understand what they say. Existing research has largely measured reader satisfaction rather than what readers comprehend, recall, or interpret. This narrative synthesis identified six publications reporting eight study samples, conducted between 1984 and 2025 in school psychology and, most recently, child neuropsychology, in which 253 readers were assigned to a standard-format report and had their comprehension scored directly. Publication-level comprehension ranged from 46.6% to 61.1% (weighted mean = 55.2%) and showed no detectable change across four decades. In every study that also scored an accessible version of the same report, readers understood it better, with scores from 60% to 82%. Recent evidence from pediatric neuropsychology shows the same gap: parents rated their understanding of their child's report highly, yet almost half could not state the child's diagnoses without error. Effective formats combined plain-language wording with one or more of domain-based organization, concrete behavioral examples, and elaborated recommendations. These findings suggest that jargon-related comprehension failure is a persistent, measurable, and modifiable problem spanning specialties within psychology. Implications for training, supervision, and evidence-based communication practice across school and clinical psychology are discussed.

[div-01]

Across specialties, psychologists have spent more than 70 years asking a basic question: Can the people who receive evaluation reports understand them? The findings have shown no clear improvement. Cuadra and Albaugh (1956) gave four evaluation reports to 56 psychologists, psychiatrists, social workers, and nurses and scored their answers against the writer's intended meaning; clinicians agreed with it on 53% of items. Rucker (1967) asked whether school psychologists and teachers shared a common understanding of the language used in psychological reports. Often they did not. The two groups shared a majority definition for only 14 of 31 terms, and for several terms each group chose a different definition, using the same words to mean different things. A recommendation describing "school phobia" or "hyperactivity" could therefore mean one thing to the psychologist who wrote the report and something entirely different to the person expected to use it.

Neither finding was isolated. Contemporaneous surveys in clinical settings found that other professionals viewed psychological reports as too technical, vague, overly long, and of limited practical value (Garfield et al., 1954; Tallent & Reiss, 1959a , 1959b , 1959c). Many of the same conventions still appear in evaluation reports: technical language, test-by-test organization, and interpretations clearer to psychologists than to the educators, families, and patients expected to act on them.

Medicine and public health set measurable standards for the materials they write for the public. The American Medical Association (AMA) targets a sixth-grade reading level for patient materials (Weiss, 2007), and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) scores public communication against 20 plain-language criteria (Baur & Prue, 2014). However, only 2.1% of patient materials in top medical journals hit the AMA's target (Rooney et al., 2021), and no state's special education notice scores below an 11th-grade reading level (Gray et al., 2019). Psychology, including neuropsychology, has no comparable number and no required audit; a recent review found that none of the studies it examined defined or formally measured readability (Crocker & Quinlan, 2026). Psychology's professional guidelines call for clear communication.

The American Psychological Association's guidelines list communication of results, tailored to the needs of the target audience, as a core competency (APA, 2020). The National Association of School Psychologists asks school psychologists to present results in clear terms and to make sure recipients understand them well enough to make informed choices (NASP, 2020). Neither sets a number a report can be checked against. Where reading levels have been measured, one pediatric neuropsychology service found its reports consistently written at a college level (Baum et al., 2018), and the summary sections of school psychologists' reports average a 12th-grade level (Zhang et al., 2026). Reports written for a child and adolescent psychiatric inpatient service were also at a college level (Illouz, 2026). Many U.S. adults have limited literacy. In 2023, 56% read below Level 3 on the international adult literacy survey, and 28% read at or below Level 1, up from 19% in 2017 (Keiper & Rampey, 2024). On that survey, adults at Level 1 can find a single piece of information in a short text, while adults at Level 3 can construct meaning across larger chunks of text or complete reading tasks with several steps (Keiper & Rampey, 2024). Health literacy, the ability to find, understand, and use health information to make decisions, goes beyond general reading ability (Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, 2020). Among U.S. parents, 28.7% have basic or below-basic health literacy (Yin et al., 2009). Limited health literacy can make it harder to evaluate information and participate in health decisions (Ogbadu-Oladapo et al., 2026). Many are also turning to AI. In a national survey of U.S. adults, 44% of those who had used AI for health information in the past 30 days had used it to interpret medical information such as test results or doctors' notes (Raynes & Maese, 2026).

Psychological evaluation reports are the basis for decisions about a child. They inform diagnosis, treatment planning, and the family's ability to take part in decisions about care. In schools, they inform eligibility, services, and instruction. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Improvement Act (IDEA, 2004) requires that the notices schools give parents be written in language understandable to the general public (34 C.F.R. § § 300.503(c), 300.504(d), 2026). No comparable standard governs the evaluation report itself. When a report is hard to read, parents and teachers have problems understanding and using the information, whether in a feedback session or an Individualized Education Program (IEP) meeting. Clinicians who read these reports describe the same problem (Illouz, 2026; Mahoney et al., 2017).

Accessibility is also consistent with the field's own definition of evidence-based practice. The APA Presidential Task Force on Evidence-Based Practice (2006) defined evidence-based practice in psychology (EBPP) as the integration of best available research with clinical expertise in the context of patient characteristics, culture, and preferences, the third of its three pillars. A report whose summary is written at a 12th-grade level is difficult to reconcile with that pillar. Within the EBPP framework, accessible reporting is part of adapting assessment communication to its intended audience, not a stylistic preference. Audiences also differ in language. Reports are usually written in English whatever the client's proficiency, a practice that may conflict with APA's ethical standards (Aldalur et al., 2022).

Three recent reviews map this literature. Umaña et al. (2020) reviewed 19 studies of 1,309 teachers, who preferred detailed, theme-based reports and rated the jargon in current reports as unsatisfactory. Crocker and Quinlan (2026) reviewed 23 studies (1975 to 2025, N = 1,274) of caregiver-informed reporting principles. Rahill and Tacovsky (2026) scoped 55 studies of school-based report writing, 40 of which relied mainly on surveys or questionnaires. Most of this evidence describes what readers prefer or how clear a report seemed to them. A reader can rate a report as clear and still miss what it says, so these reviews leave open how much of a standard report readers actually understand.

Psychoeducational and pediatric neuropsychological reports carry different names, themselves jargon to many readers, but the underlying task is the same. In both, a psychologist writes a report synthesizing relevant information, testing, findings, and recommendations that a teacher or parent must be able to use. Bejnö et al. (2025), a Swedish clinical team, suggest that findings from school reports carry over to neuropsychological ones. They built five writing rules, which they named STEPS for structure, test results, examples, psychological jargon, and sentences, partly from the school psychology studies reviewed here. Jargon reduction and sentence simplification came from Weddig (1984), and domain-based organization from Wiener (1985 Wiener ( , 1987)). Those rules improved comprehension of a neuropsychological report even though its content differed from the older school reports. It included a computerized attention test, for example, where the older reports had more behavioral observations and classroom data.

The Present Study

The present synthesis addressed the difference between what readers prefer and what they understand. It had two aims. The first was to describe the level of comprehension that standard-format reports produce, and the second was to test whether that level has changed over the period in which it has been measured. The synthesis used criterion sampling (Patton, 2015), including only studies that scored readers' comprehension of a standard-format evaluation report (the version each study treated as conventional practice; see Table 1) against a key. This focus distinguishes it from two earlier syntheses. A meta-analysis of 14 experiments estimated how much accessible formats outperform standard ones, g = 0.83, 95% CI [0.60, 1.06] (Hite, 2026a), and a cross-study synthesis examined report-quality ratings in clinical settings (Hite, 2026b). Neither estimated the level of understanding that standard reports produce. The synthesis also drew on parallel evidence from treatment acceptability research, science communication, and assessment feedback.

Accordingly, this synthesis examined four questions. First, how does the jargon problem in psychological evaluation relate to broader patterns in health and science communication? Second, to what extent has objectively measured comprehension of standard-format reports changed over the period in which it has been studied? Third, why has the jargon problem persisted despite decades of documentation and explicit professional guidelines calling for accessible communication? Fourth, what research evidence exists that report-writing practices can be changed to improve accessibility? The benchmark studies, which scored comprehension of a standard report, answered the second question. The narrative synthesis answered the first, third, and fourth, setting the benchmark findings beside the other comprehension studies, the ratings studies, and the parallel literatures. The Discussion takes up each question in turn.

Method

Design and Reporting

This narrative synthesis examined studies that measured objective comprehension of psychological evaluation reports. Narrative synthesis (Popay et al., 2006) is a methodology for integrating heterogeneous evidence in which statistical pooling is precluded by differences in design, outcome, or population. It is appropriate when the evidence base is small and methodologically diverse, as is the case here. Reporting followed the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guideline (Campbell et al., 2020), which extends PRISMA (Page et al., 2021) to reviews that do not pool effect sizes. SWiM was written for reviews of intervention effects. This synthesis reported the level of comprehension that standard reports produce as well as differences between formats, so it reported the SWiM items that apply. Table 2 lists each item and where it is addressed. The weighted means and trend reported below are descriptive and do not pool effect sizes.

Search and Selection

Candidate studies came from two sources (Figure 1). The first was the search for a recent meta-analysis of experiments that compared report formats (Hite, 2026a). That search covered PsycINFO, ERIC, PsycARTICLES, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global through January 2026, along with citation searches, and its logs are archived on OSF (https://osf.io/shbx5/). It identified 12 eligible publications, and each was screened again here against the criteria below. The second source was a set of added searches for comprehension studies the meta-analysis might not have captured. These drew on three recent reviews, Umaña et al. (2020) on teachers' perceptions of psychological reports, Crocker and Quinlan (2026) on caregiver-informed reporting principles, and Rahill and Tacovsky (2026) on school-based report writing. Together with the meta-analysis, these reviews span five decades, three reader populations, and both school and clinical settings. The added searches also included the reference lists of these sources, Google Scholar searches for later papers citing them, and keyword searches of PubMed and Google Scholar using combinations of neuropsychological report, psychological report, psychoeducational report, report writing, jargon, and AI report writing. Record counts were not logged for the added searches, so Figure 1 reports the studies assessed from them rather than the number of records retrieved. Jargon, as used here, means technical terms from psychology, such as test names, score labels, and diagnostic or construct labels, that readers outside the profession are not expected to know (Table 1). Studies entered the benchmark set, the studies that supply a comprehension score for the standard format, if they (a) gave readers without psychological training a psychological evaluation report (real or simulated) describing a child or adolescent, the same report for every reader in a condition, (b) assessed understanding through an objective comprehension measure as defined in Table 1, (c) reported a score for a standard-format condition, and (d) examined jargon as a feature of the report, either by varying it directly or by comparing the standard report with a version that removed or explained it. In most studies, the accessible version also changed the report's organization, examples, and recommendations along with its wording. Comparisons between formats therefore show the effect of these changes together and cannot isolate the contribution of jargon. Studies from any year were eligible because the second question asks whether comprehension has changed over time, which requires the full record. Studies from any country were eligible on the rationale that comprehension of jargon in evaluation reports is not jurisdiction-specific. Studies that reported only preference, satisfaction, or readability statistics were excluded from the benchmark set. The author screened all candidates and extracted all values.

Six publications met all four criteria: Weddig (1984), Wiener (1985), Wiener and Kohler (1986), Wiener (1987), Bucknavage (2007), and Bejnö et al. (2025). Wiener (1987) reported three separate studies, so the set contains eight study samples (Table 3). Bucknavage (2007) is a doctoral dissertation, and the others are journal articles. Five publications came from the meta-analysis and one, Wiener (1985), from the added searches. Figure 1 lists each excluded publication and the reason, grouped as ratings only, no score for the standard report, readers with psychological training, parents' own children's reports after feedback, no report-comprehension outcome, a planning task, or a change in score display only.

The benchmark set overlaps with, but is not identical to, the studies pooled in the meta-analysis (Hite, 2026a). The meta-analysis estimated within-study differences between formats and admitted rating outcomes, while the present synthesis requires a scored level for the standard format. Accordingly, Wiener (1985), whose three report formats described the same child, contributes a benchmark here, and Bucknavage (2007) contributes its full sample of teachers and parents. The small number of included studies reflects how rarely this literature has measured reader comprehension directly.

Reader Groups and Education

Within the benchmark set, the synthesis attended to reader education, on the grounds that comprehension failure among highly educated readers is unlikely to be explained by basic reading ability alone. In Bucknavage (2007), 95% of teacher participants held graduate degrees. In the Wiener studies (1985 Wiener studies ( , 1987)), teachers and administrators were practicing educators taking an introductory in-service course in special education, none of them qualified special education teachers; their degrees were not reported. Bejnö et al.'s (2025) readers were teacher education students, on average 3 years into their program. Parent samples included readers with and without a college degree. In Weddig (1984), 29 of the 57 parents who read the standard report held a degree. In Wiener and Kohler (1986), 6 of the 15 held a degree, 4 had some college, and 5 had a high school education.

Comprehension Measures

Each research team built its own comprehension test and scored it against a key (Table 3). Weddig (1984) wrote 19 items answered true, false, or neither, covering the referral question, the child's current functioning, and the writer's interpretation and recommendations; a panel of special educators checked the items and the key. The Wiener studies used 14 five-option multiple-choice items (15 in the third study of Wiener, 1987), checked by three psychologists and piloted with 12 special education teachers in graduate study; one item was dropped after the pilot because readers answered it at chance (Wiener, 1985; Wiener & Kohler, 1986). Bucknavage (2007) used 20 four-option recall items. Bejnö et al. (2025) asked nine open-ended questions, each scored from 0 to 2 points with a scoring guide, for 18 points in all; agreement between raters in their pilot was κ = .95. Readers answered without the report in front of them in every study except Weddig's, which did not say.

Analytic Approach

Each benchmark was expressed as the mean score for standard-format readers divided by the maximum possible score. Where a study reported subgroups, subgroup scores were combined into a single study-level value weighted by subgroup size (Weddig's two education groups; Wiener's, 1987, three studies; Bucknavage's teachers and parents). Studies were weighted by the number of participants who read the standard-format report rather than by total study sample.

Change over time was estimated with weighted least squares regression of study-level benchmark scores on publication year, reported as the estimated change across the span of the benchmark set with its 95% confidence interval. A second model weighted each study equally, to show how much the trend depended on weighting by sample size. Because the measures differed in item format, and therefore in the score expected from guessing, each benchmark was also expressed as a chance-corrected score as a sensitivity analysis (Table 1). Chance was set at one-third for Weddig's three-option items, 20% for the five-option items used in the Wiener studies, 25% for Bucknavage's four-option items, and zero for Bejnö et al.'s open-ended items. Where a study also scored an accessible version of the same report, that score is reported alongside the benchmark as a descriptive comparison. Effect sizes for these contrasts are reported elsewhere (Hite, 2026a) and were not recomputed.

Converting heterogeneous comprehension outcomes to a single percent-correct metric combines distinct tasks, such as recognizing a term's meaning, recalling a finding, and interpreting a result, onto a common scale. This approach prioritizes comparability across studies over precision about specific comprehension processes, and the chance correction addresses one known source of noncomparability. The weighted mean should be read as the average share of available points earned on these tests rather than as a measure of any one cognitive process. Certainty in the findings was judged from the number of studies, their sample sizes, the consistency of results, and how comparable the measures were; no formal rating system such as GRADE was applied.

The review was not preregistered. No new data were collected, and all analyses use values reported in the published studies. The included studies, reader groups, measures, and extracted values, with page and table locations, appear in Table 3, ordered by publication year.

Results

Characteristics of the Included Studies

The six publications, published between 1984 and 2025, reported eight study samples. Each gave readers either a standard report or a revised version of the same report, then scored what they understood. Readers were parents, teachers, school administrators, and teacher education students in the United States, Canada, and Sweden. Of the 629 readers assigned to a report, 253 read the standard version. Table 3 gives each study's sample, reports, measure, and scores.

Only one study separated jargon from the other changes. Bucknavage (2007) varied jargon and report length independently while keeping the case the same; lowering the jargon raised recall (partial η² = .27), and report length had no detectable effect. In the other five publications, the revised report changed its wording along with other features. Weddig (1984) compared an actual school report with a version rewritten at a sixth-grade reading level and cut to half the length. Wiener's (1985) standard report was a one-page short form, then preferred by many Toronto-area school boards, that used jargon to stay brief, stated conclusions without their sources, and gave recommendations without elaboration. Her revised formats organized the report by functional domain or around the referral questions, described behavior with examples, avoided or defined technical terms, and elaborated the recommendations. Wiener and Kohler (1986) and the first two studies in Wiener (1987) reused that report and test with new readers; the third study in Wiener (1987) used a new report and a 15-item test. Bejnö et al. (2025) wrote a typical clinical report of a neuropsychological assessment of an 11-year-old boy referred for ADHD, using report templates from child and adolescent psychiatric services in Stockholm. They then rewrote it under the STEPS rules, keeping the same test results. Their readers were teacher education students at Stockholm University, randomly assigned to read one of the two versions in a preregistered study.

Comprehension Benchmarks Across Studies

A benchmark is the share of available points that readers of the standard report earned on a study's comprehension test (Table 1). Across the benchmark set, readers of the standard reports earned 55.2% of the available points on average, weighted by sample size. This figure describes performance on these particular tests rather than a standard a report should meet. Publication-level averages ranged from 46.6% for parents of children with disabilities (Wiener & Kohler, 1986) to 61.1% for parents at school meetings (Weddig, 1984). Subgroup scores are in Table 3.

Scores showed no detectable change across the years the studies were published. In the primary model, the estimated change from 1984 to 2025 was -8.3 percentage points, 95% CI [-22.2, 5.6], p = .17 (Table 4). That interval includes both a sizable decline and a small gain. The record is also thin. Four of the six publications appeared between 1984 and 1987, and none between 1987 and 2007. Figure 2 shows each study's scores with the fitted line.

Two sensitivity analyses led to the same conclusion (Table 4). With the expected guessing score removed, the weighted mean fell to 43.1% and the estimated change turned slightly positive. The direction reversed because Bejnö et al.'s (2025) open-ended questions, the most recent scores, lose nothing to the guessing correction, while the older multiple-choice scores do. Weighting each study equally instead of by sample size moved the estimate closer to zero. None of the three estimates differed significantly from zero, and together their intervals run from a decline of about 24 points to a gain of about 17. The data therefore cannot show whether comprehension of standard reports has changed.

Comprehension of Accessible Versions of the Same Reports

In every study that scored an accessible version, readers of that version scored higher than readers of the standard report, with gains of 5.8 to 22.1 percentage points (Table 3). Parents in Weddig (1984) went from 61.1% to 71.4%, a gain of 10.3 points. Parents in Wiener and Kohler (1986) went from 46.6% to 65.2% and 68.1% on the two accessible formats, gains of 18.6 and 21.4 points. Elementary teachers in Wiener (1985) went from 53.1% to 70.4% and 71.1%, gains of 17.2 and 18.0 points. Administrators in Wiener (1987) went from 53.0% to 65.7% and 67.1%, gains of 12.7 and 14.1 points. Elementary teachers in Wiener (1987) went from 60.1% to 82.1%, a gain of 22.1 points, the largest in the set. Secondary teachers in Wiener (1987) went from 61.3% to 67.1% and 72.0%, gains of 5.8 and 10.7 points, though in that study the overall difference among formats was not significant. Readers in Bucknavage (2007) went from 55.9% to 71.3%, a gain of 15.4 points. Readers in Bejnö et al. (2025) went from 47.8% to 60.0%, a gain of 12.2 points. Gains are based on unrounded scores.

Accessible scores ranged from 60.0% (Bejnö et al., 2025) to 82.1% (Wiener, 1987). The advantage held for whole tests, but some individual items ran the other way. On one of Bejnö et al.'s nine items and four of Bucknavage's 20, readers of the standard report scored higher. Tables 5 and 6 illustrate how the same findings and recommendations can be written in two formats.

What Readers Missed

Item-level data in two benchmark studies show where comprehension failed. Bucknavage (2007) grouped her 20 recall items into four content categories. Readers of the high-jargon report answered 52.3% of Results items correctly, compared with 54.4% for educational background, 58.7% for recommendations, and 63.3% for background items. Results items remained the weakest category on the low-jargon report (62.8%, against 68.8% to 83.3% for the others), and the single hardest item, a Results item on the child's general intellectual functioning, was answered correctly by 7% of high-jargon readers and 34% of low-jargon readers. In Bejnö et al. (2025), the question about the child's IQ level was the hardest of nine. Only 18% of standard-report readers and 33% of revised-report readers answered it correctly, against 45% and 55% for the question about the child's diagnosis.

Who Missed It

Two benchmark studies compared reader groups directly on the same reports (Table 3). The clearest pattern is in Bucknavage (2007). Moving from the high-to the low-jargon report helped parents more than teachers, a statistically significant interaction that reversed which group scored higher. Weddig (1984) found that college-educated parents scored higher than parents without a degree, but even the college-educated parents got roughly a third of items wrong.

Reading Level of Reports

Readability studies help explain why standard reports are hard to use. Weddig (1984) found a median reading level of grade 15 in 50 school reports drawn at random from a special education cooperative serving 29 districts. Textbook exemplars used to train psychologists averaged near grade 18.5 (Harvey, 2006). Parents rated easier versions of summary sections from hypothetical pediatric neuropsychological reports as clearer and easier to follow (Gerstle et al., 2025). Reading level alone does not account for the benchmark results, since teachers with graduate degrees also scored poorly on standard reports, but it is one feature the accessible versions in Weddig (1984) and Bejnö et al. (2025) lowered.

Discussion

This synthesis examined four questions. After a section on jargon as a construct, the sections below take the questions in order. Table 7 summarizes what the evidence shows for each question and what it means for practice.

Jargon-Related Comprehension Failure as a Construct

The title asks whether jargon can be treated as a psychological construct, and the answer depends on what is being measured. Jargon itself is a feature of text. A related candidate construct is jargon-related comprehension failure, difficulty recovering the intended meaning of report content that uses specialist language. It concerns the reader's encounter with the text and is assessed through what readers understand, rather than by counting technical terms or asking how clear a report seems. Establishing it as a distinct construct would require evidence that separates the contribution of jargon from other features of reports and readers.

The benchmark studies provide evidence relevant to this proposal. They measured understanding objectively, using multiple-choice or open-ended questions. Bucknavage (2007) varied jargon and length independently and found that jargon affected recall, while length and teachers' prior exposure to reports did not have significant effects. Those null results do not establish that comprehension failure is independent of either factor. Weddig (1984) found that parents without a college degree who read the rewritten report scored about as well as college graduates who read the original, although the interaction between report version and education was not significant. In every study, readers understood more of the accessible version, but most versions changed other features along with jargon (Table 3). Readers' ratings also cannot substitute for comprehension measures. Among readers of Bucknavage's high-jargon report, ratings of understanding were unrelated to recall. The studies used researcher-developed tests, and none was designed to establish jargon-related comprehension failure as a distinct construct.

Question 1: The Jargon Problem in Health and Science Communication

Parallel Evidence From Other Fields

Three literatures that developed independently of report writing point to the same finding. Technical wording can affect what readers understand and how they judge recommendations. Treatment acceptability research finds that people judge identical interventions differently depending on whether they are described in technical or plain language, and treats understanding as one step on the path from recommendation to implementation (Elliott, 1988; Reimers et al., 1987; Witt et al., 1984). A recent experiment measured understanding directly. Among 522 U.S. adults who watched a provider explain behavior-analytic terms, removing the jargon improved recall of the terms, although viewers did not rate the jargon-free explanation as significantly clearer (Gifford et al., 2026).

Science communication research shows why definitions alone are not enough. Jargon reduces processing fluency even when terms are defined and can signal that a text is not meant for its reader (Bullock et al., 2019; Shulman et al., 2020). Plain-language summaries improve comprehension and knowledge acquisition, while adding glossaries to technical prose produces weaker and less consistent gains (Kerwer, Chasiotis, et al., 2021; Kerwer, Stoll, et al., 2021).

Assessment feedback research shows a similar gap between perceived and actual understanding. Most psychologists believed their clients usually understood feedback (Smith et al., 2007). The same confidence appears when psychologists read one another's reports. Every school psychologist who read a pediatric neuropsychological report agreed they understood it, in its traditional or shortened form, and most judged that parents would know what to do next (Robinson et al., 2025). Camodeca et al. (2026) found that at least 80% of parents reported reading each section of their child's report, and average self-rated understanding was 4.15 or higher out of 5 for every section but the appendix. Yet only 38.1% correctly described what a percentile rank means, and only 52.7% stated their child's diagnoses without error. Smetana et al. (2025) found that about a third of patients and family members answered both questions on standardized performance labels correctly, with percentile items near chance for patients and providers alike. When the same scores were described in patient-centered language, patients and family members answered correctly about half the time (Smetana et al., 2025).

The Report in a System of Communication

Written reports matter because they remain in the child's record, guide future planning, and are read by people the psychologist may never meet. But accessibility also matters in the feedback sessions that typically, though not always, follow in clinics, and in school meetings.

Surveys of report readers show how often the written report falls short. In a survey of 349 teachers and administrators, 84.2% experienced evaluation reports as at least occasionally too complicated, and only 32.3% reported always reading them in full (Hale & Brown-Chidsey, 2023). The pattern extends to medical readers. Among 81 providers who refer for neuropsychological evaluation, 42% found the reports too long, pediatric providers more often than others, and fewer than half read them in full (Mahoney et al., 2017). Only 19% of parents rated reports as easy to understand, and all identified excessive jargon as a problem (Rahill, 2018). In a qualitative reanalysis of comments on paired sample reports, parents described technical wording and score presentations as confusing and sometimes intimidating (Hite, 2026c). When the report is not read or understood, the meeting has to carry more of the communication burden. The psychologist must explain the findings, check understanding, and connect recommendations to services. Supplementing oral feedback with written summaries improved recall of recommendations but did not improve recall of diagnostic information (Gruters et al., 2022). Written reports and meetings do different work; both need to be clear. A meeting may not fully compensate for an inaccessible written report, because material that is hard to follow in writing is often just as hard to follow when spoken. Patients forget 40% to 80% of medical information immediately, and the more information they are given, the less of it they recall correctly (Kessels, 2003).

Whether better comprehension changes what happens to a child remains only partly tested. In a clinic that offered free educational advocacy, outcome data were available for 1,862 of 2,126 low-income families, and 40% of them had every recommendation implemented after their child's neuropsychological evaluation (Marcelle et al., 2026). Among families without full implementation, the barrier cited most often, in 482 of 961 cases (50%), was that the school IEP team disagreed with the clinician. Of the factors studied, only home language predicted whether every recommendation was implemented. Families who spoke mostly Spanish at home had more than twice the odds of falling short, and household income was not a predictor. Marcelle et al. suggest that the complexity and presentation of reports may also affect uptake, although their study did not measure it. The studies reviewed here measured comprehension; they did not follow readers to implemented services.

Question 2: Comprehension Over Time

Across six publications spanning 1984 to 2025, readers of standard-format psychological evaluation reports scored 47% to 61% on comprehension tests, with no change detectable over that period (-8.3 percentage points, 95% CI [-22.2, 5.6]).

Question 3: Why the Problem Persists

By 2003, researchers had been asking consumers what they wanted from assessments for more than four decades, yet assessment practice had changed little (Brenner, 2003). The pattern is not limited to psychological reports. Across 30 years of studies of documents written to inform parents, most of them in special education, recommended reading levels fell from ninth grade to fifth while actual levels rose, averaging nearly an 11th-grade level across eight studies of 461 documents (Nagro & Stein, 2016). The writer's side shows the same gap between recommendation and practice. Harvey (2006) described four challenges in writing clear reports: model reports in training that are themselves difficult to read, psychological terms left undefined, the time that clear writing requires, and confusion about how to write for several audiences at once. Recommended practice now calls for reports organized by functional domain, written at a lower reading level, and centered on the client, yet student clinicians' reports were already out of step with those practices and often written above a 12th-grade reading level (Eriksen & Cormier, 2025). Eriksen and Cormier (2024, 2025) identified training as one reason. A hospital study with a different aim points the same way. Experienced staff psychologists on a child and adolescent psychiatric service wrote at the same reading level as trainees, and the researcher questioned whether this writing behavior comes from graduate training (Illouz, 2026).

These studies suggest two problems: unfamiliar words, and reports that leave the psychologist's synthesis for the reader to assemble. The second does not depend on jargon. Cuadra and Albaugh (1956) found that readers most often missed the writer's intended emphasis and overall impressions, even in reports written to avoid technical terms. Trainees can pick up both habits from the model reports they are taught with (Harvey, 2006).

Question 4: Evidence and Options for Changing Practice Implications for Clinical Practice and Training

Report-writing skills are rarely taught explicitly, and trainees may not be evaluated on their writing directly (Eriksen & Cormier, 2024). In a survey of practicing school psychologists, 27% were dissatisfied with the assessment feedback they received in graduate school (Zhang et al., 2026). Recommended remedies include teaching the research on accessible reports directly, giving trainees exemplars written near a sixth-grade level, teaching them to check the reading level of their own reports, and having them compare domain-based and test-by-test reports (Eriksen & Cormier, 2024). Practice can change. After one pediatric neuropsychology service retrained its clinicians, its reports became shorter and easier to read, gave more space to impressions and recommendations, and reached families sooner. School intervention coordinators who used the reports rated the revised format higher in quality, including the fit of its recommendations to the child's needs (Baum et al., 2018).

A competency-benchmark model sets out how to teach and assess professional skills across training levels (Fouad et al., 2009). Eriksson and Maurex (2018) applied formative peer and instructor review specifically to report writing. Two recent reviews set out the practices this literature supports: plain language, organization by theme or referral question, and specific recommendations tied to assessment findings (Crocker & Quinlan, 2026; Rahill & Tacovsky, 2026). Established school psychology guidance already recommends them (Carriere & Hass, 2023). The studies reviewed here add evidence that readers understand accessible versions better. Slykerman and Knight (2026) add worked examples for pediatric neuropsychological reports, including accessible metaphors for technical concepts and recommendations with concrete next steps. Hite (2026d) organizes these practices under the mnemonic READER: Respond to the referral question, Explain how you know, Address strengths and context, Describe what results mean, use Everyday words, and Recommend specific actions. The benchmark studies tested versions that used everyday words, organized findings by domain, added examples, or gave specific recommendations. Bucknavage (2007) varied jargon and report length independently; the other studies changed several report features together. Each of the 153 parents rated both the traditional and accessible reports for their assigned case (Hite, 2026d). They rated the accessible versions as more useful (g = 0.77 for a child with a learning disability and g = 0.75 for a child with ADHD). That finding concerns perceived usefulness, not comprehension.

Tables 5 and 6 show a results section and a recommendations section for the same child, each written in a traditional format and again following READER.

The APA Ethics Code already requires psychologists to take reasonable steps to ensure that explanations of assessment results are given (Standard 9.10; American Psychological Association, 2017). Crocker and Quinlan (2026) argue for building these practices into supervision and training. Doing so would make accessible reporting a competency that programs teach and assess, as they do other clinical skills.

Accessibility and Legal Defensibility

In two preregistered experiments, U.S. lawyers, like laypeople, understood and recalled contract language better when it was written plainly, and they rated the plain versions as higher in quality and no less enforceable (Martínez et al., 2023). The authors concluded that lawyers write in legalese out of convenience and tradition, not because the law requires it.

Lawyers who read neuropsychological reports also want less jargon (Swanepoel & Van Rensburg, 2020). In school practice too, what makes a report defensible is sound assessment and accurate interpretation, not technical prose (Rahill & Tacovsky, 2026). Hass and Carriere (2014) argued that lengthy reports can be less defensible when they lack clear interpretation and useful recommendations or use vocabulary only specialists understand, and that boilerplate legal language does not remedy those weaknesses.

Readers understood the accessible versions better in two studies, one using a shorter report and the other using longer reports (Weddig, 1984; Wiener, 1987). Length alone does not predict how well readers understand a report, and reading grade alone does not either. In an online randomized trial, health information written at grade levels 8 to 14 produced no significant differences in adults' knowledge (Mac et al., 2025). In pediatric neuropsychology, one proposal places all scores in an appendix the school can use for documentation and certification, while the body of the report explains what they mean (Donders, 1999).

Accessible communication still requires clinical precision. Translating technical content into plain language sometimes trades a degree of conceptual precision for meaning the reader can use. A reader who learns that a child "works through routine tasks more slowly than most children the same age" has acquired a usable representation of slow processing speed, but not the full construct as a psychometrician would specify it. Plain wording may also cost the writer some standing. In science communication research, simpler language has been linked to readers judging the writer as less expert (König et al., 2025). The alternative, leaving the technical construct in place and trusting readers to acquire it from context, is what the standard formats in the benchmark studies did, and readers understood less of them in all six publications.

AI-Assisted Report Drafting

AI is already changing how reports are written. In a national sample, school psychologists who used AI for report writing most often used it to rewrite sections for clarity or to generate recommendations, and nearly all edited the output before use (Farmer et al., 2026). Psychologists rated reports from a now-outdated AI model (GPT-4, 2023), written with no instruction to write plainly, as readable as reports written by other psychologists, but rated the AI's recommendations as better (Lockwood et al., 2025). Those were psychologists' ratings, not tests of what families or teachers understood. Lockwood and Thomas (2026) propose judging an AI-assisted report by whether families and educators can understand and act on it, and by whether a plain-language rewrite keeps the meaning and limits of the original. The benchmark studies offer a point of comparison for such tests. A study could give readers the same report written by a psychologist alone and with AI assistance and compare how well readers understand each version. Responsibility for the conclusions and recommendations stays with the clinician (Bush, 2026).

Strengths and Limitations

This synthesis has two main strengths. The benchmark analysis drew only on studies that scored what readers understood against a key, not on ratings of clarity or satisfaction. In every study, readers received the same case in standard or accessible form, so differences in understanding reflect how the report was written rather than the child or the assessment.

The evidence base is small. Six publications, most of them decades old, are a narrow record for a 41-year trend, and study selection had no reliability check. Certainty in the trend estimate is therefore low. How these benchmarks generalize to reports used in routine practice remains uncertain. The studies did not characterize readers' literacy sufficiently to estimate how comprehension varies across literacy levels or familiarity with evaluation systems.

Future Research

None of the studies in this synthesis followed the full chain from report comprehension through recommendation uptake to a measured change in a child's outcome. Future work should move beyond documenting dissatisfaction and test accessible report formats in real settings, in feedback sessions and school meetings, with samples that reflect the literacy of the families who receive these reports. The same test applies to AI-assisted reports.

Conclusions

Across six publications spanning 1984 to 2025, psychological evaluation reports showed a persistent communication problem. Readers of standard-format reports averaged 55.2% on comprehension measures, with no detectable change over that period. The pattern appeared in school psychology and child neuropsychology, across parents and educators, and across multiple-choice and open-ended tests. In the two benchmark studies with item-level data, readers had the most trouble with what test scores meant. Readers of the same report rewritten in plain language scored higher in every study, from 60% to 82%. Most rewritten versions also organized findings around functional domains, added concrete examples, or gave specific recommendations. Reports serve their purpose when families and educators can understand the child's needs and use the findings to guide decisions.

Technical wording can affect what readers understand and how they judge recommendations. Defining terms does not fully fix the problem, and readers often believe they understand more than they do. 3. Why has the problem persisted?

Model reports used in training are hard to read, terms go undefined, clear writing takes time, and writers face several audiences at once. Staff psychologists write at the same level as trainees.

Teach accessible writing directly, use readable model reports, and have trainees check their own reports.

Readers of accessible versions scored 6 to 22 percentage points higher in every study that compared the two formats. READER organizes report-writing practices discussed in this review (Hite, 2026d). One service that retrained its clinicians produced shorter, easier-to-read reports.

Write following READER, keep scores in an appendix for technical readers, and judge AI-assisted drafts by whether readers understand them. (Hite, 2026d).
