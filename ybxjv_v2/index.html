Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Employees often respond to recurring workplace cues as if those cues carry the meaning of prior experience. A performance review, complaint channel, supervisor phrase, meeting routine, or organizational space may be appraised differently across employees even when the formal cue is the same. Building on established accounts of learned appraisal, this article develops a theory of cue-appraisal learning in organizations. The theory proposes that recurring organizational cues become psychologically consequential when employees repeatedly encounter them in episodes appraised in similar, concern-relevant ways. Over time, employees may develop conditioned appraisal meanings: learned relations between recurring organizational cues and specific appraisal meanings that become more likely to be activated when those cues are encountered again. This cue-level perspective explains how prior organizational experience can shape appraisal activation before a current event has fully unfolded. It also clarifies how learned cue meanings may influence workplace emotion and action tendencies through appraisal processes that remain sensitive to situational, regulatory, and behavioral constraints. The theory specifies how appraisal histories shape the cue similarities and differences governing transfer and how counter-experiences selectively revise the meanings they contradict. The article defines conditioned appraisal meaning, distinguishes it from adjacent constructs, develops propositions concerning acquisition, appraisal specificity, generalization, discrimination, revision, and downstream response, and outlines implications for research and practice.

Cue-Appraisal Learning in Organizations: A Theory of Conditioned Appraisal Meaning

Workplace emotions rarely arise from isolated events alone. Employees often respond to a performance review, supervisor email, complaint procedure, meeting room, team ritual, or managerial phrase as if the present episode carries the weight of prior experience. Event-based theories of workplace affect emphasize that affective reactions unfold in relation to work events and their consequences for employees, while appraisal theories emphasize that emotion depends on how events are evaluated in relation to goals, concerns, norms, and coping possibilities (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 2001; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). A supervisor's request for a "quick conversation" may be appraised as routine by one employee and as threatening by another. A complaint channel may be appraised as a genuine avenue for repair by some employees and as futile by others. A performance review may be appraised as developmental, humiliating, fair, arbitrary, or dangerous before the review has fully unfolded.

These differences point to an intermediate level of analysis in organizational emotion and appraisal. Current event accounts explain how workplace events become consequential for employees and organizations (Morgeson et al., 2015; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Appraisal theories explain how individuals evaluate events in relation to goals, concerns, norms, and coping possibilities (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 2001; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). Broad organizational constructs such as trust, justice, psychological safety, climate, and job attitudes explain generalized evaluations of targets, relationships, or contexts (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Dirks & de Jong, 2022; Edmondson, 1999; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Mayer et al., 1995; Schneider et al., 2013). Yet employees also respond to recurring cues that have become linked with particular appraisal meanings through prior experience. Cue-appraisal learning focuses on this intermediate level: the learned relation between a recurring organizational cue and a differentiated appraisal meaning for a particular employee.

Appraisal theory recognizes that stimuli can activate previously learned appraisal outputs, including differentiated appraisal patterns (Moors et al., 2013). Building on this foundation, cueappraisal learning explains how recurring organizational cues acquire appraisal meanings through workplace experience and how organizational arrangements shape their subsequent activation, transfer, and revision. Through cue-appraisal learning, an employee may develop a conditioned appraisal meaning: a learned relation between a recurring organizational cue and a specific appraisal meaning that becomes more likely to be activated when the cue is encountered again. The cue may become linked with appraisals of threat, degraded dignity, injustice, exclusion, accountability, anticipated safety, controllability, low repair possibility, or repairability. This focus on differentiated appraisal content builds on appraisal theory's core premise that emotions are shaped by meaning evaluations rather than by stimulus exposure or valence alone (Moors et al., 2013; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2001; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985). The theory's unit of analysis is the relation among employee, recurring cue, and appraisal content. This unit complements broader theories of attitudes, trust, justice, psychological safety, climate, and event appraisal by specifying a cue-level process through which organizational experience becomes available in later encounters (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Dirks & de Jong, 2022; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Schneider et al., 2013; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). An employee may generally trust a supervisor but still appraise performance reviews with that supervisor as threatening or dignity-degrading because of prior review episodes. Another employee may report psychological safety in a team but still appraise a particular meeting routine as exposing them to social devaluation. An employee may hold a favorable attitude toward the organization but appraise its complaint channel as low in repair possibility because prior attempts to use it were ignored or punished. Such examples are consistent with person-situation approaches, which emphasize how responses depend on individual histories and situational features, and with social information processing and sensemaking perspectives, which emphasize the interpretive role of social and organizational cues (Mischel & Shoda, 1995; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick et al., 2005).

Organizational life is structured by recognizable events, routines, practices, and repeated interactions (Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Morgeson et al., 2015). Employees interpret these features through social information and sensemaking processes, especially when situations are ambiguous or consequential (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick, 1995; Weick et al., 2005). Cueappraisal learning links these observations to appraisal theory by asking how recurring organizational features become connected with appraisal meanings across repeated encounters. This article makes three connected contributions. First, it specifies the employee-cueappraisal relation as a focal unit for explaining how organizational experience carries across episodes. This perspective connects employees' histories with recurring cues to appraisal activation in later encounters and identifies variation that broad organizational evaluations may leave unresolved. Second, it develops an account of organizational generalization and discrimination in which employees' appraisal histories shape which similarities and differences between cues govern the transfer of learned meanings. Relevant features may include form, function, procedure, relational treatment, authority, and organizational context. Third, it proposes meaning-specific revision: counter-experiences may change the appraisal meanings they contradict while leaving other meanings attached to the same cue intact. Together, these contributions explain how organizational experience becomes attached to recurring cues, persists across formal change, and can be revised through experiences addressing its specific appraisal content.

The article proceeds as follows. First, it defines organizational cues, cue-appraisal learning, and conditioned appraisal meaning. Second, it differentiates conditioned appraisal meaning from adjacent organizational constructs. Third, it develops propositions concerning acquisition, appraisal specificity, generalization, discrimination, revision, and downstream emotional and action tendencies. Fourth, it specifies scope conditions and related processes.

Finally, it outlines empirical and practical implications.

Appraisal, Emotion, and Organizational Cues

Appraisal theory begins from the premise that emotions arise from how events are evaluated in relation to what matters to the person (Arnold, 1960; Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 2001).

Appraisals concern such issues as goal relevance, goal congruence, agency, responsibility, norm violation, coping potential, controllability, certainty, and future expectancy (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Lazarus, 1991; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2001; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). Different appraisal patterns make different emotions more likely (Frijda, 1986; Moors et al., 2013; Ortony et al., 1988). Anger, anxiety, shame, guilt, gratitude, pride, hope, and relief depend not only on what happened, but on how the event is interpreted (Lazarus, 1991; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2001).

In organizations, appraisal is shaped by social and institutional conditions, including the events employees encounter, the cues available in the work environment, and the meanings employees construct around those cues (Ashkanasy & Dorris, 2017; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Employees evaluate not only tasks and outcomes but also fairness, interpersonal risk, respect, status, accountability, and the perceived possibility of speaking up or repairing problems (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Edmondson, 1999; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023;; Tyler & Blader, 2003). A workplace event may matter because it threatens dignity, signals exclusion, implies blame, creates uncertainty, blocks control, or suggests that repair is possible. Appraisals in organizations are often moral, relational, and practical at the same time, reflecting the fact that work settings involve exchange, authority, identity, evaluation, and resource dependence (Blau, 1964; Cropanzano et al., 2001; Tyler & Blader, 2003).

Employees also bring prior experience to present encounters. Repeated interactions with a supervisor, recurring exposure to a performance system, repeated use of a complaint procedure, or repeated participation in a meeting routine can make certain appraisal meanings more accessible before the current episode is fully interpreted. This assumption is consistent with social information processing theory, which argues that employees use social cues to interpret work settings, and with sensemaking theory, which emphasizes how people construct meaning in ambiguous organizational contexts (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick, 1995; Weick et al., 2005).

An organizational cue is a recurring, recognizable, organizationally situated feature of work that employees can encounter across episodes and identify as appraisively relevant.

Organizational cues may include social actors, such as supervisors, colleagues, or teams; procedures and systems, such as performance reviews, complaint channels, promotion procedures, or scheduling systems; physical or symbolic settings, such as offices, meeting rooms, or departmental spaces; and recurring communications or routines, such as leadership messages, team rituals, feedback formats, or standard phrases. This definition draws on event-based and routine-based perspectives that treat organizational life as structured by recognizable events and repeated patterns of action (Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Morgeson et al., 2015), while sensemaking perspectives clarify how such features become interpretable for employees (Weick, 1995; Weick et al., 2005). This definition gives the theory a specific focal object. A one-time event becomes relevant to the theory when it contributes to a recognizable recurring pattern. A broad job attitude or diffuse impression of culture may provide important context, while the cue-level focus concerns features that employees can encounter again and recognize as appraisively relevant because of prior experience. This distinction is important because job attitudes and climate often operate at broader target or context levels, whereas cue-appraisal learning concerns a more specific relation between a recurring cue and appraisal content (Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Schneider et al., 2013).

Conditioned appraisal meaning is a person-specific relation between an employee and a recurring organizational cue. The psychological claim is that employees learn cue-appraisal relations through their histories with organizational cues. This person-specific formulation is consistent with person-situation approaches in which the psychological meaning of a situation depends on the person's history, goals, and interpretive patterns (Mischel & Shoda, 1995). The cue matters because it can activate a learned appraisal meaning for that employee.

Cue-Appraisal Learning and Conditioned Appraisal Meaning

Cue-appraisal learning refers to the process through which recurring organizational cues become linked, for an employee, with specific appraisal meanings through repeated experience.

A conditioned appraisal meaning is the learned relation that results when a cue becomes more likely to activate a particular appraisal meaning in later encounters.

Distinguishing the learned cue-appraisal relation from its activation in a particular encounter allows the theory to examine how prior experience shapes present appraisal. The learned relation is the object whose acquisition, generalization, boundedness, and revision the theory seeks to explain; current appraisal activation is its proposed consequence when the cue is encountered again.

The construct has three defining features.

First, conditioned appraisal meaning is learned through repeated experience. A cue becomes linked with an appraisal meaning because the employee has encountered that cue across episodes in which similar appraisal meanings were activated. Evaluative-conditioning research provides one adjacent learning tradition by showing how repeated pairings can alter stimulus valence, although the present theory treats organizational cue learning as broader than valence conditioning alone (De Houwer, 2007; De Houwer et al., 2001; Hofmann et al., 2010). A complaint channel may become linked with low repair possibility if repeated attempts to use it were appraised as ignored, delayed, punished, or symbolically empty. A performance review format may become linked with accountability if repeated review episodes were appraised as specific, respectful, and improvement-oriented.

Second, conditioned appraisal meaning is cue-specific. The theory concerns learned meanings attached to recognizable organizational cues. An employee may appraise one procedure as fair and another as arbitrary, one supervisor routine as safe and another as threatening, one meeting format as developmental and another as dignity-degrading. Cue specificity allows the theory to explain variation that may be missed by broader evaluations alone. This focus complements research on job attitudes and climate, which has emphasized broader evaluative orientations and shared perceptions of work environments (Judge et al., 2017; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Schneider et al., 2013).

Third, conditioned appraisal meaning is appraisal-specific rather than merely valenced.

The cue may become linked with differentiated appraisal content: anticipated harm, degraded dignity, norm violation, low belonging, perceived responsibility, low control, low repair possibility, or repairability. These meanings matter because different appraisal meanings support different emotional and action tendencies (Frijda, 1986; Lazarus, 1991; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2001; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). This appraisal-specificity claim distinguishes cue-appraisal learning from accounts centered primarily on liking, disliking, or generalized evaluation.

The term "conditioned" is used in a broad cue-appraisal learning sense. In organizations, employees learn through social information, repeated interaction, and sensemaking processes (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick et al., 2005). The conditioning claim is therefore limited and pragmatic: repeated pairings between cues and appraisal patterns can make those appraisal meanings more likely to be activated when the cue appears again. This framing is compatible with evaluative-conditioning research on repeated cue-valence pairings while preserving the interpretive and social complexity of organizational settings (De Houwer, 2007; De Houwer et al., 2001; Hofmann et al., 2010; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick et al., 2005).

At this level, conditioning denotes an experience-dependent cue-appraisal relation; identifying the cognitive processes through which that relation is acquired remains an empirical question.

Cue-appraisal learning is related to evaluative conditioning. Evaluative conditioning typically concerns changes in the valence of a stimulus through repeated pairings (De Houwer, 2007; De Houwer et al., 2001; Hofmann et al., 2010). The present theory also concerns repeated cue-meaning pairings, but it focuses on differentiated appraisal meanings rather than valence alone and locates those meanings in recurring organizational cues. Valence change may occur, while appraisal content remains the central explanatory object.

The process logic distinguishes four influences on acquisition: recurrence, consistency, event strength, and concern relevance. Ambiguity in a later encounter may increase the relative influence of the learned meaning on current appraisal. Recurrence allows employees to recognize a cue across episodes, consistent with organizational research on recurring routines and event sequences (Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Morgeson et al., 2015). Consistency stabilizes the learned cue-appraisal relation because repeated cue-meaning pairings are more likely to become psychologically available when the cue is encountered again (De Houwer, 2007; De Houwer et al., 2001; Higgins, 1996; Hofmann et al., 2010). Event strength increases the likelihood that a cue-bearing episode will become consequential because stronger events are more novel, disruptive, or critical (Morgeson et al., 2015). Concern relevance increases the likelihood that the cue will acquire appraisal significance because appraisal theories treat relevance to goals, concerns, coping possibilities, and normative significance as central to emotion generation (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 2001; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). Ambiguity in the current encounter gives learned cue meanings greater room to shape appraisal because ambiguous organizational situations require interpretation and sensemaking before their meaning is settled (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick, 1995; Weick et al., 2005). Together, these conditions explain why some recurring cues become appraisively powerful while others remain psychologically weak.

Differentiating Conditioned Appraisal Meaning from Adjacent Constructs

Conditioned appraisal meaning is close to several established organizational constructs.

Its value lies in specifying an intermediate process: below broad organizational perceptions, but above isolated event appraisal.

Job Attitudes

Job attitudes concern evaluative orientations toward objects such as the job, organization, supervisor, or work role. They capture relatively broad affective and cognitive evaluations and have long been central to organizational psychology (Judge et al., 2017; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024). Conditioned appraisal meaning is narrower. It concerns the learned tendency for a specific recurring cue to activate particular appraisal meanings for an employee. A broad attitude toward a supervisor may be positive, while a particular feedback ritual associated with that supervisor may activate appraisals of degraded dignity or threat.

The distinction is one of level and content. Attitudes are often experience-based (Judge et al., 2017; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024). Conditioned appraisal meaning specifies cue-level appraisal content by asking not only whether the target is evaluated positively or negatively, but what a recurring cue has come to activate in appraisal terms.

Trust

Trust involves willingness to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations about another party's intentions or behavior (Mayer et al., 1995). Trust has become a core construct in organizational psychology and organizational behavior, with research examining trust in leaders, coworkers, teams, and organizations (Dirks & de Jong, 2022). A cue-appraisal perspective complements trust research by specifying how particular procedures, routines, spaces, or communicative forms may activate appraisal meanings such as degraded dignity, accountability, exclusion, low repair possibility, or repairability. This helps explain why a generally trusted actor may still be associated with negative appraisals in particular recurring situations.

Organizational Justice

Justice research examines perceived fairness in distributive, procedural, interpersonal, and informational terms (Colquitt, 2001; Colquitt et al., 2001). Justice and fairness are especially salient in organizational life because employees evaluate whether decision procedures, interpersonal treatment, information, and outcomes adhere to standards of appropriateness (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Cropanzano et al., 2001). Many conditioned appraisal meanings involve fairness or injustice. Appraisal theories also emphasize responsibility, agency, and norm compatibility as appraisal dimensions relevant to emotion (Ortony et al., 1988; Scherer, 2001; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). A cue-appraisal perspective complements justice research by showing how recurring cues may become linked with justice-related meanings before the current procedure or interaction is fully evaluated. It also includes related moral, relational, and coping meanings, such as degraded dignity, exclusion, accountability, controllability, and repairability.

Psychological Safety

Psychological safety concerns beliefs about whether interpersonal risk-taking is safe in a particular context (Edmondson, 1999). The literature links psychological safety to learning, voice, performance, and work experience across contexts (Edmondson & Bransby, 2023).

Psychological safety overlaps with cue-appraisal learning when cues are learned as safe or unsafe. The cue-level perspective adds specificity by asking how particular recurring cues become linked with appraisals such as anticipated safety, threat, repairability, or punishment. A team may generally be experienced as safe, while a particular meeting routine may still activate appraisals of status exposure, exclusion, or interpersonal risk for a particular employee.

Psychological Contracts

Psychological contracts concern employees' beliefs about obligations and promises in the employment relationship (Rousseau, 1989 (Rousseau, , 1995)). Psychological contract research has examined breach, violation, exchange, reciprocity, and the employee-organization relationship (Coyle-Shapiro et al., 2019; Morrison & Robinson, 1997; Rousseau, 1995). These ideas are relevant when recurring cues signal fulfillment, breach, betrayal, or repair. Cue-appraisal learning complements this literature by specifying how particular cues associated with obligations or promises may later activate appraisals of betrayal, accountability, or low repair possibility.

Organizational Climate

Climate refers to shared perceptions of policies, practices, and procedures, whereas culture is often defined in terms of deeper assumptions and values (Schneider et al., 2013).

Climate research is especially relevant because it conceptualizes shared perceptions of work environments and practices (Schneider et al., 2013). Conditioned appraisal meaning is primarily individual-level and cue-specific. Multiple employees may develop similar cue-specific appraisal meanings when they share repeated exposure to the same organizational cues. The present theory focuses on individual employees' learned cue-appraisal relations, while shared cue meanings represent a possible extension.

Affective Events Theory

Affective Events Theory explains how workplace events generate affective reactions that influence attitudes and behavior (Weiss & Beal, 2005; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Workplace affect research has continued to emphasize the importance of discrete events, affective dynamics, and emotion processes at multiple levels of analysis (Ashkanasy & Dorris, 2017; Elfenbein, 2023; Fisher, 2000). Cue-appraisal learning adds a cue-history layer to this event-based account.

It proposes that events are appraised partly through recurring cues that activate learned appraisal meanings from prior episodes. In this sense, the theory helps explain why similar events can evoke different emotional reactions across employees or across repeated encounters.

Appraisal Meaning Families

Conditioned appraisal meanings can be organized into broad families reflecting what recurring organizational cues have come to signify for employees. Appraisal theories identify dimensions such as goal relevance, goal congruence, certainty, agency, responsibility, coping potential, and normative significance (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Lazarus, 1991; Moors et al., 2013; Scherer, 2001; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985). The present theory draws on these dimensions to organize differentiated meanings learned through workplace experience.

The following families form an open, non-exhaustive set of overlapping organizing categories. A single cue may activate meanings from several families, and additional families may become relevant across occupations, organizational contexts, concerns, and learning histories. Their boundaries remain open to empirical refinement. Further families can be developed where they identify coherent appraisal content that is insufficiently captured by the categories presented here and generate distinguishable predictions about later appraisal activation.

Harm and Threat Meanings

Some recurring cues become linked with anticipated harm, vulnerability, danger, punishment, safety, or protection. Appraisal theories connect threat-related emotional responses to evaluations of personally significant adverse possibilities and the resources available to address them (Lazarus, 1991; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2001). A supervisor's sudden meeting request may activate threat when similar requests previously preceded punitive treatment. A complaint channel may signal exposure to retaliation, while a confidential reporting arrangement may come to signal protection.

The learned meaning concerns what the cue predicts about possible injury or safety.

Anxiety, vigilance, avoidance, and defensive preparation are potential downstream responses whose occurrence depends on the broader appraisal pattern and current context (Frijda, 1986; Lazarus, 1991). Employees may therefore recognize a cue as threatening without expressing fear or enacting avoidance.

Moral and Accountability Meanings

Recurring cues may acquire meanings concerning fairness, responsibility, blame, legitimacy, betrayal, or meaningful answerability. These meanings draw on appraisal dimensions of agency and normative significance and on organizational justice research concerning outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment, and information (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Cropanzano et al., 2001; Ortony et al., 1988; Scherer, 2001).

A performance system may signal arbitrary punishment or proportionate accountability.

A leadership message may signal bad faith when prior assurances were repeatedly disconnected from action, or credible responsibility when acknowledgement was followed by correction. The relevant appraisal content concerns how conduct is evaluated, whether responsibility is recognized, and whether consequences are justified. Anger, resentment, gratitude, or respect may follow, depending on the particular configuration of meanings activated.

Belonging and Dignity Meanings

Organizational cues may become linked with inclusion, exclusion, respect, humiliation, recognition, or social devaluation. Relational justice, group engagement, and psychological safety research provide foundations for understanding why interpersonal treatment and opportunities for participation matter to employees' experience of work (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Edmondson, 1999; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Tyler & Blader, 2003).

A meeting routine may signal that particular voices will be dismissed. A feedback format may signal public exposure and damage to standing, while a private and respectful format may signal that dignity and participation will be protected. These meanings concern the employee's anticipated relational position and treatment. They may overlap with threat or injustice while retaining a specific focus on recognition, membership, and social standing.

Self-Accountability and Self-Repair Meanings

Recurring organizational cues may become linked with appraisals of personal responsibility, performance, conduct, or compatibility with personal and professional standards.

Feedback, evaluation, mistakes, and requests for explanation may acquire different meanings depending on employees' prior experiences. A review may signal an opportunity to assess and improve one's work, or an occasion on which personal shortcomings are emphasized. These meanings draw on appraisal dimensions of self-agency, responsibility, normative significance, and coping potential (Lazarus, 1991; Ortony et al., 1988; Scherer, 2001).

Self-repair meanings concern whether employees perceive opportunities to adjust their conduct, improve performance, address consequences, or respond constructively to an identified discrepancy. Research on self-conscious emotions provides a foundation for distinguishing evaluations of particular actions from broader self-evaluations and for examining their implications for response (Tangney et al., 2007). At the level of the present theory, the focus remains on which self-relevant appraisal meanings recurring cues acquire and activate. Specific emotional and behavioral pathways require further theoretical specification and empirical examination.

Coping, Movement, and Repairability Meanings

Recurring organizational cues may become linked with appraisals of control, available resources, action possibilities, expected effectiveness, or the potential for progress and improvement. Appraisal theory identifies coping potential and future expectancy as important components of how situations become emotionally meaningful (Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 2001).

A supervisor's invitation to discuss concerns may signal access to assistance, while a complaint procedure may signal responsiveness, limited influence, or anticipated non-response.

These meanings may concern employees' own capabilities, the resources available to them, or the likelihood that relevant people and organizational processes will respond. Bandura's (1977) distinction between efficacy expectations and outcome expectations clarifies why confidence in performing an action can coexist with doubts about its consequences. Movement refers broadly to perceived possibilities for making progress, changing circumstances, or adjusting one's actions in relation to valued concerns. This conceptualization draws on goalregulation perspectives emphasizing progress toward goals and the adjustment of behavior in relation to discrepancies between current and desired conditions (Carver & Scheier, 1990).

Repairability concerns the perceived possibility of addressing problems or improving situations.

Together, these meanings connect to organizational research on whether speaking up appears safe, worthwhile, and effective (Morrison, 2014 (Morrison, , 2023)). The present theory examines how such appraisals become associated with recurring cues and influence later encounters.

Uncertainty and Predictability Meanings

Recurring cues may become linked with certainty, predictability, ambiguity, instability, or shifting expectations. Certainty and expectancy are established appraisal dimensions that contribute to the differentiation of emotional experience (Moors et al., 2013; Scherer, 2001; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985).

A scheduling message may signal reliable expectations when previous schedules were consistently honored, or unpredictability when changes repeatedly occurred without notice. A performance review may signal clear standards or shifting criteria. These meanings concern what employees expect to know about an encounter's course, requirements, and consequences.

Predictability is distinguishable from favorability: an employee may anticipate an unfavorable but predictable outcome, or a potentially favorable outcome whose conditions remain unclear.

Opportunity and Gain Meanings

Recurring cues may become linked with anticipated benefit, advancement, development, discovery, or access to valued possibilities. These meanings draw on goal relevance, goal congruence, and future expectancy, together with perspectives emphasizing pathways toward desired outcomes (Lazarus, 1991; Moors et al., 2013; Snyder, 2002).

A project invitation may signal a credible opportunity to learn, a mentoring conversation may signal access to future roles, or a recognition practice may signal that valued contributions will produce meaningful rewards. Opportunity meanings can concern the extension of an already satisfactory situation as well as improvement following difficulty. They therefore include prospective gains beyond the correction of unwanted work states. Their focal content is what a recurring cue predicts about valued possibilities, rather than whether the employee currently experiences positive emotion.

Appraisal Configurations Across Families

The families allow more specific configurations of conditioned appraisal meaning.

Appraisal theories relate emotional differentiation to patterns of appraisal involving dimensions such as goal congruence, agency, certainty, coping potential, and normative significance (Moors et al., 2013; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2001). The present framework applies this configurational logic to learned organizational cue meanings.

A recurring cue may acquire several appraisal meanings through the same history of encounters. A complaint procedure may become associated with anticipated harm, unfair treatment, and limited effectiveness. A feedback conversation may combine meanings concerning personal responsibility, dignity, and opportunities for improvement. A development discussion may activate meanings concerning valued opportunities, uncertainty, and possible progress. These combinations illustrate how different appraisal families can intersect around a particular employee-cue relation.

Such intersections provide a basis for more focused theoretical development.

Configurations involving the conduct of others, one's own accountability, or possibilities for changing work circumstances may warrant distinct explanations of their development and consequences. Their elaboration requires further specification of focal constructs, relationships, boundary conditions, and empirical predictions. The present framework identifies the general learning process through which differentiated appraisal content can become organized around recurring cues.

Across configurations, learned meaning, current appraisal, emotion, action orientation, and enacted behavior remain analytically distinct. A learned configuration may influence which appraisals become accessible in a later encounter, while emotional and behavioral consequences depend on the broader appraisal pattern and current circumstances. Goals, resources, power, norms, and regulation may further shape whether an action tendency becomes behavior (Frijda, 1986; Lazarus, 1991; Moors et al., 2013).

The Process of Cue-Appraisal Learning Cue-appraisal learning begins when a recurring organizational cue appears in episodes that employees appraise in similar ways. The cue becomes linked, for the employee, with those appraisal meanings because it is repeatedly present when the employee evaluates the situation. The more consistent, salient, and concern-relevant the appraisal pattern, the more likely the employee is to develop a conditioned appraisal meaning for that cue. This logic draws from appraisal theory's emphasis on concern relevance, event-system theory's emphasis on event strength, and evaluative-conditioning research's emphasis on repeated cue-valence pairings as an adjacent learning model (De Houwer, 2007; Morgeson et al., 2015; Scherer, 2001).

Consider a complaint channel. If an employee repeatedly uses the channel and appraises the episodes as delayed, ignored, risky, or symbolically empty, the channel may become linked with low repair possibility or threat. Later, even before the new complaint process unfolds, the cue may activate appraisals of futility or risk. If repeated use leads to timely acknowledgement, meaningful follow-through, and non-retaliation, the same category of cue may become linked with repairability or accountability. This example connects appraisal theory's coping-potential logic with voice and silence research, which emphasizes perceived safety, efficacy, and impact as important influences on whether employees speak up (Lazarus, 1991; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023;; Scherer, 2001).

Consider also a performance review. If review meetings repeatedly involve vague criticism, public comparison, or status exposure, the review format may become linked with appraisals of degraded dignity or threat. If review meetings repeatedly involve specific feedback, respectful treatment, and feasible improvement steps, the review format may become linked with accountability or development. The formal cue may be similar across employees, but its learned appraisal meaning differs because employees have different cue histories. This example is consistent with research showing that performance appraisal is interpreted within a social context, and with social information processing theory's broader claim that employees use workplace cues to interpret their environments (Levy & Williams, 2004; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978).

Propositions 3 and 4 explain generalization and discrimination through perceived similarities and differences in cue features relevant to employees' prior appraisal experience. Acquisition Under Recurrence, Consistency, and Salience

Proposition 1: Cue-appraisal learning is stronger when recurring organizational cues are repeatedly embedded in salient, concern-relevant episodes that employees appraise consistently across time.

This proposition identifies the conditions under which conditioned appraisal meanings are most likely to develop. Repetition becomes appraisively consequential when recurring cues are embedded in episodes that matter to the employee. Cues are especially likely to become linked with appraisal meanings when they recur in episodes involving dignity, justice, safety, belonging, control, accountability, or repair. This emphasis on concern relevance follows appraisal theory, while the emphasis on salience and event strength is consistent with eventsystem theory (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Lazarus, 1991; Morgeson et al., 2015; Scherer, 2001; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025).

The proposition is discriminating. A frequently repeated but trivial cue should produce weak cue-appraisal learning. A highly salient but one-time event may become memorable, but it contributes to conditioned cue meaning most clearly when it becomes part of a recurring pattern.

A recurring cue that is appraised inconsistently may activate ambivalent or unstable meaning rather than a clear conditioned appraisal meaning. Cue-appraisal learning should be strongest when recurrence, appraisal consistency, salience, and concern relevance converge.

Appraisal Specificity Beyond Valence

Proposition 2: Among recurring organizational cues with comparable overall valence, differences in prior cue-specific appraisal histories predict corresponding differences in the appraisal content activated during subsequent encounters.

This proposition extends appraisal theory's distinction between valence and differentiated appraisal content to employees' histories with recurring organizational cues (Moors et al., 2013; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2001; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985). It predicts correspondence between the content of prior cue-specific experience and later appraisal activation. Equally disliked complaint channels may activate anticipated punishment, low repair possibility, or norm violation depending on employees' histories with them. Measuring these histories therefore allows the theory to explain which meaning a cue activates, beyond whether the cue is evaluated positively or negatively.

A cue can therefore be negatively evaluated in more than one way. A complaint channel may be disliked because it is appraised as risky, because it is appraised as useless, or because it is appraised as morally illegitimate. These meanings have different implications. Threat may support vigilance or avoidance (Umbra & Fasbender, 2025); norm violation may support anger or protest; low repair possibility may support disengagement; degraded dignity may support shame, anger, withdrawal, or defensive action depending on context. Similarly, a positively evaluated cue may activate safety, accountability, belonging, or repairability, which also have different implications.

Appraisal specificity provides one empirical requirement for the theory: differentiated cue histories should predict subsequent appraisal activation beyond general cue valence and broader organizational perceptions. The organizational transfer and revision propositions introduce further requirements concerning which perceived cue similarities and differences govern transfer and which counter-experiences revise particular appraisal meanings.

Generalization Through Perceived Organizational Similarity

Proposition 3: Conditioned appraisal meanings are more likely to generalize from one organizational cue to another when employees perceive similarities between the cues in features relevant to their prior appraisal experience, including form, function, procedure, relational treatment, authority, or organizational context.

Learned cue meanings may spread. An employee who has repeatedly appraised one complaint channel as low in repair possibility may appraise a new reporting system similarly if it appears to operate under the same authority, uses similar procedures, or seems embedded in the same organizational logic. A worker who has repeatedly appraised one supervisor's "informal check-ins" as punitive may approach similar check-ins with another supervisor defensively.

In organizations, perceived similarity is not only a matter of surface resemblance. Cues may generalize because employees perceive the same authority behind different procedures, the same accountability structure beneath different labels, the same power relation across different interactions, the same history of non-response across different channels, or the same evaluative logic across different performance systems. This logic draws on social information processing and sensemaking perspectives, which emphasize that employees interpret current situations through cues, prior meanings, and perceived patterns of organizational action (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick et al., 2005). Thus, a new cue may activate an old appraisal meaning when it appears to reproduce an appraisively meaningful organizational pattern.

Discrimination and Boundedness

Proposition 4: Conditioned appraisal meanings are less likely to generalize when employees recognize differences between the current cue and previously encountered cues in features relevant to their prior appraisal experience, allowing the learned meaning to remain bounded to particular actors, procedures, settings, or circumstances.

Discrimination limits generalization (Bouton, 2004; Craske et al., 2014). Employees may recognize that a new supervisor, procedure, space, or routine differs from prior cues in ways that matter. A new complaint channel may be appraised differently if it is visibly independent, timely, confidential, or backed by credible follow-through. A different feedback process may elicit less transfer of threat meaning when employees recognize that it is private, specific, respectful, and linked to improvement rather than punishment.

The key issue is whether the employee perceives the difference as appraisively meaningful. Cosmetic change may leave conditioned appraisal meaning intact if the cue still appears to enact the same organizational logic. Conversely, even modest changes may matter if they make a cue discriminable in relation to dignity, justice, safety, control, accountability, or repair. This proposition draws on the idea that interpretation depends on perceived meaning, not only formal features, a premise shared by sensemaking and appraisal perspectives (Scherer, 2001; Weick et al., 2005). Discrimination and revision concern different changes. Discrimination limits the transfer of a learned meaning to a distinguishable cue or context, while revision changes the meaning associated with a previously encountered cue. Learning that another supervisor's feedback is different may therefore bound generalization without changing the meaning attached to the original supervisor's feedback.

The features governing transfer should depend on the appraisal history attached to the cue. Privacy may distinguish a feedback encounter previously associated with public humiliation, while investigative independence may distinguish a complaint channel previously associated with futility. Similar wording, interactional style, physical setting, or procedural function may also support generalization when employees connect those features to prior appraisal experience. The theory therefore directs attention to which cue features carry which learned meanings.

Revision Through Credible Counter-Experience

Proposition 5: Conditioned appraisal meanings are more likely to be revised when repeated credible counter-experiences contradict their specific appraisal content than when equally favorable experiences leave that content unchallenged.

Cue meanings can be revised through repeated experience, consistent with learning perspectives on updated cue associations (Chua & Sündermann, 2023; De Houwer, 2007; Engelhard et al., 2014; Schweckendiek et al., 2013). If a cue has become linked with low repair possibility, threat, or degraded dignity through repeated experience, relearning is more likely when counter-experiences are repeated, credible, and appraisively relevant.

Credibility matters because employees interpret new episodes through prior cue meanings. A manager's statement that "this process is safe now" may have limited impact if prior experience has linked the process with retaliation. Counter-experience becomes more powerful when it is appraised as a real change in what the cue predicts or makes possible, a point consistent with sensemaking perspectives on interpreted organizational meaning (Weick, 1995).

For example, a complaint process previously appraised as low in repair possibility may begin to activate repairability if employees repeatedly see concerns acknowledged, investigated, and addressed without retaliation. This logic connects with psychological safety and voice research, which emphasizes whether speaking up is safe, effective, and supported in practice (Edmondson, 1999; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023)).

Revision may therefore be selective within the same cue. Respectful treatment may weaken anticipated humiliation while leaving low repair possibility intact if concerns still receive no consequential response. Effective problem resolution may increase perceived repairability while leaving anticipated interpersonal disrespect unchanged. A generally improved experience need not revise every appraisal meaning attached to the cue.

Conditional Downstream Emotional and Action Tendencies

Proposition 6: Conditioned appraisal meanings shape emotional experiences and action tendencies indirectly by increasing the accessibility and likelihood of particular appraisals in the current encounter; actual behavior depends further on situational constraints, regulation, resources, and competing goals.

This proposition connects cue-appraisal learning to workplace emotion and behavior while preserving the theory's scope. The theory's strongest prediction concerns later appraisal activation. Emotional experiences and action tendencies are downstream and conditional, consistent with appraisal theories that distinguish appraisal, feeling, action tendency, expression, and regulation (Frijda, 1986; Gross, 1998; Scherer, 2001; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). A cue that activates threat may make anxiety, vigilance, or avoidance more likely. A cue that activates norm violation or blame may make anger or protest more likely. A cue that activates low repair possibility may make silence or withdrawal more likely. A cue that activates repairability may make voice or corrective action more likely.

Actual behavior depends on additional conditions. Current event features may confirm, weaken, or override the learned cue meaning. Power relations may constrain expression. Norms and perceived control may shape whether tendencies become behavior. Available resources may determine whether action seems possible (Ajzen, 1991; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023)). Thus, conditioned appraisal meaning shapes the appraisal conditions under which emotions and action tendencies become more likely.

Scope Conditions

The theory applies most strongly under five conditions. First, the cue must be recurring and recognizable. The theory is most directly concerned with cues that employees encounter across episodes, consistent with organizational routines and event perspectives that view organizational life as structured by repeated patterns of action and event sequences (Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Morgeson et al., 2015).

Second, the cue must be embedded in appraisal-relevant episodes. Repetition becomes theoretically important when the cue appears in episodes that matter for goals, dignity, justice, safety, belonging, control, accountability, or repair. This condition follows appraisal theory's emphasis on concern relevance and coping significance (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003; Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 2001).

Third, appraisal patterns must show some consistency. If prior encounters with the cue have been highly inconsistent, the cue may activate ambivalent or weak meaning rather than a clear conditioned appraisal meaning. This condition follows from the theory's cue-appraisal learning logic and from evaluative-conditioning research emphasizing the importance of repeated cue pairings for learned stimulus evaluation (De Houwer, 2007; Hofmann et al., 2010).

Fourth, the theory proposes that ambiguity or interpretive openness increases the relative influence of learned cue meanings. Sensemaking perspectives emphasize the importance of interpretation when situations are ambiguous (Weick, 1995; Weick et al., 2005). Clear current information may constrain or override a learned meaning, but the theory allows that cuetriggered appraisal activation can occur before such information is fully processed.

Fifth, the cue must be appraisively connected to the current episode. Learned cue meaning should have greater influence when the current situation is meaningfully related to the cue's prior appraisal history. This condition is consistent with person-situation approaches that emphasize the fit between situational features and personal meaning structures (Mischel & Shoda, 1995).

These scope conditions focus the theory on recognizable organizational cues with repeated appraisal histories. The theory applies most directly when employees respond to such cues in ways that reflect prior cue-specific experience.

Boundary Conditions and Related Processes

Cue-appraisal learning is one source of workplace emotion and action tendencies.

Current events, dispositions, goals, social norms, broader attitudes, and collective climates also shape appraisal and response (Ajzen, 1991; Ashkanasy & Dorris, 2017; Elfenbein, 2023; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Schneider et al., 2013). The theory explains how recurring cues can activate learned appraisal meanings for employees and thereby shape the likelihood of particular appraisals.

The theory is most useful when employees respond to recognizable organizational cues with histories of repeated appraisal. Emotions that arise primarily from novel events, immediate outcomes, dispositional tendencies, or broad relational conditions may be better explained by other approaches. This boundary helps clarify how cue-appraisal learning complements existing theories of workplace affect and organizational behavior.

Empirical Implications

The theory's central empirical demand is that researchers distinguish three elements: prior cue-specific history, current appraisal activation, and downstream response. This distinction follows from appraisal theory's separation of appraisal processes from emotional experience and action tendencies, and from Affective Events Theory's separation of work events, affective reactions, attitudes, and behavior (Scherer, 2001; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Because the theory concerns repeated cue encounters and later activation, longitudinal, diary, experiencesampling, and repeated-measures designs are especially relevant (Gabriel et al., 2019; Ohly et al., 2010).

Prior Cue-Specific History

Prior cue-specific history refers to how an employee has appraised previous encounters with a recurring organizational cue. For example, researchers might assess whether prior encounters with a complaint channel were appraised as low in repair possibility, risky, accountable, or responsive. They might assess whether prior performance reviews were appraised as dignity-degrading, arbitrary, developmental, or accountable. This measurement should be cue-specific. It is not enough to ask whether employees trust management, feel psychologically safe, or perceive general fairness, because those constructs operate at broader target or context levels (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Dirks & de Jong, 2022; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023). The theory requires attention to the employee's history with a particular recurring cue. Because prior cue-specific history may be vulnerable to retrospective reconstruction, longitudinal, diary, experience-sampling, or repeated-measures designs are especially important (Gabriel et al., 2019; Ohly et al., 2010).

Current Appraisal Activation

Current appraisal activation refers to the appraisal meaning activated when the employee encounters the cue again. This should be measured separately from prior history where possible.

For instance, after exposure to a complaint form, review meeting invitation, or supervisor feedback cue, researchers could assess whether the cue currently activates appraisals of threat, low repair possibility, accountability, degraded dignity, or repairability. This distinction prevents tautology. A study that only shows that current appraisals predict current emotions would replicate appraisal theory (Lazarus, 1991). A stronger test would show that prior cue-specific history predicts current appraisal activation. This logic is consistent with appraisal theory and Affective Events Theory, and with methodological work on diary and experience-sampling designs that can separate events, appraisals, affective reactions, and downstream outcomes over time (Gabriel et al., 2019; Ohly et al., 2010; Scherer, 2001; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Downstream Response

Downstream responses include emotional experience, emotional expression, action tendencies, and actual behavior. These response elements should not be collapsed, because appraisal theories distinguish appraisal, emotion, action tendency, and behavior (Frijda, 1986; Scherer, 2001). The theory predicts appraisal activation most directly. Emotional experience and action tendencies are more conditional, and actual behavior depends further on resources, constraints, norms, power relations, and regulation. This distinction is consistent with appraisal theory and with organizational behavior research showing that affective and attitudinal states influence behavior through conditional processes (Frijda, 1986; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Scherer, 2001; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Illustrative Study Design

One illustrative study could examine performance reviews. Researchers could measure employees' prior appraisal history with a review format before a review cycle begins, assessing whether previous reviews were appraised as dignity-degrading, threatening, accountable, developmental, arbitrary, or repair-oriented. When employees later receive a review invitation or enter the review setting, researchers could measure current appraisal activation before the full review content unfolds. After the review, researchers could assess emotional experience, emotional expression, action tendencies, and, where possible, actual behavior. The key test would be whether prior cue-specific history predicts current appraisal activation beyond supervisor trust, justice perceptions, psychological safety, general valence toward reviews, and current review content. This design would extend event-based and appraisal-based approaches by adding cue-specific appraisal history as an antecedent of current appraisal activation (Gabriel et al., 2019; Ohly et al., 2010; Scherer, 2001; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

A complementary experiment could examine whether appraisal history changes which cue differences govern transfer. Participants could encounter equally negative review histories emphasizing either public humiliation or arbitrary decision-making. Subsequent review cues could vary privacy and decision transparency independently. The theory predicts that privacy should reduce transfer of humiliation-related meaning more strongly, whereas transparency should reduce transfer of arbitrariness-related meaning more strongly. A revision study could similarly compare equally favorable subsequent experiences that address different appraisal meanings. Such designs would test the predicted correspondence between learned appraisal content and later cue features or counter-experiences.

Discriminant and Incremental Tests

The theory gains support if cue-specific appraisal meanings explain later appraisals or reactions beyond general attitudes, trust, justice, psychological safety, climate, and current event features (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Dirks & de Jong, 2022; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Morgeson et al., 2015; Schneider et al., 2013; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). It also requires tests of appraisal specificity. Differentiated appraisal meanings should explain later appraisal activation better than general positive or negative valence alone. These tests assess appraisal specificity, while the transfer and revision predictions require additional comparisons involving appraisal-relevant cue features and counter-experience content.

Empirical Commitments

The theory has three empirical commitments. First, prior cue-specific appraisal histories should predict subsequent appraisal activation beyond general cue valence, broader organizational perceptions, and current event features. Prior history and current activation should be measured separately to establish the proposed temporal relation.

Second, the features governing generalization and discrimination should depend on the appraisal history attached to the cue. Research should examine whether particular similarities and differences predict transfer more strongly for some learned meanings than for others. For example, privacy may be especially consequential for histories of public humiliation, while investigative independence may be especially consequential for histories of procedural futility.

Third, counter-experiences should selectively revise the appraisal meanings they contradict. Studies could compare equally favorable experiences that address different appraisal dimensions and assess whether their effects correspond to the content of the prior cue history.

Together, these commitments make the theory's account of acquisition, transfer, and revision specific and testable.

Practical Implications

If supported empirically, the theory suggests three cue-specific implications for organizational practice.

First, a cue-appraisal perspective directs attention to cue-specific appraisal meanings rather than only broad attitudes. Employees may be responding not only to the current event or to management in general, but also to a specific routine, procedure, phrase, setting, or actor that activates a learned appraisal meaning. A complaint process may activate low repair possibility even in an organization where employees report moderate trust. A performance review may activate degraded dignity even when the supervisor is generally trusted. This implication complements research on trust, psychological safety, justice, and climate by adding a more cuespecific lens to these broader constructs (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Dirks & de Jong, 2022; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Schneider et al., 2013).

Second, the theory suggests that revision is more likely when employees encounter credible evidence addressing the particular meaning learned. Rebranding a procedure, renaming a meeting, or announcing a policy may have limited impact when employees perceive continuity with prior experience. Repeated responsiveness may challenge low repair possibility, respectful treatment may challenge anticipated humiliation, and protection from retaliation may challenge threat. These predictions direct attention to the correspondence between prior appraisal content and subsequent evidence. Communication may contribute when employees regard it as credible and relevant, particularly when supported by enacted organizational change (Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023;; Weick et al., 2005).

Third, practitioners may need to make new cues appraisively discriminable from old cues. Repairing general trust may not repair the learned meaning of a specific complaint channel.

Improving climate may not alter a particular procedure's association with low repair possibility.

Announcing psychological safety may not change a recurring meeting cue that continues to activate status threat. A revised policy is more likely to limit transfer when employees recognize differences in features relevant to their prior appraisal experience, such as form, function, procedure, relational treatment, authority, or organizational context. This practical implication follows from the theory's discrimination proposition and from sensemaking perspectives that emphasize perceived meaning over formal change alone (Weick et al., 2005).

These implications follow from the theory's logic and require empirical validation.

Discussion

The theory of cue-appraisal learning offers a cue-level account of how workplace experience shapes later appraisal, emotion, and action tendencies. Its contribution concerns the employee-cue-appraisal relation across episodes, history-dependent generalization and discrimination, and meaning-specific revision.

First, the theory specifies a cue-level unit of explanation connecting organizational experience across episodes. Existing theories often focus either on current events or on broad organizational perceptions, including work events, job attitudes, climate, trust, justice, and psychological safety (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Dirks & de Jong, 2022; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Morgeson et al., 2015; Schneider et al., 2013; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Cue-appraisal learning occupies the space between these levels. It explains how recurring organizational cues become linked, for particular employees, with specific appraisal meanings that can shape later appraisals before a current episode has fully unfolded. This intermediate level helps explain why employees may respond differently to the same present cue despite similar formal circumstances, and why broad attitudes may fail to capture specific cue meanings. This contribution complements event-based accounts, climate research, job-attitude research, and person-situation approaches (Judge et al., 2017; Mischel & Shoda, 1995; Morgeson et al., 2015; Schneider et al., 2013; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Second, the theory develops an account of history-dependent generalization and discrimination. The same similarity or difference between organizational cues may matter differently depending on the appraisal meaning learned through prior experience. Privacy may distinguish a review associated with humiliation, while procedural independence may distinguish a channel associated with futility. This perspective explains why employees may generalize across formally different arrangements or discriminate between arrangements that appear similar.

Third, the theory proposes meaning-specific revision. Counter-experiences should revise the particular appraisal content they contradict, while other meanings attached to the cue may persist. A procedure may become more respectful without becoming more effective, or more effective without becoming less threatening. This prediction connects organizational repair to the differentiated content of employees' appraisal histories and explains why general improvement may leave particular learned meanings intact.

The theory also clarifies relations among several organizational constructs. Job attitudes, trust, justice, psychological safety, psychological contracts, and climate all capture important evaluations and perceptions of work (Colquitt & Zipay, 2015; Coyle-Shapiro et al., 2019; Dirks & de Jong, 2022; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023; Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2024; Schneider et al., 2013). Conditioned appraisal meaning identifies a more specific process by which particular cues become linked with appraisal meanings that may operate within, beneath, or alongside broader perceptions.

Together, these contributions explain how organizational experience carries forward through recurring cues and how its effects depend on the relation between prior appraisal content and subsequent encounters. The theory identifies specific comparisons through which researchers can examine which cue features govern transfer and which experiences support revision. It thereby connects organizational arrangements to the persistence and change of differentiated appraisal meanings.

Conclusion

Workplace emotions and action tendencies are shaped not only by current events but also by learned appraisal meanings attached to recurring organizational cues. A complaint channel, feedback format, supervisor interaction, meeting routine, policy message, or organizational space may become linked, for an employee, with appraisals of threat, degraded dignity, injustice, exclusion, accountability, anticipated safety, low repair possibility, or repairability through repeated experience.

Cue-appraisal learning explains how organizational experience carries forward through recurring cues, which similarities and differences govern the transfer of learned meanings, and why subsequent improvement may revise some meanings while leaving others intact. By connecting cue histories, appraisal-relevant features, and meaning-specific counter-experiences, the theory provides a focused account of persistence and change in employees' organizational appraisals.
