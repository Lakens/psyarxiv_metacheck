Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Full text

Abstract

Both forced and voluntary migration are incisive life events that prompt acculturation processes fundamental for the adaptation in the host society, including the formation of a second cultural identity.

One of the major challenges to the acculturation process is the discrimination by the host national groups. Previous research showed that it can both undermine the development of integrated bicultural identities and impact migrants' well-being in many ways. However, previous research investigating this process only relied on correlational methods. Our study tests how dynamics between discrimination, bicultural identity, well-being, and health evolve over time, hypothesizing a longitudinal effect of discrimination on bicultural identity conflict as one important process that may link perceived discrimination with lower life satisfaction and health. We conducted a secondary data analysis of longitudinal data with N = 285 migrants in integration courses in Germany (24,4% forced) including two time points spanning six months. In two Latent Difference Score Longitudinal Mediation models we found that baseline perceived discrimination at time one predicted the increase in bicultural identity conflict over time, and that the increase in identity conflict covaried with a decline in (1) life satisfaction and (2) health. The research highlights how the perception of discrimination can be a key risk factor that undermines the development of harmonious bicultural identity over time, with key detrimental repercussions for migrants' adaptation, life satisfaction and health.

Introduction

Migrating to another country represents a major life event that entails acculturation processes (Berry, 1997; Schwartz et al., 2010) and prompts changes in one's cultural identity configuration (Ward & Szabò, 2023). Migrants' intercultural encounters with the host national groups, ranging from positive interactions that support inclusion to negative experiences of exclusion and discrimination, have fundamental implications for fostering or constraining the development of dual cultural identities (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005). These identity processes represent a central pathway linking intercultural experiences to migrants' adaptation and well-being, being associated with positive adaptation outcomes or psychological and physical burdens (Gonzalez-Guarda et al., 2021; Pascoe & Richman, 2009; Tikhonov et al., 2023). When experiences of discrimination create tension between one's cultural heritage and newly developing national identity, people may feel emotionally burdened by these conflicting identities. Over a longer period of time, experiences of discrimination and the entailing emotional identity processes might have profound effects on life satisfaction and health. The implications of potential negative adaptations span multiple contexts, such as migrants' private lives as well as education settings and the workplace, and they affect both forced and voluntary migrants.

Drawing on longitudinal data we aim to test how the perception of discrimination can undermine over time the development of harmonious cultural identity, with ultimate detrimental repercussions for migrants' life satisfaction and health.

Migrants' cultural identities

Migrants' integration hinges on how they negotiate their dual identification with both heritage and host cultures (Berry, 2001). Models of acculturation suggest this can result in stable different identity configurations: prioritizing identification with one group, with both groups but alternatively depending on contextual factors, or integrating the two cultures into a blended and non-conflictual identity. The latter has been associated with the best well-being and adjustment outcomes (Amiot et al., 2018; Nguyen & Benet-Martínez, 2013; Ward & Szabó, 2023; Yampolsky et al., 2016).

One major framework that describes individuals' subjective perceptions of how their multiple cultural identities relate to each other is the bicultural identity integration (BII) framework (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005; Ward & Szabò, 2023). It describes two independent dimensions of BIIperceived cultural conflict and perceived cultural distance. Cultural conflict describes the degree to which a person perceives their relevant cultural identities as harmonious or conflicting, and cultural distance marks the perception of one's cultural identities as blended or compartmentalized. In other words, the cultural distance dimension covers whether someone feels part of one combined culture (e.g. a hyphenated culture like Turkish-German) or keeps them separate (e.g. describing the self as a Turkish person living in Germany). The cultural conflict dimension describes whether a person feels torn between the two cultures or perceives them as harmonious and compatible. These two dimensions are distinct and are associated with different factors (Huynh et al., 2018), where, for example, perceived discrimination is explicitly associated primarily with cultural conflict, but not cultural distance.

Therefore, the present study centers the cultural conflict dimension with its particular focus on affective processes, especially in the light of experiences of discrimination and the resulting effects on life satisfaction and health. This way, the BII can explain how a person might see their cultural identity as one that combines both national and heritage culture, but at the same time as conflicting: A migrant might happily celebrate a national holiday in their private life that is not part of their cultural heritage (i.e. low cultural distance), but at the same time feel distress from a cultural clash through discrimination experiences and intercultural tensions (i.e. high cultural conflict).

While the BII framework adds a more micro-level perspective on how individuals reconcile multiple cultural identities, the importance of migrants dual identities could be further understood in light of additional socio-psychological theoretical frameworks that focus on the main benefits of mere group identities (i.e., belonging to one or more social groups for ones' life satisfaction and health) and group-level identity continuity (i.e., finding compatible new groups after an incisive life transition). The social cure approach (Haslam et al., 2018; Jetten et al., 2012) emphasizes the implications of social identities and their compatibility for health. The approach supports that group identification can convey fundamental psychological resources like social support, need satisfaction, self-esteem, meaning, and adaptive regulatory strategies that sustain a healthy psycho-biological functioning with positive implications for life satisfaction and health. A growing body of mostly cross-sectional research points out the effect of perceived discrimination on health outcomes (Cobb et al., 2019; Gonzalez-Guarda et al., 2021; Scholaske et al., 2021). This evidence suggests the importance of considering health alongside life satisfaction to grasp a full picture of how dual identities could shape migrants' well-being outcomes.

Within the broader social cure approach, the Social Identity Model of Identity Change (SIMIC; Haslam et al., 2020) highlights the importance of multiple compatible old and new social group memberships following an incisive life event, such as migration. Specifically, the SIMIC proposes that compatibility between group memberships before and after a significant life transition is necessary to maintain a sense of identity continuity, which in turn supports well-being during adjustment. Indeed, a certain degree of identity continuity is an important psychosocial resource for well-being in such transitions (Haslam et al., 2020; Smeekes et al., 2017). It ensures successful integration into new social groups, whereas incompatibility blocks social support, and thus, poses a risk factor for psychological burdens like depression (Haslam et al., 2020; Jetten et al., 2012). The SIMIC and the BII framework converge in their assertion that compatibility is an important aspect of well-being and adaptation during life transitions. Both accounts offer differentiated explanations of how exactly migration, discrimination, and internal identity processes shape well-being outcomes. When considering both accounts, we can explain in more detail the experience of bicultural identities. High identity continuity after migration (i.e., retained group memberships related to cultural heritage) might support well-being while at the same time, perceived discrimination might induce bicultural identity conflict that burdens well-being. This way, discrimination might disrupt identity continuity in addition to causing bicultural identity conflict by blocking access to previously valued groups, potentially resulting in an exacerbated negative effect on well-being.

The importance of migrants' dual cultural identities extends beyond their direct effects on wellbeing and adaptation outcomes, as they may play a crucial role in shaping the psychological processes arising from one of the most challenging social experiences migrants face: exclusion and discrimination.

Consequences of discrimination on migrants' cultural identities, life satisfaction and health

Meta-analyses strongly point out the negative effect of perceived discrimination on both life satisfaction and multiple health outcomes (Pascoe et al., 2009; Schmitt et al., 2014). For migrants, perceived discrimination and exclusionary threat are among key detrimental acculturative stress factors that pose an additional burden to their well-being and struggle to integrate. Social exclusion negatively affects psychological needs such as belonging and self-esteem (Marinucci & Riva, 2021a; Marinucci et al., 2022) leading to chronic feelings of psychological resignation (Marinucci & Riva, 2021b), that could ultimately play a role in marginalization processes (Berry, 1997).

The impact of exclusion and discrimination also negatively shapes migrants' cultural identification processes (Jasinskaja-Lahti et al., 2009; Çelebi et al., 2017). Specifically, the rejection-(dis-) identification models (Branscombe et al., 1999; Jasinskaja-Lathi et al., 2009) suggest that the effects of perceived discrimination on the well-being of migrants are mediated by social identity processes with host national and migrant ethnic groups. While the results seem mixed for migrants' ethnic identity (e.g., Bobowik et al., 2017), it seems that discrimination increases disidentification with the national identity. At the same time, perceived discrimination is associated with more identity conflict, which describes the perception of how conflictual or compatible the host national and the ethnic identity are (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005; Huynh et al., 2018).

The role of bicultural identity conflict as a potential mediator

Previous research has identified different aspects of acculturative stress as antecedents of bicultural identity conflict, including ethnic discrimination as both an individual and systemic form of oppression (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005; Feagin, 2006; Tikhonov et al., 2023). The emotional toll and potential tension that people with high cultural conflict might then experience in many different settings in everyday life could affect their overall life satisfaction and health. Some studies have focused on the identification with the national and the heritage culture as baseline predictors of BII, concluding that a stronger identification with both cultures generally seems to strengthen BII, having more positive effects on well-being (Ferrari et al., 2015; Schwartz et al., 2015), while others found that people with similar levels of identification with heritage and national identity often experience higher bicultural identity conflict despite a lower BII distance (Spiegler et al., 2022). Social antecedents of bicultural identity conflict, such as exclusion and perceived discrimination, however, have not been studied in detail. Amiot and colleagues (2018) studied how perceived discrimination affects dual identity integration, but they did not study bicultural identity conflict specifically. Benet-Martínez and Haritatos (2005) reveal in a correlational study that acculturative stress stemming from perceived discrimination predicts higher levels of bicultural identity conflict. Thikonov and colleagues (2023) provided correlational evidence that racial discrimination among first-and second-generation immigrant students was associated with higher anxiety via increased cultural identity conflict. Huynh et al. (2018) report from a cross-sectional study that bicultural identity conflict might be a mediator of the association of perceived discrimination and life satisfaction. The potential mediating role of bicultural identity conflict, however, has not been studied longitudinally, nor has it considered the possible implications for health. Indeed, evidence of this effect is only preliminary and correlational, and it is not clear yet whether bicultural identity conflict can mediate the longitudinal impact of discrimination on both health and life satisfaction.

The present research

The present research consists of secondary data analyses (see Table 1 for data transparency, reporting one other publication by Roth et al., 2026) of a broader longitudinal project conducted in Germany with a globally diverse sample of migrants designed to better understand how to support adaptive acculturation processes among newly immigrated individuals in integration courses in Germanyfoot_0 . In the present research, we considered two available waves of data collection spanning over 6 months and the variables relevant to test the study's aims and hypotheses. Drawing on the Bicultural Identity Model (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005; Huynh et al., 2018), the study investigated whether the bicultural identity conflict might mediate the longitudinal effect of perceived discrimination on migrants' life satisfaction and health. Based on previous correlational findings (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005; Huynh et al., 2018), we hypothesized that perceived discrimination would predict an increase in identity conflict between the heritage and the German cultures over time, which, in turn, would covary with (1) life satisfaction (see also Iyer et al., 2009) and (2) health in wave 2 (see Haslam et al., 2018; Jetten et al., 2012). Methods

Sample and Procedure

After removing participants who failed the attention check or declared they had not understood the questionnaire well, the final sample consisted of 285 migrants (40.8% women, 59.2% men) of 34 years old (SD = 9.1, range = 17-63). Participants came from all over the world. The majority originated from the Middle East (33.5%; e.g., Syria, Turkey, Iran), followed by East Europe and Russia (26.8%; e.g., Albania, Poland, Ukraine), South and East Asia (11.9%; e.g., India, Pakistan, Philippines), Central and South America (11.5%; e.g., Brazil, Venezuela, Colombia), Africa (9.1%; e.g., Tunisia, Nigeria, Somalia), and the Western World (7.2%; e.g., other European countries, USA, Australia). Most of the participants were voluntary migrants (75.6%), moving to Germany for economic, study, or family reasons, whereas 24.4% were forced migrants (refugees and asylum seekers). On average, participants had been staying in Germany for 2.3 years (SD = 2.6, range = 1-30).

Of the final sample, 165 participants completed the second wave of data collection after 6 months.

Participants were attending German integration courses in Berlin, North Rhine-Westphalia, and Munich as part of a project with Germany's Ministry for Migration. Participants were recruited in person at the beginning of the courses. Overall, there were three time-points: -At T0 (the time of recruitment in the course), we assessed demographic information in a brief in-person survey -At T1, approximately one month after t0, we assessed our variables in an online-survey -At T2, six months after t1, we again assessed our variables in a second online-survey.

The in-person recruitment procedure ensured participants were really migrants. The questionnaires were available in seven different languages (Arabic, Farsi, French, Russian, Turkish, English, and German) to meet the participants' linguistic needs. Translations were made by professional translators, using back-and-forth translation methods. The questionnaire took ~30 minutes. Participants were compensated with 10 euros for their time and commitment to the project.

Measures

Variables were assessed using a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree,6 = strongly agree) unless otherwise specified. Besides the following, the broad research project assessed additional variables that fell outside the scope of the current study. The full list of variables is available in the OSF platform.

Perceived discrimination was assessed with a single itemfoot_1 (e.g., for a similar procedure, see Verkuyten, 2002): "How often do you think people from your home country are discriminated against in Germany?" (1 = never,6 = very often). Bicultural Identity Conflict was assessed with the four-item Bicultural Identity Integration Scale -I (e.g., "I feel caught between the German culture and the culture of my home country"; Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005). Life satisfaction was measured using the five items from the Satisfaction with Life Scale (e.g., I am satisfied with my life; Diener et al., 1985). Health was measured with three items from the Short-Form Health Survey (e.g., "My health is excellent"; SF-36, Ware et al., 1992) where one item used the anchors1 = extremely bad,6 = extremely good. All scales showed excellent reliability. Table 2 reports descriptive statistics and correlations.

Statistical analysis

To test the hypotheses that the increase in identity conflict over time would mediate the effect of perceived discrimination on the decrease in the two outcomes of life satisfaction (Model 1) and health (Model 2), we conducted two separate Latent Difference Score Longitudinal Mediation models (McArdle, 2009; Selig & Preacher, 2009) using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) in the RStudio software (Posit team, 2024). We adjusted Selig and Preacher (2009) model specification to investigate mediation paths in longitudinal data with only two available time points.

For both models, the measurement part estimated whether perceived discrimination, bicultural identity conflict, and life satisfaction/health significantly changed over time (i.e., the latent Δ-scores) 3.

Then, the structural part estimated all direct effects and the two possible indirect effects. The change in identity conflict was predicted by baseline perceived discrimination (a1) and its temporal change (a2). The change in life satisfaction/perceived health was predicted by the change in identity conflict (b2), the change in discrimination (c2), as well as baseline identity conflict (b1) and discrimination (c1).

The paths b2 and c2 present a covariance, rather than a strict temporal prediction, given the two-wave nature of the data and should not be interpreted causally.

Two indirect effects were computed using a bootstrapping method to test significant mediations (Preacher & Hayes, 2004). The first (a1b2) assessed whether baseline discrimination levels had a downstream impact on life satisfaction/health by fostering greater identity conflict. The second (a2b2) captured the extent to which change in discrimination indirectly reduced life satisfaction via increased identity conflict over time. Importantly, both the baseline level (T1) and the change score of the predictor and mediator were included as predictors of change in the mediator and outcome because baseline levels may reflect chronic, cumulative exposure, while change scores capture more immediate and dynamic shifts (McArdle, 2009). Modeling both enables a more nuanced understanding of which between chronic vs. dynamic shifts in the experiences of discrimination/identity conflict are more strongly implicated in shaping identity-related distress and well-being over time.

The models were estimated with full-information maximum likelihood. Models' fit was assessed with the Comparative Fit (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Indexes (TLI) (acceptable above .90), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) (acceptable below .08), and the Standardized Root Mean Squared Residual (SRMR) (acceptable below .07) (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Marsh et al., 2005).

Model 1 had an excellent fit (χ 2 (6) = 8.67, p = .193; CFI = .990; TLI = .975; RMSEA = .040; SRMR = .046) and the measurement part highlighted that, on average, perceived discrimination remained stable over the two waves of data collection, identity conflict significantly decreased over time, whereas life satisfaction increased although the change was only marginally significant. The structural part of the model showed that baseline, chronic experiences of discrimination predicted a significant increase in bicultural identity conflict over time. In turn, the increase in identity conflict was associated with a decline in life satisfaction, potentially indicating a longitudinal mediation pathway. Differently, neither the direct effects of baseline discrimination and identity conflict on the change in life satisfaction nor the change in discrimination on the change in identity conflict and life satisfaction were significant. The analysis of the indirect effect suggests that the longitudinal effect of baseline perceived discrimination on the decline in life satisfaction was significantly mediated by an increase in bicultural identity conflict (a1*b2: β = -.053; 95%CI[-.123 -.003]), however, the second part of the indirect effect presents a covariance rather than a longitudinal prediction. By contrast, the indirect effect involving the change in discrimination was not significant (a2*b2: β = .00; 95%CI[-.040 .043]). Model 2 focusing on health largely showed the same effects. The model had an excellent fit (χ 2 (6) = 9.14, p = .166; CFI = .987; TLI = .967; RMSEA = .043; SRMR = .047) and confirmed that only identity conflict changed over time, decreasing. The structural part replicated model 1, showing that baseline perceived group discrimination led to an increase in identity conflict over time, which in turn, was associated with a decrease in perceived health, with the indirect effect being statistically significant (a1*b2: β = -.044; 95%CI[-.105 -.002]). However, the second part of the indirect effect presents a covariance rather than a longitudinal prediction. As in model 1, the effect of baseline identity conflict on the change in health was not significant. Also, neither the effect of change in discrimination on change in identity, nor the indirect effect on change in health via change in identity conflict (a2*b2: β = .00; 95%CI[-.039 .032]) were statistically significant. The only difference compared to model 1 is that the baseline effect of perceived discrimination significantly predicted an improvement in perceived health over time, and similarly, the change in discrimination predicted an increase in health (although only marginally significant).

These patterns indicate suppression effects (MacKinnon et al., 2000). They imply that, net of the negative effect of discrimination on the decrease in health via identity conflict, the observed positive effect of perceived group discrimination on health may reflect the activation of compensatory coping mechanisms in response to discrimination, which may improve health outcomes. Since the present study used a single-item measure of perceived discrimination, these findings cannot be attributed to specific kinds of discrimination, such as perceived group or personal discrimination.

Discussion

The results of our study support the hypothesized mediation effect of bicultural identity conflict, with identity conflict mediating the relationship between perceived discrimination and life satisfaction as well as health: Baseline discrimination at time one predicted an increase in identity conflict at time two. Across two models, the change in identity conflict was in turn associated with the change in life satisfaction and health, respectively, as predicted. While the direct effect of baseline discrimination on life satisfaction was not significant, there was a significant positive direct effect of baseline discrimination on health. Sensitivity analyses showed that the results hold the same across forced and voluntary migrants (see supplemental materials), suggesting that the found processes are not unique to forced migrants, highlighting the value of cross-migration comparisons.

The present study extends prior work in three main ways. First, it contributes to clarifying the psychological pathways through which perceived discrimination erodes migrants' life satisfaction and health over time. By focusing specifically on the BII's cultural conflict dimension, it sheds light on how perceived discrimination leads to an internalized identity struggle for migrants, a feeling of being torn between their heritage and their national identity. It contributes to the literature on the sociocultural identitarian and psychosocial processes conveying the negative impact of perceived discrimination on migrants' life satisfaction and health.Second, inspired by the broader Social Cure Approach (Jetten et al., 2012; Haslam et al., 2018), which postulates that social identities are key determinants of not only psychological well-being but also physical health, the present study incorporates health outcomes alongside life satisfaction. In doing so, we directly build upon the work of Huynh et al. (2018). While the non-significant correlations in their cross-sectional study lead to the conclusion that self-reported physical health might not be related to BII, our longitudinal data suggest substantial covariation of cultural identity conflict and health over time. Our findings extend the research on the effect of perceived discrimination on health (Pascoe & Smart Richmann, 2009) by highlighting that bicultural identity conflict and its derived psychological burden could be another key mechanism in the chain of processes linking discrimination and health and well-being outcomes. Third, by adopting a longitudinal approach, this study extends previous literature on the link between perceived discrimination and bicultural identity conflict, which has so far been only preliminarily supported by cross-sectional evidence (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005; Huynh et al., 2018). Within this theoretical framework, the present longitudinal study suggests that perceived discrimination led to negative life satisfaction and health consequences over time, potentially indirectly by increasing the perceived conflict between migrants' dual cultural identities. This suggests that perceiving one's migrant national group as discriminated against can generate a lasting and hard-to-solve conflict within bicultural identity. Being discriminated against by the host national group may signal that migrants' heritage national identity is not seen as fitting or compatible with the host national identity, thereby triggering enduring psychological feelings of being trapped between fixed features of who migrants are by heritage and who they are expected to become through adjustment. Over time, this perceived incompatibility may undermine both health and life satisfaction.

Overall, the results suggest a nuanced pattern of temporal effects. On the one hand, it was the chronic -rather than the change in -experience of discrimination that played a more central role in predicting bicultural identity conflict and its consequences for life satisfaction. This suggests that the negative impact of chronic experiences of discrimination on inducing identity conflict might be persistent and not easily offset by short-term improvements in experienced discrimination. On the other hand, it was the dynamic, short-term change in identity conflict rather than its chronic level that was associated more strongly with changes in life satisfaction, indicating that dynamic fluctuations in identity conflict might be a more proximal and immediate driver of life satisfaction than enduring levels of conflict.

The explanation is consistent with the differential temporal dynamics of the study variables.

Indeed, perceived discrimination remained stable over time, whereas bicultural identity conflict showed a significant decrease. As such, the predictive power of chronic rather than changing discrimination likely reflects its stable nature, while the greater explanatory role of change in identity conflict may stem from its significant within-person fluctuations over time, making it a more transient and context-dependent psychological construct. In the present study, we focus on a broad measure of perceived discrimination that does not distinguish group and personal discrimination, which might not catch all variation in different kinds of perceived discrimination over a period of six months. Perceived group discrimination encompasses multiple contexts, such as work, housing markets, and institutions and surpasses individual instances, implying a more pervasive nature compared to perceived personal discrimination. The non-significant change in perceived discrimination could also explain why the change score did not predict identity conflict over time. Rather, our model shows that those with higher baseline level discrimination can, over time, experience an increase in identity conflict. The results highlighting perceived discrimination as a stable temporal predictor align with other studies using related predictors, such as social exclusion. They show the long-term negative effect of chronic social exclusion on migrants' and other marginalized groups' well-being (Aureli et al., 2020; Marinucci et al., 2022 Marinucci et al., , 2023;; Marinucci & Riva, 2021a , 2024). Indeed, a previous longitudinal study identified six months as the temporal framework within which both the perception of social exclusion and its negative impact on well-being become stable and chronic (Marinucci & Riva, 2021b).

Expanding the literature focusing on migrants' acculturation, cultural identity and life satisfaction (e.g., Ward & Szabò, 2023), the present research emphasizes that chronic experiences of discrimination may exert a stable and difficult-to-reverse negative impact also on identity-related processes, particularly on bicultural identity conflict. However, its ultimate cascade negative impact on well-being might be interrupted and more easily targeted by leveraging fluctuations in bicultural identity conflict. This highlights the potential value of interventions aimed at addressing and reducing identity conflict over time, suggesting that even in the face of persistent discrimination, fostering positive shifts in identity integration may offer a viable path to improving well-being.

The same reasoning applies also when considering perceived health as the ultimate outcome.

The only difference concerns a positive direct effect of baseline discrimination on health change, suggesting that in the absence of increased identity conflict, individuals high in perceived discrimination may mobilize compensatory resources (e.g., resilience, solidarity, identity affirmation) that positively impact well-being. Specifically, after accounting for the negative effects of perceived discrimination on health via identity conflict, there was a residual positive longitudinal link between perceived discrimination and improvement of health. The result pattern reflects a potential suppression effect, in which the mediating variable (identity conflict) accounts for the harmful pathway, thereby unmasking a residual positive effect of discrimination on health that might be explained by variables not considered in the current research -such as strengthening ingroup ties or the enactment of healthy coping mechanisms to face discrimination that would, in turn, benefit health. This interpretation is consistent with the literature deriving from the Rejection-Identification Model (Branscombe et al., 1999), according to which perceived personal and group discrimination might lead minorities to strengthen their ingroup identity in order to cope with the enduring exclusion, as such unlocking beneficial effects for life satisfaction and health (Bourguignon et al., 2006). Indeed, empirical findings on people with HIV/AIDS, showing that perceived group discrimination might lead to an improvement in the perception of health according to two pathways: a hiding path -through concealing one's stigmatized identity which in turn reduces the exposure to personal discrimination -and an identification path -by which group discrimination enhances ingroup identification, in turn fostering collective action in support of one's ingroup (Molero et al., 2011). Besides strengthening ingroup identification, migrants may rely on alternative psychological and behavioral coping strategies, such as relying on religion and spirituality (Wesselmann & Williams, 2010; Aydin et al., 2010), or physical exercise (Riva, 2016). Indeed, literature shows that both spiritual and physical exercise practices are particularly effective in reducing physiological activation of stress responses (Haney & Lane, 2024), including research focusing on refugees (Aydin et al., 2010; Sjögren et al., 2021).

Notably, the suppression-like pattern was observed only when the outcome was general health, not on life satisfaction. This pattern might indicate that life satisfaction, as a more global evaluative construct, may be less influenced by coping strategies against discrimination, which might instead be more effective in improving specific health-related outcomes, like the reduction of physical stress (Pascoe & Richman, 2009).

Limitations and Future Directions

Future research should clarify whether a suppression effect for health outcomes can be replicated in other samples or whether it is specific to the present research context. Further work should also consider potential third variables that may account for the observed positive association between discrimination and improved health and explore why this effect appears to emerge specifically for health-related outcomes. In this way, the present research also invites to consider possible coping mechanisms that could explain the positive link between perceived discrimination and improved health. The found effect could serve as a starting point in research examining potential mediators. For instance, future research could consider the role of other bicultural identity features, such as the blended integration of elements from both cultural identities. Indeed, the current dataset did not assess this relevant feature of migrants' bicultural identity, which should be further investigated.

Similarly, it could be worth considering the psychosocial mechanisms proposed by the Social Cure approach (Haslam et al., 2018) -such as the strengthening of social ties and identification with ingroup members -which could unlock possible beneficial coping mechanisms against discrimination that would eventually protect well-being, such as health-promoting group norms.

The study used a single-item scale for perceived discrimination, and personal discrimination was not assessed. Future studies should use more nuanced, comprehensive scales to assess whether the hypothesized processes vary across types of perceived discrimination. Indeed, literature suggests that personal and group discrimination might have differentiated implications on well-being, called the "'personal-group discrimination discrepancy" (Taylor et al., 1994). Some studies found that group discrimination might be more strongly and frequently perceived by minorities compared to personal discrimination (Verkuyten, 2002), and that the two might have quantitatively different effects on well-being through distinct mechanisms (Bourguignon et al., 2006; Molero et al., 2011; Stevens & Thijs, 2018). Based on the Rejection-Identification model (Branscombe et al., 1999), it is specifically perceiving group discrimination that can have positive implications for self-esteem (Bourguignon et al., 2006), which might act as a buffer against perceived personal discrimination.

It is important to note that the change scores in identity conflict and life satisfaction were both derived from the same two time points and thus refer to changes occurring over the same period. As such, the path from change in identity conflict to change in life satisfaction should be interpreted more as a contemporaneous association than a strictly longitudinal prediction. This limits causal inference regarding the direction of influence and suggests that identity-related distress and subjective wellbeing may co-vary over time, rather than one clearly driving the other. As such, future research should consider conducting longitudinal studies with more than 2 waves of data collection, as to be able to conclusively identify the temporal chain of the effects between all the considered variables.

Conclusion

Research on migrants' acculturation experiences and identity processes in response to a new social and cultural environment is key to informing multiple stakeholders outside academia in social and workplace policy development on integration. Specifically, our findings underline that bicultural identity conflict is a key process in BII. Finding ways to foster BII amidst perceived discrimination could potentially buffer the negative effects of bicultural identity conflict on life satisfaction and health. Reducing discrimination should be a primary goal, however, preventing all discrimination would still not be feasible, therefore calling for multiple simultaneous approaches. It is both the behavior of individuals and groups, as well as institutional aspects that contribute to migrants' perception of discrimination against their own group (Marinucci et al., 2022), with detrimental effects on multiple outcomes, as found in the present study. We argue that the prevention of discrimination and social exclusion and the resulting resignation in migrants is a central factor in the broader societal task of integration. Further, in the context of discrimination, bicultural identity and well-being, both voluntary and forced migrants can be affected equally, highlighting the importance of a holistic approach to combating discrimination. By including a diverse sample of migrants, we can highlight some crucial similarities in the psychological processes and outcomes following perceived discrimination. Future research should address the potentially mediating coping mechanisms that migrants employ that could buffer the effects of discrimination on health.
