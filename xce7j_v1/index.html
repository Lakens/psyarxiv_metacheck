How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Bayesian graphical modeling (BGM) is an emerging approach to psychological network analysis that offers proper uncertainty quantification and allows for incorporating prior information. However, resources that integrate the full analytic workflow within user-friendly software and provide guidance for transparent reporting remain scarce. In this tutorial, we address this gap by offering an integrated analysis and reporting guide for BGM. We first briefly outline the theoretical foundations of the framework. We then introduce the updated Bayesian network analysis module in the open-source software JASP and demonstrate three core analyses using an empirical dataset: edge selection and parameter estimation, clustering analysis, and comparing networks across groups. For each analysis, we provide minimal template paragraphs for the Methods and Results sections. We conclude with recommendations for prior robustness analyses and open research practices.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, network analysis of multivariate cross-sectional data has become an established method in psychology and the social and behavioral sciences (Borsboom et al., 2021; Marsman & Rhemtulla, 2022; Robinaugh, Hoekstra, Toner, & Borsboom, 2020). This methodological development initially stems from a broader theoretical movement in which psychological constructs are conceptualized as systems of mutually interacting components rather than as manifestations of a single latent cause (Cramer, Waldorp, van der Maas, & Borsboom, 2010; Finnemann, Waldorp, Borsboom, Marsman, & van der Maas, 2026; van Bork, Epskamp, Rhemtulla, Borsboom, & van der Maas, 2017; van der Maas et al., 2006). Alongside this conceptual shift, the field now known as network psychometrics has seen rapid methodological advances in estimating and interpreting psychological network models from empirical data (Isvoranu, Epskamp, Waldorp, & Borsboom, 2022; Marsman & Rhemtulla, 2022). One of the more recent developments is the Bayesian graphical modeling framework (BGM; Huth et al., 2023; Marsman, Huth, Waldorp, & Ntzoufras, 2022; Marsman, van den Bergh, & Haslbeck, 2025; Mohammadi & Wit, 2015; Williams, 2021), which provides a fully Bayesian approach to network estimation.

The BGM methodology offers several advantages over alternative approaches, two of which are particularly important. First, it explicitly accounts for uncertainty about the network structure. When this uncertainty is not properly quantified, researchers may interpret a weakly supported effect (in this case an edge in the network) as reliably present or treat an omitted edge as evidence that the corresponding relationship is absent, thereby becoming overconfident in the estimated network (Hinne, Gronau, van den Bergh, & Wagenmakers, 2020; Hoeting, Madigan, Raftery, & Volinsky, 1999; Sekulovski, Keetelaar, Huth, et al., 2024). The lack of this uncertainty quantification can also contribute to disagreement between networks estimated using different samples (e.g., Forbes, Wright, Markon, & Krueger, 2017, in press), since edges for which the evidence is inconclusive may be selected in one sample and omitted in another. A recent Bayesian reanalysis of 293 networks from 126 published studies found that, for the majority of edges, the data did not provide strong evidence for either inclusion or exclusion (Huth, Haslbeck, Keetelaar, van Holst, & Marsman, 2026). BGM addresses this problem by using inclusion Bayes factors to distinguish evidence for inclusion, evidence for exclusion, and absence of evidence (Marsman, van den Bergh, & Haslbeck, 2025; Sekulovski, Keetelaar, Huth, et al., 2024). Treating inconclusive evidence as a meaningful outcome makes explicit what the data can and cannot support and helps limit overinterpretation. Second, theoretical and prior knowledge about the network can be incorporated through the prior distributions (Sekulovski, Arena, et al., 2026; Sekulovski, Keetelaar, Haslbeck, & Marsman, 2024; Sekulovski, Waaijers, & Arena, 2026), which can make inference more robust by restricting parameters to more realistic values and providing a principled way to combine extensive expertise with data as well as incorporate theory.

Despite these benefits, the BGM methodology remains technically demanding, creating a clear need for accessible software implementations and reporting guidelines. Existing resources address individual components of the framework or specific software implementations (e.g., Huth et al., 2023; Huth, Keetelaar, Sekulovski, van den Bergh, & Marsman, 2024), but a unified guide is still lacking; one that integrates the full range of the available BGM methods, demonstrates their practical utility using user-friendly and open-source software, and offers guidance for transparent reporting of the results.

The present paper addresses this gap with an integrated tutorial and reporting guide for Bayesian graphical modeling. In the next section, we give a high-level overview of the BGM methodology and discuss the most important aspects of each analysis with minimal technical depth. We then introduce the updated Bayesian Network Analysis module in the open-source, point-and-click statistical software JASP (JASP Team, 2026; Love et al., 2019) and demonstrate three types of BGM analyses using an openly available empirical dataset from Martarelli, Baillifard, and Audrin (2023). For each of these analyses, we offer a unified reporting checklist, illustrated with minimal generic template paragraphs that researchers can adapt to their own work. We close with a brief discussion highlighting the main takeaways from this tutorial. At the end of the paper, we also include a glossary of the most important terminology. Annotated .jasp files for the example analyses are available at https://osf.io/fj7z5. In Appendix A, we expand on some of the theoretical concepts in more detail. Technical details about the analyses can be found in Marsman, van den Bergh, and Haslbeck (2025); Marsman, Waldorp, Sekulovski, and Haslbeck (2025); Sekulovski, Arena, et al. (2026), another accessible introduction to part of the BGM framework in Huth et al. (2023) and an introduction to the prior distributions in Sekulovski, Keetelaar, Haslbeck, and Marsman (2024). For a general introduction to Bayesian statistics in psychology and the social sciences we refer the readers to Wagenmakers, Lodewyckx, Kuriyal, and Grasman (2010); Wagenmakers et al. Bayesian Graphical Modeling: A Brief Overview of the Methodology

The network models used in psychology, also known as graphical models, consist of nodes representing variables and edges encoding conditional dependence relations. An edge describes how a pair of variables relate to one another after conditioning on all remaining variables in the network, where the combination of present and absent edges constitutes the network structure. Each edge is parametrized by a partial association parameter, denoted as θ, that captures both the strength and the sign (positive or negative) of the conditional relationship. Therefore, when θ = 0, the two nodes are conditionally independent (i.e., the edge is absent).

To make this idea concrete, consider a symptom network for depression: a positive edge (θ > 0) between fatigue and concentration problems indicates that these symptoms tend to co-occur even after conditioning on all other symptoms in the network, whereas a negative edge (θ < 0) between sleep quality and irritability suggests that better sleep is directly associated with lower irritability and vice versa. The absence of an edge (θ = 0) between, say, appetite changes and concentration problems would indicate that these symptoms are conditionally independent; any marginal association between them is fully explained by the other symptoms in the network. To support such substantive interpretations, a network analysis must accomplish two key tasks: it must determine whether a conditional association between two variables is present or absent (or, equally important, if there is insufficient evidence to draw a conclusion), and, if present, provide an estimate of the strength and direction of that relationship (i.e., θ). In other words, we require a framework that allows researchers to both test for the existence of an effect (the edge) and estimate the partial association parameter associated with a present edge.

Bayesian Graphical Modeling (BGM) elegantly addresses both of these requirements. To see how, it helps to first consider the space of possible network structures. For a network with p variables, every pair of nodes is either connected by an edge or not. There are k = p2 = p(p-1) /2 possible edges to consider, and since each of these edges can be either

To properly account for this vast space of possible network structures, BGM uses Bayesian model averaging (BMA, Hinne et al., 2020; Hoeting et al., 1999), which incorporates the uncertainty about the network structure into the estimation process. The approach proceeds in two steps. Researchers first specify their prior beliefs about which edges are present or absent, through a prior on the network (also called graph) structure, denoted p(G). Then, conditional on that structure, they specify their prior beliefs about the strength of the partial associations corresponding to the present edges, using a prior on the parameters, denoted p(Θ | G). Both sets of prior beliefs are then updated by the observed data via Bayes' rule, yielding a joint posterior distribution over network structures and model parameters. 1Figure 1 displays the equation for the joint posterior distribution p(Θ, G | data), denoting the probability of the partial association parameters (Θ) and network structure (G) given the data. The posterior distribution expresses our updated beliefs about the network structure and parameters after seeing the data. In other words, network structures that predict the data well get a boost and become more likely in the posterior distribution, and networks that predict the data poorly are penalized.

The joint posterior contains two important pieces of information. First, for each of the k possible edges, we obtain the probability that it is present by adding the posterior probabilities of all candidate structures containing that edge. Because these structures can differ in their other edges, this probability accounts for uncertainty about the rest of the network. It is also used in the hypothesis tests explained below. Second, the estimated strength of a partial association can differ across network structures. Bayesian model averaging combines the estimates across all candidate structures, giving more weight to structures with higher posterior probabilities. The resulting structure-averaged estimates carry uncertainty about which edges are present into uncertainty about their effect sizes. In the following subsections, we explain each element of the joint posterior distribution in more detail.

Priors on the network structure p(G)

Reading the equation for the BGM joint posterior (Figure 1) right to left, the first element that researchers are required to specify is the prior on the network structure p(G). Consider a simple example with three variables, which yields 2 ( 32 ) = 8 possible network structures (Figure 2 depicts these 8 network structures). The prior on the network structure can be used to encode theoretical beliefs or findings from previous studies about the plausibility of each of the eight possible network structures or the individual edges (hence why this prior is also known as the edge prior). Three options are available for this prior in the current BGM software, each encoding a different structural assumption: • Bernoulli prior: treats each edge as independently present with a fixed prior edge inclusion probability (with a default value of 0.5, which corresponds to a uniform prior over all possible network structures).

Figure 2 illustrates this default case: above each network we show the corresponding prior probability assigned to that structure, and with the inclusion probability set to 0.5, this yields an equal prior probability across all 2 k possible structures. Changing the prior inclusion probability also changes how the prior mass is distributed across structures. For example, setting it to 0.3 would place more mass on networks with fewer edges, which makes the empty network the most probable and the fully connected network the least probable. The Bernoulli prior is useful when researchers have prior knowledge or hypotheses about the inclusion of specific edges.foot_1 • Beta-Bernoulli prior: places a Beta prior distribution on a shared inclusion probability across edges, so the prior adapts to the overall sparsity or density of the network suggested by the data.

It has two hyperparameters, Shape parameter 1 and Shape parameter 2, both with a default value of 1, which yields an equal prior distribution over network densities (i.e., proportion of present edges). Below each network in Figure 2, we show the corresponding prior probability assigned by this specification. As can be seen, the prior now favors either very sparse or very dense networks. This prior is useful when researchers have prior knowledge or hypotheses about the global network density (see further below on how to specify the hyperparameters).

• Stochastic block model (SBM) prior (Holland, Laskey, & Leinhardt, 1983; Sekulovski, Arena, et al., 2026): expresses the expectation that variables form clusters, with separate within-cluster and between-cluster edge inclusion probabilities. It has six hyperparameters: Within cluster shape parameter 1 and Within cluster shape parameter 2, specifying the Beta prior on the within-cluster inclusion probability; Between cluster shape parameter 1 and Between cluster shape parameter 2, specifying the Beta prior on the between-cluster inclusion probability; the Parameter for the number of clusters λ, which sets the expected number of clusters to 1 + λ;foot_2 and a Concentration parameter on the cluster sizes. This hyperparameter controls how balanced we expect cluster sizes to be, with larger values encouraging clusters of similar size, smaller values encouraging uneven or potentially sparse cluster structures, and the default value of 1 corresponding to a neutral prior in which all partitions of nodes are equally likely, without preferring equal or unequal cluster sizes. The SBM prior underpins the clustering analysis discussed below. Guidance on how to adjust the hyperparameters of this prior is given in Appendix A as well as in Sekulovski, Arena, et al. (2026). Top value in each box: independent Bernoulli(0.5) prior on each edge, which results in each structure receiving a 1 /8 prior probability. Bottom value: Beta-Bernoulli(1, 1) prior, which places mass uniformly over the number of edges (network density) and then uniformly within each edge-count.

The Beta-Bernoulli and SBM priors both use Beta distributions to express expectations about edge inclusion probabilities. Their shape parameters have the same interpretation: setting the first shape parameter larger than the second favors higher inclusion probabilities and therefore more edges, whereas the reverse favors fewer edges. Increasing both shape parameters by the same factor expresses greater certainty about the expected density. For the Beta-Bernoulli prior, these settings apply to the network as a whole. For the SBM prior, they apply separately to connections within and between clusters. For example, setting the first shape parameter larger than the second for within-cluster connections, and the second larger than the first for between-cluster connections, favors networks with more connections within clusters and fewer connections between clusters, which is consistent with the underlying definition of clustering.

Priors on the partial associations p(Θ | G)

The second element that researchers are required to specify is the prior on the partial associations p(Θ | G) (see Figure 1). The prior on the partial associations, given a network structure, determines the probability of different effect sizes for the present edges. Three distributions are available in the software, all centered at zero: • Normal prior (the default): indexed by a single scale hyperparameter (default 1) that controls the width of the prior.

• Cauchy prior: indexed by a single scale hyperparameter (default 1) that controls the width of the prior.

• Beta-prime prior: indexed by two shape hyperparameters that jointly control the location and tail behavior of the prior.

For more details on both sets of priors, we refer the reader to Appendix A, as well as to Sekulovski, Keetelaar, Haslbeck, and Marsman (2024).

The Graphical Model p(data | Θ)

The network models used in this framework are Markov random field (MRF) graphical models (Kindermann & Snell, 1980), which give rise to different model families depending on the measurement level of the data: the Gaussian graphical model for continuous variables (Lauritzen, 1996), the Ising model for binary variables (Ising, 1925), the recently proposed ordinal Markov random field for ordinal (and binary) data (OMRF, Marsman, van den Bergh, & Haslbeck, 2025), as well as MRFs that accommodate a combination of continuous and discrete variables.

As discussed earlier, the parameters of most interest in the model are the partial association parameters. The partial association parameters are generic measures of conditional dependence that apply across all model types in the BGM framework. Each partial association, denoted as θ ij , captures the size and direction of the pairwise effect between variables X i and X j after conditioning on all remaining variables in the network. A positive θ ij means that higher values of one variable tend to co-occur with higher values of the other (after conditioning on the remaining variables in the network), and a negative θ ij means the opposite. Crucially, the partial associations are on the same scale regardless of the measurement level of the data.

Edges between two continuous variables can equivalently be expressed as partial correlations, which are bounded between -1 and 1. Edges between binary or ordinal variables can be expressed as log adjacent-category odds ratios, which describe how the odds of moving up one category on one variable change when the other variable also moves up one category; for ease of communication, these values can be further converted to a percentage change in the odds. For example, a log adjacent-category odds ratio of 0.41 corresponds to (e 0.41 -1)×100% ≈ 51%, meaning that moving up one category on one variable is associated with a 51% increase in the odds of moving up one category on the other. More details are provided in Appendix A. For networks that contain interactions between continuous and discrete variables, the edge parameters are reported only as partial associations.

In addition to partial association parameters, network models include so-called main parameters (e.g., means for continuous variables or category threshold parameters for discrete variables). These parameters capture each node's tendency to take on certain values that are not explained by the remaining variables in the network; the main parameters are typically treated as nuisance parameters. The same three prior distributions previously introduced for the partial association parameters are also available for the main parameters; with a Beta-prime(0.5, 0.5) distribution as the default. 4For ordinal variables with a neutral baseline category, such as a neutral middle point on a Likert scale, the OMRF can be parameterized as a Blume-Capel model (Blume, 1966; Capel, 1966; van der Maas, Borsboom, & Waldorp, 2026). The model is particularly suited to attitude or opinion data, where a neutral midpoint differs qualitatively from the other response options. The Blume-Capel model parametrizes the category thresholds as a function of distance from the baseline with two estimates. First, the linear estimate captures a directional response tendency: positive values indicate a greater tendency to respond in categories above the baseline, whereas negative values indicate a greater tendency to respond in categories below it. Second, the quadratic estimate captures response concentration: negative values indicate a preference for categories near the baseline, whereas positive values indicate a preference for categories farther from it. In the software, researchers can specify which category serves as the baseline for each Blume-Capel variable. JASP additionally reports the estimated linear and quadratic main-effect contributions for every variable specified in this way.

The joint posterior distribution p(Θ, G | data)

Now, assume we have collected N observations on the three variables we introduced earlier (Figure 2). Suppose we have no prior knowledge about the network structure, meaning every edge is equally likely to be present. In that case, each edge receives a Bernoulli prior with a prior edge inclusion probability of 0.5 (Figure 2). We then run the BGM analysis on the collected data to obtain a posterior distribution (Figure 1). Figure 3 shows the posterior probability p(G • | data) of the 8 possible structures. Some structures become more plausible after seeing the data (e.g., the probability of G 6 increases from 0.125 to 0.432), while others become less plausible (e.g., G 1 decreases from 0.125 to 0.043). In the next two subsections, we explain how we use the information provided by the posterior distribution to test whether edges are present and accordingly interpret the corresponding partial association parameter estimates.

Posterior Inclusion Probability & Inclusion Bayes Factor

After obtaining the joint posterior distribution of the network, we wish to quantify the evidence for the presence of an edge between, say, variables X 1 and X 2 . To do so, we need to sum the posterior probabilities of all structures in which this edge is present. In this example, those are structures 2, 5, 6, and 8 (Figure 3), whose posterior probabilities sum to 0.432 + 0.023 + 0.432 +0.023 = 0.91. This is known as the posterior inclusion probability for the edge between X 1 and X 2 . One could decide based on this alone to include all edges whose posterior inclusion probability is above 0.50, known as the median probability model (Barbieri & Berger, 2004). However, one can go further and formally test the hypotheses H 1 : there is an edge between X 1 and X 2 , against H 0 : there is no edge between X 1 and X 2 . The outcome of the hypothesis test is quantified in the inclusion Bayes factor (Jeffreys, 1935; Kass & Raftery, 1995), which details how much the data shift the evidence for one hypothesis relative to the other and can therefore be used as a measure of evidence for the two hypotheses.

To obtain the inclusion Bayes factor, we contrast the posterior inclusion odds with the prior inclusion odds (see Equation 1). The prior inclusion odds are the ratio of the prior inclusion probability (i.e., p(H 1 )) against the prior exclusion probability (i.e., p(H 0 )), respectively also for the posterior inclusion odds. In our example, we specified all edges to have a prior inclusion probability of p(H 1 ) = 0.5, which yields prior inclusion odds for the presence of an edge between X 1 and X 2 equal to p(H 1 ) /p(H 0 ) = 0.foot_4 /0.5 = 1.5 The posterior odds are given by the ratio of the posterior inclusion probability p(H 1 | data) = 0.91 to the posterior exclusion probability p(H 0 | data) = 1 -p(H 1 | data) = 0.09, yielding a value of 0.91 /0.09 = 10.1. Dividing the posterior odds by the prior odds gives the inclusion Bayes factor, BF10 = 10.1 /1 = 10.1, indicating that the data are approximately 10 times more likely under H 1 than under H 0 . A useful property of this test is that it naturally accounts for uncertainty about the rest of the network edges, integrating over all possible configurations of the remaining edges (Sekulovski, Keetelaar, Huth, et al., 2024).

The inclusion Bayes factor acts as an updating factor, it transforms prior beliefs about edge inclusion into posterior beliefs, providing a direct measure of the evidence supplied by the data in favor of a hypothesis (Equation 1). The subscripts in the Bayes factor indicate the direction of the evidence. For example, BF10 indicates the evidence in the data in favor of H 1 , and BF01 = 1 /BF10 indicates the evidence in the data in favor of H 0.

The Bayes factor ranges from 0 to infinity and provides a continuous measure of evidence. Several authors have proposed interpretive guidelines (e.g., Jeffreys, 1935; Kass & Raftery, 1995) for the strength of evidence that a Bayes factor provides. Table 1 summarizes the classification of Jeffreys (1961), which is used as a default in the software. By default, a threshold of BF10 = 10 is used for strong evidence in favor of edge inclusion and BF = 10 for strong evidence in favor of exclusion. We emphasize that these thresholds are conventions rather than absolute standards. The software may report Inf (infinity) when the estimated posterior inclusion or exclusion probability is 1, leaving an exclusion probability of 0 and causing division by zero. For reporting purposes, this can be summarized as an estimated BF10 > 100. The same applies to the difference and clustering Bayes factors introduced in the following subsections.

A crucial feature of the inclusion Bayes factor is that it provides a three-way distinction: evidence for inclusion (BF10 ≫ 1), evidence for exclusion (BF10 ≪ 1, that is, 1 /BF10 = BF01 ≫ 1), and absence of evidence (BF10 ≈ 1). In the last case, the data simply do not contain enough information to decide whether or not the edge is present. This distinction is particularly valuable in psychological network analysis, where a missing edge is otherwise ambiguous. Did the method find evidence that the conditional association is truly zero, or was there simply insufficient data to detect its presence? The Bayes factor makes this distinction explicit. Huth et al. (2026) demonstrated the practical importance of this distinction by reanalyzing 293 networks from 126 published studies: for the majority of edges, the data did not provide strong support on whether the edge should be present or absent, underscoring that uncertainty about individual edges is the norm rather than the exception.

Parameter Estimation & Interpretation

Once we have established which edges are present in the network, we turn to interpreting the posterior estimates of the partial association parameters. In the BGM framework, edge selection (as explained in the previous subsection) and parameter estimation are carried out jointly: a partial association is estimated only when the corresponding edge is included, and is otherwise fixed at zero (for further details, see Hinne et al., 2020; Marsman, van den Bergh, & Haslbeck, 2025).

The determine which edge is included, we first turn to report the inclusion Bayes factor (Equation 1), which is typically conveyed using an edge evidence plot (Figure 4a). For each included edge, we summarize the posterior distribution of its partial association using the posterior mean as a point estimate, together with an x% highest density interval (HDI) to quantify the uncertainty around the estimate (Figure 4c). The HDI is the shortest interval containing x% of the posterior probability mass, and it has a direct probability interpretation: given the observed data, there is an x% probability that the parameter lies within it. A wide HDI reflects substantial uncertainty about the true partial association, whereas a narrow HDI indicates that it has been estimated precisely. The model-averaged network is then shown in a separate network plot, which displays, for each present edge, the estimated strength and direction of its partial association parameter (Figure 4b).

We illustrate these three steps using our running three-variable example. Figure 4a shows the edge evidence plot, where the edge between X 1 and X 2 is depicted in blue, indicating evidence for inclusion (BF10 ≈ 10); the edge between X 2 and X 3 is grey, indicating inconclusive evidence (BF10 ≈ 1); and the edge between X 3 and X 1 is yellow, indicating strong evidence for exclusion (BF01 ≈ 20). Figure 4b shows the model-averaged network, which displays partial association estimates for the included edges; in this example, only the edge between X 1 and X 2 is included since it had BF10 ≈ 10 . This estimate is positive, hence the edge is colored blue (negative edges would be shown in red). The thickness of the edge indicates the relative strength of the partial association parameter estimate. Finally, Figure 4c shows the posterior distribution of the partial association for the edge between X 1 and X 2 , together with the posterior mean (the point estimate) and the 95% HDI. Here, the edge of interest has a model-averaged posterior mean of θ12 = 0.22 and a 95% HDI ranging from 0.04 to 0.44.

This concludes the central part of any BGM analysis, which pertains to edge selection and parameter estimation. Users can also perform two more advanced analyses using this framework (a clustering analysis and a comparison of networks across groups). We explain these in the next two subsections. In the final subsection we also briefly comment on convergence of the estimation algorithm, which is relevant for all BGM analyses.

Clustering Analysis

By using the Stochastic Block Model (SBM) as a prior on the network structure, researchers can simultaneously test and estimate whether the network exhibits clustering (Sekulovski, Arena, et al., 2026). The estimation for the clustering analysis is similar as the one described previously and detailed in Figure 1. In addition to the previous results, researchers also obtain an estimate for the number of clusters, the assignment of variables to clusters, and the within-and between-cluster inclusion probabilities.

Testing for clustering.

The model provides posterior probabilities for all possible numbers of clusters (ranging from 1 to p). From these posterior cluster probabilities two Bayes factor tests are available for assessing the evidence in the data for clustering. The first compares the null hypothesis H 0 : B = 1 (no clustering) against the alternative hypothesis H 1 : B > 1 (at least two clusters). The second compares two specific hypotheses about the number of clusters, for example H 1 : B = b 1 versus H 2 : B = b 2 , where the values b • range from 1 to p. This allows a finer-grained testing of the evidence for a particular clustering solution. This can be useful in cases where researchers have a specific hypothesis about the number of clusters, or when the first Bayes factor shows evidence in favor of clustering but two of the cluster solutions receive relatively equal posterior probabilities.

Cluster allocations. The model also provides a posterior cluster allocation of the nodes, which is an estimate of how the nodes are allocated to the clusters. The SBM yields two complementary cluster-allocation estimates: a posterior mean allocation, i.e., the average allocation in the posterior distribution, and a posterior mode allocation, which selects the assignment that most commonly appears in the posterior distribution. In addition, a posterior co-clustering matrix summarizes, for each pair of variables, the proportion of times in which the two nodes were assigned to the same cluster. Coherent blocks in this matrix indicate stable clusters, while diffuse patterns indicate variables with uncertain cluster membership.

Comparing Networks Across Groups

The BGM framework can be extended to test whether network parameters differ between two or more independent groups (Marsman, Waldorp, et al., 2025). Rather than estimating separate networks per group and comparing the network structures qualitatively, the model estimates a single shared network capturing what the groups have in common, together with explicit difference parameters that capture how each group deviates from the shared network. The same logic as with testing and estimating edges for a single network is applied to each difference parameter: it is either set to zero (i.e., the groups share the same partial association on that edge) or free to take a nonzero value (i.e., the groups differ). This yields posterior difference probabilities and difference Bayes factors, with the same three-way interpretation as before: evidence that the groups differ, evidence that they are equivalent, or absence of evidence. By default, difference selection is applied only to the pairwise interaction parameters, not to the category thresholds, and is currently only available for networks of binary and/or ordinal data.

MCMC Sampling and Convergence

So far, we have ignored how exactly the data updates the prior, thus, how the posterior probability comes about (Figure 1). This is approximated using so-called Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling. MCMC generates a sequence of random draws that, after enough iterations, are representative of the posterior. Inclusion probabilities and parameter estimates (such as means and HDIs) are then computed from these draws. The software runs multiple sequences (chains) from different starting values; each chain begins with a warmup (or burn-in) phase that is discarded, after which the remaining draws are used for the final computations. By default, four parallel chains are run. For an accessible introduction to MCMC sampling for Bayesian statistics, see Gamerman and Lopes (2006).

Since we are sampling from the posterior, we must assess the quality of the samples; in other words, we need to assess whether we have sampled enough and the MCMC has converged. To assess chain agreement, the software reports the R statistic (Gelman & Rubin, 1992), which compares variability between chains to variability within each chain. When the chains yield similar posterior distributions, these two sources of variability are similar and R is close to 1. We use R < 1.01 as a working criterion for adequate chain agreement (Vehtari, Gelman, Simpson, Carpenter, & Bürkner, 2021). Researchers should inspect and report the maximum R for each parameter class for which this diagnostic is available in the software. If any reported value is equal to or greater than 1.01, the model should be refitted with a longer burn-in period and more post-burn-in iterations, after which convergence should be reassessed. Meeting this criterion supports chain agreement but does not, by itself, guarantee that every aspect of the posterior distribution has been explored adequately. The available MCMC update algorithms (the default No-U-Turn Sampler, adaptive Metropolis-Hastings or Gibbs sampler) and additional convergence diagnostics available in R are described in Appendix A.

Prior Robustness Analyses

Because the choice of priors can influence Bayesian inference, we strongly recommend that researchers preregister and report at least one robustness (sensitivity) analysis to demonstrate the robustness of the main findings (Berger, 1990; Sinharay & Stern, 2002). The following aspects of the prior specifications should be varied: • Scale of the prior on the partial associations: Report the results under at least one alternative scale for the Normal (default) or Cauchy prior (e.g., 0.5 and 1.5 in addition to the default scale of 1), or the shape hyperparameters of the Beta-prime prior.

• Prior on the network structure: report results under at least one alternative hyperparameter setting within each of the three structure priors, and, ideally, an analysis with a different structure prior.

-In case of a Clustering analysis, the rate parameter of the prior on the number of clusters as well as the shape parameters for the within and between cluster inclusion probabilities should be varied. Emphasis should be placed on how the different outputs are affected, since the posterior cluster probabilities and cluster Bayes factors are expected to be more sensitive to the choice of prior than the estimated allocation of the nodes and the co-clustering matrix. In other words, fluctuations in the posterior cluster probabilities and cluster Bayes factors alone, while the estimated cluster allocation vectors remain relatively robust, should not be interpreted as evidence of substantial prior sensitivity in the overall results.

The full sensitivity analyses typically belong in an appendix, with a brief description provided in the Methods section and a summary of the outcomes in the Results section. Since network models contain many parameters, small differences across prior specifications are expected; the aim of the prior robustness analysis is to examine whether these differences are large enough to alter the overall conclusions a researcher would draw. Any observed differences should always be interpreted in light of the prior assumptions and whether the observed changes are consistent with those assumptions.

On the topic of prior robustness, we note that the choice of priors, particularly the structure priors, requires careful thought and should be justified in the Methods section. To assist researchers in this process, Sekulovski, Waaijers, and Arena (2026) recently proposed a proof-of-concept framework for prior elicitation using large language models, in which the vast knowledge from the literature is systematically translated into informative hyperparameters for the three structure priors. Researchers could then possibly include the elicited prior among the prior choices in their sensitivity analysis.

Discussion

In this paper, we provided an integrated tutorial and reporting guide for Bayesian graphical modeling of cross-sectional data. We introduced the theoretical foundations of the BGM framework, demonstrated three core analyses (i.e., network estimation, clustering, and group comparison) using the updated Bayesian Network Analysis module in JASP (JASP Team, 2026), and offered reporting guidelines with template paragraphs that researchers can adapt to their own work.

A central theme throughout this paper has been the three-way evidential distinction that the BGM framework provides: evidence for the presence of an edge, evidence for its absence, and absence of evidence. We wish to stress once more that this distinction is the main substantive contribution of the BGM framework to network analysis. Evidence for the absence of an edge is a finding in its own right, not merely a failure to reject the null hypothesis. Equally, when the evidence is inconclusive, acknowledging this openly is more informative than forcing a binary present-or-absent decision. As Huth et al. (2026) showed, inconclusive evidence is the norm for the majority of edges in published psychological networks.

We also wish to highlight several open research practices that apply to BGM analyses just as they do to any empirical study. Preregistration of the analysis plan, including the choice of model, priors, Bayes factor thresholds, which edges or clusters are of primary interest and prior robustness analysis hyperparameters, helps to distinguish confirmatory from exploratory findings. Researchers should, whenever possible, share the data openly and always share their .jasp file to further support transparency and reproducibility. A recent complement to preregistration is the blinding approach for network analysis proposed by Hoekstra, Huth, Sekulovski, Delhalle, and Sarafoglou (in press), in which the analyst is blinded to the relevant aspects of the results until all analytic decisions have been made, reducing the risk that substantive expectations influence analytic decisions. 12The BGM methodology is developing rapidly, and both the software and the available analyses will continue to evolve. Some of the specific software details and default settings described in this tutorial will inevitably become outdated as new features are added and existing ones are refined. Nevertheless, we believe that the core contribution of this paper, bringing the methodology closer to applied researchers and providing reporting guidelines grounded in the principles of the framework, will remain relevant beyond any particular software version.

Glossary Adjacent-category odds ratio

The model-specific interpretation of the partial association for ordinal (and binary) variables. It quantifies how the odds of scoring in category c + 1 versus c on one variable change when the other variable increases by one category. Related to the partial association by θ ij = 1 2 log OR ij.

Bayesian model averaging (BMA)

A technique in which inference is not based on a single model but averaged over a set of candidate models, weighted by their posterior probabilities. In the BGM framework, each possible network structure is a candidate model.

Bernoulli prior A prior on the network structure in which each edge is independently included with probability π. The default is π = 0.5.

Beta-Bernoulli prior

A prior on the network structure in which all edges share a common inclusion probability π that itself receives a Beta(α, β) prior. This allows the overall network density to be learned from the data.

Cluster allocation

The estimated assignment of nodes to clusters under the SBM prior. Available as a posterior mean allocation (best approximation of the average coclustering) or a posterior mode allocation (most frequently visited assignment).

Clustering Bayes factor A Bayes factor that tests whether the network exhibits a clustered structure (B > 1) versus no clustering (B = 1), or compares two specific numbers of clusters.

Difference Bayes factor

In a group comparison analysis, a Bayes factor that quantifies the evidence for a difference in a partial association between groups. Uses the same three-way interpretation as the inclusion Bayes factor: evidence for a difference, evidence for equality, or absence of evidence.

Edge A connection between two nodes indicating a conditional dependence relation. A present edge means the two variables are statistically associated after conditioning on all remaining variables; an absent edge means they are conditionally independent.

Evidence for absence vs. absence of evidence Two distinct outcomes of a Bayes factor test. Evidence for absence (BF01 ≫ 1) means the data support the hypothesis that the edge is not present. Absence of evidence (BF10 ≈ 1) means the data are uninformative; the edge may or may not be present.

Highest density interval (HDI)

The shortest credible interval containing a specified proportion (by default 95%) of the posterior probability mass. Unlike a frequentist confidence interval, a 95% HDI has a direct probability interpretation: there is a 95% posterior probability that the parameter lies within the interval.

Inclusion Bayes factor (BF10 ) A measure of the evidence provided by the data for including an edge (relative to excluding it). A BF10 ≫ 1 indicates evidence for inclusion; BF10 ≪ 1 indicates evidence for exclusion; BF10 ≈ 1 indicates absence of evidence.

Main effects Parameters capturing each node's marginal tendency to take on certain values, independent of the other variables. These include means (for continuous variables) and category threshold parameters (for ordinal variables). Typically treated as nuisance parameters.

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

A computational method for approximating posterior distributions by generating sequences of random draws. BGM uses either the No-U-Turn (NUTS), adaptive Metropolis-Hastings or Gibbs sampler.

Markov random field (MRF)

A class of undirected graphical models in which the joint distribution of all variables factorizes according to the graph structure. The specific MRF depends on the measurement level: Gaussian graphical model (continuous), ordinal MRF (ordinal/binary), Ising model (binary), or mixed MRF (both continuous and discrete).

Node A variable in the network (e.g., an item response, a symptom, or a behavioral measure). Nodes are depicted as circles in network plots.

Partial association

The parameter θ ij associated with a present edge, capturing the strength and sign of the conditional relationship between two variables. Partial associations range from -∞ to +∞ and can be used as common scale estimates across all model types.

Partial correlation

The model-specific interpretation of the partial association for continuous variables. It is the standardized version of the precision matrix element and ranges from -1 to +1.

Posterior co-clustering matrix A matrix in which each entry gives the proportion of posterior samples in which a pair of nodes was assigned to the same cluster. Used to assess the stability of the estimated cluster solution and to uncover potential fuzzy nodes.

Posterior inclusion probability

The probability, after observing the data, that a given edge is present. Computed as the proportion of posterior samples in which the edge was included.

Prior inclusion probability

The probability, specified before seeing the data, that a given edge is present. Set to 0.5 by default, reflecting no prior preference for presence or absence.

Prior on the partial associations (Slab scale)

The scale parameter of the Normal (default) or Cauchy slab prior on the partial associations. Controls the range of effect sizes considered plausible under the alternative hypothesis. The default scale is 1. The Beta-prime slab is instead governed by two shape parameters.

Prior robustness analysis An analysis in which the model is refitted under alternative prior specifications (e.g., different slab scales or structure priors) to assess whether the qualitative conclusions are robust to the choice of priors.

R (R-hat)

A convergence diagnostic that compares variability between and within MCMC chains. Values close to 1 indicate agreement between chains. In this paper, R < 1.01 is used as a working criterion (Vehtari et al., 2021); if any reported value is equal to or greater than 1.01, the model should be refitted with a longer burn-in period and more post-burn-in iterations.

Stochastic block model (SBM) prior

A prior on the network structure that assigns nodes to latent clusters which are learned from the data, with separate inclusion probabilities for within-cluster and between-cluster edges. Used for clustering analyses.

Mixed Markov random field.

When the network contains both discrete and continuous variables, the mixed Markov random field unites the ordinal MRF and the GGM into a single joint distribution (Lauritzen, 1996; Lauritzen & Wermuth, 1989). The partial associations decompose into three types: discrete-discrete interactions (interpretable as log odds ratios, as in the ordinal MRF), continuous-continuous interactions (interpretable via the partial correlations, as in the GGM), and cross-type interactions, expressed only as partial associations, that capture the direct association between a discrete and a continuous variable after accounting for all other variables. Priors

Priors on the network structure. Here we provide further details on the three structure priors.

Bernoulli prior.

The simplest choice treats each edge independently: an indicator γ ij follows a Bernoulli distribution with a prior edge inclusion probability π ij . Setting π ij = 0.5 (the default) for all edges assigns equal prior weight to edge presence and absence, yielding a uniform distribution over all possible network structures. Researchers can adjust this hyperparameter to encode prior beliefs about edge density. JASP applies a common prior inclusion probability to all edges; edge-specific prior inclusion probabilities are available only through the R implementations described in Appendix B. The prior inclusion odds under this structure prior are equal to π ij/(1-π ij ).

Beta-Bernoulli prior.

With this prior, all edges share a single inclusion probability π, which receives a Beta(α, β) hyperprior (Scott & Berger, 2010). This can be viewed as a prior on the network density (number of present edges). Edges are no longer independent a priori: the inclusion of one edge makes others slightly more likely, so the prior adapts to the overall density of the network suggested by the data. An important special case arises when α = β = 1 (the default), which places a uniform distribution over possible levels of network density. Within each density level (e.g., G 2 , G 3 , and G 4 in Figure 2 have the same network density of 1/3), all structures of the same size receive equal probability. By adjusting the shape parameters α (Shape parameter 1 ) and β (Shape parameter 2 ), researchers can encode expectations about sparsity or density. For example, setting α < β encodes a prior expectation that the network is sparse. The prior inclusion odds under this structure prior are equal to α /β.

Stochastic block model (SBM) prior.

The SBM prior (Holland et al., 1983; Sekulovski, Arena, et al., 2026) extends the Beta-Bernoulli prior by incorporating the expectation that variables form clusters. Variables are assigned to latent clusters, and edges within the same cluster share a within-cluster inclusion probability, whereas edges between different clusters share a between-cluster inclusion probability. Separate Beta-Bernoulli priors are specified for within-and between-cluster edges, allowing the model to capture different levels of connectivity within and between clusters. The number of clusters B is estimated from the data using a shifted Poisson prior with rate parameter λ, that is, B -1 ∼ Poisson(λ), while relative cluster sizes are governed by a Dirichlet concentration parameter.

In practice, the SBM prior requires specifying: • Beta shape hyperparameters for the within-cluster inclusion probability (Within cluster shape parameter 1 and 2, also denoted as α w and β w , respectively), • Beta shape hyperparameters for the between-cluster inclusion probability (Between cluster shape parameter 1 and 2, also denoted as α b and β b , respectively), • The shifted Poisson rate parameter λ (Parameter for the number of clusters), • The Dirichlet Concentration parameter.

When clustering is expected, it is natural to favor dense within-cluster connectivity by setting α w > β w , and sparse between-cluster connectivity by setting α b < β b . The expected edge inclusion probability implied by a Beta(α, β) distribution is given by α /α+β.

Under this prior the expected number of clusters is exactly 1 + λ, and the prior probability of a single cluster is p(B = 1) = e -λ . Table A1 summarizes this relationship. An expected number of exactly one cluster is only approached as λ → 0; for a conservative prior that still permits clustering, a small value such as λ = 0.5 is a reasonable choice. Note that this is a change from the implementation described in Sekulovski, Arena, et al. (2026), which placed a zero-truncated Poisson prior on the number of clusters, with expectation λ /(1-e -λ ). The values of λ reported there (e.g., λ = 1.59 for two clusters) therefore no longer correspond to the same expected number of clusters.

The spike-and-slab specification

The two sets of priors, on the network structure and on the partial association parameters, are linked through a so-called spike-and-slab construction. For each pair of variables i and j, a binary indicator γ ij ∈ {0, 1} determines whether the partial association θ ij is fixed at zero (the spike component, γ ij = 0: the edge is absent and the parameter equals zero with probability one) or is free to take a nonzero value (the slab component, γ ij = 1: the edge is present and the parameter is drawn from a continuous prior distribution, i.e., a Normal, Cauchy, or Beta-prime distribution). The full collection of indicators {γ ij } constitutes the network structure G, while the slab distribution serves as the prior on the partial association of each present edge.

The MCMC algorithm then updates the indicators and the parameters jointly. At each iteration, every edge is either included or excluded, and the partial association is sampled only when the edge is included; when the edge is excluded, the partial association is set to exactly zero. This is what produces the simultaneous edge selection and parameter estimation referenced in the main text, and explains why edges with low posterior inclusion probability are automatically shrunk toward zero in the structure-averaged posterior.

Slab priors on the partial association parameters.

The slab determines the prior plausibility of different effect sizes for present edges. Three families are available in the software: Normal (the default), Cauchy, and Beta-prime.

The choice of prior on the partial associations has consequences for both estimation and edge selection: because this prior also plays the role of the alternative hypothesis in the inclusion Bayes factor, a more diffuse prior makes it harder for the Bayes factor to favor inclusion when the true effect is small (a manifestation of the Jeffreys-Lindley paradox; see Lindley, 1957; Wagenmakers & Ly, 2022). This phenomenon should be taken into account when inspecting the results of the prior robustness analyses.

Interpretation of partial associations by variable type

Ordinal Markov random field and Ising model. For networks of binary or ordinal variables, the partial association θ ij is half the log adjacent-category odds ratio:

The adjacent-category odds ratio compares, for two variables X i and X j , how the odds of being in category c + 1 versus c for one variable X i change when the other variable X j moves up one category. This ratio is constant across all category pairs and does not depend on the values of the remaining variables. A positive θ ij means that observing a higher category on one variable X i is associated with a higher category on the other variable X j , after conditioning on all remaining variables. For binary variables, the model reduces to the Ising model (Ising, 1925), where the partial association has the same interpretation but with only two categories. In the software, both the partial associations and the log adjacentcategory odds ratios are provided. For easier communication, researchers can transform the log odds ratio into a percentage change by first exponentiating it to obtain the odds ratio (OR) and then computing (OR -1) × 100.

For networks of continuous variables, the partial association is related to the precision matrix Ω = Σ -1 (the inverse of the covariance matrix) by θ ij = -1 2 ω ij . A positive θ ij indicates that, holding all other variables constant, higher values of X i are associated with higher values of X j . When NUTS is used, sampler-specific diagnostics such as divergent transitions and energy-based measures are also available. These additional diagnostics are not currently available in JASP and are therefore not part of the reporting checklist in Table 2, however, they should be reported in case the analysis was run in R. Convergence diagnostics are also available for the pairwise co-clustering proportions when performing a clustering analysis using bgms.

Missing data handling

In R, the software offers two ways of handling missing observations. The default option is listwise deletion (also the option used in JASP), which removes all rows containing any missing values. This is appropriate when missingness is minimal and the missing-completelyat-random (MCAR) assumption is plausible. Alternatively, missing data imputation can be embedded directly in the MCMC algorithm. At each iteration, missing entries are drawn from their full conditional distribution given the current parameter values and the observed data. This approach uses all available data, propagates the uncertainty introduced by missingness into the posterior estimates, and is valid under the weaker missing-at-random (MAR) assumption. When a large proportion of observations have missing values (e.g., more than 10%), imputation typically outperforms listwise deletion in terms of bias and efficiency.
