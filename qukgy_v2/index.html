Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Full text

Abstract

Atypical prosody is a recognized feature of autism and a candidate marker for automated screening, yet findings replicate poorly across languages and tasks. We asked whether acoustic differences vary with development and individual skills, rather than diagnosis alone. We analyzed 376 US English parent-child home interactions (79,229 child turns) across six visits over two years from 32 autistic children (mean entry age 32.7 months) and 35 non-autistic children (20.3 months) matched on expressive language.

Bayesian hierarchical distributional models estimated group differences, developmental change, and turn-to-turn variability in five acoustic features, and their relation to socialization, language and motor skills. Causal graphs of the language-matched design guided control analyses for age, skill overlap, and speech sampling.

Autistic children showed higher and more variable pitch, faster articulation, and fewer, shorter pauses. Accounting for age removed the pause-count contrast, attenuated the articulationrate and pause-duration contrasts, and strengthened the pitch differences. At shared skill profiles, higher pitch variability disappeared, higher pitch and fewer, shorter pauses weakened, and faster articulation persisted. When non-autistic children's socialization or motor skills increased, they produced lower pitch and smaller pitch swings, respectively; autistic children showed neither association. Across visits, pitch and timing features became increasingly associated in autistic children, with little within-turn covariation in either group.

Group differences were present, but varied in size and pattern with age, skills, and feature. These findings caution against treating acoustic features as generalizable diagnostic markers and call for studying how speech acoustics vary with development and interactional context.

• Longitudinal study of 67 autistic and non-autistic children over two years.

• Acoustic differences track developmental age and skills, not only diagnosis.

• Socialization and motor skills show divergent acoustic mappings across groups.

• Causal graphs clarify language-matching assumptions and guide bias controls.

• Challenges the pursuit of diagnostic acoustic biomarkers for autism.

Introduction

Distinctive prosodic patterns in autistic speech have been reported from the very first descriptions of the condition (Asperger, 1991; Kanner, 1943). Clinicians, caregivers, and researchers continue to observe qualitative differences in pitch, rhythm, and pacing relative to non-autistic peers (Bonneh et al., 2011; Grice et al., 2023; McCann & Peppé, 2003; Shriberg et al., 2001). These differences are often perceptually salient: non-autistic listeners can detect vocal atypicality within seconds of exposure (Grossman, 2015), and brief samples of autistic speech elicit rapid, persistent impressions that shape listeners' willingness to engage socially (Sasson & Morrison, 2019). Clinicians likewise weigh atypical prosody when forming diagnostic impressions (Gabrielsen et al., 2015). These observations have motivated a substantial body of acoustic research aimed at identifying stable, replicable markers of autistic communication, sometimes to understand autism as a condition, for example, by mapping how vocal and speech features are shaped by neurodevelopmental differences and experiential input, but often with the aim of supporting or even replacing clinical screening.

However, there are reasons to be cautious about treating acoustic features as markers of autism. Deploying automated voice screening in non-clinical settings such as employment or education carries serious ethical risks (Rocca et al., 2024). Further, the very concept of a speech marker implies a monolithic, overly reductionist view of both speech and autism (Fletcher-Watson, 2024; Rocca et al., 2024). More fundamentally, speech acoustics vary with task, interlocutor, and interactional context, and therefore cannot be treated as context-invariant properties of a speaker (Cox et al., 2025; Dideriksen, Christiansen, Tylén, et al., 2023; R. Fusaroli et al., 2023b; Nielsen et al., 2026). Children, particularly autistic children, also differ widely in language, social, and motor skills, and these skills develop unevenly. A diagnostic label alone cannot capture this variation, which is central to understanding how acoustic patterns arise in interaction (Bottema-Beutel, 2017; Sterponi et al., 2015).

We therefore treat the idea of an "autistic voice" as a hypothesis with three claims. 1) Distinctiveness: autistic and non-autistic children can be distinguished acoustically. 2) Generalization: the differences generalize across cohorts and contexts. 3) Specificity: the differences arise from autism itself rather than from associated developmental and skill profiles. A related assumption is that group differences reflect delayed or impaired development, rather than a different developmental pathway.

Previous research on acoustic features

An early review of 16 perceptual studies of speech in autism found it difficult to draw any firm conclusions due to the small sample sizes and heterogeneity in methods (McCann & Peppé, 2003). This was followed up by a systematic review and meta-analysis of the quantitative analyses of distinctive acoustic profiles in autism (R. Fusaroli et al., 2017). A minimal acoustic profile of autism was identified, which included source and temporal features: higher pitch median (d ≈ 0.38) and greater pitch variability (d ≈ 0.48), and statistically unreliable trends for more frequent and longer pauses. The findings were replicated in successive meta-analyses, though largely based on the same data (Asghari et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2024). A cross-linguistic study attempted to systematically replicate the meta-analytic effects on US and Danish children (R. Fusaroli et al., 2022). Higher pitch and longer pausing did hold up; however, individual variability in acoustic patterns by age, sex, and clinical characteristics was found to be large.

Harnessing speech processing and machine learning techniques to more rigorously deal with a larger number of acoustic features has not stabilized the findings. Classifiers could be trained to discriminate autistic from non-autistic individuals at above chance levels, within one language and task. However, performance was practically at chance when applying to different languages or even to the same participants performing a very similar task (i.e. from retelling a story to describing a video) (Rybner et al., 2022). Further, when similar machine learning tools, as well as language models, were applied to a differential diagnostic context (i.e., discriminating among autistic individuals, non-psychiatric controls, and psychiatric patients with partially overlapping clinical and cognitive characteristics), performance was again practically at chance (Hansen et al., 2023).

Similar findings have been published for other conditions, like schizophrenia and Parkinsonism (Arora et al., 2019; Fumel et al., 2024; M. Fusaroli et al., 2023; Parola et al., 2026; Parola, Lin, et al., 2023; Parola et al., 2020; Parola, Simonsen, et al., 2023).

The notion of a universal "autistic voice" can thus be questioned: acoustic profiles are heterogeneous, context-dependent, and modulated by individual-level factors. Consistent with this, recent work combining individual-difference and network approaches finds that prosodic features cluster participants into communities that cut across the diagnostic boundary rather than tracking it, and that timing features are better predicted by language skill than by diagnosis (Liu et al., 2026; Weed et al., 2024). Converging evidence comes from cross-linguistic work on autistic children and research on intonation style in autistic adults, which highlight how group-level differences are subject to substantial individual variation and are modulated by speaker sex (R. Fusaroli et al., 2022; Wehrle, 2023; Wehrle et al., 2020).

Development and Individual Differences

Pitch, articulation rate, and pausing change as vocal-fold anatomy, laryngeal control, and articulatory motor control mature (Lee et al., 1999; Robb & Saxman, 1990; Smith & Zelaznik, 2004).

Speakers also modulate these features for expressivity, emphasis, planning, and accommodation to a partner (Cox et al., 2025; R. Fusaroli & Tylén, 2016; Wynn et al., 2023) and this modulation may depend on linguistic and social skills. Further, children do not develop their anatomy and skill profiles in lockstep. Children follow different trajectories, and children at the same age may differ substantially in the skills that shape their speech (Iverson et al., 2023). As articulatory control, linguistic resources, and conversational experience mature, children's speech may also become more consistently regulated, making within-turn and turn-to-turn variability informative indices of developing motor control (Smith & Zelaznik, 2004). Moreover, because speech requires coordinating laryngeal, respiratory, and articulatory subsystems, individual acoustic dimensions may become coupled or decouple as children learn to coordinate them across development (Smith & Zelaznik, 2004; Turvey, 1990). Cross-sectional group-mean designs cannot capture these dynamic developmental trajectories. They may also conceal group differences that vary with age, contributing to the between-study heterogeneity documented in meta-analyses (R. Fusaroli et al., 2017; R. Fusaroli et al., 2018).

Acoustic behavior depends on several interacting factors: children's developmental stage and individual skills, the task, and the dynamics of the interaction with their partner (Dideriksen, Christiansen, Dingemanse, et al., 2023; Dideriksen, Christiansen, Tylén, et al., 2023). The present study focuses on differences in language, adaptive socialization, and motor skill. We examine these dimensions in semi-structured play with a familiar caregiver, keeping the interactional setting relatively constant.

Following children over time lets us ask whether prosodic differences track diagnosis or their broader developmental profiles. If diagnosis alone explained the differences, a given skill should relate to speech acoustics in much the same way in both groups. If those relations differ by group, the pattern would be more consistent with different developmental mappings than with a simple developmental delay. Speech motor maturation also shapes the vocal patterns children can produce (Goffman, 1999; Smith & Zelaznik, 2004). Because pitch, rate, and pausing are produced by overlapping vocal systems, we also examine whether their relations to one another change across groups and visits.

The Present Study

We analyze a longitudinal corpus of semi-naturalistic caregiver-child interactions in which autistic and non-autistic children, matched on expressive language at the first visit, took part in six semistructured play sessions over approximately two years (R. Fusaroli et al., 2019; Naigles, 2021; Naigles & Fein, 2017). This design lets us compare the acoustic characteristics of children with similar expressive-language levels at entry, rather than differences in how much they speak. We examine five features identified in a previous meta-analysis: pitch median, pitch variability, articulation rate, pause count, and pause duration (R. Fusaroli et al., 2017).

Question 1: Do autistic and non-autistic children differ in the level, developmental change, and turn-to-turn variability of specific acoustic features? We estimate these group contrasts across the full sample and evaluate their stability against targeted control analyses that address the age gap, skill overlap, and session dynamics.

Question 2: Does acoustic distinctiveness survive adjustment for individual differences in communicative and motor skills, and do acoustic features relate to those skills in similar ways across groups? We evaluate whether group differences in acoustics persist once these skills are accounted for, or whether they reflect differences in underlying communicative and motor trajectories. Crucially, because longitudinal gains within an individual child can diverge from stable differences between children, we partition each skill into within-child developmental change and between-child individual differences.

Question 3: Does the internal organization of acoustic features diverge between autistic and non-autistic children, and does that organization change across development? At the between-child level, we test whether children who show higher values on one acoustic feature also exhibit higher values on another. At the turn level, we evaluate whether features move together from turn to turn within an ongoing interaction, testing whether autistic and non-autistic children organize and coordinate their speech subsystems differently over short time spans.

Together, these three questions test whether diagnosis alone provides an adequate account of acoustic differences, or whether development, individual skills, and covariation among features provide additional explanatory information.

Methods

Sample

Participants were 67 parent-child dyads drawn from a longitudinal corpus of early language development at the University of Connecticut (collected between 2005 and 2013; for full corpus description, see Naigles & Fein, 2017). Thirty-two children had a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD; mean age at first visit: 32.7 months, 95% CI [30.8, 34.5]; 4 girls, 28 boys), and 35 were non-autistic (TD; mean age at first visit: 20.3 months, 95% CI [19.8, 21]; 6 girls, 29 boys).

Children's sex was reported by their parents. In line with recommendations from autistic selfadvocates and recent style guidelines (Bottema-Beutel et al., 2021; Dwyer, 2022; Kenny et al., 2016), we adopt identity-first language ("autistic children" and "non-autistic children") throughout the text. To increase legibility in figures and tables, we retain the abbreviations ASD and TD where appropriate. The two groups were matched on expressive language ability; as a result, autistic children were older than non-autistic children at each visit. Note that while matching is crucial to ensure comparable baseline expressive abilities, it induces a within-sample association between chronological age and diagnosis that does not exist in the broader population (see Statistical Analysis and Supplementary Materials S1.3 for how we explicitly model and control for this confound). Autistic children were recruited through treatment facilities and schools in the Northeastern United States. They had been previously diagnosed with autistic disorder or pervasive developmental disorder-not otherwise specified by physicians or psychologists, and diagnosis was confirmed at study entry with the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Module 1 (ADOS; Lord et al., 2000).

All autistic children were receiving at least 20 hours per week of Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) intervention at the time of participation. Non-autistic children were recruited from a database maintained by the UConn Child Language Lab; they had no reported history of developmental concerns, language delay, or neurological diagnosis, and were additionally administered ADOS Module 1, with none showing elevated total scores. All children had English as their primary language. The study was approved by the University of Connecticut Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participating parents.

Standardized Assessments

At each visit, children were assessed using the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL; Mullen, 1995) for cognitive and language ability, and the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Second Edition (VABS-II; Sparrow et al., 2005) for adaptive behavior, including the Socialization and Motor Skills subscales. The Vineland-II Survey Interview Form scale is administered as a semi-structured parent interview, where responses are rated on a scale of 0 (never present), 1 (sometimes present), 2 (always present), or DK (don't know).

The socialization subscale consisted of items like: "Makes or tries to make social contact (e.g., smiles, makes noises, etc.)" or "Answers when familiar adults make small talk (e.g., if asked, "How are you?" says, "I'm fine"; if told, "You look nice," says, "Thank you"; etc.)." In autistic children, the Vineland scale has been found to strongly correlate with joint attention skills (Poon et al., 2012; Toth et al., 2006) and ADOS scores (Klin et al., 2007; Paul et al., 2014) and is frequently used as a measure of social competence in special populations like ADHD and ASD (Charman et al., 2001; Oswald & Ollendick, 1989). The motor subscale consisted of items such as "Walks downstairs facing forward, placing both feet on each step" and "opens and closes scissors with one hand" and "Uses twisting hand-wrist motion (e.g., winding up toy)." This subscale was normed on typically developing children from birth through age 6, and its validity for capturing developmental delays was assessed in additional autistic and intellectually disabled samples (Sparrow et al., 2005). Mean length of utterance in morphemes (MLU) was computed from manually verified transcripts at each visit, providing a direct index of structural language complexity that bears on speech pacing and articulatory planning (Chon et al., 2012; Ezeizabarrena & Garcia Fernandez, 2018; Scarborough et al., 1991).

Socialization scores, Motor Skills scores, and MLU values at each visit served as individualdifference predictors of acoustic behavior. These measures capture different developmental dimensions. Vineland scores are age-normed standard scores indexing adaptive competence relative to same-age peers, whereas MLU is an unstandardized measure of expressive complexity that goes hand in hand with language development. The differing relationships these metrics have with chronological age and their implications for causal inference are addressed under Statistical Analysis and in Supplementary Materials S1.4.

Corpus and Procedure

Families were scheduled for six home visits at approximately four-month intervals over two years.

At each visit, a trained researcher recorded 30 minutes of caregiver-child play in the home. The first 15 minutes followed the structure of the Screening Tool for Autism in Two-Year-Olds (STAT; Stone et al., 2000), comprising 12 play-based activities involving pretend play, interactive play with toys, imitative action, and joint-attention prompts. Parents were given index cards specifying each activity. The remaining 15 minutes involved unstructured free play, with parents instructed to play as they usually would at home.

Twenty-six sessions were not recorded because of equipment malfunctions or difficulties completing the visit. Of the 376 recorded sessions, 366 contained child turns meeting the acousticanalysis criteria, and 351 had complete assessment data across all three developmental skills.

Conversations were transcribed in CHAT format following CHILDES conventions (MacWhinney, 2014). Voice activity detection and speaker diarization were performed using the pipeline developed and validated for this corpus in R. Fusaroli et al. (2025). A turn was defined as a sequence of vocalization not interrupted by the interlocutor. After preprocessing, the corpus contained 158,274 speech turns, of which 79,229 were produced by children.

The corpus has previously supported studies of early language development and caregiver input (R. Fusaroli et al., 2019), linguistic alignment (R. Fusaroli et al., 2023a Fusaroli et al., , 2023b)), and turntaking timing (R. Fusaroli et al., 2025). The present study extends this work to the acoustic features of child speech. While the recordings cannot be shared due to ethical and privacy constraints, the data used in the analyses is provided in an open repository (see below) and anonymized transcripts are available at databrary.org/volume/1852.

Acoustic Feature Extraction

Five acoustic features were extracted from each speech turn identified by the diarization procedure: pitch median, pitch variability (IQR), articulation rate, pause count, and pause duration.

Extraction was performed using custom pipelines integrating Praat (Boersma, 2001) and openSMILE (Eyben et al., 2010).

Pitch was estimated as F0 extracted via sub-harmonic summation and Viterbi-smoothed across frames in openSMILE with default settings. Two pitch measures were derived per turn: pitch median (Hz), capturing the central tendency of F0 within a turn, and pitch IQR (Hz), capturing within-turn pitch variability. Turns with fewer than five voiced frames were set to missing prior to analysis.

Articulation rate was computed as the number of syllables per second of phonated speech (i.e., excluding within-turn silent intervals). Syllable nuclei were detected automatically from the audio, as intensity peaks that exceed a relative threshold above the surrounding signal and that coincide with voiced speech (de Jong & Wempe, 2009). Turns with fewer than two detected syllable nuclei were set to missing prior to analysis.

Two pause features were extracted from each turn: pause count, the number of within-turn silent intervals exceeding 200 ms, and mean pause duration, the average duration of those withinturn silent intervals in seconds. Turns with no within-turn pauses were retained and contributed zero counts; pause duration is defined only for turns with at least one pause.

Turns were flagged as likely noise-contaminated if pitch variability or articulation rate was exactly zero, which cannot arise from genuine speech: a voiced turn has non-zero within-turn pitch spread, and a turn with detected syllable nuclei has a non-zero rate. Because such a turn's acoustic estimates are unreliable as a set rather than feature by feature, all five features were set to missing for a flagged turn. Of the 79,229 child turns in the corpus, 71,537 (90.3%) survived this rule together with the voiced-frame and syllable-nucleus criteria above and entered the analyses. The proportion excluded was 8.7% in non-autistic and 10.8% in autistic children. Because the exclusion is applied jointly, all five features are estimated on the same set of turns.

Statistical Analysis

All models were estimated as Bayesian hierarchical distributional models using the brms package in R, with Stan as the backend sampler (Bürkner, 2017), except for the copula analysis, which was directly coded in Stan. Full priors, model structures, code and processed data are available on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24402593).

Distributional models condition not only the location parameter of the outcome distribution on predictors, but also its scale (and, where relevant, shape) parameters. In a standard linear regression (a Gaussian model), for instance, the mean is allowed to vary by predictors such as diagnostic group and time, while the residual variability (sigma) is treated as a single value shared across all observations. In contrast, a distributional model lets sigma itself vary by group and time, so that we can ask whether one group is more turn-to-turn heterogeneous than the other, alongside asking whether its mean is higher or lower. The same logic extends to the shape parameters of skewed or hurdle distributions, where group differences in skew or in the probability of a zero outcome can be estimated directly. Claims that one group is more heterogeneous concern the scale of the outcome distribution rather than its location. Modeling scale is also important because differences in variability can affect estimates of mean differences. We therefore use distributional models to estimate both.

Likelihood families were chosen to match the distributional properties of each outcome.

Pitch median, pitch IQR, and articulation rate are continuous, strictly positive, and right-skewed: values cluster around a typical range but occasional turns produce much higher values, and zero or negative values are not meaningful.

We therefore modeled them with lognormal distributions, which capture this skew on the natural scale by assuming the logarithm of the outcome is approximately Gaussian. This avoids the bias that a standard Gaussian likelihood would introduce when fit to skewed positive data (e.g., predicting negative pitch values in the left tail) and gives more accurate intervals.

Pause count required a different treatment. Many turns contain no within-turn pauses at all, as short turns or fluent stretches simply offer no opportunity for a pause to occur. This produces a two-part data-generating process: first, whether any within-turn pause occurs at all; second, given that at least one does, how many there are; a process that no single standard distribution captures well. We therefore used hurdle distributions that model these two parts separately: a Bernoulli component for the probability of producing a zero outcome (i.e., producing no within-turn pause), and a Poisson component, appropriate for counts of discrete events, for the count conditional on it being non-zero. Each component can be conditioned on predictors independently, which lets us ask, for example, whether autistic children differ from non-autistic children in how often they produce within-turn pauses at all, separately from how many they do. If the effects of both components (the no-pause probability and the Poisson count) go in the same direction (e.g. fewer zeros and more frequent pauses), we report effects at the expected outcome level, that is, combining both components. When the components diverge or when the no-pause probability is itself substantively informative (e.g. whether groups differ in how often they produce any within-turn pause at all), we report them separately. Mean pause duration, defined only for turns with at least one pause, operates without a hurdle component: it is positive and right-skewed, and was modeled as lognormal with linear and quadratic terms in log turn duration, centered at 2s. Estimates are reported as posterior medians and 95% compatibility intervals (CIs), that is, the parameter values compatible with the combination of data and model assumptions. We complement these with evidence ratios (ERs), computed as the portion of the posterior favoring a directional hypothesis divided by the portion against it. For example, the ER for a positive group difference is the proportion of posterior samples in which the difference is positive divided by the proportion in which it is null or negative. As a rough heuristic, we describe ERs above 10 as strong evidence, between 3 and 10 as moderate, and below 3 as weak or uncertain, while keeping in mind that any such cutoffs are conventional (Wagenmakers et al., 2011).

Statistical analysis: individual differences (Aim 2)

To investigate whether developmental change in acoustic behavior is better captured by individual differences in communicative skill than by time alone, and whether those skill-acoustic relationships differ between groups, we extended the previous structure with three individualdifference predictors, child MLU, Vineland Socialization, and Vineland Motor Skills. Visits missing any of the three skill scores were left out of these models, which therefore rest on 351 of the 366 analyzed visits (96%) and 64 of the 67 children. Estimating a single slope per skill conflates two distinct questions: whether children with higher overall adaptive skills sound different from their peers (stable between-child differences), and whether an individual child sounds different at visits when their skills are higher than their own average (within-child developmental change). To resolve this, we separated each skill into within-child developmental deviations and between-child individual differences (Mundlak, 1978), both standardized using the pooled within-group standard deviation of each skill (Supplementary Materials S3.1). Both components entered in interaction with diagnostic group, so that the model estimates separate within-and between-child skillacoustic slopes for autistic and non-autistic children and the contrast between them. The same fixed-effect structure was applied to μ, to σ and, for pause count, to the hurdle probability. The location model carried by-participant random intercepts, developmental slopes and random slopes on the three within-child components (the between-child components are constant within a child), estimated separately by group, with regularizing priors on the slope standard deviations; the scale sub-model kept a group-specific random intercept only, because letting slopes vary by participant on the scale parameter prevented stable sampling. To evaluate remaining diagnostic differences after accounting for individual skills, we compare groups within the skill profiles they share, defined in two ways: narrowly defined (strict shared) profiles, children whose average scores on all three skills lie inside the range both groups occupy (16 non-autistic and 4 autistic children), and wider shared profiles, visits inside that range (31 non-autistic and 25 autistic children; Supplementary Materials S1.3c).

Control Analyses: Addressing Age and Skill Overlap (Aims 1 and 2)

The language-based matching that assembled this sample creates a within-sample age-diagnosis association that does not exist in the population (S1.3). Five control analyses (Controls 1-5; Supplementary Table S3) assess the impact of this design-induced association from different directions; we treat a finding as robust only when it holds across them. By rules set before examining the results (Supplementary Materials S1.6), a contrast holds under a control when it keeps its evidence tier (strong or moderate), weakens when it drops from strong to moderate, and is lost when its ER falls below 3. These control analyses answer three questions: whether a difference holds when the groups are compared at the same ages (the three age refits), whether a group difference in skill slope holds where both groups have the same skill scores (common support), and whether it reflects how much the child talked rather than the skill (session covariates).

Control 1: age axis (Aim 1). We re-estimated the primary group and developmental models with time indexed by absolute chronological age rather than visit number, centered at 30 months, so that both groups are evaluated at identical ages. While this retains all observations, the groups' age distributions overlap only partially; consequently, at ages where one group has sparse data, estimates rely more heavily on parametric extrapolation. Developmental slopes are evaluated across 24-42 months, the range most densely occupied by both groups.

Control 2: shared age band (Aims 1 and 2). We restricted the data to the participant-visits falling inside the intersection of the two groups' observed age ranges (18.8-45.1 months; 275 of the 355 analyzed visits with a recorded age, 77%) and refit both the developmental and the individualdifferences models. This avoids extrapolation across non-overlapping ages at the cost of discarding the youngest non-autistic and oldest autistic visits. A group contrast that holds on the full age axis (Control 1) but is lost in the shared age band (Control 2) indicates an effect driven primarily by the non-overlapping age tails.

Control 3: common support (Aim 2). A group by skill interaction estimated over the full sample can be carried by regions of the skill range that only one group occupies, where the group contrast is extrapolation rather than actual comparison. For each of the three skills, we identified the shared range, restricted both groups to the observations falling inside it on all three skills simultaneously (retaining 281 of the 351 visits, i.e., 80%, and 63 of the 64 children) and refit the individual difference interaction models. This control eliminates skill extrapolation while leaving chronological age free to vary (Crump et al., 2009).

Control 4: skill-model age axis (Aim 2). The visit-indexed individual-difference models adjust the skill slopes for time in the study but not for age at entry, which among children matched on language is itself related to the skills: in autistic children, all three skills are lower in children who are older at a given visit (Supplementary Materials S1.4). We therefore refit these models with time indexed by chronological age, which adjusts every skill slope for age and compares developmental slopes at the same ages in both groups. The control analysis cannot remove the near-collinearity of MLU with age in non-autistic children (r ≈ .74, against r ≈ .02 in autistic children), so in that group a developmental slope adjusted for MLU remains weakly identified.

Control 5: session covariates (Aim 2). MLU and the acoustic features are two summaries of a single speech sample, so anything that affects how many turns the child produced, or how long they spoke, can affect both and create a confound. Therefore, we refit the individual differences models adding the two observable summaries of the sample (turn count and mean turn duration, computed per speaker per visit), standardized like the skills and interacting with group and interlocutor so each group carries its own slope (Supplementary Materials S1.2, S5.4).

Supplementary Materials S1.5 maps each control onto an explicit operation on the causal graph that generated these data, and S1.6 provides an interpretive framework for reading the pattern of results across controls. See also Supplementary Table S1 for an overview and Supplementary Table S4 for the full analytical reference matrix.

Covariation networks among acoustic features (Aim 3)

Acoustic features may covary, and this dependence can reveal whether their organization differs between groups or changes over development. A first dimension of covariation is whether children who are high on one feature tend to be high on another. To characterize such profile-level dependence among acoustic features, we computed Pearson and partial correlation matrices from the model estimates of the individual-level values (the expected value of each child at each of the six visits, from the group-level and child-specific estimates). We summarize each network by the signed mean of its ten edges and by their mean absolute value. Intervals carry the sampling of children as well as model uncertainty: at each posterior draw, the correlations are computed with Bayesian-bootstrap weights over children (Rubin, 1981).

A second dimension of covariation is whether within a single child at a single visit, a turn that is high on one feature tends to be high on another, a statement about how features are coupled in the local act of speaking. To characterize the turn by turn level dependence amongst features, we employed a generalized Gaussian Copula approach, adjusted to the likelihoods used for our data (Joe, 2014; Joe & Xu, 1996). The copula therefore isolates within-child, within-visit residual dependence, complementing the descriptive networks which capture between-child covariation at each visit. Fitting the margins first and their dependence second stands in for a joint model of the five features, which their different likelihood families make impractical; the copula is fit to 20 posterior draws of the margins, so their uncertainty carries into the network (Supplementary Materials S7.2). For each measure we estimated six 5x5 networks separately for autistic and nonautistic children. Two checks of the between-child networks, one built from the children's observed visit means and one from the age-axis refits with every child evaluated at the same ages, are reported with the results (Supplementary Materials S7.1, S7.5).

Group and Developmental Differences (Aim 1)

Averaged across visits, autistic children produced speech with higher pitch median than nonautistic children (270.84 Hz vs. 252.11 Hz; difference = +18.63 Hz, 95% CI [-2.8, +40.6]; ER = 21.5), a difference of about 0.5 between-child standard deviations that held, with stronger evidence, on the age axis and in the shared age band (Controls 1 and 2; Table 3). Caregiver median pitch was virtually identical across groups (324 Hz for non-autistic vs. 328 Hz for autistic dyads; Supplementary Table S8), indicating that the child pitch elevation cannot be attributed to differences in caregiver vocal pitch modeling or infant-directed speech register. A sensitivity refit excluding child turns with median pitch < 150 Hz made the contrast about a quarter smaller with unchanged directional evidence (13.90 Hz [-1.72, 28.85], ER = 22.8 without < 150 Hz turns vs. 18.63 Hz [-2.85, 40.57], ER = 21.5 in the full sample; Supplementary Table S9). Both groups showed a negative developmental slope (ASD: -3.11 Hz per visit, 95% CI [-5.4, -0.7]; TD: -4.17 Hz per visit, 95% CI [-7.2, -1.7]; contrast ER = 2.70). There was moderate evidence that the autistic group was more variable in their pitch median across turns (sigma, estimated on the log scale, 0.265 vs. 0.251; ER = 4.49). Turn-to-turn heterogeneity decreased over time in the autistic but not in the non-autistic group (ASD: -0.005 per visit, ER = 18.0; TD: +0.001, ER = 1.66; contrast ER = 8.35). Both the baseline difference and the developmental decrease in heterogeneity held on the age axis (Control 1), whereas only the developmental decrease held in the shared age band (Control 2; Supplementary Materials S5.2).

Autistic children showed greater within-turn pitch variability (ASD: 103.33 Hz [95% CI 94.9, 112.9]; TD: 91.28 Hz [95% CI 83.8, 100.8]; group difference = +12.03 Hz [95% CI -0.8, +24.0]; ER = 30.0), about 0.8 between-child standard deviations, a contrast that held, with stronger evidence, under both age controls (Table 3). Both groups decreased in pitch variability over time (ASD: -3.48 Hz per visit [95% CI -5.24, -1.70], ER > 1000; TD: -4.00 Hz per visit [95% CI -6.21, -1.68], ER > 1000, contrast ER = 1.72). There was moderate evidence that autistic children showed slightly lower turn-to-turn heterogeneity in pitch variability (sigma, on the log scale, 0.975 vs. 1.007; ER = 6.07). This heterogeneity decreased over development in the autistic but not in the non-autistic group (ASD: -0.012 per visit, ER = 49.0; TD: -0.001, ER = 1.13; contrast ER = 6.58).

Thus, autistic children showed greater pitch variability within individual turns while showing somewhat less turn-to-turn heterogeneity in that variability. The heterogeneity difference was lost under both age controls; the difference in its trajectory held under both (Supplementary Materials S5.2). Excluding turns < 150 Hz similarly preserved the elevated pitch variability in autistic children, again about a quarter smaller (contrast = 9.12 Hz [-1.34, 20.01], ER = 19.7 without < 150 Hz turns vs. 12.03 Hz [-0.77, 23.99], ER = 30.0 in the full sample; Supplementary Table S9).

Autistic children spoke faster than non-autistic children (ASD: 4.50 syll/s [95% CI 4.36, 4.64]; TD 4.18 syll/s [95% CI 3.99, 4.39]; group difference = +0.31 syll/s [95% CI 0.056, 0.566]; ER = 132), a difference that was smaller on the age axis but held under both age controls (Table 3). There was at most moderate evidence for articulation rate increasing across visits over the study period (ASD: 0.017 syll/s per visit; 95% CI [-0.023, 0.062]; ER = 4.14; TD: 0.014 syll/s per visit; 95% CI [-0.039, 0.066]; ER = 2.22; contrast ER = 1.21). Autistic children exhibited lower turn-toturn heterogeneity in articulation rate (σ, on the log scale, 0.345 vs. 0.382; ER = 27.2), and this heterogeneity declined developmentally in both groups, more steeply in the autistic one (ASD: -0.007 per visit, ER = 104; TD: -0.004, ER = 5.29; contrast ER = 3.60). The steeper autistic decline in heterogeneity held in the shared age band but was lost on the age axis; the level difference was lost under both (Supplementary Materials S5.2). In a turn of 2s, close to the median turn length, autistic children produced fewer within-turn pauses than non-autistic children at the average visit (ASD: 0.54 [95% CI 0.42, 0.67]; TD: 0.66 [95% CI 0.56, 0.77] pauses; group difference = -0.12 [95% CI -0.29, +0.04], ER = 14.0), and more often produced none (probability of a turn without pauses, ASD: 0.64 [95% CI 0.54, 0.72]; TD: 0.56 [95% CI 0.49, 0.63]; ER = 12.0). Both groups paused less over development (TD: -0.031 [95% CI -0.064, +0.004] pauses per visit, ER = 24.8; ASD: -0.034 [95% CI -0.075, +0.014], ER = 12.1; contrast ER = 1.21), and the probability of a turn without pauses rose in both (TD: +0.020 per visit, ER = 21.1; ASD: +0.021, ER = 9.18; contrast ER = 1.12). The group difference was lost under both age controls (Table 3). Pause duration is reported for a turn of 6s, the median length of the turns that contain a pause. There was strong evidence that autistic children produced shorter pauses than non-autistic children (ASD: 0.885 [95% CI 0.822, 0.957] s; TD: 0.976 [95% CI 0.918, 1.031] s; group difference = -0.089 [95% CI -0.176, +0.004] s, ER = 34.1), a difference that held on the age axis but weakened in the shared age band (Table 3), and is smaller in shorter turns (ER = 7.16 for a 2s turn). Pauses shortened over development in non-autistic children (-0.023 [95% CI -0.033, -0.014] s per visit, ER > 1000) but barely in autistic children (-0.006 [95% CI -0.021, +0.009] s per visit, ER = 4.23; slope contrast ER = 34.4), a contrast that held on the age axis but was lost in the shared age band (Supplementary Materials S5.3). The groups did not differ in the turn-to-turn heterogeneity of pause duration.

Sensitivity analyses indicate that these group differences are quite robust to unmeasured confounders. For instance, if we imagine a binary factor more prevalent in the autistic group by 20% compared to the non-autistic group would need to shift pitch median by 2.5 between-child standard deviations (93 Hz), pitch variability by 3.8 (60 Hz), and articulation rate by 4.2 (1.6 syll/s) to produce the effects we observe, all implausibly large (Supplementary Materials S1.3d; Figure S5).

In summary, Aim 1 shows that autistic children produced higher pitch median, greater within-turn pitch variability, faster articulation, and fewer and shorter within-turn pauses than language-matched non-autistic peers. Controlling for the ~1-year age gap preserved and even strengthened the pitch differences, attenuated the articulation-rate and pause-duration differences, and eliminated the pause-count difference. Turn-to-turn heterogeneity (sigma) narrowed faster over development in autistic children for pitch median and pitch variability, even when age was controlled.

Covariation Networks (Aim 3)

We examined how the acoustic features covary over development, and whether their associations reflect turn-level relations or differences between children's acoustic profiles. Edges represent residual covariation after each child's baseline level and developmental trajectory have been accounted for (edges and nodes drawn as in Figure 4). In both groups, features are only weakly coupled turn by turn: pitch median with pitch variability and with articulation rate, positively, and pause count with pause duration, negatively (in a turn of a given length, more pauses are shorter ones); averaged across visits, every other edge stays at or below |r| = .10. across groups. We estimated group differences in feature levels, turn-to-turn dispersion, and covariation across children and within turns.

Consistent with previous work, we found that autistic children exhibit distinct acoustic profiles in conversational interaction: higher and more variable pitch, faster articulation, and fewer and shorter pauses than language-matched non-autistic children. These effects were not equally impacted by control analyses. Compared at the same age, the pitch differences strengthened, the rate and pause-duration differences attenuated, and the pause-count difference disappeared.

Compared at the skill profiles the groups share (MLU, motor skills, socialization), the pitchvariability difference disappeared and faster articulation remained, while the evidence for higher pitch and fewer pauses depended on how narrowly the shared profiles were drawn. Autistic children also showed a steeper developmental narrowing of turn-to-turn dispersion across visits, which held in the shared age band. Covariation among acoustic features diverged across groups but only at later visits, and this structure was primarily between children: once individual profiles and developmental trajectories were accounted for, little group-specific covariation remained at the turn level. The groups did not simply follow the same developmental trajectories at different rates: the relationships between individual skills and acoustic features differed across groups.

The diagnostic-marker interpretation

Of the three claims of the strongest version of the "autistic voice" hypothesis (see Introduction), distinctiveness has the strongest support in previous work: thin-slice and first-impression studies find that listeners perceive differences in autistic speech, and prosody is among the features clinicians weigh when forming diagnostic impressions (Gabrielsen et al., 2015; Grossman, 2015; Sasson & Morrison, 2019). Our findings also support distinctiveness in this sample (higher pitch median, wider within-turn pitch variability, faster articulation, and fewer and shorter within-turn pauses). They provide more limited support for specificity: group differences varied across features and, for pitch variability, disappeared when the groups were compared over shared skill profiles.

Evidence for generalization is mixed. Our results suggest one reason why group differences may not transfer across studies: if their magnitude depends on the age and skill profiles of a cohort, they should not necessarily generalize to cohorts with different profiles. Group differences in level were also hard to explain away by unmeasured common causes of diagnosis and acoustics (such as unrecorded clinical, emotional, or physiological factors). An unmeasured factor would need to shift each feature by several between-child standard deviations to account for the group contrasts (Results; Supplementary Materials S1.3d), so a single unmeasured confounder is unlikely to explain them.

Higher and more variable pitch matches meta-analytic estimates in direction, and the differences of about 0.5 and 0.8 between-child standard deviations are comparable to or larger than the pooled effects (d ≈ 0.38 and 0.48; R. Fusaroli et al., 2017). Caregivers' pitch did not differ between groups (Supplementary Table S8), so the children's higher pitch is not simply inherited from the register addressed to them. Pausing behaved differently from earlier findings: the metaanalytic trends pointed, unreliably, to more frequent and longer pauses, and longer pauses held up in a cross-linguistic replication (R. Fusaroli et al., 2022), whereas here autistic children paused less often and more briefly. Our pauses are measured within the child's conversational turns with a familiar caregiver at home and conditioned on turn length, so differences in how pausing is defined and in the speaking situation may account for part of the discrepancy, as may age: here the pausecount difference was lost at a common age and the pause-duration difference weakened in the shared age band (Table 3). Faster articulation also contrasts with previous results: the most recent meta-analysis reports near-null pooled effects for speech rate and duration (Ma et al., 2024), and the nearest longitudinal comparison finds slower articulation in autistic children, attributable to language skill rather than diagnosis (Liu et al., 2026). Part of the difference may lie in the design: matching on expressive language removes, by construction, the part of the group difference that runs through language at entry (Supplementary Materials S1.3a), which is where Liu et al. locate the slower autistic articulation.

Turning to specificity: once language, socialization, and motor development are in the models, how much the diagnostic label still adds depends on the feature. For pitch variability it adds nothing. For pause duration it adds little, and nothing at a common age. For pitch height the difference did not shrink: at shared profiles it was larger than the unadjusted one (33 vs.

19 Hz), but its uncertainty grew, and the evidence was strong only at the strict shared profiles, which contain four autistic children. Pause frequency behaved similarly and, unlike the unadjusted difference, did not vanish at a common age. Articulation rate is the difference the skills explain least and the age gap explains most: it stayed strong at shared profiles and became moderate once age was also equalized. The age gap thus inflated the rate difference with or without skill adjustment, whereas the masking of pitch seen in the unadjusted contrasts did not recur once the skills were modeled. Crucially, these findings are about the relative information in the different predictors, not yet an identification of causes, since they could reflect different causal structures (Supplementary Materials S1.3c). Why does part of the articulation-rate difference resist skill adjustment when pitch variability does not? Beyond age, it may reflect what the measured skills leave out: general adaptive motor scales do not tap speech-motor control. While the Vineland motor domain captures broad adaptive milestones (e.g., walking downstairs, manual coordination), it does not directly measure the millisecond-level articulatory kinematics or speech-motor coordination required for speech pacing.

A residual rate difference could instead reflect speech-specific motor timing constraints, altered sensory-motor feedback loops, or differences in linguistic formulation. For instance, it has been argued that autistic children often produce memorized multi-word phrases (Prizant, 1983; Sterponi & Shankey, 2014), and phrases retrieved whole may be produced faster than utterances assembled word by word. It could also be possible that they are less responsive to their listener's presumed or observed comprehension of their utterances, which might lead them to speak at a rate more consistent with their motor or linguistic formulations rather than with their listener's comprehension; or on the contrary, that they optimize for a faster tempo, which has been shown to be associated with higher affiliation in conversations (Templeton et al., 2022) and is also shown in how quickly they respond to their interlocutors (Cox et al., 2025; R. Fusaroli et al., 2025).

These results complicate the diagnostic interpretation of acoustic differences. Pitch height and variability, the features most often treated as prosodic markers of autism, remained different after age adjustment, but pitch variability no longer differed when the groups were compared at shared skill profiles. Articulation rate remained somewhat higher in autistic children after skill adjustment, but only moderately so once age was also taken into account. Thus, acoustic features may distinguish groups within a particular cohort or setting without being stable properties of autism across cohorts and contexts. The poor cross-context generalizability of acoustic classifiers may at least partly reflect this dependence on the age and skill profiles of the sampled children (Hansen et al., 2023; Rybner et al., 2022). Differences between studies may therefore reflect differences in cohort composition as well as sample size and analytic choices. Our own longitudinal data illustrate this possibility: an isolated study at the first visit would have found shorter pauses in the autistic children's speech and similar feature networks in the two groups, whereas a study performed on the same children two years later would find a narrower pause-duration gap and different networks of features in the two groups. Matching choices add to this cohort dependence.

Studies of autistic prosody match their groups on age, on language, or on nonverbal IQ, and each choice conditions on a different variable that diagnosis affects, so each targets a different contrast, and relatedly constrains the variance in the control group, which usually gets recruited to specifically match the harder to recruit autistic group.

Cohort dependence is also an argument for interpretable features over black-box ones, since only the former let us check whether a classifier's success rests on the skill and age profile of its training cohort rather than on diagnosis. It also raises a question for the perceptual literature.

Listeners detect something within seconds, but it is not clear what that is. For instance, they may be attuned to cues of atypicality in general rather than autism-specific cues; or to whichever contrast is most salient in a given cohort and task.

Developmental delay or differential recruitment

The skill-acoustic mappings fall into three patterns. First, language mapped onto acoustics similarly in both groups: at visits when a child's MLU was above their own average, they spoke faster and paused longer. Second, several within-child mappings were group-specific but in complementary domains: at visits of above-average socialization or motor skill, non-autistic children spoke with lower pitch and less pitch variability, respectively, whereas autistic children showed near-zero pitch slopes; conversely, at visits of above-average motor skill (and across children with higher average motor skill), autistic children produced more pause-free turns and fewer pauses overall, whereas non-autistic children's overall pause counts did not change. Third, between children, two mappings ran in opposite directions: higher average motor skill went with longer pauses in nonautistic children and shorter pauses in autistic children, converging with autistic children's fewer overall pauses, and higher average socialization went with faster speech in non-autistic children and, as a trend, slower speech in autistic children. A fourth apparent pattern, fewer pauses at visits of above-average MLU in non-autistic children only, was lost under common support and in the shared age band, and probably reflects MLU's close tracking of age in that group (Table 4).

These patterns are more consistent with differential recruitment than with a simple developmental-delay account. If autistic children followed the same developmental trajectories as non-autistic children, differing only in their position along those trajectories, the same skills should relate to acoustics in the same direction in both groups. The evidence differs in strength. Oppositesigned slopes provide the clearest evidence. Here they are between-child associations, which are more exposed to stable confounders such as entry age (Supplementary Materials S1.4), but both held under every control, including the skill-model age axis, which adjusts for entry age through current age (Table 4). Differences between present and absent slopes are more common and also more nuanced; because they are within-child associations, they are less exposed to confounders that are stable within a child. The main ones also held across the controls: pitch variability with motor skill, the pause rate with socialization and pitch median with socialization, all under Controls 2-5 (Table 4).

One alternative interpretation is that the near-zero pitch slopes in autistic children reflect reduced modulation of vocal pitch with socialization or motor skills. Yet that interpretation does not fit the broader picture: autistic children's speech timing varied not only with language (withinchild MLU), just as in non-autistic peers, but also with motor skill (where visits of above-average motor skill went with fewer overall pauses in autistic children rather than narrower pitch variability as in non-autistic children), and between children, skills predicted pause duration and speech rate in opposite directions across groups. Thus, rather than pointing to a general deficit in prosodic modulation, our findings suggest that autistic and non-autistic children recruit and coordinate communicative and motor skills differently as they develop. Note that these differences might be more or less effective in social interactions, and their effectiveness depend also on interlocutors' expectations and skills. Nevertheless, they should not be read as a general lack of prosodic modulation or communicative responsiveness.

A related caution is that our measures of motor and social skills depend on caregiver reports, and a child's acoustic behavior, such as atypical pitch variability or pausing, could affect the caregiver's perception of the child's competence and therefore their Vineland ratings. In other words, not only might the child's acoustics be a reflection of their skills in other domains, their acoustics might also shape their skill scores.

Dispersion and covariation among acoustic features

Beyond mean levels, the groups differed in turn-to-turn dispersion (σ) and in how it changed.

Because dispersion narrows with maturation, the older autistic group is expected to look less dispersed on the visit axis, and it did for pitch variability and articulation rate, but these level differences did not hold at a common age. Pitch median ran the other way: autistic children were somewhat more dispersed, against the direction age would be expected to produce, a difference that held on the age axis but not in the shared age band. The trajectories instead held under both age controls. Dispersion in pitch median and pitch variability narrowed faster across visits in autistic children, moderately on the age axis and strongly in the shared age band (Supplementary Table S18). These trajectories have at least two possible interpretations. In developmental motor control, decreasing variability usually indexes consolidation, the stabilization of control with maturation (Smith & Zelaznik, 2004), which is how we framed dispersion in the Introduction.

Faster narrowing could instead indicate early canalization into a narrower expressive band that adapts less to changing interactional demands (Stergiou & Decker, 2011; Wehrle et al., 2024). Our data cannot separate the two, because they do not measure how dispersion responds to demand; the structured and unstructured halves of each session offer a direct test. Either way, part of autistic children's distinctive vocal impression may be grounded less in any single turn than in how consistent they sound across turns.

The covariation analyses add a crucial finding: whereas cross-sectional prosodic networks cluster speakers into communities that cut across diagnosis (Weed et al., 2024), tracking feature organization longitudinally showed that over development autistic children's acoustic profiles became organized along a single axis, so that a child high on one feature tended to be high on the others, while non-autistic children's did not. Two couplings were common to both groups: pitch median and variability covary across children and across turns, and pause count and pause duration covary positively across children but negatively within a turn of a given length, where more pauses are shorter ones. The groups differ in how the profile develops: by Visit 6, autistic children's features are more strongly and more positively coupled across children, most of all the links of pausing with pitch. The same divergence appears in correlations among the children's observed visit means, so it is not produced by the linear trajectories the networks are built from (Supplementary Materials S7.1).

The pause features show why the level of analysis matters: across children, those who pause more also pause longer, as a single factor such as planning load would predict, whereas within a turn of a given length more pauses are shorter ones, as if the turn's silence were shared between the pauses. The covariation between the two pitch features is harder to interpret, since median and IQR are formally distinct summaries (one of location, one of spread) that need not move together, and they do not obviously serve the same communicative function, although a high and widely modulated pitch may jointly convey arousal, enthusiasm or engagement (Banse & Scherer, 1996). A simpler explanation could be the measurement scale. Pitch variability is measured in Hz, and absolute F0 spread scales with F0 level; on a relative scale (IQR over median) the turn-level association all but disappears (Supplementary Materials S2.2), so the edge seems largely a property of the measure rather than of the speaker.

The connections between pitch and pausing are less expected still, and they diverge between the groups over development. At Visit 1 the two groups look alike: children with higher pitch pause somewhat more often. By Visit 6 these links have faded in non-autistic children, while in autistic children they have strengthened, so that an autistic child who is high on one feature tends to be high on the others. In dynamical systems and motor control frameworks (Bernstein, 1967; R. Fusaroli et al., 2014; R. Fusaroli & Tylén, 2016; Latash, 2008; Turvey, 1990), systematic covariation represents a functional reduction in the degrees of freedom of the behavioral space.

Because these are between-child correlations, what becomes more constrained is the space of profiles autistic children occupy as a group, not, as far as these data show, any one child's repertoire: autistic children increasingly differ from one another along a single dimension rather than several.

Two caveats preclude reading this as an online speech-motor synergy. First, the structure does not appear turn by turn: in the copula, features remain largely uncoupled within individual turns. The copula is a lower bound, since the child-specific intercepts and slopes it conditions on may absorb some within-child coupling, and because turn length enters only the pause margins, its pitch-pause edges measure coupling beyond turn length, as do the between-child edges, whose pause values are predicted at a fixed turn length (Supplementary Materials S7.1, S7.2, S7.4). The wider age spread among autistic children could also produce the between-child divergence, but it does not do so alone: with every child evaluated at the same ages, the divergence was smaller but still grew with age (Results; Supplementary Materials S7.5). Second, because synergetic reductions in degrees of freedom are inherently person-specific coordinative structures, population-pooled networks cannot be assumed to characterize individual children (the ergodicity problem; Fisher et al., 2018; Molenaar, 2004). Without qualitative coding (e.g., distinguishing hesitations from syntactic junctures or breaths, assessing preceding sentence complexity, identifying diverse speech acts), systematic contextual demand variations, and idiographic, person-specific networks (Bringmann et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2026), whether this divergence reflects a functional adaptation or a narrowing of options remains open. It converges with the faster narrowing of turn-to-turn dispersion: in two independent analyses, autistic vocal behavior becomes more constrained over development. Whether that constraint is consolidation, adaptation to different communicative goals, or reduced flexibility is, we think, the central question these results raise.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations qualify the interpretation of these findings and point to directions for future work. First, matching on early expressive language left the autistic group about a year older at every visit, and inside the sample entry age and diagnosis are almost collinear (r = .84). Rather than treat this as a nuisance to adjust away, we modeled the design explicitly. A causal graph of recruitment, development, and measurement (Supplementary Materials S1) shows that matching makes study inclusion a collider, and it yields specific predictions, which we explored before implementing the control analyses and assessing their results (Supplementary Materials S1.6).

Level contrasts should be exposed to the age gap and slopes much less so; entering entry age as a covariate would extrapolate across empty data space rather than remove the confound; and the bias should have a known direction for each feature, masking the pitch differences and inflating the rate and pause-duration differences. For the unadjusted contrasts, the age controls behaved as predicted: the pitch contrasts strengthened, articulation rate and pause duration attenuated, and the pause-count difference disappeared. Separating developmental timing from diagnosis more cleanly will require combining language-matched and age-matched comparison groups, or creating corpora large enough to match on both, for example with propensity scores.

Second, both our linguistic predictor (MLU) and our acoustic measures were extracted from the same recorded speech turns. Consequently, session-level dynamics, such as how frequently and how long a child speaks, can simultaneously influence both linguistic complexity and acoustic pacing. Diagnostic checks in our sample indicate that this relationship differs somewhat by group: turn count correlates more strongly with MLU and articulation rate in the autistic group, whereas mean turn duration correlates with both metrics across both groups (Supplementary Materials S1.2, S1.4, S5.4).

Conditioning on the two observable summaries of the session left most contrasts in place (Table 4; the within-child socialization contrast of articulation rate was lost), but it cannot settle the question: the summaries are not the session itself, and because skills shape how much a child talks, conditioning on them also removes substantive paths from skills through talkativeness to acoustics (Supplementary Materials S5.4). Individual-difference contrasts involving MLU must therefore be interpreted with greater caution than those involving independent caregiver assessments (the Vineland scales), though jointly estimating them exposes the Vineland slopes to the shared recording as well. Future work would benefit from decoupling linguistic complexity from acoustic extraction, for example, by contrasting structured communicative elicitations against spontaneous free play, or evaluating utterance-level syntactic and prosodic planning.

Third, our corpus focuses on a single interactional context-semi-structured play with a familiar caregiver at home-within a demographically homogeneous, predominantly male sample in which all autistic children received intensive early behavioral intervention. Consequently, our findings characterize acoustic recruitment under high support (of a specific kind) and familiar interactive conditions. Whether these developmental trajectories generalize to autistic children with different intervention histories, to autistic girls, or to families from more diverse cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic backgrounds remains an important empirical question. Finally, prosodic patterns are deeply shaped by the communicative setting; examining acoustics across diverse interactional settings (Cox et al., 2025) will be critical for determining how acoustic distinctiveness manifests outside caregiver-child play, and with varying cognitive and social demands.

Fourth, our measures of individual skills and speech execution remain coarse. The Vineland motor subscale captures general adaptive motor milestones rather than the fine-grained oral-motor synergies and respiratory coordination required for speech articulation and vocal modulation (Smith & Zelaznik, 2004), while clinical dimensions such as sensory sensitivities and repetitive behaviors were unrecorded. On the acoustic side, automatic pitch and syllable tracking, while robust to sensitivity checks like excluding the 4-5% of turns susceptible to toddler pitch-halving errors (Supplementary Table S9), cannot replace manual phonetic scrutiny of voice quality, intonation-contour morphology, or turn-by-turn interpersonal coordination. Moreover, developmental trajectories were modeled linearly across visits (checked against observed visit means in Supplementary Materials S4.2). Future work should complement automated acoustic extraction with direct measures of speech-motor kinematics and qualitative conversation analysis (see also R. Fusaroli et al., 2025).

Finally, we must draw a principled distinction between characterizing how acoustic features operate in naturalistic dialogue and deploying acoustic classifiers for automated screening or diagnostic prediction. These findings should not be taken as a rationale for using acoustic features to screen or diagnose children. Any translational work should be developed in partnership with autistic individuals, families, and clinicians, and should be guided by the purposes and settings in which it may be used (Chandler et al., 2022; Diaz-Asper et al., 2024; Rocca et al., 2024). Here, the aim is to understand how children with different developmental profiles use speech acoustics in interaction, and to warn against reductionistic approaches to clinically aimed vocal markers.

Conclusion

Children's conversational acoustics are dynamic patterns of vocal production shaped by development, communicative demands, and individual resources. By tracing these acoustics longitudinally and against children's communicative skills, we found that diagnostic distinctiveness does not reflect a static or monolithic vocal marker. Rather, it reflects specific developmental divergences: higher pitch persists across ages and, less certainly, across skill profiles; wider pitch excursions persist across ages but not across shared skill profiles; faster articulation partly reflects the age gap; and the pause differences largely reflect age and skills. Furthermore, the internal organization of acoustic features diverges across development, indicating that autistic and nonautistic children balance communicative and motor demands through distinct organizational profiles. Clinicians' opinions about their own ability to detect autistic prosody may rely in part on the more stable acoustic differences, but that ability may be overestimated when they do not account for more subtle or variable acoustic features or for developmental skills. It is also possible that they are relying on acoustic features not yet quantitatively identified or assessed, or on a sensibility to within-context and cohort differences. Advancing our understanding of autistic prosody requires moving beyond static group contrasts to examine how acoustic features develop, how they integrate with children's broader developmental profiles, and how they function within interactive communication.
