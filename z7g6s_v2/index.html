To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .178 Interestingly, the highest refusal task overall, becoming 10 years older, showed no significant gender difference (74.9% vs 69.2%; p = .178).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

For educational material on preventing the misinterpretation of p values, see Improving Your Statistical Inferences.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: This module has not been validated. All checks in the codebook_check module have unknown error rates. Carefully evaluate the output of this module. You can help improve this module by reporting an issue on GitHub.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Money is a universal measure of value that influences most decisions. For centuries, humans have used it to exchange goods and experiences. Research in psychology and behavioral economics, however, shows that there may be limits to what individuals are willing to exchange for money. This study examined the monetary values people assign to personal experiences, and whether willingness to engage in monetary valuation differs between males and females. Five hundred and twenty-nine participants completed an online survey in which they provided the minimum amount of monetary compensation it would require to complete ten separate hypothetical scenarios, with the option to refuse any task. The hypothetical tasks ranged from temporary restrictions and social isolation to public disclosure, loss of memories and permanent identity changes. Based on the results from previous economic decision-making studies, we hypothesized that females would refuse to price tasks more than males and that males would request higher compensation for priced tasks. We also hypothesized that permanent or identity-altering tasks would result in higher refusal rates than temporary ones in both genders. Whether resistance to monetary valuation is a consistent individual tendency was also examined. Our results showed that participants who refused to price one hypothetical task were more likely to refuse other tasks. While no gender differences were observed for any task regarding the monetary value requested, females refused significantly more tasks than males (4.43 vs. 3.17, p < .001). Refusal rates were highest for permanent or identity-altering tasks and lowest for temporary tasks. Lastly, refusal rates for loss of digital pictures/videos were nearly identical to losing contact with friends and family. Together, these findings suggest that monetary valuation involves two separate decisions: whether or not to assign a monetary value and, if so, how much to request. Individuals differ consistently in their willingness to engage in monetary valuation, and gender influences the decision to engage rather than with the amount requested.

Introduction

People assign monetary values to things every day. The price an individual is willing to pay for a new car, the salary requested for a particular job, or the proposed budget for a family vacation are all decisions that have an inherent financial valuation. However, deciding on a particular price for ending all contact with a best friend or forever discontinuing a favorite hobby is more challenging. For many individuals, assigning a monetary value to certain meaningful aspects of life is not only difficult, but feels morally inappropriate (Fiske & Tetlock, 1997; Tetlock, 2003). Instead of identifying a price, they refuse to participate in the task altogether (Tetlock, 2003; Berns et al., 2012). Importantly, this suggests that monetary valuation may involve an initial decision as to whether to engage prior to assigning a monetary amount.

Previous research has focused on the central factors that influence whether individuals are willing to assign a monetary value to certain outcomes or experiences. Fiske and Tetlock (1997) describe a concept known as "taboo trade-offs", which occurs when people are asked to provide a monetary value to something meaningful that the person believes should not be bought or sold. The researchers assert that certain social rules are associated with specific relationship domains. For example, financial decisions are usually based on economic reasoning, whereas decisions involving interpersonal relationships are influenced by social and moral beliefs. When people are asked to assign a price to certain relationships or experiences, it is perceived as a violation of these principles. Therefore, certain scenarios may be resistant to monetary valuation because assigning a price elicits moral outrage as opposed to hesitation about identifying an appropriate amount (Fiske & Tetlock, 1997). Tetlock (2003) extended this work to include sacred values-beliefs or relationships that are so important that people believe they should not be exchanged for money. The Sacred Value Protection Model states that when people are asked to establish a price for a sacred value, they may refuse due to the belief that the value should not be measured in monetary terms. While many of the scenarios proposed in the current study would not traditionally be considered sacred values, Tetlock's research suggests that individuals may resist valuation whenever they feel some meaningful aspect of life falls outside the range of economic exchange (Tetlock, 2003).

Previous research supports the Sacred Value Protection Model. For example, Tetlock and colleagues (2000) found that when participants were asked to consider monetary trade-offs for sacred values, they frequently displayed strong moral reactions and rejected the proposed exchange. Moreover, human neuroimaging studies by Berns et al. (2012) showed that participants' refusal to assign a monetary value to a sacred belief were associated with increased brain activity in regions implicated in moral judgment and rule retrieval, and not regions typically involved in evaluating costs and benefits. These protected values were highly stable over time, with over 96% showing no change at a 6-14-month follow-up (Berns et al., 2012). Taken together, these studies suggest that refusing to assign a price to a sacred value is a unique reaction that is distinct from assigning an extremely high monetary value.

While the majority of previous research has focused on the resistance to valuation and the types of scenarios frequently rejected, much less attention has been given to how or if participant characteristics influence the decision-making process. Specifically, do individuals differ in their willingness to place monetary values on meaningful aspects of life, and is the individuals' tendency to reject monetary trade-offs consistent across different types of scenarios? If people who refuse to identify a price on one type of experience are also likely to refuse other types of experiences, then resistance to monetary valuation may be a relatively stable individual characteristic rather than a series of unrelated decisions. Examination of the consistency in decision making is important as previous research suggests that the line between the values an individual believes are priceless and those they are willing to monetize is not completely fixed but rather is quite plastic. For example, McGraw et al. (2003) reported that individuals were more likely to accept certain trade-offs when they perceived those trade-offs as inevitable, tragic, or routine, in contrast to when they voluntarily priced an exchange, suggesting how a situation is presented can influence whether people are willing to assign a monetary value. Therefore, if situations can influence a person's willingness to identify a monetary value for an experience or object, then individuals may also show differences in their willingness to engage in the process (McGraw et al., 2003). This concept is called "commodification willingness" and is defined as a person's general tendency to engage in monetary valuation of meaningful experiences. If commodification willingness is consistent across different scenarios, it may be a primary factor that influences how people make decisions regarding valuation.

An area of interest that is relatively unexplored is whether gender differences exist in valuation behavior. Economic decision-making studies report that males and females display differences in their willingness to participate in situations that appear competitive or involve monetary incentives. For example, Niederle and Vesterlund (2007) found that men and women performed similarly on a math computation task, yet men were almost twice as likely to choose a competitive performance-based payment option. The authors concluded that this difference between men and women was likely driven by male overconfidence and preference for competition as opposed to believing they were superior in ability. These findings suggest that men and women may differ not only in economic decision making, but also in their willingness to engage in monetary valuation (Niederle & Vesterlund, 2007).

If gender differences do exist in valuation tasks, an important question is where they emerge in the decision-making process. Prior to assigning a monetary value to an experience, individuals must first decide whether they are willing to evaluate the experience monetarily. Next, a decision regarding the amount of compensation required is made. Therefore, gender differences may exist in the decision to engage in monetary valuation, in the amount assigned, or both. Decades of research on taboo trade-offs and sacred values have primarily focused on valuation responses in males, without considering potential gender differences in these two stages of decision making (Fiske & Tetlock, 1997; Tetlock, 2003; Berns et al., 2012).

The current study addressed this gap in the literature by examining commodification willingness in a sample of 529 participants. Males and females responded to ten hypothetical scenarios involving personal sacrifices and were given the option to either provide a monetary value or refuse valuation. The scenarios ranged from temporary discomfort and public disclosure to social isolation, loss of personal memories, and permanent changes in identity. The study further investigated whether refusal behavior is consistent across different scenarios, suggesting a stable individual tendency, and if there are gender differences in the willingness to engage in monetary valuation. This study also assessed whether certain types of sacrifices are more resistant to valuation than others. Based on previous research on economic decision making and taboo trade-offs, we hypothesized that refusal behavior would be consistent across scenarios, that females would display reduced willingness to engage in monetary valuation, and that males would assign higher monetary values than females overall. We also hypothesized that permanent or identity-altering scenarios would produce higher refusal rates than temporary scenarios, regardless of gender.

Methods

Participants

Five hundred and thirty-three participants completed an online survey from March-June 2026. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach through the authors' personal and professional social networks, including social media, family and community networks, and online groups. The survey was completed anonymously, and no personal identification information was collected. Subjects were asked to identify only if they were male, female or other. As no physiological samples were obtained to ascertain biological sex, the term "gender", defined here as the sex a person identifies with, was used. Overall, 275 females (52.0%), 247 males (46.7%), and 7 who identified as "other" or preferred not to report (1.3%) participated in this study. The seven participants in the latter category were included in all analyses except male/female comparisons. Four subjects were removed due to failure to follow instructions.

Materials & Procedures

Participants were instructed to input the amount of money they would require to complete ten hypothetical tasks. Participants were instructed to respond with "no amount of money" if the task was something they would not do for any price. Input values ranged from $0 to $1,000,000,000; participants were informed that responses over $1 billion dollars would be viewed the same as "no amount of money". Each task was independent of the others.

The ten hypothetical scenarios were: 1.

Give up your phone and all personal technology for one full year.

2. Move far away and not see your closest friends or family for two years. You cannot talk/communicate to said friends and family for the duration of the time away.

3. Let your entire search history be shown publicly (to everyone you know).

4. Have every text message you've ever sent be read out loud to a group of people (friends/family).

5. Spend 60 days completely alone in a room with no internet, phone, or outside contact. (Still fed/living conditions) 6. Give up all online/social entertainment (video games, television, social media, YouTube, etc.) for one year.

7. Lose all your saved photos/videos permanently.

8. Speak in front of 1,000 people about your biggest mistake.

9. Give up your favorite hobby or activity forever.

10. Immediately become 10 years older than you are now.

Measurements & Analysis

All responses were converted to standard numbers. Responses over $1,000,000,000, as stated in the survey's instructions, were treated as a refusal to assign a monetary value. Responses in which participants indicated they would perform the task for free were converted to $0. Four respondents provided answers that were clearly jokes with no recoverable monetary values; these participants were removed from all analyses.

The number of tasks in which the participant refused to assign a monetary value was quantified for each person. As the dollar amounts varied greatly for tasks ($0 to $1,000,000,000), mean values were not representative and therefore the median was used as the measure of central tendency. Responses of $0 were included in the monetary analyses. Responses were separated by gender; tasks were categorized as permanent (involved an irreversible loss or change of state) or non-permanent (temporary event even if it had lasting consequences).

Chi-Square tests of independence were used to compare refusal rates between males and females for each of the ten scenarios; p < .05 was considered significant. Bonferroni correction was applied to maintain the type 1 error rate across the ten comparisons (adjusted α = .005).

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Because the values were heavily skewed, the nonparametric Mann Whitney U test (MWU), which does not assume a normal distribution, was used to compare median monetary values between males and females. Effect sizes are shown as rank-biserial correlations (r), with .10 (small), .30 (medium), and .50 (large) effects, respectively.

To assess whether refusal was a consistent response within individuals (i.e., if people who refuse one task also tend to refuse other related and unrelated tasks), phi coefficients were calculated for the 45 pairs of tasks. Each participant was coded as 1 for refusing a task and 0 for providing a monetary value. A correlation across all participants was calculated to determine the relatedness of the two binary variables. Chi-square tests were used to calculate p-values, and a Bonferroni correction was applied to the 45 comparisons (adjusted α = .0011).

Willingness to engage in monetization was assessed by quantifying the number of tasks (out of 10) each participant assigned a monetary value. Gender differences in the average number of monetized tasks was assessed using an independent-samples t-test. Mean ± SD values are reported for each gender, and Cohen's d was used to determine effect size.

To assess whether the overall pattern of association between tasks was similar across genders, correlation matrices were created for all participants (combined and separately for females and males). The 45 female and male phi coefficients were then compared using Pearson's correlation (r) to assess the similarity between the two matrices. Finally, the number of task pairs that differed in statistical significance after Bonferroni correction was quantified.

Results

Highest refusal rates associated with irreversible experiences

We first examined refusal rates for permanent versus temporary tasks. Overall, refusal rates ranged from 10.0% (give up online entertainment for one year) to 72.0% (become ten years older) (Table 1). The three tasks with the highest refusal rates were permanent, while the three tasks with the lowest refusal rates were temporary. In total, 53.7% of participants refused to relinquish all photos/videos forever, 55.2% refused to permanently give up their favorite hobby, and 72.0% refused to age 10 years. In contrast, the three tasks with the lowest refusal rate were all temporary (give up entertainment, 10.0%; public speaking, 14.2%; no electronics, 18.0%). Refusal rates were almost identical for refusing to lose all photos/videos forever (53.7%) and moving away from friends/family for 2 years (53.5%).

For participants who named a price, the median values across all ten scenarios ranged from $50,000 for speaking in front of 1,000 people about their biggest mistake to $5,000,000 for becoming 10 years older (Table 1). The three tasks with the highest median monetary values were all permanent scenarios. Specifically, the median amount requested was $1,000,000 to lose all photos/videos forever, $1,000,000 to permanently give up a favorite hobby, and $5,000,000 to become ten years older (Table 1). Of these three, giving up a favorite hobby forever had the smallest range of monetary values.

Refusal Behavior Clusters Within Individuals

To assess whether resistance to monetary valuation is a consistent tendency within an individual, phi correlations were calculated for all 45 pairs of tasks. Most correlations were positive: 43 of the 45 pairs, and 34 of the 45 pairs were significant (α = .0011). The phi correlation matrices show the pattern of associations between refusal responses across tasks (Figure 1). These analyses suggest that participants who refused one task were more likely to refuse other tasks. The strongest correlation was between the two digital sacrifice tasks (φ = .40); however, clustering was not limited to similar scenarios. For example, a significant correlation was also noted between "move away 2 yrs" and "lose all photos/videos" (φ = .37) and giving up online entertainment and speaking in front of 1,000 people (φ = .33) despite the tasks being seemingly unrelated. Taken together, the data suggests the tendency to resist monetary valuation as a whole is such that individuals who refuse to put a value on a single task are more likely to refuse others. This pattern was observed across both related and unrelated scenarios.

Female and Male Refusal Patterns Were Similar but Not Identical

We next compared the 45 phi correlations between females and males. The overall patterns of associations were similar (Pearson r = .72). Seven of the 10 strongest correlations were the same for both genders. However, the patterns were not identical, and 7 of the 45 task pairs (16%) showed statistically significant associations for one gender but not the other. Two task pairs showed the clearest gender differences: search history × giving up online entertainment and search history × speaking to 1,000 people were related for females (φ ≈ .21-.24) but not for males (φ ≈ .05-.07) (Figure 1).

Females Refuse More, Task by Task Gender differences in refusal rates across all ten tasks are shown in Figure 2. Females refused more often than males in 7 of the 10 tasks, with significant gender differences in 4 of the 10 tasks. The largest gender difference in refusal rate (34.6%) was in the task "lose photos/videos" (69.8% females vs 35.2% males; p < .001). Significantly higher refusal rates were observed for females in 60 days alone (45.1% females vs 20.6% males; p < .001), moving away for two years (64.7% females vs 40.9% males; p < .001), and giving up electronics for a year (22.9% females vs 12.1% ; p = .002). Two other tasks, speaking in front of 1,000 people (p = .032) and giving up a favorite hobby (p = .019), showed gender differences, but were not significant. Interestingly, the highest refusal task overall, becoming 10 years older, showed no significant gender difference (74.9% vs 69.2%; p = .178).

No Gender Gap in Monetary Amount Requested

We next examined whether males and females who chose to price a task requested different amounts of money. Overall, there were no significant gender differences in the amounts requested for any of the 10 tasks (Figure 3). The median amounts for females and males, along with the MWU test results and effect sizes, are shown in Figure 3 and Table 2. For public speaking, females asked for a higher median amount than males (p = .021), but this difference was not significant.

Males Price More Tasks Than Females

To test whether females were more likely to refuse to assign a monetary value than males, we compared the average number of tasks each gender chose to price. Males priced an average of 6.83 out of 10 tasks (SD = 2.09), compared with 5.57 for females (SD = 2.28) (p < .001; Cohen's d = 0.56) (Figure 4). Overall, 55.9% of males and 31.3% of females priced 7 or more (χ² = 32.14, p < .001; φ = .248) (Figure 4). These results suggest that the main gender difference is primarily in regards to opting out of monetization rather than pricing differently.

Discussion

The present study examined whether individuals differ in their willingness to assign monetary values to meaningful experiences across a range of scenarios. It also assessed whether gender differences exist in monetization and refusal behavior, as well as in valuation amount. The data show that refusal behavior was consistent across scenarios, such that individuals who refuse valuation on one task were more likely to resist monetization in general. This result suggests that willingness to engage in monetary valuation is a strong individual tendency and not a series of unrelated choices. We further found that the highest refusal rates among participants were observed almost exclusively in tasks that were permanent or irreversible, consistent with previous research on sacred values and taboo trade-offs (Fiske & Tetlock, 1997; Tetlock, 2003). Gender differences were mainly observed in the participants' willingness to engage in monetary valuation instead of the values they assigned. Females displayed higher refusal rates for valuation of the tasks and monetized fewer tasks compared to males. For the tasks that participants did choose to monetize, however, there were no significant gender differences in the amounts requested. Females asked for more money for public speaking than males, but this difference was not significant. Finally, refusal rates for the permanent loss of all digital pictures and videos were nearly identical to losing contact with friends and family for two years.

The current results regarding refusal behavior are consistent with previous research on taboo trade-offs and sacred values. According to this line of research, individuals are unwilling to monetize certain relationships or values because it violates the social and moral standards associated with those domains (Fiske & Tetlock, 1997; Tetlock, 2003). Our findings suggest that refusing to assign a monetary value to certain scenarios is not only about the type of experience being evaluated, but also about the individual making that decision. Participants who refused to put a price on one task were more likely to refuse other tasks, including unrelated ones. This observed pattern was in 34 of the 45 task pairs. Furthermore, many of the strongest relationships were observed between dissimilar types of tasks. These results suggest that willingness to engage in monetization of meaningful experiences is a consistent individual tendency across different domains.

Interestingly, as stated above, the relationships between tasks were not restricted to similar scenarios. While participants who refused one technology-related experience frequently refused others in the same category, there were many other significant associations across distinct and unrelated domains including personal relationships, memories, privacy, and hobbies. One interesting finding was that participants displayed similar refusal rates to giving up all personal technology for one year and spending 60 days in complete isolation, suggesting that participants found these two very different experiences similarly resistant to monetization. Instead of considering each scenario independently, some participants seemed to be very willing to commodify meaningful experiences, whereas others who refused did not. Future studies should focus on whether this is a stable individual difference in commodification willingness and the types of factors influencing this decision behavior, such as age and level of education.

Our findings also identified where gender differences exist in the monetary valuation process. Females refused to assign monetary values significantly more often than males in four of the ten scenarios, and on average, assigned prices to a lower number of tasks. In contrast, males priced more tasks on average than females. This suggests that the main gender difference was in whether participants chose to assign a monetary value, rather than in how much money they requested once they chose to monetize. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that males and females display differences in their willingness to enter into environments that are perceived as competitive or economic, even when performance levels are similar (Niederle & Vesterlund, 2007). Before assigning a price to a meaningful experience, an initial decision must be made as to whether or not to participate in the valuation process. Our results suggest that females are less likely to engage in this initial step. Once the decision is made to monetize an experience, no gender differences in monetary amounts were noted across the ten tasks. This is consistent with previous research on taboo trade-offs in which the idea of monetizing certain aspects of life feels morally inappropriate (Fiske & Tetlock, 1997; Tetlock, 2003). The gender differences observed here are mainly in regards to the willingness to engage in monetization rather than differences in the actual valuation of meaningful experiences. Females who priced the public speaking task requested a higher amount than males, but this difference was not significant. Public speaking differs from the other scenarios in the study because it involves being evaluated by others and performing in front of an audience as opposed to giving up something meaningful or experiencing a permanent loss.

There was a clear pattern in the scenarios which were the most and least resistant to monetary valuation. Specifically, the three scenarios with the highest rates of refusal all involved permanent or irreversible losses and the three lowest refusals involved temporary disruptions. These results are consistent with previous research on sacred values suggesting that some values are inherently resistant to monetization because they are viewed as non-negotiable or morally offensive (Fiske & Tetlock, 1997; Tetlock, 2003). Therefore, permanence and irreversibility may be characteristics of an experience that increase the likelihood of refusing monetary valuation, and this pattern was evident in both males and females.

This present study revealed an interesting finding regarding stored digital photos and videos. Participants refused to assign monetary values to permanently deleting their photos at almost the same rate as they refused to cut off all contact with loved ones for two years. While these experiences may seem distinct, digital photos are becoming a modern collection of autobiographical and social memories. This concept is consistent with previous research showing that digital property, including photos, videos, social media profiles, and email/text messages, have become part of an individual's extended personal identity in the digital age (Belk, 2013 , Honarvar & Sepehrinia, 2025). Therefore, it is likely that participants treated the permanent loss of digital memories as a major sacrifice they were not willing to monetize. It would be interesting to assess in future studies if attachment to digital property differs across generations and cultures.

The present findings have important implications for research on taboo trade-offs and moral decision making. Consistent with previous research on sacred values and taboo trade-offs, the findings here suggest that some experiences are considered inappropriate to monetize (Fiske & Tetlock, 1997; Tetlock, 2003; Atran & Ginges, 2012). Prior studies have primarily focused on determining what types of experiences are resistant to monetization (Atran & Ginges, 2012). The current study extends this literature by highlighting the characteristics of the individuals making the decisions as an important factor. Furthermore, our work suggests that monetary valuation is not a single decision but is actually a two-step process that involves the initial decision of whether to assign a monetary value and then determining the amount to request. Because refusal behavior clustered within individuals across both related and unrelated scenarios, future studies should focus on why some individuals consistently reject monetary valuation while others readily engage in this type of exchange. These findings suggest that commodification willingness may be a meaningful individual difference that impacts how a person responds when asked to assign monetary values to important relationships and life experiences. This research will provide greater insight into how people make economic decisions when they involve sacred values, close relationships, and moral beliefs.

The current results have important implications for future research on related topics. Specifically, research on taboo trade-offs should differentiate between two separate responses: the decision to assign a monetary value and the amount requested. Moreover, instead of considering a refusal response as missing data, it should be viewed as a meaningful decision. Our findings also suggest that the individual differences in the choice to engage in monetary valuation are much larger than in the amount of compensation requested once people have decided to participate.

Several limitations of the current study should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the study relied on hypothetical scenarios instead of real financial incentives; therefore, participant responses may be different than their actual behavior. Second, because many of the characteristics of the scenarios were different (i.e., permanent loss, personally meaningful, public evaluation), it is unknown which features affected the choice to refuse or assign a value. Third, although the refusal behavior was very consistent across scenarios, the current study did not examine whether these effects would be stable across time. Fourth, asking participants to either assign a monetary value or refuse may oversimplify the willingness of people to put a price on meaningful experiences. Last, participants were recruited using a voluntary online convenience sample, and other than gender, no demographic information was collected, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future studies may want to collect additional data, including age, level of education, and socioeconomic status.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that assigning monetary values to meaningful experiences involves two different decisions: whether to participate in monetary valuation, and if so, how much compensation is required. The current findings suggest that people differ in their willingness to put a price on meaningful experiences, and that gender differences occur mainly in the decision to do so, rather than in the amount of money requested once participants choose to engage. Taken together, these findings add to existing literature on taboo trade-offs by showing that understanding who chooses to monetize meaningful experiences may be just as important as understanding which experiences are valued.
