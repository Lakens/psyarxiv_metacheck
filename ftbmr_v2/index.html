Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Single-session interventions (SSIs) are structured, self-guided programmes that deliver one evidence-based component of psychotherapy in a single sitting of 20 to 30 minutes. In the largest trial of their kind, Schleider and colleagues showed that two web-based SSIs significantly reduced hopelessness and, three months later, depressive symptoms among adolescents in the United States. However, that sample was aged 13 to 16, predominantly White, and almost entirely female, so whether SSIs work for East Asian young people living under different social, cultural, and economic conditions remains to be determined. Here, we culturally adapted the SSIs Project ABC (behavioural activation) and Project Personality (growth mindset), together with the placebo control Sharing Feelings, to the Vietnamese context, and tested in a randomised controlled replication trial whether they reduce hopelessness and depressive symptoms immediately after administration. We show that the adapted programmes were highly acceptable, with ratings matching those of the original English programmes in all three programmes. Interestingly, hopelessness and depressive 1 symptoms fell significantly within every programme, including the placebo control, and neither SSI produced statistically significant mental health benefits over the control. Our results therefore underline the challenge of replicating mental health interventions across cultures with different attitudes and stigma towards mental health disorders, as well as different practices competing for alleviating mental distress. Further studies are required to understand the design principles of digital mental health interventions that are specifically effective for Vietnamese youth.

Introduction

Vietnam is a young country, with 101 million inhabitants that have a median age of 33 years (Kemp, 2025), and the 2019 census counted 20.4 million people aged 10 to 24, about a fifth of the population (UNFPA Viet Nam, n.d.). At the same time, the country has one of the thinnest mental health workforces in the region, with fewer than one psychiatrist per 100,000 inhabitants and around 150 clinical psychologists and psychotherapists in its public psychiatric hospitals (Mobashery et al., 2024), that mainly serve patients with severe psychiatric conditions.

In the National Adolescent Mental Health Survey, 3.3% of adolescents aged 10 to 17 met criteria for a mental disorder in the previous 12 months (Erskine et al., 2024), which is likely an underestimate given that stigma, cost, and the need for parental involvement keep most young people away from the few services that exist (UNICEF, 2022). Importantly, smaller surveys that use screening instruments point to a much larger burden: among 1,159 secondary school students in Can Tho, 41% scored above the threshold for depressive symptoms, 23% scored above the threshold for anxiety, and 26% had seriously considered suicide (Nguyen et al., 2013).

Vietnamese culture also differs in important ways from the Western settings in which most psychological interventions are developed and tested. Like China and its other East Asian neighbours, Vietnam is deeply shaped by Confucian traditions that often place the family above the individual, prize emotional restraint, and attach great weight to preserving outer status and reputation (Jamieson, 1993; Yang et al., 2007). Consequently, mental illness still carries strong stigma, and in community surveys depression and anxiety are rarely named as such. Mental distress is more often described as general stress, as "thinking too much", and only a minority of young adults recognise depression in modern medical terms when it is described to them (Nguyen Thai & Nguyen, 2018; van der Ham et al., 2011).

In addition, Vietnamese mental health professionals rate the mental health literacy of the public as low, and services have historically concentrated on severe psychotic disorders rather than on common mental disorders (Dang et al., 2020; Niemi et al., 2010). The country also carries the legacy of decades of war, and post-traumatic stress symptoms remain common among the generation that lived through that period (Korinek et al., 2017; Zimmer et al., 2021), and although intergenerational effects of trauma have been documented in other populations (Yehuda & Lehrner, 2018). Their transmission to today's intergenerational trauma in Vietnamese young people is plausible yet largely unstudied.

Mental wellness is deeply interwoven with daily spiritual practices: ancestor worship, Buddhist temple practices, astrology, numerology, and fortune-telling often serve the purpose of emotional relief, so that they form an established, culturally accepted standard to modern western mental health care (Jellema, 2007; Nguyen, 2014). Despite these differences, Vietnam is highly digitised, as nearly four in five inhabitants use the internet, three in four use social media, and there are more mobile connections than people (Kemp, 2025). This circumstance makes a web-based replication study feasible.

Single-session interventions (SSIs) are structured programmes of 20 to 30 minutes, designed to be completed in one sitting and built on four design principles that their developers summarise under the acronym BEST (Schleider et al., 2020). B stands for brain science, since each programme opens with an accessible explanation of how the brain works, which normalises distress and lends credibility to the idea that feelings and behaviour can change. E stands for empowerment, because the user is positioned as an expert and helper rather than as a patient. S stands for saying-is-believing, whereby users explain the programme's ideas in their own words to a hypothetical future user through guided writing exercises, which turns persuasion into self-persuasion. T stands for testimonials, in which stories from older peers who have applied the skill make the message concrete and relatable. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that single sessions built in this way can produce small but meaningful improvements in youth mental health (Schleider & Weisz, 2017).

In the largest test of self-guided SSIs to date, Schleider et al. (2022) randomised 2,452 adolescents aged 13 to 16 in the United States to two SSIs, Project ABC (behavioural activation) and Project Personality (growth mindset), or to Sharing Feelings, a length-matched supportive control. Immediately after the programme, both SSIs significantly reduced hopelessness relative to the control, and three months later both had significantly reduced depressive symptoms, with the two SSIs performing alike. However, the sample was two thirds White and almost nine in ten female, so that the generality of these effects beyond the United States remains an open question. Early results from other countries are mixed, because a digital single-session programme reduced depressive symptoms among Kenyan secondary school students (Osborn et al., 2020), whereas an online growth mindset session in the United Kingdom changed mindset beliefs but neither anxiety nor depression at one-month follow-up (Ball et al., 2025).

In our study, we sought to understand whether SSIs are effective in Vietnamese youth, which could give insights into the foundations required for future research into the cultural adaptation of digital mental health interventions specifically tuned to East Asian cultures. Our randomised controlled replication trial enrolled young people aged 13 to 25 in Vietnam and yielded four main findings. First, the culturally adapted programmes were as acceptable to Vietnamese young people as the original programmes were to adolescents in the United States, and equally so in all three programmes, which reproduces the acceptability of the original trial. Second, hopelessness and depressive symptoms fell significantly within each programme immediately after the programme, including the placebo control, so that taking part in any of the three programmes was followed by a small improvement. Third, neither Project ABC nor Project Personality reduced hopelessness or depressive symptoms relative to Sharing Feelings, with estimates close to zero whose confidence intervals exclude the between-programme effects of the original trial; therefore, the specific advantage of the two SSIs over the supportive control did not reproduce. Fourth, a contextual survey of the population we recruited from showed that these young people already turn to AI chatbots before friends, family, or professionals when they are distressed, a condition that did not exist when the original trial was run. Our study therefore underlines the importance of culturally replicating psychological studies and the challenges of doing so, and it paves the way for exciting future research on how digital mental health support should be designed for East Asian youth.

Methods

Design, participants, and procedure

This study was a three-arm, parallel-group, randomised, placebo-controlled trial conducted online in Vietnamese between October 2024 and September 2026 (Figure 1). We recruited participants through social media advertisements and through partner organisations working with high school and university students. Eligible participants were aged 13 to 25 and scored 2 or more on the PHQ-2 (Kroenke et al., 2003), the threshold of the original trial, whereas ineligible respondents were directed to a list of Vietnamese mental health resources. Participants under 18 completed a short capacity-to-consent assessment adopted from the original trial, which checks that the young person understands the purpose and procedures of the study, and they could enrol after passing it within two attempts. After the baseline surveys, participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to Project ABC, Project Personality, or Sharing Feelings by the survey platform, and they were not told which programme served as the control. Immediately after the programme, participants completed the post-programme survey and received a 100,000 VND voucher (about 4 USD) by email.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Fulbright University Vietnam (protocol number 00125932, approved 12 May 2024). All participants gave informed electronic consent before the baseline survey, and the contextual survey of AI chatbot use was conducted with electronic consent under the same programme of work. For minors, the IRB approved a waiver of parental permission following the procedure of the original trial. The waiver was justified on the grounds that the study recruits adolescents with elevated depressive symptoms, some of whom have strained relationships with their parents or parents who would, for reasons of stigma, prevent them from seeking support, so that requiring parental permission would expose them to distress and would keep away precisely those who stand to benefit most; moreover, parental permission cannot be verified in an online study, and the large gap in mental health knowledge between Vietnamese young people and their parents means that parental permission would not in itself constitute informed consent. In its place, the protocol substituted developmentally appropriate consent materials, the individual capacity-to-consent assessment described above, access to Vietnamese mental health resources and to the investigators throughout the study, and programmes that have been shown to be safe in adolescents with moderate to high symptoms. The approach is consistent with the mature-minor provisions under which adolescents in most states of the United States may consent to mental health care on their own (Sharko et al., 2022), which served as the legal reference for the study in the absence of specific informed consent legislation in Vietnam.

Cultural adaptation

All three programmes were developed by Schleider and colleagues and are shared under a Creative Commons licence (Schleider et al., 2022), which permits their adaptation for research purposes. Project ABC (Action Brings Change) teaches behavioural activation, namely that actions shape feelings and that connecting with people, pursuing meaningful goals, and enjoying solitary activities can reverse a negative spiral, whereas Project Personality teaches that personality and emotional responses can change because the brain changes with experience. Sharing Feelings, the active control of the original trial, is matched in length, format, and number of writing exercises, and it encourages emotional expression without teaching a specific skill. We followed the framework the developers used for their community and Spanish-language versions of the programmes (Shroff et al., 2023) and the general framework for adapting scalable psychological interventions (Heim & Kohrt, 2019). We translated all programme text under the supervision of the principal investigators, and they kept the structure and the number of activities unchanged so that the three programmes remained matched. Mental health concepts that were ambiguous to Vietnamese youth were resolved with an expert panel of Vietnamese psychologists and translators. Next, the peer stories and testimonials were rewritten around situations familiar to Vietnamese students, and the audio testimonials were re-recorded by Vietnamese young people with northern, central, and southern accents. Finally, we piloted the adapted programmes on a small sample of high school and university students (data not shown) and, in consultation with the developer, judged them suitable for the trial. The programmes were offered to participants under the project name "Kudos".

Measures

Vietnamese versions of all instruments were supplied by the study's counselling advisor through the University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Ho Chi Minh City. Hopelessness, the prespecified immediate outcome, was measured with the four-item Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS-4; Beck et al., 1974; Perczel Forintos et al., 2013) in its true-or-false format and scored as the number of pessimistic statements endorsed (0 to 4), whereas the original trial used a four-point agreement format with totals of 0 to 12. Depressive symptoms were measured with the 10-item Children's Depression Inventory Short Form (CDI-SF; Kovacs, 1992), each item scored 0 to 2 (total 0 to 20). Five CDI-SF items are presented with reversed response options and were recoded before scoring, and we verified the direction of coding of every scale empirically (all item-rest correlations positive; the baseline scales correlated positively with one another, r = 0.40 to 0.63). Anxiety was measured at baseline with the GAD-7 (Spitzer et al., 2006). Acceptability was measured after the programme with the seven-item Program Feedback Scale (PFS; Schleider et al., 2020), rated from 1 to 5 and averaged, with a mean of 3 as the developers' threshold of acceptability, and it was followed by two open-ended questions asking what participants liked most about the programme and what they would change. The protocol also listed the agency subscale of the State Hope Scale and a four-week follow-up of the CDI-SF and GAD-7; however, the agency subscale was not administered and the follow-up was not conducted (see Discussion).

Data integrity screening and data quality audit

Because eligibility was self-reported, the voucher was gifted on completion, and the programme ran unsupervised, we checked data integrity in two stages, both specified after data collection and before any outcome analysis, drawing on the insufficient-effortresponding and internet-fraud literatures (Chandler & Paolacci, 2017; Curran, 2016; Huang et al., 2012; Meade & Craig, 2012; Teitcher et al., 2015). Stage 1 screened every recorded session for duplicate, fraudulent, and careless responding and removed the sessions that failed, whereas stage 2 audited the quality of data entry among the sessions that remained and tested whether any residual signal altered the results.

Stage 1: session-level integrity screen.

The following nine checks were applied in sequence to the 506 sessions in the platform export, so that a session is counted under the first check it fails: 1.

Duplicate enrolment: a second or later session from the same email address (the first session was kept in the programme to which it was randomised). 2. Study-team test session: a session entered by a named member of the study team. 3. Age inconsistency: a four-digit birth year embedded in the email address implying an age more than three years from the stated age. 4. Data analysis

The analysis set comprised all valid responders with BHS-4 and CDI-SF scores at both time points. Within-programme change (after minus before) is summarised with means and 95% confidence intervals and tested with paired t-tests, with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as a distribution-free check and Holm adjustment within each outcome as a sensitivity check; in addition, the proportions whose score fell, stayed the same, or rose are reported, and change scores were compared between programmes with Welch's t-tests and one-way ANOVA, overall and within each gender. The pre-specified model for each outcome was a linear regression of the post-programme score on condition (control as reference), baseline score, and age, with heteroscedasticity-robust (HC3) standard errors (Vickers & Altman, 2001). Four contrasts were estimated, namely each SSI versus control, both SSIs pooled versus control, and Project ABC versus Project Personality, and the adjusted differences were converted to Cohen's d by dividing by the pooled baseline standard deviation, with the sign reversed so that positive values favour the SSI, as in the original trial. Mann-Whitney tests on change scores provided a distribution-free check, and default Jeffreys-Zellner-Siow Bayes factors on change scores (Rouder et al., 2009; prior scale 0.707) quantified support for the null (BF01). Reliable change on the CDI-SF was defined as 5 points or more (Jacobson & Truax, 1991). Exploratory moderation by gender, age (under 18 versus 18 and over, and continuous age), baseline hopelessness (two or more statements endorsed), and baseline anxiety (GAD-7 of 10 or more) was tested with interaction terms, and subgroup estimates are reported. Sensitivity analyses repeated the models in the unscreened sessions (duplicate and test sessions removed), without the age covariate, with gender added as a covariate, without the sessions with unusable timestamps, and with the one participant outside the age range included. Acceptability was compared between programmes with Welch's t-tests and one-way ANOVA. The answers to the two open-ended feedback questions were coded into themes with Vietnamese keyword rules that we developed by reading all answers and refined by reviewing the matches and the unmatched answers; an answer could carry several themes, and the percentage of respondents mentioning each theme is reported overall and by programme.

With 99 participants in the control group and 127 to 128 in each SSI group, the trial had 80% power to detect d = 0.38 for a single contrast and d = 0.33 for the pooled contrast, and 49% to 55% power for the original trial's immediate hopelessness effects. Analyses were run in Python 3.13 with pandas, SciPy, and statsmodels, and the de-identified raw data are available on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/C26JN).

Contextual survey of AI chatbot use

Between 8 August and 19 September 2026, we circulated a short Vietnamese online survey through the same channels, asking about the use of AI chatbots for emotional or mental health difficulties, perceived helpfulness, trust and concerns, usual sources of support, and, for those who had completed the programme, retrospective views of it. It is reported briefly as context for the population the trial drew on.

Results

Figure 1 and Table 1 summarise the study design and the sample characteristics, respectively. Before testing the programmes, we sought to determine how much of the data collected by an unsupervised, incentivised online trial could be trusted, and the answer was sobering: of the 506 sessions that completed both surveys, 151 (30%) failed at least one of the nine integrity checks, most often because the post-programme survey had been opened within five minutes of the baseline submission, a time in which none of the programmes can be completed. However, the failures did not differ between programmes (33%, 25%, and 32%; p = 0.24), so the screen did not bias the comparison, and the 354 valid responders who remained were well balanced on age, screening score, and baseline anxiety. The culturally adapted programmes were highly acceptable in all three programmes

We first asked whether Vietnamese young people would accept programmes that were written for American teenagers and translated into their language and their situations. Acceptability was high and uniform, since the mean PFS score exceeded 4.5 of 5 in every programme, far above the developers' threshold of 3, and the three programmes did not differ from one another (Figure 2). These ratings match the original trial, in which every PFS item was rated above the pre-registered cut-off of 3.5 in all three conditions (Schleider et al., 2022). Notably, the placebo control was rated as highly as the two SSIs, so the adapted materials were received as credible programmes rather than as translations.

To understand what lay behind these ratings, we next investigated the answers to the two open-ended feedback questions by coding them into themes (Figure 3). When asked what they liked most, participants most often named the hopeful message that feelings and thoughts can change, the relatable stories and situations drawn from Vietnamese school and family life, the gentle and professional tone, and the help in understanding their emotions and their brain. Interestingly, the themes followed the content of the programmes, because the message of change was named most often in the two SSI programmes, the understanding of how the brain changes most often in Project Personality, and being able to share and express one's feelings almost exclusively in the Sharing Feelings control, where one participant in seven volunteered it ("the programme helped me express my true thoughts"; "I learned that it is not only me, many people are like me"). When asked what they would change, more than half of the participants asked for nothing or simply praised the programme, and a further one in six answered with a personal resolution rather than a change to the programme ("I will try to change my own way of thinking"). The changes that were requested were more examples and stories, more interaction, games, or videos, a shorter programme with less text, and a wider reach to other age groups and to parents. Moreover, a small number of participants asked for the Vietnamese elements to go deeper still, for example by combining family and traditional values with self-care, and a few would have preferred to take part offline because the online format did not let them say everything they were thinking.

Hopelessness and depressive symptoms fell in every programme, including the placebo control

We next sought to determine whether the programmes changed how participants felt. Interestingly, both self-reported hopelessness and depressive symptoms declined significantly immediately after the programme in all three programmes, including the placebo control (Figure 4). The falls were small, about a tenth to a fifth of a pessimistic statement on the BHS-4 and about half a point on the CDI-SF, and they were also remarkably uniform, because the size of the fall did not differ between programmes on either outcome. The same pattern held when we looked at women and men separately, where the within-programme falls were of similar size in both genders and reached significance in four of the twelve gender-specific comparisons (hopelessness in women in Project Personality and in men in Project ABC; depressive symptoms in women in the control and in women in Project ABC), with no between-programme difference in either gender. Surprisingly, most participants did not change their answers at all: 43% had endorsed all four pessimistic statements at baseline, 87% gave exactly the same hopelessness total after the programme as before, and two thirds gave the same CDI-SF total, so that the mean change was carried by a minority who moved by one or two points. Consistent with this, reliable improvement on the CDI-SF (5 points or more) occurred in 2% to 5% of participants per programme and reliable worsening in 0% to 2%, with no difference between programmes. Because the control group improved as much as the two SSI groups, these within-programme falls cannot be attributed to the content of the programmes; therefore, the baseline-adjusted between-programme contrasts in the next section are the test of the interventions. The finding stands in contrast to the original trial, in which both SSIs reduced hopelessness relative to Sharing Feelings (Schleider et al., 2022), and it suggests that cultural differences in what the control condition means to participants may account for the discrepancy (see Discussion).

The central question we sought to answer was whether either SSI added anything beyond the supportive control, because this between-programme difference, rather than the change within each programme, is what the original trial reported and what our trial was designed to replicate. Women and men responded alike, and only the youngest participants hinted at a benefit of Project ABC

Because the original trial was almost entirely female, we next asked whether gender shaped the response. Women were slightly less hopeless than men at baseline; however, in both genders neither SSI differed from the control on either outcome, and gender did not interact with condition (Figure 5, lower block).

Next, we explored whether age, baseline hopelessness, or baseline anxiety moderated the effects, and no interaction reached significance. Interestingly, among the 38 participants under 18, the age group for which the programmes were written, Project ABC was associated with a larger fall in hopelessness than the control, whereas Project Personality was not, and among the adults both estimates were close to zero. Finally, the main results held in every sensitivity analysis described in the Methods.

The majority of the participants we recruited turn to AI chatbots first for mental health support

Finally, because the trial ran during the rapid spread of large language model chatbots, we asked what else the young people in this population were doing for their mental health. Surprisingly, almost all of the 67 respondents to the contextual survey had used an AI chatbot for emotional support, and two thirds named a chatbot among the first sources they turn to when distressed, more often than friends, family, or a mental health professional (Supplementary Figure S1). Most also found it easier to share with a chatbot than with a person, whereas half worried about inaccurate information and about privacy.

Discussion

We set out to replicate, in Vietnamese young people, the largest randomised trial of selfguided single-session interventions, and our data reveal three main findings. First, the cultural adaptation succeeded on its own terms, because Vietnamese young people understood, liked, and endorsed the programmes, with ratings equal to those of the original English programmes. Second, and intriguingly, hopelessness and depressive symptoms fell significantly within every programme immediately after participation, including the Sharing Feelings control. Third, neither Project ABC nor Project Personality reduced hopelessness or depressive symptoms relative to the control. In what follows, we consider why a replication that succeeded in acceptability could have failed in efficacy.

The most striking feature of our data is that the control worked as well as the interventions, and we believe this could be due to the cultural meaning of the control itself. Sharing Feelings was designed as a placebo for American adolescents, for whom talking about emotions is ordinary. Vietnamese culture, by contrast, prizes emotional restraint, because expressing negative feelings openly is widely regarded as a loss of face and as a burden on the family (Matsumoto et al., 2008; Yang et al., 2007). For a young Vietnamese person, therefore, a structured half hour spent naming and writing about one's own feelings may be anything but inert. Expressive writing about emotional experiences has been shown for decades to produce small improvements in psychological and physical health (Frattaroli, 2006; Pennebaker & Beall, 1986), and our participants told us as much, because being able to share and express their feelings was the one theme that participants in the control raised spontaneously. Consequently, if simply expressing feelings produced a measurable effect on wellbeing in this population, the gap between the SSIs and the control would shrink even when the SSIs were working. We also cannot rule out that the common factors shared by all three programmes, such as the expectation of benefit (Wampold, 2015), together with regression to the mean and the repetition of the same questionnaires within half an hour (Barnett et al., 2005), contributed to the uniform decline.

A second possibility concerns what the SSIs ask of their users, because the BEST framework presupposes a minimal degree of mental health awareness and education. Its brain-science element assumes that a young person already frames low mood as something the brain does and that can be changed, and its saying-is-believing exercises assume a vocabulary in which to explain depressive thinking to a hypothetical peer. In Vietnam, however, these assumptions cannot be taken for granted, since community members describe depression as stress or as "thinking too much" and rarely use the clinical word (Niemi et al., 2010; van der Ham et al., 2011), and public mental health literacy is low (Dang et al., 2020; Mobashery et al., 2024; Nguyen Thai & Nguyen, 2018). We therefore consider it conceivable that the specific ingredients that distinguish the SSIs from Sharing Feelings would have been understood as interesting information rather than converted into new beliefs within 25 minutes. If this interpretation were right, the BEST elements could need an additional on-ramp in settings like Vietnam that builds the vocabulary before the skill is taught.

A third consideration is a confounder that did not exist when the original trial was run. Our contextual survey shows that the young people this trial drew on already turn to AI chatbots before friends, family, or professionals when they are distressed, and a similar pattern has recently been reported among adolescents and young adults in the United States (McBain et al., 2026). Against a background of daily, unlimited, and responsive conversations with a large language model, the added benefit of a 25-minute structured programme would be difficult to detect. Fourth, Vietnam is a spiritual country, and we believe that digital interventions compete with other, long-established ways of relieving distress. Visiting a temple, consulting an astrologer, or seeking out a spirit medium are common responses to emotional difficulty (Jellema, 2007; Nguyen, 2014), and these practices offer something the SSIs in their current form do not, namely a framing of distress and recovery that is rooted in the person's own culture. We therefore regard culturally grounded framings of the SSI content, rather than translated ones, as an obvious next step.

Our sample was mainly young adults, whereas the programmes were written for 13 to 16 year olds, and the exploratory signal for Project ABC among the 38 participants under 18 rests on too few participants to be more than a hypothesis for the next trial. Moreover, the four-item true-or-false hopelessness measure left little room for change, and we did not conduct the four-week follow-up specified in the protocol, because participants were recruited anonymously online and the attrition typical of online interventions (Eysenbach, 2005) would have left a follow-up sample too small to interpret. All three programmes were followed by a small improvement in mood among young people who often rarely have anyone to talk to about such issues. We believe, therefore, that any intervention that gives Vietnamese young people a safe, structured opportunity to express their feelings could be effective, and that the design principles behind the SSIs could be carried into the tools these young people already use. A chatbot that is deliberately designed around these frameworks, rather than left to improvise, may well prove more effective than either a static programme or an unguided chatbot, and future experiments in our group will investigate precisely this.
