Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Understanding how personal, social, and structural conditions shape recovery capital is important for explaining differences in addiction recovery and wellbeing. This study examined strengths and barriers during active addiction and recovery and their associations with addiction complexity, recovery trajectories, social and structural conditions, perceived change, and wellbeing. A cross-sectional national survey included 1,598 adults in recovery in Spain who completed the Spanish-adapted Life in Recovery survey and measures of social and living conditions, quality of life, and harmony in life.

Analyses included paired t tests, correlations, group comparisons, and hierarchical regression models. Participants retrospectively reported substantially more strengths (mean difference = 4.23, d = 1.03) and fewer barriers (mean difference = -4.74, d = 1.33) in recovery than during active addiction. Strength growth and barrier reduction were modestly associated (r = -.29), supporting their interpretation as related but distinct dimensions of recovery capital. Higher strengths in recovery were associated with more consolidated self-identified recovery stages, earlier problem identification and recovery initiation, use of multiple recovery pathways, and greater satisfaction with social and living conditions (R² = .128). Barriers were more strongly patterned by structural conditions, including employment, housing, and perceived economic situation (R² = .070). Higher strengths, fewer current barriers, and greater perceived barrier reduction were associated with better quality of life (R² = .092) and harmony in life (R² = .097), while women reported lower wellbeing after adjustment. The findings support a contextual and multidimensional understanding of recovery capital and highlight the importance of strengthening personal and social resources while addressing persistent material and structural barriers in recovery-oriented care.

1. Introduction

In recent decades, the understanding of addiction recovery has moved beyond abstinence and symptom remission towards a broader view of recovery as a dynamic process of change across personal, relational and community domains, involving improvements in wellbeing and the reconstruction of a meaningful life (Ashford et al., 2019; Florence et al., 2024). This shift has been accompanied by increasing attention to recovery-oriented policies and services that incorporate lived experience, long-term and flexible support, community-based care and social participation. International frameworks promoting person-centred, rights-based, and recovery-oriented care similarly emphasise that recovery is shaped not only by individual change but also by the social environments and material conditions in which people live (World Health Organization, 2021 , 2023).

Recovery capital provides a useful framework for examining these conditions. It refers to the resources that individuals can mobilise to initiate and sustain recovery (Granfield & Cloud, 1999) and encompasses personal, social, and community resources, including health, coping skills, supportive relationships, housing, income, service access, and supportive community and policy environments (Bunaciu et al., 2024; Hennessy, 2017). Importantly, recovery is not shaped only by the availability of resources. Cloud and Granfield (2008) emphasised that recovery capital should also be understood through a positive-negative continuum, recognising barriers such as stigma, social exclusion, housing instability, unemployment, and substance-using social networks that may constrain recovery. Distinguishing between strengths and barriers may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of recovery than considering resource accumulation alone, while also identifying conditions that may be amenable to intervention through services and social policy (Best et al., 2020; Best & Hennessy, 2022).

The Life in Recovery survey (LiR; Laudet, 2013) has been used internationally to examine recovery trajectories, recovery pathways, recovery-related life changes, and recovery capital (Best et al., 2015 (Best et al., , 2021;; De Meyer et al., 2024; McQuaid et al., 2017).

Drawing on LiR life-experience items, the Strengths and Barriers Recovery Scale (SABRS; Best et al., 2020) was developed to assess positive recovery capital (strengths) and negative recovery capital (barriers) retrospectively during active addiction and currently in recovery. LiR research has consistently shown substantial differences between experiences during active addiction and recovery, while also demonstrating considerable heterogeneity in recovery trajectories (Best et al., 2020 (Best et al., , 2021 (Best et al., , 2024)). Such heterogeneity is consistent with an ecological understanding of recovery in which individual change is embedded within relational, material, service, and broader social contexts.

Evidence from LiR studies and the broader recovery literature indicates that recovery experiences vary according to gender, life course, addiction complexity, time and stage of recovery, employment, housing, economic security, relationships, and access to formal and informal support (Abreu Minero et al., 2022; Best et al., 2021; Kelly et al., 2019; Ross-Houle & Porcellato, 2023). Structural and material conditions may be particularly important because access to stable housing, employment, adequate income, and supportive social environments can facilitate opportunities for participation and resource accumulation, whereas disadvantage in these areas may continue to constrain recovery even when personal strengths increase. Recovery capital may therefore reflect not only individual capacities and social relationships but also unequal access to resources and opportunities within the environments in which recovery takes place.

Despite growing research on recovery capital, several questions remain. First, recovery has often been operationalised through time-based stages, which may not fully capture its dynamic and non-linear nature (Best et al., 2021 (Best et al., , 2024)). Subjective recovery phases may provide a complementary way to understand recovery trajectories. Second, although structural conditions are increasingly recognised within recovery capital frameworks, further research is needed to examine whether positive and negative recovery capital are differently associated with social and material circumstances such as employment, housing, economic security, and satisfaction with living conditions (Best et al., 2020; McQuaid et al., 2017). Third, while LiR research has documented substantial perceived improvements across life domains during recovery, less is known about whether current strengths and barriers, and perceived differences between active addiction and recovery, are independently associated with wellbeing (Best & Hennessy, 2022; Elms et al., 2018). Examining these dimensions together may help clarify whether recovery wellbeing is associated primarily with accumulating strengths, reducing barriers, or both.

Against this background, the present study examined recovery capital and wellbeing among adults in addiction recovery in Spain from an ecosystem-based perspective. Specifically, it aimed to: 1) examine perceived differences in strengths and barriers between active addiction and recovery; 2) analyse differences in recovery capital according to relational and structural conditions; 3) identify addiction-, recovery-, and context-related variables associated with strengths and barriers in recovery; and 4) examine the associations of current recovery capital and perceived change with wellbeing. By considering positive and negative recovery capital alongside self-identified recovery stages, social and material conditions, and wellbeing, the study seeks to contribute to a more contextual understanding of how recovery resources and barriers are configured across recovery experiences.

2.4. Data Analysis

Data were screened and cleaned following standard recommendations (Field, 2024; Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019), including checks for valid ranges, missing values, response coherence, and duplicated responses. Cases with fewer than 25% valid responses were excluded. After this process, 223 participants were excluded, resulting in a final analytical sample of 1,598 participants. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0.2.0.

First, paired-samples t tests examined changes in strengths and barriers from active addiction to recovery, and Pearson correlations assessed continuity between stages and associations between change indices.

Second, group comparisons examined differences in strengths and barriers in recovery according to relational and structural conditions, including relationship status, family responsibilities, employment, housing and perceived economic situation.

Independent-samples t tests and one-way ANOVAs were used as appropriate, with post hoc tests selected according to group size and variance assumptions (Tukey's HSD, Gabriel, or Hochberg's GT2). Welch's ANOVA and Games-Howell tests were applied when homogeneity was violated (Field, 2024).

Third, hierarchical multiple regression models were estimated to identify variables associated with strengths and barriers in recovery from an ecosystem-based perspective. Predictors were entered in four blocks (Best et al., 2024; De Meyer et al., 2024): 1) sociodemographic controls, including age and gender; (2) addiction complexity, including age at onset, number of problematic substances and time from addiction onset to recovery initiation; 3) recovery trajectory, including self-identified recovery stage, number of relapses and number of recovery pathways accessed; and 4) psychosocial vulnerability, including use of mental health services, use of services for high-risk situations and satisfaction with social and living conditions. Recovery stage was dummy coded, with initiating recovery as the reference category.

Finally, additional hierarchical regression models examined associations between recovery capital, perceived change and wellbeing. Predictors were entered in three blocks (Best & Hennessy, 2022; Best et al., 2020): 1) sociodemographic controls, including age and gender; 2) recovery capital, including strengths and barriers in recovery; and 3) perceived change, including the Strengths Growth Index and Barriers Reduction Index. Across regression models, multicollinearity, independence of errors and influential observations were examined. Changes in explained variance (ΔR²), standardised coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals and overall model statistics were reported.

3.1. Changes in Recovery Capital from Active Addiction to Recovery

Participants reported more strengths and fewer barriers during recovery than during active addiction (see Table 2). The mean increase in strengths was 4.23 points, and the mean reduction in barriers was 4.74 points. Both differences were statistically significant and showed large effect sizes.

Correlations indicated limited continuity between active addiction and recovery.

Strengths in active addiction were weakly and negatively associated with strengths in recovery (r = -0.12, p < 0.001), whereas barriers in active addiction were not associated with barriers in recovery (r = 0.01, p = 0.596). The Strengths Growth Index and Barriers Reduction Index were small-to-moderate negatively associated (r = -0.29, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.33, -0.24]), indicating that greater growth in strengths was associated with greater reductions in barriers.

3.2. Recovery Capital According to Relational and Structural Conditions

Regarding relational context, relationship status was significantly associated with barriers in recovery, but not strengths. Participants in a relationship reported fewer barriers than those who were single, although the effect size was small. No significant differences in strengths (t(1422) = -1.32, p = 0.187, d = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.18, -0.04]) or barriers (t(1421) = 0.21, p = 0.836, d = 0.01, 95% CI [-0.10, 0.12]) were observed according to family responsibilities. Structural variables showed a more consistent pattern, with effect sizes ranging from small to moderate. Employment status was significantly associated with both strengths and barriers in recovery. Employed participants and those on medical leave reported higher strengths than unemployed and permanently work-disabled participants, while employed participants reported fewer barriers than all other groups.

Housing-related variables were mainly associated with barriers. Participants living in institutional housing reported more barriers than those in independent housing, and those living with a partner reported fewer barriers than those living alone, with their family of origin or in shared housing. 3.3. Variables Associated with Strengths and Barriers in Recovery

Two hierarchical regression models were estimated to examine variables associated with strengths and barriers in recovery. The final models were statistically significant for both strengths (F(14, 1031) = 11.97, p < 0.001) and barriers (F(14, 1030) = 6.61, p < 0.001). Explained variance was modest and higher for strengths (adjusted R² = 0.128) than for barriers (adjusted R² = 0.070). Diagnostic statistics indicated independence of errors (Durbin-Watson = 1.94 for strengths and 1.97 for barriers) and no problematic multicollinearity (VIF < 3.2).

In both models, the block including recovery trajectory variables accounted for the largest increase in explained variance. Addiction complexity and psychosocial vulnerability also contributed to the models, whereas the sociodemographic block explained only a small proportion of variance (see in the OSF document 'Hierarchical Regression Block Contributions')

In the strengths model, older age, earlier age at onset of the addiction problem, shorter time from addiction onset to recovery initiation, more advanced self-identified recovery stages, attendance at multiple recovery pathways, and greater satisfaction with social and living conditions were significantly associated with higher strengths in recovery. In the barriers model, fewer variables showed independent associations. Attendance at multiple recovery pathways and lower satisfaction with social and living conditions were significantly associated with higher barriers in recovery (see Table 3).

3.4. Recovery Capital, Perceived Change and Wellbeing

Two hierarchical regression models were estimated to examine the associations between recovery capital, perceived change and wellbeing. The final models were statistically significant for both quality of life (F(6, 1367) = 24.17, p < 0.001) and harmony in life (F(6, 1327) = 24.82, p < 0.001). Explained variance was modest in both outcomes (adjusted R² = 0.092 for quality of life and 0.097 for harmony). Diagnostic tests indicated independence of errors (Durbin-Watson = 1.89 for quality of life and 1.95 for harmony) and the absence of problematic multicollinearity (VIF < 2.6).

In both models, the recovery capital block accounted for the largest increase in explained variance, with a greater contribution to quality of life. The sociodemographic block also contributed significantly, whereas perceived change experiences accounted for a small but statistically significant additional increment in explained variance. Both of these blocks showed a greater contribution to harmony in life (see in the OSF document 'Hierarchical Regression Block Contributions').

Older age, higher strengths in recovery, fewer barriers in recovery, and greater perceived reduction in barriers were significantly associated with higher levels of quality of life and harmony in life. Female gender was significantly associated with lower levels of both wellbeing indicators. Perceived growth in strengths was not independently associated with either outcome after accounting for current recovery capital and perceived reduction in barriers (see Table 4).

4. Discussion

This study examined recovery capital and wellbeing among adults in addiction recovery in Spain from an ecosystem-based perspective. Overall, participants retrospectively reported substantially more strengths and fewer barriers in recovery than during active addiction. Current strengths and barriers showed partly different patterns of association: strengths were more closely related to recovery trajectories and mobilised supports, whereas barriers were particularly patterned by social and material conditions. Recovery capital was also associated with quality of life and harmony in life, with perceived barrier reduction showing an additional association with both wellbeing indicators. Taken together, these findings support a contextual understanding of recovery capital in which recovery resources and barriers are related but distinct dimensions embedded within broader social and material conditions.

Consistent with previous LiR-based studies, participants reported marked differences in strengths and barriers between active addiction and recovery (Best et al., 2020 (Best et al., , 2021)). Although these retrospective differences cannot be interpreted as observed longitudinal change, their magnitude is consistent with the idea that recovery involves a substantial reconfiguration of recovery capital. The weak or absent associations between active-addiction and recovery scores further suggest that experiences in active addiction do not fully determine recovery capital in recovery. This points to the transformational potential of recovery, even among individuals who reported low strengths or high barriers in active addiction. In addition, greater perceived growth in strengths was associated with greater perceived reduction in barriers, although this association was modest and weaker than that reported by Best et al. (2021). This pattern supports the view that positive and negative recovery capital are related but not interchangeable dimensions (Best et al., 2020; Cloud & Granfield, 2008): accumulating resources does not necessarily imply an equivalent reduction in the barriers that may constrain recovery.

Differences according to relational and structural conditions reinforce this distinction, particularly in relation to barriers. Participants who were in a relationship or living with a partner reported fewer barriers, consistent with research highlighting the role of meaningful social bonds in supporting identity and behavioural change (Best & de Alwis, 2017). Family responsibilities, however, were not associated with recovery capital, potentially reflecting the heterogeneous role of family relationships and responsibilities across individual recovery experiences (Andersson et al., 2018; Schamp et al., 2020). More consistently, employment, housing, and perceived economic situation were associated primarily with barriers. Participants experiencing unemployment, more dependent or institutional housing situations, and greater economic hardship tended to report more barriers in recovery. Although the crosssectional design does not establish the direction of these associations, the findings are consistent with the view that recovery takes place within unequal social and material conditions that may both shape and be shaped by recovery trajectories (Best & Ivers, 2022).

The multivariable analyses provided further evidence that strengths and barriers may reflect partly different aspects of recovery capital. The modest explained variance suggests that the predictors captured meaningful but partial aspects of recovery capital, consistent with its complex and multifactorial nature (Best & Hennessy, 2022; Cloud & Granfield, 2008). Recovery trajectory emerged as particularly relevant for the development of strengths. More consolidated self-identified stages of recovery were associated with greater strengths, whereas comparable differences were not observed for barriers. This may indicate that strengths are more closely aligned with perceived progress in recovery, while barriers remain comparatively dependent on contextual circumstances. Importantly, these findings also support the value of considering selfidentified recovery stages alongside chronological indicators such as time in recovery, particularly given the heterogeneous and non-linear nature of recovery experiences (Best et al., 2021 (Best et al., , 2024)).

Indicators related to the timing of addiction and recovery were also associated with strengths. Earlier identification of the addiction problem and earlier initiation of recovery were independently associated with higher current strengths. These associations should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the retrospective nature of these measures, but they suggest that the timing of recovery initiation may be relevant to subsequent recovery capital. This is consistent with arguments supporting accessible and flexible opportunities to initiate recovery before addiction-related harms and social disadvantage become further entrenched (Cloud & Granfield, 2008; Davidson et al., 2021).

The number of recovery pathways accessed showed a distinctive pattern, being associated with both greater strengths and greater barriers. Rather than indicating a straightforward beneficial effect of multiple pathways, this finding may reflect two complementary processes. Access to diverse formal and informal supports may provide opportunities to accumulate recovery resources, while people experiencing greater or more persistent barriers may also need to mobilise a broader range of services and supports. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that recovery pathways are heterogeneous and that formal treatment, peer support, community resources, and natural recovery processes may play different roles across recovery trajectories (Best & de Alwis, 2017; Laudet & Humphreys, 2013).

Satisfaction with social and living conditions was one of the most consistent correlates of recovery capital, being positively associated with strengths and negatively associated with barriers. Together with the group differences observed for employment, housing, and economic situation, this finding highlights the importance of considering the broader social and structural context in which recovery takes place. Access to adequate living conditions, economic security, supportive relationships, and opportunities for social participation may constitute important components of the contexts in which recovery is initiated and sustained (Best & Ivers, 2022; McQuaid & Dell, 2018). From this perspective, recovery capital is not solely something that individuals possess or accumulate but is also contingent on the resources and opportunities available within their social and material environments.

Recovery capital was associated with both quality of life and harmony in life, consistent with previous evidence linking recovery resources to broader wellbeing (Elms et al., 2018; Hennessy, 2017). Notably, perceived barrier reduction remained associated with both wellbeing indicators after accounting for recovery capital, whereas strength growth did not. Although the cross-sectional and retrospective design precludes conclusions about temporal ordering, this pattern suggests that wellbeing in recovery may be particularly sensitive to the extent to which previously experienced barriers are perceived to have diminished. Recovery may therefore involve not only building resources but also creating social and material conditions in which persistent barriers become less restrictive.

Finally, women reported lower quality of life and harmony in life after adjustment for current recovery capital and perceived change. Previous research has identified gendered differences in recovery experiences, including greater exposure among women to stigma, caregiving demands, relational difficulties, trauma, and barriers to treatment and social resources (Lloyd, 2010; McQuaid & Dell, 2018; Tweed et al., 2022). The present analyses cannot determine which mechanisms account for the observed gender differences, and gender itself was not independently associated with current strengths or barriers. Nevertheless, the persistence of differences in wellbeing after adjustment indicates the need for further research into gendered social and structural conditions that may not be fully captured by conventional recovery capital measures.

4.1. Implications for Advancing Recovery-Oriented Care

The findings have several implications for recovery-oriented care. First, recovery capital assessment may benefit from considering strengths and barriers separately rather than treating recovery capital as a single continuum. Progress in developing personal and social resources may coexist with persistent barriers related to housing, employment, economic security, or social circumstances. Routine assessment of both dimensions could therefore provide a more comprehensive picture of recovery needs and help identify areas in which progress is occurring alongside continuing vulnerability.

Second, the associations between recovery capital and social and material conditions highlight the limits of approaches focused predominantly on individual change. Recovery-oriented systems may benefit from stronger coordination between addiction treatment, mental health care, social services, housing, employment support, and community-based resources. Such coordination may be particularly relevant for individuals who perceive progress in recovery while continuing to experience material or relational barriers.

Third, the association between recovery capital and wellbeing suggests that recovery-oriented care should consider quality of life and broader wellbeing alongside substance-use outcomes. The additional association between perceived barrier reduction and wellbeing further suggests the potential value of addressing conditions that restrict participation and quality of life, rather than focusing exclusively on strengthening individual recovery resources. Finally, the lower wellbeing reported by women warrants further attention to gender-related needs and to social and structural conditions that may differentially shape recovery experiences.

4.2. Strengths, Limitations, and Future Research

This study has several strengths, including a large national sample recruited across Spain and the integration of recovery trajectories, social and material conditions, recovery capital, and wellbeing within a common analytical framework. The inclusion of self-identified recovery stages also provided a complementary perspective to strictly time-based measures and allowed recovery to be examined in relation to participants' perceived position in their recovery journey.

Some limitations should nevertheless be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow the temporal relationships between recovery capital, social and material conditions, and wellbeing to be established.

Second, strengths and barriers during active addiction were assessed retrospectively.

Consequently, the Strengths Growth and Barriers Reduction indices represent perceived differences between recalled active-addiction experiences and current recovery rather than prospectively observed change. These estimates may be affected by recall bias or by participants' current recovery circumstances and should therefore not be interpreted as longitudinal measures of change.

Third, the barriers-in-recovery index of the SABRS showed relatively low internal consistency, which may have attenuated some associations involving this measure. In addition, the Spanish versions of the SSQ and HLS were translated following standardised procedures and showed adequate preliminary psychometric properties in the present sample but have not yet undergone full independent validation in Spain. Findings involving these measures should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Longitudinal research is needed to examine how strengths, barriers, and wellbeing evolve within individuals and whether changes in employment, housing, economic security, service access, or social participation precede subsequent changes in recovery capital and wellbeing. Future studies should also examine how gender and other dimensions of social inequality interact with recovery resources and barriers, and whether routine assessment of positive and negative recovery capital can contribute to more responsive recovery-oriented services.

5. Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of considering both strengths and barriers when examining recovery capital. Participants retrospectively reported substantially more strengths and fewer barriers in recovery than during active addiction, while the two dimensions showed partly different patterns of association. Strengths were more closely related to recovery trajectories and mobilised supports, whereas barriers were particularly patterned by social and material conditions, including employment, housing, and economic circumstances. Current recovery capital was also associated with quality of life and harmony in life, with perceived barrier reduction showing an additional association with both wellbeing indicators. These findings support a contextual understanding of recovery capital in which building personal and social resources and reducing persistent barriers represent related but distinct aspects of recovery. Recovery-oriented systems may therefore benefit from addressing not only individual and social resources but also the material and structural conditions that can constrain wellbeing and participation throughout recovery.
