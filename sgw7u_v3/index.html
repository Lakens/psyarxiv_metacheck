What we found

We found 22 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. Full text

Abstract

As surface-travelling animals, humans are thought to encode vertical space coarsely, a view rarely tested during real locomotion. Here, blindfolded participants walked an outbound path, either flat or over a bridge, then returned to the start along a straight, flat path. In Experiment 1, they returned accurately after the flat path but stopped short of home after crossing a bridge, opposite to the overshoot expected from measuring distance along the walked surface. Contrary to coarse coding, homing after the bridge was no less precise than after the flat path. Experiment 2 tied the undershoot to vertical movement itself: it occurred over bridges with different inclinations and over stairs with horizontal treads, and more than tripled when participants crawled over the bridge, whereas a matched flat path left homing unaffected during walking. The undershoot may stem from the wellknown overestimation of slopes: participants indeed overestimated every bridge's inclination, and their perceived slopes explained most of the mean error in Experiment 1, but not all results. Vertical movement thus appears to be processed metrically, like horizontal movement, but from biased perception. This bias, often thought to leave action unaffected, may therefore shape how we find our way home.

Introduction

Navigation -moving through the environment to find food, shelter or the way home -is among the most fundamental abilities of animals, from insects to humans. Because it can be observed in natural behaviour and linked to identified neural codes, navigation has become a model system for understanding how the brain represents space [1, 2], an effort recognised by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine awarded to John O'Keefe, May-Britt Moser and Edvard I. Moser for the discovery of place and grid cells. Most of this work, however, has studied navigation on flat ground, whereas natural terrain rises and falls. Only in the last two decades have studies begun to examine navigation over slopes, stairs and multiple floors. They have centred on one question: is spatial processing isotropic, treating the horizontal and vertical dimensions alike, or anisotropic, with properties and possibly dedicated mechanisms that differ between them [3]? Here we ask a more specific, behavioural version of this question: when people walk up and down, do they correctly account for the vertical component of their movement when estimating the horizontal distance home?

Throughout this paper, we use "2D" to refer to movement confined to the horizontal plane and "3D" to movement that includes a vertical component, such as walking over a slope, climbing stairs or changing levels. An inclined surface is geometrically two-dimensional, but moving along it changes elevation; we therefore treat it as 3D.

Humans live and move predominantly in 2D. Settlements cluster in valleys rather than on mountain tops, built environments keep our feet on level ground (stairs, for instance, turn a slope into a series of horizontal treads), and the human body is poorly suited to steep inclines: even trained mountain runners cannot keep their balance on a treadmill inclined at more than about 40° [4]. It is therefore not surprising that humans often process horizontal space more accurately than vertical space, a pattern termed the horizontal advantage. It has been reported across very different tasks, including the reproduction of passively travelled distances [5], wayfinding in multilevel buildings [6, 7] and spatial learning [8]. The horizontal advantage is not universal, however. Some studies report differences between horizontal and vertical performance without an advantage for either [9] [10] [11], and one found no behavioural difference but distinct neural responses for the two [12]. Taken together, these findings indicate that human spatial processing is shaped by the predominantly horizontal environment we move in, but they leave open how the vertical component of self-motion is processed when we do move in 3D.

A prominent account of this anisotropy links spatial processing to how an animal moves. According to the bicoded-map hypothesis [3], animals that travel through volumes, such as flying and swimming species, represent space metrically in all three dimensions, whereas surface-travelling animals represent the horizontal plane metrically and the vertical dimension only coarsely [3, [13] [14] [15] [16].

Comparative studies broadly support this view: rats and dogs are more accurate in the horizontal than in the vertical dimension [17] [18] [19] [20], whereas hummingbirds and fish perform similarly in all directions [18, 21]. Neural recordings show a matching contrast. Place and grid cells in rats encode vertical space less precisely than horizontal space [22, 23], whereas freely flying bats have place cells and headdirection cells tuned in 3D [24, 25]. Desert ants add an instructive twist. After walking over hills, they return home over the correct ground distance, that is, the horizontal projection of their path [26] [27] [28].

Yet they do not appear to store the vertical component in their home vector [29]. Accounting for vertical movement and representing it are thus separable: an animal can correct for slopes while navigating with a 2D representation.

Recent findings complicate a strict link between locomotion and dimensionality. In humans, an fMRI study found 3D place information in the hippocampus [30], and memory for horizontal and vertical locations can be equally accurate [30, 31]. In mice, head-direction cells are tuned in 3D relative to gravity [32]. The studies with humans, however, relied on vision in virtual environments or on memory from a single viewpoint, without the body-based signals that accompany real locomotion. Moreover, comparisons across species confound how animals move with many other differences in ecology, anatomy and brain. A complementary approach is to change how the same individual moves. Humans normally walk upright, but they can also crawl on hands and knees. Crawling is unfamiliar, slower and more effortful, and it pitches the trunk along the slope instead of keeping it upright, a factor known to affect how travelled distance is represented [5]. If the way we move shapes how vertical movement is processed [33], crawling over the same terrain should change how elevation enters the estimate of the way home.

In this context, we asked how vertical movement enters human path integration, the estimation of the way home from self-motion [34] [35] [36]. Path integration is commonly modelled as the continuous summation of self-motion vectors [34, 37]. On flat ground, each step contributes its full length to the horizontal home vector; when the path rises and falls, only the horizontal component of each step should count. Previous work on vertical navigation in humans has mostly examined spatial memory and learning [8, 31], multilevel buildings [6, 8, 11], virtual environments [5, 12, 30, 31] or passive transport in motion simulators [9, 10], and active locomotion over elevation changes has rarely been studied [38]. We therefore used a homing task modelled on the desert-ant experiments described above [26] [27] [28]. Blindfolded participants walked an outbound path that was flat or led over a bridge or stairs, and then returned to the start along a straight, flat path. Because home lay at the same height as the end of the outbound path, the correct return distance equals the horizontal projection of the outbound path. The task thus tests whether vertical movement is correctly accounted for when computing the horizontal distance home. It does not test whether elevation itself is represented, that is, whether the underlying representation is 2D or 3D. This design yields distinct predictions for different accounts of how vertical movement is processed (Fig. 1).

(i)

Accurate integration. If vertical movement is processed as accurately as horizontal movement, participants should return to the correct location after both flat and elevated paths. This holds whether the system is isotropic or, like that of desert ants, correctly projects each step onto the horizontal plane.

(ii) Path-length odometry. If the vertical structure of the path is ignored and distance is estimated along the walked surface, for instance by counting steps, participants should overshoot after elevated paths, because the path over the bridge is longer than its ground projection (by 33 cm, or 8.5%, in Experiment 1).

(iii) Coarse vertical coding. If vertical movement is encoded only coarsely, as proposed by the bicoded-map hypothesis [3], homing after elevated paths should be more variable than after flat paths, without a specific prediction for the direction of the error.

(iv) Metric integration from biased inputs. Vertical movement may be integrated metrically, but from biased estimates of the terrain. Slopes are consistently overestimated by a factor of about 1.5-2 [39] [40] [41] [42] [43], and projecting a path with an overestimated slope yields a shorter horizontal extent, predicting an undershoot.

Accounts (ii)-(iv) differ in the sign and spread of the errors, so these two measures distinguish between them.

Experiment 1 compared homing after a flat, straight path with homing after a bridge with 24.5° slopes, and measured how participants perceived the bridge's inclination and height from body-based cues.

Participants undershot after the bridge but not after the flat path, the pattern predicted by account (iv).

We therefore tested whether projecting the path with each participant's perceived slopes reproduced the size of the undershoot. Experiment 2 tested this account against alternatives with four manipulations. 1) Inclination (12° vs. 24°): if the undershoot results from slope overestimation, it should be larger for the steeper slope, whereas path-length odometry predicts a larger overshoot there. 2) Stairs: stairs produce the same elevation change, but the feet remain on horizontal treads. If the undershoot depends on sensing an inclined surface underfoot, it should vanish on stairs; if it depends on vertical movement per se, it should persist. 3) Flat control: laying the bridge's profile flat on the floor reproduced the path's shape and length within the horizontal plane, so any error caused by the turns or the longer path, rather than by vertical movement, should appear here as well. 4) Locomotion mode: to test whether the way we move shapes how vertical movement is processed [33], participants also crawled over the bridge and the flat paths.

If familiar, upright locomotion supports accurate integration, crawling should increase the error relative to walking, even after correcting for each mode's own straight-path baseline.

General Methods

Ethics.

Both experiments were approved by the ethics committee of Ulm University (23/19) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants gave written informed consent, reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no hearing, vestibular or balance impairments, and received course credit or monetary compensation (€10 per hour).

Homing task.

Both experiments used a homing task modelled on experiments in desert ants [26] [27] [28].

Participants walked (in Experiment 2 also crawled) an outbound path until the experimenter said "stop" as they crossed the end line. They were then turned by 180°, guided onto a straight, flat return track and walked (or crawled) back, stopping where they judged their starting position (home) to be.

Home lay at the same height as the end of the outbound path, so the correct return distance always equalled the horizontal projection of the outbound path. The return distance was measured with a laser distance meter (Bosch PLR 40 C) from the start of the return path to the participant's heels in Experiment 2 and to the toes in Experiment 1, where each participant's shoe length was added to every measurement. Homing error was defined as the measured distance minus the correct return distance, so negative values denote undershoots. After each trial, participants were led back by the experimenter to the start along a random detour.

Isolating path integration.

To ensure that participants relied on self-motion information alone [34], they wore a blindfold and headphones playing white noise throughout, received no feedback, and saw the setup only at the end of the experiment. The guidance devices described below kept participants on a straight course [44] without providing tactile information about distance.

Statistical analysis.

For each participant and condition, homing errors were averaged over the 12 repetitions. No participants or trials were excluded, and no participant's condition mean in the analysed data (baseline-corrected in Experiment 2) deviated by more than 3 SD from the group mean.

Repeated-measures ANOVAs were Greenhouse-Geisser corrected when Mauchly's test indicated a violation of sphericity, and post-hoc comparisons were Bonferroni-corrected paired t-tests. Normality was checked with Shapiro-Wilk tests on the variables entering each t-test and correlation (participant means or paired differences) and on the residuals of each ANOVA; 3 of 43 tests indicated a deviation (p < .05), and for these, nonparametric tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Friedman) led to the same conclusions. Nonsignificant results are reported as the absence of a detected difference; no equivalence tests were performed. Analyses were run in MATLAB R2024a (MathWorks).

Experiment 1

Experiment 1 asked whether walking over a bridge biases the estimated distance home, and in which direction. Accurate integration predicts no error, path-length odometry an overshoot, and integration from overestimated slopes an undershoot.

Methods

Participants.

Twenty-seven participants (17 women, 10 men; aged 18-27 years, M = 22.0, SD = 2.3) were recruited through the university's participant pool. With 27 participants, a paired t-test had 90% power to detect within-subject effects of dz ≥ 0.65 (α = .05, two-tailed).

Design and apparatus.

Participants returned home after two types of outbound paths (Fig. 1). The flatstraight path was 387 cm long. The bridge path led over two 184.5-cm slopes inclined at 24.5° and a 51-cm plateau at a height of 76.5 cm (Fig. 1). Its total length was 420 cm, but its horizontal projection was again 387 cm. The correct return distance was therefore identical in both conditions. The paths differed only in their vertical component, which made the bridge path 33 cm longer than its ground projection. The return track ran directly parallel to the bridge (Fig. 1). At the end of the outbound path, participants were turned and guided onto it. In all conditions, participants held a custom guide: a small handle mounted on a carriage that ran freely along two rails, like a toy train on its track. Because the handle travelled with the hand, it provided a directional reference and light support for balance without any sliding contact, and thus without tactile motion cues about the distance travelled.

Procedure. Each condition was repeated 12 times (24 trials in total), in blocked order counterbalanced across participants. Participants took breaks every 6 trials to prevent fatigue, and the session lasted about 1 h 15 min.

Perception of the bridge.

To test whether slope misperception could account for homing errors, we measured how participants perceived the bridge from body-based cues alone, following Hajnal et al. [39]. Before the homing task, so that estimates were not influenced by homing experience, blindfolded participants crossed the bridge seven times. On each crossing, they verbally estimated the uphill inclination in degrees (0° = horizontal, 90° = vertical), the height of the plateau in centimetres, and the downhill inclination. Estimates were averaged across crossings.

Analysis.

Homing errors after the bridge and after the flat-straight path were compared with a paired t-test, and each condition was compared with zero using one-sample t-tests. Because the flat-straight path served as the within-participant reference, the bridge-flat comparison corresponds to the baseline correction used also in Experiment 2. To test for coarse vertical coding, within-participant SDs of homing errors (over the 12 repetitions) were compared between conditions with a paired t-test, in centimetres and relative to the mean return distance. To derive the return distance predicted by each participant's perception, we projected the bridge onto the horizontal plane using the perceived uphill (θ̂_ up ) and downhill (θ̂_ down ) angles: D pred = p + s (cos θ̂_ up + cos θ̂_ down ), with plateau p = 51 cm and slope length s = 184.5 cm. This assumes that distance along each slope was registered correctly and that only its inclination was misperceived. Predicted and observed return distances were compared with a paired t-test, and perceived inclination was correlated with homing error across participants. The reliability of both measures was estimated with odd-even split-half correlations, Spearman-Brown corrected.

Results

Homing. After the flat-straight path, homing errors did not differ from zero (M = 2.2 cm, 95% CI [-12, 17]; t(26) = 0.31, p = .76). After the bridge, participants stopped on average 89 cm short of home (SE = 10.4 cm, 95% CI [-110, -67]). This is an undershoot of 23% of the homebound distance, t(26) = -8.56, p < .001. The difference between conditions was large, t(26) = -9.85, p < .001, dz = 1.89 (Fig. 2). The undershoot contradicts both accurate integration (0 cm) and path-length odometry (+33 cm). It would, however, follow if participants integrated the path correctly but with overestimated slopes: an undershoot of 89 cm corresponds to perceived slopes of about 48° (assuming equal misestimation uphill and downhill), roughly twice the true inclination of 24.5°. This is within the range of slope overestimation commonly reported in the literature, by factors of about 1.5 to 2 [39] [40] [41] [42] [43]. Homing was not more variable after the bridge. The within-participant SD was 42.1 cm after the flat path and 35.5 cm after the bridge, t(26) = -2.26, p = .032; relative to the mean return distance, it was 10.9% and 12.2%, t(26) = 1.57, p = .13.

Perception.

In the rating task, participants also strongly overestimated the bridge. Its height of 76.5 cm was judged as 104 cm (SD = 34), and its 24.5° slopes as 44.0° uphill (SD = 11.5) and 39.6° downhill (SD = 10.3). This overestimation by a factor of 1.7 lies within the range of 1.5 to 2 reported for perceived slant [39] [40] [41] [42] [43].

Perception and homing.

The mean perceived slope of about 42° was thus not far from the 48° implied by the homing errors. Conversely, projecting the bridge with each participant's perceived slopes predicted a mean return of 321 cm (SD = 43.5), an undershoot of 66 cm, compared with the observed 298 cm (SD = 53.8), an undershoot of 89 cm. At the group level, perceived slopes thus accounted for about three quarters of the undershoot; the remaining difference of -22.9 cm (95% CI [-51.4, 5.5]) was not significant, t(26) = -1.66, p = .11.

At the individual level, perceived inclination did not correlate with homing error, r = .08, p = .70. Both measures were highly reliable (split-half reliability of .99 for perceived slope and .98 for homing errors, allowing correlations up to about .98), so measurement noise is unlikely to explain the absence of a correlation.

Experiment 2

Experiment 1 revealed an undershoot consistent with integration from overestimated slopes.

Experiment 2 tested this account against alternatives with four manipulations: 1) inclination, 2) stairs, 3) a flat control matched in shape and length, and 4) locomotion mode.

Methods

Participants.

Sixteen new participants (7 women, 9 men; aged 19-34 years, M = 25.1, SD = 3.7) were recruited through the university's participant pool and personal contacts. Based on the bridge effect in Experiment 1 (dz = 1.89), 6 participants would have provided 90% power. With 16 participants, the design had 90% power to detect within-subject effects of dz ≥ 0.87 (α = .05, two-tailed).

Conditions.

Terrain was crossed with locomotion mode (Fig. 3). Each condition addressed a specific question: • Straight-flat path (278 cm; walked and crawled).

The individual baseline for each locomotion mode, separating terrain effects from a general miscalibration of walked or crawled distance.

• Bridge at 12° and 24° (walked and crawled). Tests whether the undershoot scales with inclination, as slope overestimation predicts.

• Stairs corresponding to 12° and 24° (walked only, as stairs are hard to crawl). They produce an elevation change while the feet remain on horizontal treads, testing whether the undershoot depends on an inclined surface underfoot or on vertical movement per se.

• Flat control at 12° and 24° (walked and crawled). The bridge's profile was laid flat on the floor, reproducing the path's length and turning angles in the horizontal plane. Tests whether path shape or length alone affects homing.

The horizontal extent of all outbound paths, and thus the correct return distance, was 278 cm.

Apparatus.

Two staircases (150 cm wide, 96 cm deep, four steps with 24-cm treads) were placed backto-back to create an ascent, a plateau, and a descent. For the bridges, wooden ramps (150 cm wide; 117 cm long at 12°, 126 cm at 24°) were fitted over the staircases. This gave heights of 24 and 51 cm, a plateau of 48 cm and path lengths of 283 and 300 cm; the stairs had the same heights as the corresponding bridges. The bridge and stairs stood against a wall, which participants used as a handrail while wearing a thick glove to prevent tactile motion cues. On the return path, walking participants pushed a trolley with fixed, straight-line wheels, which kept them on a straight course without providing tactile or other distance cues. Crawling participants were aligned with the return direction and asked to move as straight as possible; they wore knee pads for comfort and goalkeeper gloves to prevent tactile cues. The flat-control paths were marked with chalk on the floor of a separate room, and the experimenter turned participants by the shoulders at each corner to keep them on the path, walking or crawling.

Procedure. The 12 conditions were distributed over three sessions of about 2 h each on separate days (Table 1). Each session combined walking and crawling to limit fatigue. Each session was divided into two blocks with six trials per condition, giving 12 repetitions per condition and 144 trials per participant. The order of sessions and of conditions within a session was randomised and counterbalanced across participants, and both blocks of a session followed the same order. Perception of the bridge. At the end of each bridge session, after the homing trials, participants drew the bridge on paper (in a neighbouring room) without ever having seen the bridge, once for each locomotion mode. They also verbally estimated the height of the bridge and of the stairs. Perceived angles were read from the drawings, and each drawing provided a single estimate per condition.

Session Conditions

Analysis.

Crawling trials started with the hands at the start line, whereas the return distance was measured behind the feet. Each participant's body span, measured with a tape measure, was therefore subtracted from crawled distances. All homing errors were baseline-corrected by subtracting each participant's error on the straight-flat path of the same locomotion mode. A corrected error of zero therefore denotes performance equal to baseline. Corrected errors were analysed with a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (elevated condition: bridge walking, bridge crawling, stairs walking × inclination: 12°, 24°). They were also analysed, averaged over inclinations, with a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA including the flat control. Each condition was compared with the baseline using one-sample t-tests. Perceived inclinations were analysed with a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (locomotion mode × inclination), with follow-up contrasts between inclinations within each mode (Bonferroni-corrected). Return distances predicted from the drawn angles were computed as in Experiment 1 and compared with baseline-corrected undershoots. The six correlations between baseline-corrected homing errors and perception errors were treated as one exploratory family and Bonferroni-corrected; unadjusted p-values and pointwise 95% CIs are also reported. The correlations covered bridge angles for each inclination and locomotion mode, and stair heights for each inclination.

Results

Inclination.

Inclination had no detectable effect on homing errors, F(1, 15) = 0.79, p = .39, partial η² = .05. It did not interact with condition, F(1.36, 20.47) = 0.59, p = .50, partial η² = .04. Likewise, the 12° and 24° flat controls did not differ, t(15) = -0.83, p = .42, 95% CI [-14.1, 6.2] cm. We therefore averaged over inclinations (Fig. 3).

Elevation.

The flat control did not differ from the straight-flat baseline (M = 1.1 cm, 95% CI [-14.0, 16.1]; t(15) = 0.15, p = .88). A longer path with the same turns as the bridge, but without vertical movement, therefore had no detectable effect on homing during walking. By contrast, participants undershot relative to baseline after walking over the bridge (M = -39.9 cm, 95% CI [-59.4, -20.4]; t(15) = -4.35, p < .001) and over the stairs (M = -51.1 cm, 95% CI [-70.8, -31.4]; t(15) = -5.52, p < .001).

Conditions differed overall, F(2.24, 33.56) = 77.43, p < .001, partial η² = .84. Both bridge (p = .004) and stairs (p = .001) differed from the flat control, but did not differ detectably from each other (p = .19).

The undershoot thus did not require an inclined surface underfoot. The raw error after the bridge was only -15.9 cm, because the walking baseline itself overshot home by 24.0 cm (SD = 20.9).

Locomotion mode.

Crawling over the bridge produced the largest undershoot (M = -135.5 cm, 95% CI [-160.5, -110.6]; t(15) = -11.58, p < .001). This was more than three times that of walking over the bridge (difference -95.7 cm, 95% CI [-117.8, -73.5], p < .001) and larger than that of walking over the stairs (-84.5 cm, 95% CI [-109.4, -59.6], p < .001). Crawling also produced an undershoot on the flat control (M = -57.9 cm, SD = 49.9; t(15) = -4.64, p < .001), but a smaller one than over the bridge (difference -77.7 cm; t(15) = -6.31, p < .001). Crawling therefore increased homing errors in general, but most of the increase occurred when crawling involved elevation.

Perception.

As in Experiment 1, participants strongly overestimated the bridge. Averaged over uphill and downhill slopes, the 12° bridge was drawn at 23.8° (SD = 7.6) after walking and 26.8° (SD = 7.2) after crawling. The 24° bridge was drawn at 40.0° (SD = 6.9) and 38.3° (SD = 6.8). When walking, slopes were thus overestimated by a factor of 2.0 at 12° and 1.7 at 24°, consistent with the literature [39, 42, 43]. Heights were overestimated by about 30 cm for the bridge and by 20 cm (SD = 15) and 22 cm (SD = 22) for the 12° and 24° stairs. Perception errors (drawn minus true inclination) did not differ detectably between locomotion modes, F(1, 15) = 0.44, p = .52, partial η² = .03, or inclinations, F(1, 15) = 1.75, p = .21, partial η² = .10. The interaction was marginal, F(1, 15) = 4.36, p = .054, partial η² = .23. When walking, overestimation was 4.2° larger at 24° than at 12° (t(15) = 2.16, unadjusted p = .048, adjusted p = .095). When crawling, the difference was -0.5° (t(15) = -0.33, adjusted p = 1.00).

Perception and homing.

Projecting the bridge with the drawn angles predicted an undershoot, relative to flat walking, of 18 cm at 12° and 38 cm at 24°. The observed baseline-corrected undershoot was 40 cm at both inclinations. Perceived slopes thus matched the observed undershoot at 24° but underestimated it at 12°. Above all, they predicted a doubling between inclinations that did not occur in homing. At the individual level, the correlation between homing and perception errors was negative for walking over the 24° bridge (r = -.53, unadjusted p = .037, 95% CI [-.81, -.04]; Fig. 4), but did not survive correction (adjusted p = .22). The remaining correlations were nonsignificant even before correction: 12° walking r = -.14, p = .61; 12° crawling r = -.35, p = .18; 24° crawling r = .01, p = .99; stair heights 12° r = -.25, p = .36; 24° r = .19, p = .48. Because each condition yielded a single drawing made after the session, the reliability of individual perception estimates was limited, which constrains the correlations.

Comparison of Experiments 1 and 2

Because the homebound distance differed between experiments (387 vs. 278 cm), we expressed the difference in homing error between the flat-straight path and the 24° bridge (walking) as a percentage of the homebound distance. This terrain effect was larger in Experiment 1 (23.5%) than in Experiment 2 (14.3%), t(41) = 2.08, p = .044, 95% CI [0.3, 18.0] percentage points, d = 0.66.

To test for practice effects, we compared the first and second halves of the repetitions in each condition. In Experiment 2, participants returned 14.6 cm further in the second block across the six 3D conditions (t(15) = 4.03, p = .001), but not across the six flat conditions (0.2 cm; t(15) = 0.08, p = .94).

The difference between these changes was significant, t(15) = 2.88, p = .011. In Experiment 1, the undershoot did not decrease over the 12 bridge trials (trials 1-6: -86 cm; trials 7-12: -91 cm; t(26) = -0.74, p = .46).

Discussion

We asked whether people correctly account for vertical movement when estimating the horizontal distance home. They did not, and the error went in the opposite direction to the simplest anisotropic prediction. Measuring distance along the walked surface would have produced an overshoot. Instead, participants consistently undershot after walking over a bridge: by 89 cm (23% of the homebound distance) in Experiment 1, and by 40 cm (14%) relative to a straight baseline in Experiment 2. During walking, a flat path with the bridge's shape and length produced no error, so the undershoot cannot be attributed to path length or turns. It also occurred on stairs, where the feet remained on horizontal treads, and it more than tripled when participants crawled. These results rule out both accurate integration and path-length odometry, as well as effort-based distance estimation, which would predict longer perceived distances for more effortful paths. Vertical movement thus systematically shortens the estimated way home.

Systematic bias rather than coarse coding.

The bicoded-map hypothesis proposes that surfacetravelling animals represent the vertical dimension only coarsely and non-metrically [3, 45]. Coarse coding predicts imprecise homing after elevated paths, but not a consistent error in one direction.

Instead, the undershoot was large and consistent across participants (dz = 1.89 in Experiment 1), and homing was not more variable after the bridge than after the flat path (within-participant SD of 36 vs. 42 cm). This pattern is most consistent with account (iv): vertical movement is taken into account metrically, but from biased inputs. It is compatible with evidence for 3D spatial coding in the human hippocampus and in mouse head-direction cells [30, 32]. It also suggests that the horizontal advantage reported in humans may partly reflect biased inputs rather than a coarser representation of the vertical dimension.

Slope overestimation as a candidate input. The most obvious biased input is perceived slope. Slopes are overestimated by a factor of about 1.5 to 2 [39] [40] [41] [42] [43], and integrating a path with overestimated slopes yields a shorter horizontal projection. The magnitudes fit at the group level. In Experiment 1, the undershoot corresponded to perceived slopes of about 48°, close to the 42° participants reported, and projections with the reported slopes accounted for about three quarters of the undershoot. For the 24° bridge in Experiment 2, the predicted (38 cm) and observed (40 cm) undershoots relative to baseline were nearly identical. Slope overestimation has often been considered to have little consequence for action [39, 40, 43, 46]. Our results suggest that it may matter for navigation, where perceived geometry must be integrated over the path. It may also help explain why travelled distances are reproduced less accurately when the body is pitched backward [5], and why stationary virtualreality tasks, which lack body-based slope signals, often find no horizontal-vertical difference [12, 30].

Limits of a purely perceptual account.

Several findings show that perceived slope is not the whole story. First, perceived slope did not predict individual homing errors in either experiment. This may partly reflect the limited reliability of a single drawing per condition in Experiment 2, but not in Experiment 1, where both perceived slope and homing errors were highly reliable (split-half reliability ≥ .98). The account is therefore supported only at the group level. Second, perceived slopes predicted twice the undershoot at 24° as at 12°, yet homing errors did not differ between inclinations. Third, stairs produced an undershoot that did not differ from that over the bridge (-51 vs. -40 cm), although no inclined surface was underfoot, and crawling tripled the undershoot without a detectable change in perceived slope. Three broader accounts could reconcile these findings. (a) The critical bias may concern the estimated vertical displacement of the body rather than the slope of the surface.

Participants overestimated the height of both bridge and stairs, and if horizontal distance is derived from an estimate of 3D displacement, an overestimated vertical component would shorten it whenever elevation changes, regardless of the surface. (b) Elevated segments take longer to traverse, and leaky integration [47] would lose part of the outbound path, more so for slower crawling. (c) Participants may have applied a cognitive correction, shortening their response by a roughly fixed amount once they knew they had gone up and down. This would explain why no inclination effect was detected, and the larger effect in Experiment 1, where the perception task preceded homing and made the bridge salient. Distinguishing these accounts requires perceptual measures on every trial, paths that dissociate slope from height and duration, and manipulations of the perceptual input itself.

How we move matters. Changing the mode of locomotion within the same individuals changed how elevation affected homing. Each mode was corrected by its own straight baseline, so the larger undershoot when crawling does not reflect a general miscalibration of crawled distance. Crawling on the flat control produced a much smaller undershoot than crawling over the bridge (-58 vs. -136 cm), so most of the increase is tied to crawling over elevation. This within-species manipulation complements comparisons between species [13, 18] and supports the idea that the way an animal moves shapes how it processes vertical space [33]. Several features of crawling may contribute. The trunk is pitched along the slope rather than upright, and travelled distance is represented relative to the body [5]. Crawling is unfamiliar, so its self-motion signals may be less well calibrated. It is also slower and more effortful, which would increase losses from leaky integration [47] [48] [49]. Perceived slope did not differ detectably between modes. However, participants drew the bridge only at the end of the session, after crossing it in both modes, and may have recognised it as the same structure; several reported informally that it felt steeper when crawling.

Trial-by-trial perceptual measures are needed to test whether locomotion mode alters perceived slope.

Experience with elevation.

The terrain effect was smaller in Experiment 2 than in Experiment 1 (14% vs. 23% of the homebound distance). Within Experiment 2, the undershoot decreased from the first to the second block in elevated but not in flat conditions. Experiment 2 involved far more elevation changes (72 vs. 19 trials, including the seven bridge crossings of the perception task in Experiment 1), suggesting that the undershoot diminishes with exposure. This is consistent with the idea that the horizontal advantage partly reflects our limited experience with vertical movement. Repeating a single bridge was not sufficient, however: the undershoot in Experiment 1 did not decrease over trials. Other differences between the experiments may also have contributed. In Experiment 1, the perception task preceded homing and may have made the vertical extent of the bridge salient. The homing distance and guidance also differed between experiments. The bridge was also higher in Experiment 1 (76.5 vs.

51 cm), which may have contributed, although homing did not differ between the 24-cm and 51-cm bridges of Experiment 2. Informally, participants were surprised at the end by how small the bridge was, most strongly in Experiment 1.

2D or 3D representation?

Accounting for vertical movement does not require representing it: desert ants compute the ground distance of hilly paths but store a 2D home vector [28, 29]. Because home and the end of the outbound path lie at the same height, our task cannot distinguish a 3D representation from projection onto a horizontal plane. Paths that end at a different height from home, requiring a home vector with a vertical component, would address this question. They would also separate ascent from descent, which were combined here and perceived differently (44° vs. 40°).

Work on spatial updating after stepladder descent [38] shows that such paradigms are feasible. After descending a stepladder, observers update the horizontal but not the vertical component of their position [38], consistent with a ground-plane reference. Finally, our participants relied on body-based cues alone over short laboratory distances. How these biases interact with vision and accumulate over longer, natural routes remains open. The answer matters beyond the laboratory, for wayfinding in hilly terrain and multilevel buildings [6, 7] and for virtual-reality locomotion systems that simulate elevation.

Conclusion.

Going up and down shortens the estimated way home. The undershoot is opposite to path-length odometry, not detected on matched flat paths during walking, present on stairs, and amplified by crawling. This pattern is most consistent with humans taking vertical movement into account metrically, but from biased inputs. Overestimated slopes explain much of the group-mean effect, though not its full pattern, pointing to biases in the estimated vertical displacement of the body more generally. Whether vertical movement is integrated within a 3D representation or projected onto a 2D one, as in desert ants, remains to be tested.
