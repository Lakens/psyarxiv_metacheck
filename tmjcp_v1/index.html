Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) is one of the most researched, freely available self-report measures of depression designed to be used in large-scale research. Although the four-factor structure originally identified by Radloff (1977) is wellreplicated with Euro Americans, in whom it was developed, evidence suggests that the latent factor structure of the CES-D varies by demographic backgrounds. Thus, reliance on the original CES-D factor structure could mischaracterize depressive symptoms in non-Euro American individuals. Further, past studies have not clearly differentiated the roles of ethnic heritage and socializing culture in equivalence of the CES-D structure across groups. Specifically, both Asian American and Asian national (international) students would be categorized as "Asian" group in the United States but their socializing background and cultural experiences differ. This study investigated the CES-D factor structure and correlates in three ethnoculturally different samples to test for the effects of socializing culture and racial/ethnic differences using measurement invariance (MI) and nomological network analyses. Data were collected from 144 Asian American, 127 Euro American, and 168 Asian national college students. Asian American and Euro American students share a mainstream socializing culture while Asian American and Asian national students share racial/ethnic heritage. Euro American and Asian national students do not share either. MI analyses suggest that Radloff's four-factor structure met strict invariance between Asian American and Euro American students. The four-factor structure was not identified in the Asian national group. Removing the Interpersonal Problems factor and Item 7 resulted in achieving up to scalar invariance across the three groups. Further, the external correlates of the modified CES-D factor scores indicated similar nomological network of cultural experiences of depressive symptoms in Asian American and Euro American students, as well as Asian American and Asian national students. However, Euro American and Asian national students did not share as much. These findings suggest socialization background to be a source variance underlying factor-structures of depression, and association of depressive symptoms and cultural experiences are affected by both. This study highlights the need to consider contextual information to fully understand how depression may manifest differently across individuals.

Disentangling Ethnicity from Nationality: Equivalence and Correlates of the CES-D Among Asian American, Asian National, and Euro American College Students

Depression disorder is one of the most common mental health conditions with 7% to 14% of adults estimated to experience clinically significant depression symptoms at some point in their life globally (McGrath et al., 2023). Depression is also a leading cause of disability worldwide and is related to higher risk for other health conditions, including anxiety and hazardous substance use, self-injurious behaviors and suicide, and it predisposes people to infection, diabetes, and other physical illnesses (Friedrich, 2017; Liu et al., 2024; Vos et al., 2017). Hence, depression affects both a significant proportion of the population and contributes to a significant health and economic burden. Particularly, people with Asian heritage comprise the largest group in the global population, and depression symptom prevalence estimates in Asian Americans vary across studies (as low as about 15% in Chinese-Americans or higher than 30% in Korean-and Filipino-Americans; H. J. Kim et al., 2015). Asia also refers to a broad region, diverse in itself, and the depression prevalence rates differ across countries (Santomauro et al., 2021). However, these estimates are based on measures developed primarily with Euro Americans, and the validity of the measures have been questioned (G. Kim et al., 2011), suggesting that the prevalence rate may not accurately represent the experiences of those from Asian heritage. Limitations in existing research on understanding the similarities and differences in depression across groups are compounded by conflating ethnic heritage with socializing culture. For example, when comparing Asian Americans and Euro Americans in the United States, any observed group differences may be attributed to the cultural differences in how depression is understood and expressed in their family (i.e., ethnic heritage and cultural values), national contexts such as minority experiences (i.e., nationality and cultural experiences), or both. A research design that allows disentangling such sources is needed to clarify what factors contribute to variation in depression symptom manifestation.

Depression manifestations may be affected by cultural values and norms (Kalibatseva & Leong, 2011). Whereas people of the Asian heritage may or may not be inherently at higher risks for depression than people of Euro American backgrounds (Kubik et al., 2003), there is evidence indicating the critical roles of cultural socialization in shaping the reports of depression among Asian national and Asian American individuals (Kalibatseva & Leong, 2011). For example, East Asians may present more somatic symptoms (e.g., abdominal upset, weakness, etc.) than Euro Americans, given a more holistic view of mind and body (Ryder et al., 2008). Likewise, researchers have posited that the affective consequences of depression among East Asians may skew towards depression of positive affect (as opposed to increases in negative affect), because of cultural emphasis on presenting a calmer affect by suppressing one's emotion in order to maintain social cohesion (Jang et al., 2005; Oh et al., 2002). Also, Asian-heritage people in both Asian countries and within the United States report high levels of stigma against depression and low rates of professional help-seeking for related symptoms (P. Y. Kim & Yon, 2019; Leong et al., 2011; Tuliao et al., 2016). Therefore, important correlates that are unique to non-Euro American people, including those of Asian heritage, may have been missed in past research that do not consider heterogeneity within their samples.

There is also a body of research suggesting depressive symptoms are associated with cultural factors and may have different nomological network across groups (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). Three potentially relevant factors associated with Asian cultures are loss of face, individualism and collectivism, and the ethnic experience. Some studies have found a correlation between Loss of Face (i.e., perceived threats to/loss of social integrity) and depression symptoms (Kalibatseva et al., 2017). One study found that higher Individualism and Collectivism were associated with lower depression symptoms among Chinese American college students than Euro American college students (Zhang & Han, 2023). A study also found that having a strong identification with both one's ethnic and national identities had a protective effect and observed that this group did not show evidence for the same correlation of depression and perceived discrimination as in other groups (Huynh et al., 2014). These collectively indicate a complex interaction of cultural experiences and context to depression, and potential utility in understanding prevention and treatment strategy of depression across clients with diverse backgrounds.

Measurement Invariance

While there have been many investigations on cross-national and ethnocultural similarities and differences in depression symptomatology (Kalibatseva & Leong, 2011), depression measures have primarily been developed and validated with predominantly Euro American samples, assuming that it is also valid in Asian people. Other than cultural influences on how people experience depression or report severity levels of these symptoms, group differences in depression may reflect differential functioning of depression measurements, which could underlie the variations in the prevalence estimates (H. J. Kim et al., 2015). If there are cultural differences in expression of depressive symptoms, depression measures developed in one cultural context with any given population may suffer from systematic validity concerns when exported to other contexts with other populations. Understanding the manifestation and etiology of depression is conditioned on valid assessment of depressive symptoms across populations. Unfortunately, despite some research linking cultural factors to observed depressive symptoms, there remains insufficient attention to whether depression symptoms are measured validly with Asian-heritage people using existing scales. Findings from exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses investigating depression symptom manifestations across Asian-and Euro-heritage people show mixed findings to support this assumption (G. Kim et al., 2011), thus questions arise about the group comparisons founded on such an assumption. These pose a challenge to past findings of depression in Asian people as well as how depression correlates differently with external variables across groups. Therefore, establishing the validity of depression assessment in Asian people is critical in early detection for intervention and scientific understanding of its etiology. Measurement invariance (MI) is a set of analyses that allows for the testing of such validity of measures across multiple groups.

MI is a set of analyses that tests for psychometric equivalence of constructs betweengroups. These analyses typically involve a four-tier testing procedure wherein the ostensibly equivalent data of two or more groups are subjected to increasingly strict model specifications (Meredith, 1993). Configural invariance analysis, the first level, tests the assumption that the same number of factors underlie the set of scale items across groups (regardless of group differences in strength of item-factor correlations, item intercepts, or residual variances). Metric invariance analysis, the second level, tests the assumption that the overall factor structure and strength of factor loadings are equal across groups (regardless of group differences in item intercepts or residual variances). Scalar invariance analysis, the third level, tests the assumption that factor structure, loadings, and item intercepts are equivalent across groups (regardless of group differences in residual variances). With scalar invariance, researchers can compare latent construct means across groups and test for differences in severity of depressive symptoms. Strict invariance, the fourth and final level, tests the assumption that factor structure, loadings, item intercepts, and items' residual variances are equal across groups. MI is needed for valid group comparisons. With strict invariance, researchers can compare scale scores computed based on observed scores across groups (e.g., average or sum of items). This is the level needed to compare groups without factor analytic framework. Not meeting any level of MI suggests that the corresponding assumptions are not met, and group comparisons could be due to either measurement bias or true group differences, and the two cannot be disentangled.

Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression Scale (CES-D): A Case Example

The CES-D (Radloff, 1977) is one of the most common and freely available measures for screening for depression and estimating prevalence across general and clinical populations (Shafer, 2006). The popularity and the amount of its past psychometric investigation makes CES-D a well-suited measure to test for validity of depression assessment. When developing the CES-D, Radloff (1977) included items that assess Depressive Affect, Positive Affect, Somatic Symptoms, and Interpersonal Problems. Results from validation studies supported the four-factor structure in both clinical and nonclinical samples (Cosco et al., 2020; Radloff, 1991). A major limitation of the scale, however, was that it was developed and initially tested with samples that were 97% Euro Americans (Radloff, 1977). Even subsequent meta-analyses and systematic reviews on the structural validity of the CES-D data included primarily Euro American participants (Blodgett et al., 2021; Shafer, 2006). Thus, current knowledge base and interpretations about depression likely represent Euro Americans' experiences well but may lack generalizability to groups including Asian American and Asian national people. Support for the four-factor model of the CES-D is less consistent with minority groups and international studies. For example, Kim and colleagues (2011) conducted a meta-analysis on the CES-D factor structures across White, Black, Asian, Hispanic, and American Indian groups.

They concluded that, though the original CES-D four factors generally emerge, the factor structures vary by racial and ethnic group when examined in an exploratory factor analysis framework, indicating slight variations in symptom manifestation or structure across groups. In the more restrictive confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) approach, they found that the four factors were equivalent across all groups, except the Interpersonal Problems factor in the Asian samples.

Another study investigated MI across English speaking individuals from the US, Australia, India, the Philippines, and Singapore (Kato, 2021). They reached scalar invariance and, while nuances emerged for factor differences, they found that Asian participants tended to score in the direction of worse/severe scores compared to US and Australian Euro-heritage participants. Taken together, the literature shows that researchers can extract latent scores assessing Depressed Affect, Positive Affect, and Somatic Symptoms reliably across groups within factor analytic framework, and the Interpersonal Problems factor is often unstable, particularly when considering Asian or Asian American people's experiences.

A major limitation of past studies on the equivalence of the CES-D across groups is their conflation of national boundary and ethnic heritage background (e.g., comparing Chinese people in China vs. Euro American people in the US), which does not identify the potential source of similarities or differences in depression factor structure. Previous studies have either regarded cross-cultural differences or used race/heritage as a proxy for cultural values that may shape the definition and correlates of depression. This limits the advancement in understanding depression across populations and culturally valid assessments of depressive symptoms. As described above, there are several potential reasons why CES-D (and depression symptomatology) may be measurement variant. For example, Asian Americans may express depressive symptoms differently than Euro Americans because of their ethnic heritage or because of being a minority group in the US (Huynh et al., 2014; P. Y. Kim & Yon, 2019). One approach to identify the potential characteristic that underlies depression structure is to collect data from samples that differ in one aspect at a time as much as possible.

The Present Study

To address these gaps, MI of the CES-D in college students of Asian American, Asian national, and Euro American backgrounds were tested in this project. This allows for an investigation of MI across both ethnic heritage (i.e., comparing Euro-descent and Asian-descent people) and socializing backgrounds (i.e., comparing domestic Americans and Asian nationals), which may identify a potential source of variance. Whereas previous studies have shown partial or non-MI of CES-D data across ethno-racial groups and tested for group differences in depression factor scores, this difference in ethnicity/race and national boundary simultaneously have rarely been investigated. Thus, it is unclear whether race/ethnicity (i.e., growing up in an Asian family regardless of country they grew up in), growing up in an Asian culture (being socialized in an Asian cultural milieu), or both, underlies structural differences in CES-D depression scores. Further, distinct cultural values and experiences such as collectivism and discrimination have been associated with depression in people with different backgrounds.

Method Participants and Recruitment Procedures

All study procedures were approved by [Masked for review] institutional review board.

Data came from college students (N = 439, 50.3% women, Mage = 20.26) at a large, public, predominantly Euro-American university located in the United States Midwest. Participants included students aged 18 years and older who identified as either Asian American (n = 144, 57.6% women, Mage = 20.80), Asian national (n = 168, 40.5% women, Mage = 20.07), or Euro American (n = 127, 55.1% women, Mage = 19.89). Most Asian American participants (64.6%) were US-born to Asian parents (64.6%) whereas the rest were with parents who immigrated to the US. Asian nationals were international students. Most Euro American participants were thirdor fourth-generation Americans (91.3%).

Participants were recruited primarily through the Psychology Subject Pool. Additional

Asian American participants were recruited via student-directed mass emails managed by the Office of the Registrar. Subject pool participants received research credits whereas students recruited via mass emails received monetary compensation. All participants reviewed and provided electronic informed consent. They then completed a demographic survey and a set of self-reported questionnaires.

Measures

Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression Scale (CES-D)

The CES-D (Radloff, 1977) is a 20-item self-report measure assessing depressive symptoms over the past week. Participants rated the frequency in which they experienced each symptom on a scale from 1 (rarely or none of the time/less than one day) to 4 (most or all of the time/five to seven days). The original data indicated a four-factor structure: Depressive Affect, Positive Affect, Somatic Symptoms, and Interpersonal Problems. The scale has been used globally, across age groups, and with general and clinical samples (Skriner & Chu, 2014). Prior research reported adequate scale reliability and expected correlations with external validators, such as anxiety symptoms, well-being, and aggression (Li & Hicks, 2010). The internal consistency were acceptable for Total (Cronbach's alpha = .906), Depressive Affect (.918), and Positive Affect (.832), but were suboptimal for Somatic Symptoms (.735) and Interpersonal Problems (.769) in the full sample (Clark & Watson, 2019).

External Measures of Cultural Values and Experiences

Loss of Face Questionnaire (Zane & Yeh, 2002). This is a 21-item self-report measure of sensitivity to appraised embarrassment, shame, or loss of status across face-threatening situations. Participants were asked to rate their agreement with each statement, on a scale of 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 7 (Strongly Agree). The scale data have shown internal consistency reliability, studies have used Asian-heritage people and Euro Americans, and findings include robust correlation but not redundancies with collectivistic valuation (Chen et al., 2024; Leong et al., 2018; Lui & Rollock, 2018). Loss of Face has been shown to be a culturally salient predictor for mental illness stigma and attitudes toward seeking professional services (P. Y. Kim & Yon, 2019; Leong et al., 2011; Tuliao et al., 2016). The internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach's alpha = .918) in the full sample.

Individualism/Collectivism Scale (Singelis et al., 1995). This is a 32-item self-report measure of an individual's alignment with values in Individualism and Collectivism, intersecting with the Horizontal vs. Vertical dimension (i.e., people sharing equal social status versus people being organized within social hierarchies). Participants rated their agreement with each statement on a scale of 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 9 (Strongly Agree). Individualism emphasizes the personal boundaries, self-reliance, and autonomy of personhood, whereas Collectivism emphasizes interpersonal relationships that define the personhood. Horizontal Individualism captures the beliefs about the uniqueness of individuals while recognizing equal statuses. Vertical Individualism captures the beliefs that people are distinct from one another and that there are inherent inequalities that give rise to competition and power differences. Horizontal Collectivism captures the beliefs that personhood is defined by the "collective" (such as national origin or racial categorization) and people from the same collective should enjoy equal treatment. Vertical Collectivism captures the beliefs that people identify with a collective but accept that inequality within the collective exists. The subscales have been used widely across segments of the global population, including with Asian American and Asian national groups (Fatehi et al., 2020; Renzhi et al., 2013). The internal consistency reliability was acceptable for Horizontal Individualism (Cronbach's alpha = .855), Vertical Individualism (.816), and Horizontal Collectivism (.857), but suboptimal for Vertical Collectivism (.776) in the full sample. Participants rated their agreement with each statement on a scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Data Analyses

Overall Approach to MI Testing MI of the CES-D scores was tested, aiming to achieve at least partial scalar invariance, which would allow for latent factor score to be used for group mean comparisons and to correlate with external variables. Theoretical alignment and model parsimony in the MI testing were preferred whenever possible, and statistical analysis were iteratively conducted based on the observed results within this framework. Radloff's (1977) original four-factor structure was used as the foundational theoretical model. If the data did not support this factor structure in groupspecific and group equivalence analysis, the original structure was retained as much as possible for continuity and consistency with past research. If data indicated non-MI across three groups, two groups were planned to be compared, one pair at a time, to locate the source of the omnibus non-MI. The overall process is summarized in Figure 1 and specific steps are elaborated below.

A Priori Three-Group Model Testing

First, the fit between Radloff's four-factor model (using all 20 items) with the full sample data was tested using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). If adequate or close fit was achieved at this point, configural, metric, scalar, and strict levels of invariance were to be tested across the three groups. Configural invariance would support equivalence of the four-factor structure across all three groups, keeping the factor loadings, item intercepts, and item residuals to be freely estimated for each group. Metric invariance would support equivalence of the structure and factor loadings across all three groups, leaving item intercepts and item residuals freely estimated for each group. Scalar invariance would support equivalence in the structure, factor loadings, and item intercepts across groups, and allowing valid comparisons of latent factor means. Strict invariance would support equivalence in the factor structure, loadings, item intercepts, and item residuals across the three groups. If configural invariance failed to converge or be supported by data, CFAs were to be conducted for each group separately to explore whether one or more group(s), factor(s), or specific item(s) might contribute to the variance of the factor structure.

Data-Driven Three-Group Model Testing

Achieving at least partial scalar invariance is a prerequisite to compare the latent factor scores across the three groups for further investigation (e.g., compare group means, correlating extracted factors with external factors). Hence, two data-driven pieces of information were used to identify the best fitting model in the full sample. First, modification indices were used to identify a set of items that would achieve configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance across the three groups. Second, each of the 20 items was removed one at a time until a set of CES-D items met at least scalar MI. Theory and past research were used to guide the removal of items when multiple competing options were attained. For example, past studies have found the Interpersonal Problems factor to be the least stable and it includes only two items, hence, likely is psychometrically unstable.

Pair-wise Model Testing

If scalar MI was not achieved across all three groups, MI was tested between two groups at a time to identify the group that was most distinct from the others. Here, model parsimony was preferred and data-driven approaches to identify the best fitting model and set of items equivalent across three pairs: Asian Americans and Asian nationals, Asian Americans and Euro Americans, and Asian nationals and Euro Americans.

Cultural Correlates of Depression Across Groups

Identified CES-D depression factor scores were then correlated to external cultural variables to identify possible values and experiences that may be similar or different across groups and explore how measurement invariant depressive symptoms manifest similarly or differently across groups. First, correlations between factor scores and each of the external factors were calculated: Loss of Face, Individualism and Collectivism, and Ethnic Experience variables. Second, to examine the nomological network similarities of depressive factors across groups, r alerting-CV was calculated (Westen & Rosenthal, 2003). This metric quantifies similarities and differences in the correlations with external correlates of the constructs of interest. The r alerting- CV will be high if the depressive symptoms manifest similarly across groups (closer to +1), and low if they manifest differently (closer to 0), as in correlations.

Statistical Software and Thresholds

Single-and multigroup factor analyses were conducted in Mplus version 8.1 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) and the other analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2026) using base and psych packages (Revelle, 2024). Because of the CES-D ordinal categorical variables, mean-and variance-adjusted weighted least squares (WLSMV) estimator was used in the factor analyses. Mplus defaults were used for other settings (e.g., geomin rotation). Model fit indices were used to evaluate the fit of the factor solutions with the observed data. Root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ .08, comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ .90, and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) ≥ .90 would indicate an acceptable fit. RMSEA ≤ .05, CFI ≥ .95, and TLI ≥ .95 would indicate a close fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Because more restrictive models nest within the models with more free parameter estimates (e.g., metric invariance model nested within configural invariance model), nonsignificant change in chi-square (using the DIFFTEST option) and a change in CFI (CFI) < .01 would indicate empirical support for invariance at each level (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002). If chi-square difference test and CFI yield inconsistent results, CFI was preferred, because chi-square tests are known for inappropriate sensitivity for large samples (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002). If any analyses resulted in non-positive definite matrices, the specified models were considered inappropriate. When comparing latent means across groups, p < .01 was used as the statistical significance threshold.

To quantify the cultural experiences similarities across groups, the r alerting-CV was calculated using the methods described in Westen & Rosenthal (2003). First, all correlations between CES-D factors and external validators within each group were z-transformed, resulting in a number of columns equal to three (one per group) times the number of factors (total of 12 possible columns if all four factors were measurement invariant). Then, two columns (rows representing each external validator) with the same corresponding z-transformed correlations from the same factor (e.g., CES-D Depressive Affect) for two different groups (e.g., Asian American and Asian national groups) were correlated. The resulting correlation quantifies the similarities in the constructs' associations to external validators.

Results

Four-Factor CFAs and MI

No participants in the Asian American and Euro American groups endorsed option 4 (Most or all of the time [5-7 days]) on CES-D item 17 ("I had crying spells"), and six participants in the Asian national group did. To allow testing for intercepts (scalar MI and beyond), the number of thresholds on the same item must be equal across all three groups. Thus, these option 4 responses were recoded to option 3 for this item.

Three-Factor CFAs and MI

To identify an adjusted model to compare across all three groups, including Asian national students, a data-driven approach was taken. First, modification indices quantifying the expected improvement based in the chi-square by freeing each parameter while retaining all items was investigated. Modification indicated adding cross-loadings of item 7 ("I felt that everything I did was an effort"; Somatic Symptoms) onto both Depressive Affect and Positive Affect factors. This adjustment still resulted in a non-positive definite latent matrix, suggesting that the Radloff's four-factor solution was unlikely to be simultaneously equivalent across Asian American, Asian national, and Euro American groups.

To the extent that the Interpersonal Problems factor from Radloff's model contained only two items and past studies finding it to be the least replicable factor in cross-cultural studies, a three-factor model with Depressive Affect, Positive Affect, and Somatic Symptoms (without the previously flagged item 7) was tested (results and process summarized in Figure 1; Right half). This modified three-factor model fit the full sample data adequately (N = 419; RMSEA = .070, 90% CI = [.062, .078]; CFI = .968; TLI = .963) and was tested for MI across all three groups (Table 2; Top quarter). Results indicated support for configural, metric, and scalar invariance for all three groups simultaneously. Strict invariance was not achieved, again due to a non-positive definite latent covariance matrix. Then, multigroup CFAs involving two groups at a time (Asian American vs Euro American; Asian national vs Euro American) were conducted to identify the potential unique group from the omnibus test (Table 2; Bottom three quarters). These analyses supported strict invariance of the modified three-factor structure between Asian American and Euro American groups, similar to Radloff's original four-factor model. Scalar invariance was achieved between Asian American and Asian national groups, and between Asian national and Euro American groups.

Differences in Depressive Affect, Positive Affect, and Somatic Symptoms across the Three

Groups

Scalar invariance of the modified three-factor CES-D model allowed comparison of latent means across all three groups (Table 3). Compared to the Asian American group, Euro Americans did not differ on any of the factor means but Asian nationals showed statistically higher latent scores indicating Depressive Affect and lower scores indicating Positive Affect (and did not differ in Somatic Symptoms). Using the group-invariant latent factor scores, correlations with the set of culture variables and r alerting-CV were calculated (Table 4). Associated cultural values and experiences with each domain across groups showed similar profiles for Depressive Affect (r = .61), Positive Affect (r = .59), and Somatic Symptoms (r = .66) between Asian American and Euro American groups. There were similar large associations of cultural variables (r ranging from .50 to .73) between Asian American and Asian national groups. However, Asian national and Euro American groups showed weaker similarities in associations with cultural factors overall: Positive Affect (r = .45), Depressive Affect (r = .22), and Somatic Symptoms (r = .21).

Although no a priori hypotheses were made, a closer examination of the correlation matrix linking the CES-D latent factors to specific cultural variables revealed unique patterns across groups (see Table S1). Specifically, in Asian American group, higher levels of Loss of Face correlated with higher scores indicating Depressive Affect (r = .27) and Somatic Symptom (r = .28). We did not observe consistent and robust correlations between ethnic experiences with CES-D scores in the Asian American group. By contrast, among Asian national students, lower levels of Ethnic Identity were consistently correlated with higher Depressive Affect (r = -.26), and Somatic Symptoms (r = -.23) and lower Positive Affect (r =.24). Higher Positive Affect was related to the Horizontal Individualism (r =.20) and Collectivism (r =.29). Finally, Euro American individuals who scored lower on Mainstream Comfort with the dominant culture also reported higher levels of Depressive Affect (r = -.31) and Somatic Symptoms (r = -.29) and lower Positive Affect, a similar pattern to Asian national students' Ethnic Identity findings.

Discussion

In a sample consisting of three ethnocultural groups that differed in race/heritage and socializing backgrounds, this study provides a unique insight into the expression and characteristics of depression symptoms as conceptualized in the CES-D. Overall, the original four-factor structure identified by Radloff (1977) was replicated in Asian and Euro American students (i.e., different race/heritage, similar mainstream socializing background), but not in Asian national students (i.e., different socializing background). This combination of findings challenges past studies equating race and socialization background. Further, the correlational patterns to external correlates showed a more nuanced picture than socialization background solely driving depression experience. Rather, Asian American people had similar cultural experiences associations across depression factors to both Euro American and Asian National students, while Euro American and Asian National students had different associations. This suggests different processes underlying or following depression symptoms across groups, and that both ethnic heritage and socialization background influences how depression is experienced.

The original Radloff's four-factor model fit was structurally equivalent in the Asian American and Euro American students. This validates past research findings with these two particular groups (Shafer, 2006). A meta-analysis on this group has found that the Asian American people have to different structures (G. Kim et al., 2011), but the current sample were likely more similar than typical such efforts (e.g., students attending a predominantly Euro American college in the Midwest). This pattern held even with the modified three-factor model, and their latent means did not differ, suggesting similar depression manifestation and levels across these two groups when only depression is examined (Skriner & Chu, 2014). These suggest the stressor as a minority member in the American society (Asian American) may not manifest as depression. At the same time, this lack of difference might reflect the self-selection process of attending the same University leading to more similar backgrounds and values. These different findings across studies potentially reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of the Asian American experiences in the US, and more research is needed on Asian American people are needed to resolve them. Asian national students did not have a similar CES-D factor structure to other groups examined here. There were consistent inappropriate model issues, including in the original Radloff four-factor model and when examined concurrently with Asian and Euro American groups. Removing the three items (two from the Interpersonal Problems factor and item 7) were necessary to achieve scalar invariance across all three groups. This difficulty of finding a factor structure in that Asian national group similar to that identified in the American groups and the particular issue with the Interpersonal Problems factor are consistent with previous MI investigation (G. Kim et al., 2011). Nonetheless, the Depressive Affect, Positive Affect, and Somatic Symptoms (except one item) reached scalar invariance across all three groups. At the same time, strict invariance was also not achieved for the Asian national group with either Asian or Euro American groups, suggesting that observed scores are biased and may not accurately reflect similar constructs. Collectively, these suggest that, when modeled in a factor analytic framework, depression factors may be compared, but caution is needed when interpreting the observed factor levels. Using the items in the modified factor structure, Asian national students seem to be experiencing more distress (Depressive Affect) and less joy (Positive Affect) than their peers. Combined with the lack of difference between the Euro and Asian American students, this suggests that socializing culture contributes to how "much" depression traits manifest, and as well as the stress of being overseas (for Asian national students; Oduwaye et al., 2023). The difference found between Asian American and Asian national students warns against equating race and psychological processes. Both groups would be categorized within the "Asian" racial group, but Asian nationals are at higher risk for depression (based on latent means) than Asian Americans. While this study could not delineate the processes underlying the differences and future research mindful of the cultural background, and not only race/ethnicity, is clearly needed.

The cultural nomological network of the factors provide complementary context to how the depression factors differ in "cultural meanings" across groups. The summary index of the cultural experiences suggests that the Asian and Euro American students had generally similar nomological network across all three depression domains, consistent with the MI and latent mean comparisons. These reinforce the role of socialization in American values as a major influence on how depression is experienced in these groups. Also consistent with the MI analyses, Asian national students differed from Euro American students, continuing to suggest different manifestation of depression across these two groups (even when an equivalent three factor model was achieved). However, unlike the MI findings, Asian American students had similar profiles to Asian national students as well, suggesting that their heritage background also affect how depression is manifested (Kalibatseva et al., 2017). This is consistent with some anecdotal reports from Asian American people that they feel both Asian and American. As external correlates, this project investigated understudied cultural experiences and used constructs designed to capture cultural ambivalence (mainstream vs. heritage), particularly among Asian American people. However, there are likely other external correlates that need to be investigated to fully understand how the processes of depression differ across groups, even if MI is achieved.

For both researchers and clinicians, this highlights the need to be sensitive to how these depressive symptoms manifest and affect individuals with different backgrounds. For example, in Asian American individuals, increased Loss of Face appears to be the strongest correlates of depression factors. While, in Asian nationals, retaining Ethnic Identity appears to be a protective factor (Huynh et al., 2014). Similarly, in Euro Americans, Mainstream Comfort is the strongest correlate. Being aware of these different correlates could help clinicians identifying depression, personalizing care, and considering interventions.

Some limitations need to be noted. First, the sample consisted of students who selfselected to attend the same university. Thus, they may have shared more characteristics than individuals in the community. Therefore, MI was more likely to be achieved in such homogeneity across groups, and it could limit the generalizability to the broader community members. Given this potential shared characteristic, the lack of MI of Asian national students with their American peers stands out as an important warning against conflating race and background experiences. Second is heterogeneity within each group, particularly in the Asian groups. The sample was of Pan-Asian background and acculturation was not accounted for. East Asian groups are likely to report more depression symptoms than South Asian groups (Rahman & Rollock, 2004). Further, some evidence also points to the non-equivalence of CES-D within this group (e.g., East Asians, South Asians; H. J. Kim et al., 2015; Santomauro et al., 2021).

Investigating the heterogeneity within the groups would be needed and would require a much larger sample size.

In sum, the current findings suggest that Asian American and Euro American groups (i.e., different race, but similar socialization background) share the four-factor structure of Depressive Affect, Positive Affect, Somatic Symptoms, and Interpersonal Problems as replicated in various previous studies. These factors achieved strict measurement invariance, supporting the continued use of Radloff's original four-factor scores calculated from participant and patient responses.

However, the Asian national group did not have the same structure, and this study found that removing the Interpersonal Problems factor and an item from the Somatic Symptoms factor was necessary. The modified 3-factor model could be the basis for a "universal" CES-D structure, if that is attainable, although more research is needed. Further, even with the same structure, the characteristics of depressive experience differ across groups, which suggests caution is necessary when monitoring depressive symptoms and considering intervention.
