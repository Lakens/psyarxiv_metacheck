Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Alcohol-valence Implicit Association Tests (IATs) assess relative compatibility between response mappings, but findings in alcohol use disorder (AUD) are mixed. We examined alcohol-valence congruency effects in abstinent, treatment-engaged adults with AUD using a shortened Spanish-language bipolar IAT. Thirty-four participants (17 with AUD, abstinent for 2-24 months and receiving community-based treatment in Buenos Aires, Argentina; 17 controls) classified alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverage words and positive and negative valence words. Reaction times were analyzed using a 2 × 2 × 2 mixed ANOVA (Congruency × Stimulus Type × Group). Responses were faster overall for the alcohol-negative/non-alcohol-positive pairing than for the reverse pairing. Critically, the Congruency × Group interaction was significant, F(1, 32) = 10.73, p = .003, ηp² = .251. The mean congruency effect was 266.0 ms in controls and 83.2 ms in AUD. The corresponding interaction in accuracy was not significant, and the reaction-time interaction remained significant after natural-log transformation of participant-level mean reaction times. The three-way Congruency × Stimulus Type × Group interaction was not significant. These findings indicate an attenuated relative alcohol-valence congruency effect in abstinent AUD, without identifying which individual associative components account for this difference.

Introduction

Contemporary cognitive accounts of alcohol use disorder (AUD) emphasize the interaction between deliberative control and relatively automatic responses to alcohol-related cues and learned associations (Lindgren et al., 2019). These processes may remain relevant during abstinence, when alcohol consumption has ceased but alcohol-related cues continue to be encountered. Characterizing associative responses in abstinent individuals with AUD is therefore important for understanding alcohol-related cognition beyond active drinking.

Indirect behavioral tasks offer one approach to examining how alcohol-related concepts are processed in this clinical context.

The Implicit Association Test (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?8HjUu7 Greenwald et al., 1998) is a widely used indirect behavioral measure of relative compatibility between categories. In this speeded classification task, target and attribute categories share response keys in alternative mappings.

Faster performance in one mapping than the other is interpreted as indicating greater relative compatibility between the paired categories. Importantly, the term "implicit" refers to the indirect nature of the measurement procedure; IAT performance does not, by itself, establish that the underlying psychological processes are unconscious (Greenwald et al., 2022).

Alcohol-related IATs assess distinct associative dimensions, including evaluative valence, arousal, approach-avoidance, and drinking identity, rather than a single implicit alcohol attitude (Schwippel et al., 2022; Wiers et al., 2002). In a bipolar alcohol-valence IAT, alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages are categorized alongside positive and negative attributes. Wiers et al. (2002) found that both light and heavy drinkers responded faster when alcohol shared a response key with negative attributes and soft drinks with positive attributes than under the reverse pairing. In contrast, an alcohol-arousal IAT revealed an alcohol-arousal effect primarily among heavy drinkers. These findings highlight the importance of distinguishing alcohol-valence associations from other dimensions of alcohol-related implicit cognition.

The bipolar structure of an alcohol-valence IAT limits the interpretation of its congruency effects. Faster responses when alcohol is paired with negative attributes and non-alcoholic beverages with positive attributes may reflect stronger alcohol-negative associations, stronger non-alcohol-positive associations, or both. Moreover, the bipolar contrast does not establish whether positive and negative alcohol associations coexist.

Although task features such as category labels and salience asymmetries may influence performance, previous research supports the utility of alcohol-valence IATs for assessing relative associative compatibility (Houben & Wiers, 2006). Accordingly, congruency effects should be interpreted as reflecting compatibility between combined response mappings rather than as independent measures of specific alcohol-related associations.

Evidence from clinical samples illustrates the interpretive challenges posed by bipolar alcohol-valence IAT effects. De Houwer et al. (2004) administered a bipolar IAT and an Extrinsic Affective Simon Task (EAST) to patients receiving treatment for alcohol problems.

Although the IAT indicated a more negative relative evaluation of alcohol than soft drinks, EAST results suggested a positive evaluation of soft drinks alongside a comparatively neutral evaluation of alcohol. The authors noted that apparent neutrality toward alcohol could reflect coexisting positive and negative associations. Dickson et al. (2013) further examined alcohol-valence associations in recently detoxified alcohol-dependent patients using separate positive-neutral and negative-neutral unipolar IATs. Compared with social-drinking controls, patients showed weaker alcohol-negative associations but no significant group difference in alcohol-positive associations. Both tasks nevertheless retained alcoholic versus non-alcoholic beverages as the target contrast. These findings offer a plausible explanation for attenuated bipolar alcohol-valence congruency effects in clinical samples, although this explanation cannot be established from bipolar IAT performance alone.

Behavioral evidence on alcohol-valence compatibility in abstinent clinical samples remains limited. In recently abstinent patients undergoing inpatient treatment, Tschuemperlin et al. (2020) found no statistically significant patient-control difference in the behavioral effect of a bipolar alcohol-valence IAT, although event-related potentials differed between groups. Their task used pictures of patients' preferred alcoholic beverages contrasted with water, and behavioral analyses focused on alcohol-picture trials. Differences in stimulus format, trial selection, and treatment context complicate direct comparisons with studies using word-based stimuli and broader trial-level classifications. Examining relative alcohol-valence compatibility in abstinent individuals receiving community-based treatment using an abbreviated word-based IAT therefore provides complementary evidence under distinct clinical and methodological conditions.

The present study examined relative alcohol-valence compatibility in abstinent, treatment-engaged individuals with AUD and controls without AUD using an abbreviated Spanish-language bipolar IAT. We operationally labeled the alcohol-negative/non-alcohol-positive pairing as congruent and the reverse pairing as incongruent; these labels describe the experimental conditions rather than universally compatible or incompatible associations. The primary objective was to determine whether the reaction-time congruency effect (incongruent minus congruent) differed between groups.

Based on previous clinical evidence, we hypothesized a smaller congruency effect in the AUD group. Secondary analyses examined whether the between-group difference in congruency effects varied according to the type of stimulus being classified (beverage versus valence words) and characterized group differences in response speed during single-category classification blocks. Eligibility for the AUD group required continuous abstinence from alcohol for 2 months to 2 years, established through self-report and corroborated by clinical information from treating physicians. Participants were also required to have abstained from other drugs of abuse for at least 2 months before testing. A history of other substance use or substance use disorders was permitted provided that alcohol was the primary substance-related disorder.

Methods

Exclusion criteria for both groups included current or previous neurological or psychotic disorders. Anxiety and depressive disorders were not exclusionary. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital El Cruce, Florencio Varela, Buenos Aires, Argentina (Number 023/2010, Folio 062 del libro de Actas N° 1).

Clinical and psychological assessment

Alcohol-related problems were assessed in both groups using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?XaSpUR Babor et al., 2001). Depressive symptoms were assessed with the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; Beck et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?yRaBAP 1996) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ALPPwc and state and trait anxiety with the Spanish adaptation of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; Spielberg et al., 1982) , with each scale ranging from 0 to 60. These measures were used to characterize the clinical profile of the sample rather than as primary experimental outcomes. For participants with AUD, abstinence duration, age at onset of problematic alcohol use, and history of other substance use were also recorded during the clinical interview.

Alcohol-valence Implicit Association Test

Relative alcohol-valence compatibility was assessed using an abbreviated Spanish-language bipolar Implicit Association Test (IAT; Greenwald et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xCvYYu 1998) , implemented in E-Prime.

The task was originally designed with potential future application in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in mind, although the present study was conducted outside the scanner. All stimuli were Spanish words belonging to four categories: alcoholic beverages, non-alcoholic beverages, positive valence, and negative valence. Each category comprised eight exemplars. The complete stimulus list and English translations are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

The task began with two single-category classification blocks comprising 24 trials in total. Twelve trials required classification of beverage words as alcoholic or non-alcoholic, and 12 required classification of valence words as positive or negative. Participants subsequently completed two combined IAT blocks of 42 trials each, comprising 21 beverage-word trials and 21 valence-word trials. In the operationally congruent condition, alcoholic beverages shared a response key with negative valence and non-alcoholic beverages with positive valence. In the incongruent condition, these pairings were reversed. The order of the combined conditions was counterbalanced across participants.

On each trial, a single word appeared at the center of the screen. During combined blocks, the upper-left and upper-right corners displayed the beverage and valence categories assigned to each response key. Participants classified stimuli using the left or right arrow key, according to the category of the presented word. Category-label positions varied across blocks. Because beverage-word and valence-word trials contributed to the same combined response mappings, Stimulus Type refers to the dimension of the word being classified rather than an independent associative measure (Figure 1).

Participants completed practice trials until they demonstrated adequate understanding of the classification rules. No accuracy feedback was provided during experimental blocks.

Stimuli remained on screen until a response was made or a 4-s response deadline elapsed.

Excluding practice trials, the task comprised 108 classification trials.

Procedure

Assessments were conducted in person. After providing written informed consent, participants completed the clinical and psychiatric assessment, including demographic information, alcohol-use history, and relevant psychiatric and substance-use history, followed by the AUDIT, BDI-II, and STAI. The computerized IAT was subsequently administered using E-Prime. For the AUD group, information regarding abstinence duration and previous substance use was cross-checked against available clinical information provided by the treating physician.

Data preprocessing

Reaction times more than two standard deviations above or below the mean of the corresponding group and combined condition were excluded, and participant-level condition means were then computed. The congruency effect was defined as the difference in mean RT between the incongruent and congruent combined conditions. Accuracy was expressed as the percentage of correct responses in each condition.

Statistical analysis

Sample characteristics were summarized by group. Age was compared using Welch's independent-samples t test and education level using an omnibus Fisher's exact test with Monte Carlo estimation because of sparse cells. Sex was reported descriptively because its distribution was matched by design. AUDIT, BDI-II, and STAI scores and the AUD-specific clinical variables were used for descriptive characterization.

The primary reaction time (RT) analysis used a 2 × 2 × 2 mixed-design ANOVA with Congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) and Stimulus Type (beverage vs. valence word) as within-subject factors and Group (AUD vs. control) as the between-subject factor. The primary effect of interest was the Congruency × Group interaction. The ANOVA was implemented using participant-level means and within-subject contrasts analyzed through linear models. Partial eta squared (ηp²) is reported for ANOVA effects. The same factorial structure was applied to accuracy as a secondary performance measure.

When the Congruency × Group interaction was significant, paired-samples t tests compared congruent and incongruent RTs separately within each group. A Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of α = .025 was applied to these two tests, and Cohen's dz was reported as the effect-size measure for paired comparisons.

Supplementary analyses calculated RT congruency-effect scores (RT incongruent -RT congruent ; positive values indicate faster performance in the congruent condition) separately for beverage-word and valence-word trials. These scores were compared between groups using Welch's independent-samples t tests with Bonferroni correction across the two comparisons. Because both stimulus types contributed to the same combined response mappings, these analyses characterized the expression of the congruency effect rather than independent associative components (Nosek et al., 2005). The Congruency × Stimulus Type × Group interaction was used as the formal test of whether the between-group difference varied by stimulus type. RTs from single-category classification blocks were also compared between groups using Welch's t tests, with Bonferroni correction across the two block comparisons. Hedges' g was reported for independent-group comparisons. Reported 95% confidence intervals for supplementary comparisons were not adjusted for multiple testing.

All tests were two-tailed, with α = .05 unless otherwise specified. Analyses were conducted in Python using pandas, SciPy, and statsmodels. As a sensitivity analysis, the primary RT ANOVA was repeated using log-transformed reaction times to examine the robustness of the Congruency × Group interaction to differences in overall response speed. A sensitivity power analysis using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007) indicated that, with 17 participants per group and α = .05, the design had 80% power to detect a Congruency × Group interaction of f = 0.50 (ηp² = .20).

Results

Participant and clinical characteristics

Participant characteristics are shown in Table 1. The groups did not differ significantly in age, Welch's t(31.07) = 0.46, p = .647, or education level (omnibus Fisher's exact p = .333), and were matched by design on sex (7 women and 10 men in each group).

Within the AUD group, mean abstinence duration was 6.76 months (SD = 3.91), mean age at onset of problematic alcohol use was 19.35 years (SD = 6.38), and 11 participants (64.7%) had a documented history of other substance use.

AUDIT, BDI-II, and STAI state and trait scores were descriptively higher in the AUD group than in controls (Table 1). Sex-stratified descriptive scores are presented in Supplementary Table S1. These clinical measures were not subjected to inferential testing.

Accuracy

Accuracy in the combined IAT blocks was high in both groups (Supplementary Table S2). The 2 × 2 × 2 mixed ANOVA on accuracy (Supplementary Table S3) revealed no significant main effects or interactions, including the Congruency × Group interaction, F(1, 32) = 0.48, p = .494, ηp² = .015. Thus, the RT pattern described below was not accompanied by a statistically significant Congruency × Group interaction in accuracy.

Reaction times in the combined IAT

RT descriptives are presented in Supplementary Table S4 and the mixed ANOVA in Table 2. Across groups and stimulus types, responses were faster in the congruent condition (M = 960.65 ms) than in the incongruent condition (M = 1135.25 ms), F(1, 32) = 39.16, p < .001, ηp² = .550. Importantly, there was a significant Congruency × Group interaction, F(1, 32) = 10.73, p = .003, ηp² = .251, indicating a smaller congruency effect in the AUD group than in controls.

Follow-up paired comparisons (Figure 2; Supplementary Table S5) showed that controls responded 266.0 ms faster in the congruent than in the incongruent condition, 95% CI [174.9, 357.1], t(16) = 6.19, Bonferroni-adjusted p < .001, Cohen's dz = 1.50. In the AUD group, the corresponding difference was 83.2 ms, 95% CI [7.8, 158.7], t(16) = 2.34, Bonferroni-adjusted p = .065, dz = 0.57. Thus, although the mean congruency effect was positive in both groups, only the control group's within-group comparison remained statistically significant after correction. The reported 95% confidence intervals were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

The Congruency × Stimulus Type × Group interaction was not statistically significant, F(1, 32) = 1.35, p = .254, ηp² = .041, providing no significant evidence that the between-group difference in congruency effects varied according to whether beverage or valence words were classified. No other main effects or interactions reached statistical significance (Table 2).

Supplementary analyses and sensitivity checks

Congruency-effect scores were examined separately by Stimulus Type (Supplementary Table S6). For beverage-word trials, the mean congruency effect was larger in controls than in AUD (284.6 vs. 54.5 ms), with a between-group difference of 230.1 ms (Bonferroni-adjusted p = .006, Hedges' g = 1.07). For valence-word trials, the corresponding effects were 247.4 and 111.9 ms, respectively, with a between-group difference of 135.4 ms (adjusted p = .098, Hedges' g = 0.68). However, the nonsignificant Congruency × Stimulus Type × Group interaction did not support a conclusion that the between-group difference was specific to beverage-word trials.

Participants with AUD also showed slower responses during single-category classification (Supplementary Table S7). Mean RTs were 1102.9 ms in AUD versus 865.6 ms in controls for beverage classification (adjusted p < .001), and 1156.1 versus 865.0 ms for valence classification (adjusted p = .001). These findings indicate slower classification performance in AUD even when only one categorization dimension was active.

In the sensitivity analysis using natural-log-transformed participant-level mean RTs, the Congruency × Group interaction remained significant, F(1, 32) = 12.82, p = .001, ηp² = .286. This finding supports the robustness of the between-group difference in congruency effects to the scale of RT measurement.

Discussion

The present study found a reduced alcohol-valence congruency effect in abstinent, treatment-engaged individuals with alcohol use disorder (AUD) compared with controls. The significant Congruency × Group interaction indicated that the response-time advantage for the alcohol-negative/non-alcohol-positive pairing over the reverse pairing was approximately three times larger in controls (266.0 ms) than in AUD (83.2 ms). Importantly, the mean effect in AUD remained in the same direction as in controls, although the within-group comparison did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction. Thus, the findings indicate an attenuation of the relative congruency effect rather than a reversal in the preferred response pairing.

Because the bipolar IAT measures relative compatibility between alternative response mappings, the attenuated congruency effect cannot be attributed to any single associative component. Evidence from a unipolar IAT provides one possible explanation: Dickson et al.

(https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?YntMZ5) found weaker alcohol-negative associations, without a corresponding increase in alcohol-positive associations, in recently detoxified alcohol-dependent patients compared with controls. Their findings suggest that weakened negative alcohol associations could contribute to the attenuation observed here. However, the present design cannot distinguish this possibility from differences in alcohol-positive associations or associations involving the non-alcoholic reference category. Previous research also indicates that positive and negative alcohol-related associations need not be mutually exclusive (De Houwer et al., 2004; Houben & Wiers, 2006; Wiers et al., 2002). Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as evidence of altered relative alcohol-valence compatibility rather than a specific change in negative or positive alcohol associations.

The clinical context of the present sample is relevant to interpreting these findings.

Participants with AUD had been abstinent for 2-24 months and were engaged in ongoing treatment and recovery. The reduced alcohol-valence congruency effect was therefore observed in individuals who were already abstinent at the time of assessment. However, the cross-sectional design cannot establish whether this difference preceded AUD, developed during prolonged problematic drinking, or changes over the course of recovery. Previous studies in nonclinical samples have shown that alcohol-valence associations vary with drinking patterns and may change within individuals over time (Dormal et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CffWLP 2021; Paige et al., 2022) , highlighting the importance of longitudinal research to characterize their evolution during abstinence.

Findings from other abstinent AUD samples have not been entirely consistent. Tschuemperlin et al. (2020) found no statistically significant behavioral difference between patients with AUD and controls on a bipolar alcohol-valence IAT, although patients showed descriptively less negative D-scores and group differences emerged in event-related potentials within the late positive potential timeframe. Direct comparison with the present findings is limited by methodological differences. Tschuemperlin et al. used pictures of preferred alcoholic beverages contrasted with water and trait adjectives, and their behavioral RT analyses focused on alcohol-picture trials. In contrast, our task used written beverage and affective-valence stimuli, and the primary analysis included both beverage-word and valence-word trials. Clinical characteristics also differed: their patients were assessed after approximately six weeks of abstinence during inpatient treatment, whereas our participants had been abstinent for 2-24 months and were engaged in community-based treatment. Their controls also reported greater alcohol involvement than ours (mean AUDIT approximately 4.5 vs. 1.2). These methodological and clinical differences limit direct comparison, and the reasons for the differing behavioral findings remain uncertain.

However, in the combined IAT, the between-group RT difference was substantially larger in the congruent condition (approximately 193 ms) than in the incongruent condition (approximately 11 ms), a pattern not readily explained by uniform slowing across conditions. Moreover, the Congruency × Group interaction remained significant after natural-log transformation of participant-level mean RTs. This sensitivity analysis supports the robustness of the interaction to the scale of RT measurement, although it does not exclude all possible influences of general response-speed differences. Depressive and anxiety symptoms were also descriptively higher in the AUD group. Because these symptoms were not included as covariates, their potential contribution to classification performance and congruency effects remains uncertain.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

The present study included a clinically characterized sample of abstinent, treatment-engaged individuals with AUD, alongside demographically comparable controls, and employed a counterbalanced within-subject design. Task accuracy was high, and the primary Congruency × Group interaction remained significant after log transformation of reaction times. Nevertheless, several methodological limitations warrant consideration. The sample was small (17 participants per group) and recruited by convenience, with no a priori sample-size calculation, limiting the precision and generalizability of the findings and the ability to detect smaller effects. The abbreviated IAT implementation was not independently evaluated for internal consistency or test-retest reliability. Furthermore, the dataset available for reproducibility contains participant-level aggregates rather than trial-level records, preventing independent reconstruction of RT preprocessing and more detailed examination of trial-level performance.

Additional limitations concern the interpretation of the observed group difference.

The cross-sectional design cannot establish whether the attenuated congruency effect preceded AUD, developed during problematic alcohol use, or changes during recovery.

Moreover, the bipolar IAT cannot isolate individual positive or negative alcohol associations.

Control participants reported very low alcohol involvement, potentially limiting generalizability to other non-AUD populations. Most participants with AUD had a history of other substance use, and depressive and anxiety symptoms were descriptively higher in this group; their potential contributions to task performance cannot be excluded. Future research should replicate these findings in larger and more diverse clinical samples using psychometrically characterized IAT procedures and trial-level data. Longitudinal studies combining bipolar and unipolar alcohol-valence measures could further clarify the associative components underlying congruency effects, their evolution during abstinence, and their potential relationship with subsequent drinking outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, abstinent, treatment-engaged individuals with AUD showed a significantly attenuated alcohol-valence congruency effect compared with controls. This group difference remained significant after log transformation of reaction times, although the bipolar IAT design does not allow the specific associative components underlying the effect to be identified. These findings provide preliminary evidence of altered relative alcohol-valence compatibility in abstinent AUD and warrant replication in larger clinical samples using measures that can distinguish associative components and examine their potential changes during recovery.
