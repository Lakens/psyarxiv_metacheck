This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

People and their environments continuously shape one another across time. Understanding personenvironment transactions is essential for describing, predicting, and explaining the dynamics and development of individual differences. Yet, progress on this has been constrained by two common limitations: study designs driven by practical rather than theoretical considerations and neglecting how person-environment processes unfold across multiple timescales. Here, we present the rationale and design of the My Personality--Wrapped (MPW) Study, a measurement-burst study designed to investigate how individual differences in personality, well-being, attitudes, social and interpersonal behaviors, and health develop and dynamically fluctuate alongside environmental influences across multiple timescales. Across 1 year, participants (Nmax = 1,230) completed baseline (T0) and post-study (T6) assessment batteries, five bimonthly surveys (T1-T5), and 15 pseudorandomly scheduled 1-week bursts comprising mobile sensing, experience sampling, daily, and weekly surveys. This intensive, multi-method study provides comprehensive cross-timescale data on psychological constructs and contextual experiences. The MPW Study is designed to advance the field's understanding of temporal dynamics underlying individual differences by disentangling within-person processes from between-person differences, facilitating theory development and testing of multi-timescale person-environment transactions, and informing future theory-driven research on the dynamics of personality and well-being, social and health processes, and life disruptions.

My Personality--Wrapped: Protocol for a Measurement-Burst Design Study to Investigate the Development and Dynamics of Individual Differences Across Time

Individual differences in psychological characteristics like personality, well-being, and attitudes capture how people tend to think, feel, and behave (Albarracin & Shavitt, 2018; Eid & Diener, 2004; Roberts & Yoon, 2022). These constructs are related to many aspects of people's lives (Roberts & Nickel, 2021; Wilmot et al., 2025), including health, work-related success, and relationships (Beck & Jackson, 2022; Leikas et al., 2018; Reist et al., 2023; Soto, 2015 Soto, , 2019;; A. J. Wright & Jackson, 2022). These characteristics also predict the selection into and navigation of social environments (Bühler et al., 2023; Converse et al., 2018; Haehner, Krämer, et al., 2026; Rauthmann et al., 2015; Roemer et al., 2026; Rohrer & Lucas, 2018), with research supporting bidirectional transactions between people and their environments. Psychological constructs thus shape selection into varied environments, while environments and experiences therewithin predict subsequent psychological changes (Borghuis et al., 2020; Hoff et al., 2021; Jokela et al., 2018; Mund & Neyer, 2014; Piccolo, 2008; Reitz, 2022; Soto, 2015; A. J. Wright & Jackson, 2023).

Reciprocal and dynamic temporal relations between people and their environments are referred to as person-environment transactions (Rauthmann, 2021). For personality and related individual differences, understanding such transactions is key to accurately describing, predicting, and explaining short-term dynamics and long-term development (Jackson & Wright, 2024; Kandler & Rauthmann, 2022; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). However, some aspects of past research have made it difficult to achieve these goals. First, the implications of time are often neglected (Hopwood et al., 2022; A. G. C. Wright, 2026). Theories rarely specify concrete predictions and boundary conditions for the temporality of psychological processes, often leading designs to be guided by practical over theoretical choices. Second, data in single studies are typically assessed at fixed intervals, like daily for short-term dynamics or yearly for long-term change (Kuper et al., 2024). Psychological processes do not occur on singular, discrete intervals, though, leading these data to offer limited insight on target phenomena. Third, it is difficult to integrate cross-timescale results across studies. Effects at one timescale may not emerge at others (e.g., Bülow et al., 2025; Haehner, Schellenberg, et al., 2026; Krämer, Schaefer, et al., 2026), but when this is inferred from between-study comparisons, it cannot be determined whether such differences reflect substantive theoretical processes or differences in study designs. Fourth, many studies are not designed to disentangle within-versus between-person differences, complicating causal inference and limiting questions that can be answered (Costantini & Perugini, 2018; Hopwood et al., 2025). Together, this has led to a limited theoretical understanding of psychological change mechanisms and often imprecise empirical articulations of underlying processes (Bleidorn, 2024; Wagner et al., 2020).

Addressing these limitations can strengthen the field in multiple ways. Theoretically, understanding temporal resolutions of different processes provides the necessary foundation to develop integrative, precise theories about psychological phenomena (Andresen et al., 2025; Bleidorn, 2024; Hamaker, 2023). Empirically, data collected from theoretically informed designs bolsters the conclusions that a study can make, expands the range of research questions that can be tested, and facilitates integrating cross-study results. Identifying the timescales at which different person-environment transactions unfold also has important practical implications. Insight into the timing of different psychological processes can improve research by guiding optimal design features (Hopwood et al., 2022; A. G. C. Wright, 2026), and inform which assessment schedules offer temporal resolutions needed to detect specific effects while minimizing participant burden.

Moreover, this knowledge can be used to guide the timing and personalization of event-contingent and just-in-time interventions (Nahum-Shani et al., 2018; A. G. C. Wright & Woods, 2020).

Here, we present the protocol for the My Personality--Wrapped (MPW) Study, which leveraged a multi-method measurement-burst design to investigate the development and dynamics of person-environment links across multiple timescales. Across 1 year, participants completed a baseline assessment battery (T0), five bimonthly surveys (T1-T5), and a post-study assessment battery (T6) of measures for personality; well-being; attitudes; social and interpersonal variables; events and experiences; and health, diet, and lifestyle variables. Further, between T0 and T6, participants completed up to 15 1-week measurement bursts, comprising packages of mobilesensing data collection and experience-sampling, daily, and weekly surveys.

By combining multi-method and multi-timescale assessments of individual differences and environmental factors, the MPW Study seeks to fill conceptual and empirical gaps concerning the temporality of psychological processes. Specifically, it will establish a rich resource for the study of person-environment transactions, facilitate theoretically informed studies of cross-timescale dynamics and within-versus between-person processes, and provide an empirical foundation for advancing theories of person-environment transactions in personality science and beyond.

Conceptualizing Individual Differences Across Time

Many psychological frameworks converge on the idea that individual difference constructs can be assessed at different temporal resolutions and levels of abstraction (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Fleeson, 2007; Geukes et al., 2018; Jackson & Wright, 2024; McAdams & Pals, 2006; McCrae & Costa, 2008; Roberts & Pomerantz, 2004; E. L. Rosenberg, 1998; A. J. Wright et al., 2026a; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). Personality theories, for example, often distinguish broader traits versus narrower states (Figure 1A). Trait-level constructs are person-typical patterns of interrelated thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that are relatively stable and consistent across situations, whereas state-level constructs are momentary trait expressions that are situation specific, highly malleable, and variable in range and frequency. Some frameworks also define an intermediate habit level (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Jackson & Wright, 2024), or learned, routine clusters of traitrepresentative thoughts, feelings, or behaviors in specific contexts/domains (Verplanken & Orbell, 2022). Trait, habit, and state levels of a construct are interconnected such that change in one level can facilitate change in another via bottom-up (i.e., state ® trait) or top-down (i.e., trait ® state) processes. At each level, constructs also vary in their aspects, or relative proportions of affective, behavioral, cognitive, and desiderative content (ABCD; Wilt & Revelle, 2015; Zillig et al., 2002).

Theories on person-environment transactions stipulate these different levels are not merely instances of different temporal resolutions but rather that causal relations between levels explain long-term development and short-term dynamics (Bleidorn et al., 2026; Jackson & Wright, 2024; Mischel & Shoda, 1995). For example, momentary experiences of failure or success are believed to shape state self-esteem, with repeated state changes predicting trait self-esteem change (Reitz, 2022). Similarly, most people experience temporary feelings of loneliness, but their manner of coping with these and any (mal)adaptive reconnection behaviors are theorized to predict who experiences trait loneliness (Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010). For personality, some theories view traits as density distributions of cumulative state expressions (Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015), whereby states are products of momentary person-situation interactions guided by social-cognitive forces. Repeated novel state-situation reactions shift the central tendency and skewness of state distributions and, over time, constitute trait change. Similar theories posit that trait change occurs from exposure to situations that elicit novel states, states being reinforced via reactions from one's environment, repeated state-situation links generalizing to other contexts, and, over time, being integrated into one's trait self-concept (Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). Of note, despite the general mechanistic focus on psychological processes from brief to longer timescales, the reverse path is also relevant. For instance, broadly held moral beliefs and identities shape one's moral attitudes and behaviors across situations (Reynolds & Ceranic, 2007; Sparks & Shepherd, 2002), and salient cues to one's self-concept can influence state expressions (Jackson & Wright, 2024). Cross-Timescale Data Collection

One aspect that is a recurring theme in limitations of past research, a key design element in empirical studies, and integral to understanding both development and dynamics in psychological constructs is time (Hopwood et al., 2022). Theoretical considerations largely pertain to the timescales of different psychological processes and the temporal resolutions over which changes or environmentally mediated effects are expected to occur. Empirically, time-related features of studies are assessment intervals (e.g., hours, months), timings of measures (e.g., end of day, eventcontingent), total assessment durations (e.g., 2 weeks, 6 months), and instructed reference periods of measures (e.g., "in general", "at this moment"; Hamaker, 2023).

In addition to their conceptual distinctions, constructs at different levels of abstraction tend to have their own customary assessment frequencies and reference periods (Figure 1B). Narrower state-level constructs are often assessed by asking people to report on their current/recent thoughts, feelings, or behaviors with experience sampling methods (ESM) or ecological momentary assessment (EMA: Hektner et al., 2007; Shiffman et al., 2008). A benefit of state measures is that people can provide dynamic, real-time feedback about their environments and psychological responses in different situations (Conner & Barrett, 2012). Collecting reliable information at such narrow temporal resolutions does generally require more frequent assessments within a given period, making state measures more burdensome for participants than trait measures.

Broader trait-level constructs, in contrast, are usually assessed with global or retrospective measures in which people report on their general or aggregate experiences over extended periods.

A benefit of trait measures is that people can provide consolidated overviews of their cumulative experiences and long-term identities that they use to navigate and make decisions within their environments (Conner & Barrett, 2012). As a single trait assessment offers sufficient information for this level, trait measures are often less burdensome than state measures; however, they customarily offer only a global snapshot of the assessed constructs.

Despite scholars detailing the empirical and theoretical importance of time (Andresen et al., 2025; Hopwood et al., 2022), optimal timescales for different research questions remain poorly understood. Empirical precedence and practical feasibility often guide design choices like total study durations or measurement time lags. General guidelines from past work may be adequate for many questions, like how mean levels of a trait develop over 1 year or how states covary with different situations. However, default design choices are likely less sensitive to processes that are more nuanced or complex than previously assumed, such as distinct lagged relationships between variables across timescales (Andresen et al., 2025; Haehner et al., 2025; Krämer, Schaefer, et al., 2026; A. J. Wright et al., 2026b) or heterogeneous forms of nonlinear change in psychological constructs (Philippi et al., 2026; A. J. Wright & Jackson, 2024). Moreover, even if conventional design choices detect effects, that itself is not evidence that the implemented features are optimal.

Unless time-related design features are systematically varied and compared (e.g., Büchner et al., 2026), it remains unclear which assessment schedules and durations are best suited to capture different psychological processes. Aligning study designs with theoretical expectations about the temporal dynamics of target processes is therefore critical yet uncommon in current research.

Measurement-Burst Design

Measurement-burst designs allow researchers to flexibly assess and track many individual differences and person-environment transactions across different timescales and contexts, while mitigating participant burden compared to a more contiguous assessment schedule (Cho et al., 2019; Nesselroade, 1991; Sliwinski, 2008). In this design, longitudinal assessments occur in successive "bursts" that each consist of a concentrated period of high-density measures (e.g., ESM or daily diary) and are separated by weeks, months, or years. Measurement-burst studies therefore combine features of intensive longitudinal studies--frequent, brief measures over shorter periods--with long-term longitudinal studies--multiple trait assessments spaced over longer periods.

Measurement-burst studies offer several advantages over other designs (Sliwinski, 2008).

For instance, they allow dynamic within-person processes to be captured alongside a range of environmental and contextual information. Additionally, by collecting data across multiple timescales, within-person dynamic processes can be isolated from person-typical individual differences in dynamics. For example, with data from a measurement-burst design, researchers can examine participants' state neuroticism expressions in different contexts for single bursts, parse this variability into different state-context links versus people's burst-typical magnitudes of within-person state neuroticism variability, and investigate how these links may differ for brief stressful situations, daily hassles, or more long-term negative events. Overall, the breadth and multi-timescale nature of data from these designs permit thorough examinations of many personenvironment transactions, while offering greater precision and statistical power to detect effects.

Existing measurement-burst studies (e.g., Chopik et al., 2026; Grob et al., 2025; Pasquini, 2022; Quintus et al., 2021; Rush et al., 2025; Wrzus et al., 2021) have demonstrated many of these advantages. However, most of these studies assessed constructs at only two timescales, typically combining global surveys with a single intensive timescale a (e.g., daily measure). Although this design provides a richer perspective on psychological processes than traditional approaches, it still offers only a limited view of how these processes unfold across timescales. A measurement-burst study with more than two timescales of assessment, parallel measures of the same constructs across timescales, and measures beyond self-reports may help overcome these limitations.

The My Personality--Wrapped (MPW) Study

The MPW Study leveraged a measurement-burst design across 1 year to comprehensively examine the development and dynamics of person-environment transactions across momentary, daily, weekly, and bimonthly timescales. This design was used to collect data across multiple temporal resolutions, isolate personal and environmental dynamics across timescales and levels of analysis, disentangle effects driven by stable person characteristics and within-person processes, and connect short-and long-term psychological processes. For the study, we selected variables that are of broad interest in personality psychology and for which person-environment transactions have been theoretically proposed or empirically examined. Our selection was intended to balance constructs with broad relevance to the field, such as the Big Five and self-esteem, and constructs reflecting more targeted interests and expertise within the research team, such as attachment and animal-product consumption. This facilitates the examination of person-environment transactions across multiple timescales using both well-established and more domain-specific individual difference variables. Ultimately, the key aims of the MPW Study are to investigate (1) how different indices of personality and related psychological or contextual constructs (e.g., stress, loneliness, dietary habits) develop over time, (2) how variables' dynamics vary and relate to one another across timescales, and (3) whether these variables and their associations vary as a function of environmental factors across different temporal resolutions.

Participants and Recruitment

German-speaking adults between the ages of 18 and 40 were recruited to participate in the study. This age range was targeted because personality tends to fluctuate and exhibit greater malleability in young and early adulthood than it does in periods like middle adulthood (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts & Nickel, 2021; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018). Normative timings for many life events also fall within this age range (Heckhausen et al., 2010; Wrzus et al., 2013), maximizing the chances that participants would be able to report on these variables.

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling methods, including social media platforms, newspapers, mailing lists, flyers, and websites. For study participation, participants can receive financial compensation of up to CHF 173.50. Exact compensation is contingent upon participants' completion rates for different surveys across the study (Table S3).

The target final sample size of the MPW Study is N = 1,000. As some attrition is expected, we recruited slightly beyond this sample size and aimed to have approximately 1,200 participants complete the T0 survey. At the end of the registration period, 1,674 people provided informed consent and 1,240 participants responded to the T0 survey. Initial descriptive information for these participants is an average age of 26.60 years (SD = 5.66, Min = 18, Max = 40); 67% female; 60% with at least a Bachelor's degree; and 29% from Switzerland, 60% from Germany, and 9% from Austria. With this sample size, a correlation of r = .08 and a within-person mean-level difference of d = 0.08 can be detected with 80% power at a = .05.

We took several steps to counter attrition in the MPW Study. Namely, we sent regular, automatized reminders to participants; brief messages to reinforce participation and updated compensation information after answering surveys; and additional personalized email reminders that were manually sent to participants who had not responded to surveys in m-Path for more than 6 weeks. As of September 2026, approximately 72% of participants have responded to 3 or more bimonthly surveys and approximately 70% of participants have responded to the different surveys in 3 or more weekly packages.

Challenges and Deviations in Data Collection and Study Implementation

Large-scale studies with complex longitudinal designs will likely have some unanticipated implementation challenges. Several occurred for this study, largely stemming from technical issues with the assessment softwares. First, a technical issue with the software used for the T0-T6 surveys caused some invitations to be sent earlier than planned (nmax = 2 per survey). Assessment intervals between some bimonthly surveys were thus more variable than intended. Some variation in these intervals was already expected as participants had between 9 and 14 days to complete the surveys after being invited, but the few early invitations introduced additional heterogeneity.

A second technical issue involved the app used to administer weekly package surveys. The planned multi-week gaps between weekly packages caused the app to become inactive on some smartphones because participants were not engaging with it during these periods. Once inactive, the app could not send notifications. After identifying this issue, we asked participants to interact with the app at least once per week to prevent it from going inactive. Before this solution was implemented, however, some participants received fewer or no notifications for their scheduled surveys. This problem likely persisted for participants who still did not interact with the app on a regular basis. For the weekly packages, initial estimates indicate that approximately 63% of participants received all 540 notifications and 81% received 500 or more notifications.

Other deviations reflected practical challenges in implementing the planned design. Each pseudorandomized schedule for weekly packages had to be created manually across the 12-month period. We prioritized allowing sufficient time to check all schedules' accuracy and thus created 15 protocols rather than our originally planned 20 protocols because this process was more timeintensive than anticipated. We also preregistered that ESM surveys would be sent four times per day at random within prespecified two-hour intervals. In practice, notifications for these surveys were sent at fixed times of day, similar to the notification schedule of daily and weekly surveys.

These deviations illustrate some challenges involved in implementing longitudinal studies with complex temporal designs. These cannot always be anticipated nor avoided and underscore the importance of transparently documenting deviations and considering variation in actual assessment timing and completion when formulating research questions and analytic approaches.

Personality State-Trait Consolidation Processes

Personality is theorized to largely change via bottom-up processes (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017), but top-down pathways have also been outlined (Jackson & Wright, 2024) and identified in empirical research (A. J. Wright et al., 2026b). Personality thus likely changes through a combination of both processes, but the degree to which a single person's personality tends to change via bottom-up versus top-down processes, and whether there exists heterogeneity in this combination of directional processes across people, remain unknown.

Testing person-specific change processes requires studies that can capture a range of person-environment transactions, have data at multiple temporal resolutions across an extended period, and sufficiently large samples--like the MPW Study. To best utilize empirical advantages offered by these data, analytic approaches that can flexibly model within-person dynamics and separate between-person differences in dynamics are needed. For example, (residual) dynamic structural equation models ([R]DSEMs; Asparouhov & Muthén, 2022; Hamaker et al., 2018), mixed-effects location scale models (MLSEMs; Hedeker et al., 2008; A. J. Wright & Jackson, 2025) and group iterative multiple model estimation (GIMME; Gates & Molenaar, 2012; A. G. C. Wright et al., 2019) each offer unique ways of disentangling stable between-person differences, within-person dynamics, and heterogeneity thereof.

Theoretically, findings from such a study can inform to what degree personality change emerges across multiple timescales, direction(s) in which these changes unfold, and extent of heterogeneity in person-specific processes (Baumert et al., 2017; Bleidorn et al., 2026; Hopwood et al., 2022; Olaru et al., 2026; A. J. Wright et al., 2026b). For instance, insight can be gained on whether fluctuations in personality ABCDs unfold across distinct timescales, which contextual factors differentially trigger these fluctuations, whether they accumulate into habitual patterns at different rates (e.g., days, months), how temporal networks shift across successive measurement bursts, and whether timescale-specific dynamics have unique outcome relations. Conversely, it can be gleaned whether higher-level processes help drive or sustain repetitive state-level fluctuations, thereby constituting a top-down change pathway. Such processes may emerge in the form of selfreinforcing feedback loops between states and contexts or broader trait-level changes that reshape people's self-concepts, goals, and expectations (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Jackson & Wright, 2024; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). Overall, gaining theoretical insight on cross-timescale personenvironment dynamics requires a design like a measurement-burst study to comprehensively integrate intensive longitudinal data with data from medium-and longer-term follow-ups.

Life Events & Well-Being

Well-being theories posit that life events like a entering a romantic relationship, the work force, or graduating can elicit lasting changes in people's emotions and evaluations of their life (Headey, 2010; Luhmann & Intelisano, 2018). At the same time, these theories also propose that people adapt to such life changes, whereby effects of life events on well-being are expected to diminish over time (Cummins, 2010; Lyubomirsky, 2011). Although empirical research generally confirms both theoretical predictions (Haehner, Krämer, et al., 2026; Luhmann et al., 2012), important questions about the mechanisms and timescales of well-being changes in response to life events remain unanswered. For example, it is still unclear how long the effects of different life events typically last and why people differ in the shape or speed of their adaptation processes.

Data from the MPW Study can help address these questions and provide novel insight into effects of life events on well-being. By capturing different aspects of well-being (life satisfaction, positive affect, negative affect) across hours, days, weeks, and months, the data can reveal precise well-being change trajectories. Further, by assessing different life experiences, like major life and daily events, the MPW Study can inform which types of experiences predict which kinds of wellbeing change. Finally, by assessing objective and perceived event characteristics, the data can help understand individual differences in the shape and speed of event-related well-being changes. To address such questions, statistical methods able to accommodate data across different timescales and estimate complex nonlinear changes should be used. For example, Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) can be used to depict cross-timescale trajectories of different well-being indicators, whereas continuous-time models can be used to model effects of life events as external shocks to a well-being system (Driver & Voelkle, 2018; Haehner et al., 2025).

Dynamic Regulatory Processes of Solitude

Solitude is a behavioral state defined by the absence of social interaction whose psychological consequences encompass adverse experiences like loneliness or boredom and adaptive experiences like relaxation or reflection (Long & Averill, 2003; Nguyen & Rodriguez, 2024; Weinstein et al., 2023). Although people differ in how much solitude they seek and enjoy (Burger, 1995; Krämer, Sloksnath, et al., 2026; Long et al., 2003), the conditions under which solitude becomes adaptive or adverse for different people remain poorly understood. In particular, relatively stable personal characteristics are rarely integrated with the situational contexts and momentary desires that shape solitude experiences and social behavior in daily life (cf. Ren et al., 2023; Wrzus et al., 2025; Weinstein et al., 2023). Specific timescales of such processes also remain unclear, as studies typically use a single sampling scheme and assessment method (e.g., daily diaries for 2 weeks). Understanding the person-context interplay and dynamic regulation of solitude across timescales may help reveal when solitude is a resource versus a risk for well-being.

Theories state that solitude is adaptive and helps regulate discrepancies between current social demands and individual preferences when desired, but adverse when involuntary or due to avoidance/shyness (Hall & Davis, 2017; Nguyen & Rodriguez, 2024; Weingarten et al., 2026). For example, after prolonged social interaction, solitude may downregulate fatigue or high-arousal negative affect and promote restoration (Krämer et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2025), whereas being alone for longer periods while desiring social contact may increase loneliness and reinforce cycles of withdrawal and negative affect. Such inherently dynamic processes likely unfold over multiple timescales, with repeated momentary experiences accumulating into habitual patterns that shape longer-term loneliness, preference for solitude, and well-being. Thus, a comprehensive account of solitude dynamics requires understanding not only broad individual differences in preference for solitude, but also how momentary desires, affect, and social contexts dynamically regulate when people seek solitude and how these experiences are incorporated into a person's habits and traits.

Testing these processes requires data that capture solitude experiences, social behavior, and contexts in which they occur at multiple temporal resolutions. MPW data are thus well suited to distinguish between-person differences from within-person processes and examine how solitude regulation processes unfold and accumulate over moments, days, weeks, and months. For instance, multilevel models can be used to examine when and for whom solitude is associated with adaptive or adverse outcomes (e.g., affect, loneliness), and how these associations depend on personal or contextual factors. Continuous-time models (Driver & Voelkle, 2018) and DSEMs (Asparouhov et al., 2018; Hamaker et al., 2018) can then examine cross-timescale reciprocal and lagged relations, including whether short-term solitude processes contribute to longer-term changes in preference for solitude, loneliness, or well-being. Combining multi-timescale self-report data with passive mobile-sensing data also provides complementary behavioral and contextual indicators of social behavior and its surrounding contexts (Harari & Gosling, 2023).

Conclusion

As psychological theories become increasingly sophisticated and empirical questions more complex, study designs and methodological tools must evolve accordingly. Addressing important questions about how psychological processes unfold and influence one another across timescales, within-person processes engender between-person differences, and people dynamically interact with their environments requires theoretically informed, temporally rich, and methodologically diverse data. With its multi-timescale and multi-method design, the MPW Study is a unique resource for addressing these questions. By facilitating investigations of cross-timescale dynamics and person-environment transactions, it stands to advance theory and research on personality expression, well-being, social relationships, and adaptation to life events.
