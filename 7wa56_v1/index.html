How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

This module retrieves information from repositories.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Abstract

Providing incentives encoding reliably enhances subsequent memory, yet their effects at retrieval remain unclear. We contrasted prospective Gains, Losses, and Neutral conditions at retrieval to examine their effects, and we additionally manipulated the nature of feedback provided across three groups (N total = 238). Under Score Change feedback, participants received point increases for accurate responses under Gain trials and point decreases for errors under Loss trials; no other outcomes affected totals. Under Score Change plus Accuracy feedback, participants additionally received trial-by-trial recognition accuracy feedback for all responses, regardless of whether they affected point totals. These groups were contrasted with a No Feedback control group. Across groups, prospective Gains reliably increased recognition sensitivity relative to Neutral prospects by selectively increasing hit rates. Loss prospects produced a mild sensitivity improvement, eliciting a lowered false alarm rate relative to Neutral prospects but only for the Score Change feedback group. Drift diffusion modeling revealed that regardless of feedback group, prospective Gains selectively increased drift rates for old items relative to Neutral and Loss prospects, indicating enhanced recovery of mnemonic evidence. In contrast, prospective Gains and Losses increased boundary separation relative to Neutral trials, reflecting greater response caution. Thus, at retrieval, prospective Gains and Losses have dissociable effects on memory, and the former increase sensitivity providing an adaptive means of prioritizing accurate memory when retrieval yields rewards.

Prospective Gains Enhance Recognition Sensitivity

In the movie Slumdog Millionaire, the protagonist Jamal Malik competes on the television game show Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?, where each successive question carries increasingly large monetary rewards. As depicted throughout the movie, Jamal succeeds because each question evokes the recollection of a specific past episode, a childhood encounter, a conversation, or an incidental observation, from which the correct answer can be derived.

Importantly, these experiences were originally encoded as ordinary events, without any expectation that they would later become valuable. Yet within the high-stakes context of the game show, retrieving these memories becomes increasingly consequential as the potential rewards grow larger.

Although fictional, this scenario captures an important feature of memory outside the laboratory: the value of information is often not known at the time it is encoded. Instead, information that was initially acquired incidentally may later become highly consequential when circumstances change. A witness to a crime, for example, may later be asked to recall details that seemed unimportant when they were first experienced. Similarly, a patient may be asked to recall the onset or frequency of symptoms that seemed unremarkable when they first occurred but later become critical for diagnosis and treatment. In these situations, the motivational importance of a memory emerges at retrieval rather than encoding. This distinction is theoretically important because research on motivation and memory has overwhelmingly focused on incentives present during encoding. When individuals know that information will be rewarded later, they selectively allocate more attention and/or elaboration toward that information, resulting in enhanced subsequent memory (Castel et al., 2002; Castel et al., 2012; Ariel & Castel, 2014; Ariel et al., 2015; Cohen et al., 2017; Elliott & Brewer, 2019; Hennessee et al., 2019; Stefanidi et al., 2018; Knowlton & Castel, 2022; Filiz & Dobbins, 2024; Filiz et al., 2025; Murphy et al., 2025; Adcock et al., 2006; Murty & Adcock, 2014). In contrast, much less is known about how incentives affect recognition memory when they are introduced only at retrieval, after the information has already been encoded. Because these incentives cannot influence the initial encoding of the information, an important question is whether they can still improve recognition performance and, if so, whether these improvements reflect changes in the quality of retrieved memory evidence, decision-making processes, or both.

Several studies have reported improved recognition performance when rewards are introduced at retrieval (Shigemune et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2018 Yan et al., , 2023)). For example, in Shigemune et al. (2017) a high versus low reward condition significantly increased the proportion of high confidence hits during verbal recognition. However, it did so for only difficult to recognize probes, and false alarms were assessed collapsed across confidence. Given this, high reward may have shifted the reporting of confidence without necessarily increasing sensitivity.

In Yan et al. (2018) reward effects were also selective with significantly greater sensitivity (hits minus false alarms: Pr) for emotional (positive and negative) pictures under reward versus no reward trials. However, the reward benefit appeared largely confined to reductions in false alarms with little change in hit rates, and there was no reward improvement for neutral pictures. Yan et al. (2023) appears to provide the strongest evidence of reward effects on sensitivity. Here, object picture recognition sensitivity (Pr) was higher for reward versus no reward conditions for object pictures studied under two different encoding tasks. This sensitivity was accompanied by a statistically significant reduction in false alarms under reward versus no reward. Although the authors did not test hit rates in an analogous fashion, the data suggested these increased under reward conditions although the statistical reliability of this increase is unknown. Interestingly, their reward manipulation appears to have entailed only the possibility of gain. That is, participants were monetarily rewarded for correct judgments but not penalized when incorrect.

Nonetheless, of the three reports, only Yan et al. (2023) clearly suggests that rewards increase sensitivity and improve hit rates suggesting facilitated retrieval.

Although Yan et al. (2023) indicates a reward-linked sensitivity increase, other studies have found that retrieval incentives alter recognition behavior, including response speed and confidence, but do not yield corresponding improvements in discrimination (d′) or changes in old/new response bias (Dobbins, 2021; Han et al., 2010). Thus, taken as a whole, prior findings do not strongly support the idea that participants can improve recognition retrieval when incentives are introduced during recognition testing. One potential explanation for these mixed findings is that prior research did not isolate the effects of prospective gains and losses during recognition retrieval, although Yan et al. appear to have used the former. In other words, prior studies typically combined gain and loss possibilities such that during reward conditions participants could receive a reward for a correct response while simultaneously facing a penalty for incorrect responses (e.g., Dobbins, 2021; Han et al., 2010). The present study is the first to separate prospective gains and prospective losses across trials, allowing their potential effects to be examined independently and contrasted with a neutral condition.

Motivation and Readiness to Remember

The possibility that motivational incentives introduced during retrieval influence recognition memory is consistent with the Readiness-to-Remember (R2R) framework (Madore & Wagner, 2022), which proposes that successful remembering depends not only on stored mnemonic representations but also on the preparatory state in which retrieval cues are encountered. Specifically, the framework distinguishes between preparatory attention, encompassing factors such as arousal, vigilance, and motivation, and preparatory goal states, which guide the processing of subsequent retrieval cues and the evaluation of retrieved information. These preparatory processes may influence both the mnemonic evidence elicited by a retrieval cue and how that evidence is used to guide a response. This framework is particularly relevant to the present paradigm, in which gain, loss, or neutral prospects are established before each recognition decision and could therefore influence the cognitive state in which retrieval occurs.

Importantly, much of the evidence supporting the attentional component of R2R comes from studies examining physiological activity, such as posterior alpha power and pupil diameter, preceding successful versus unsuccessful retrieval attempts. Thus, readiness has largely been investigated through naturally occurring fluctuations in preparatory states rather than through their direct experimental manipulation. Madore and Wagner (2022) identified the development of interventions capable of altering these states as an important direction for future research (see also Schwartz et al., 2025). Presenting motivational incentives before retrieval offers one potential means of influencing such preparatory states and examining their consequences for recognition performance.

Although both gain and loss prospects might be expected to motivate individuals to improve their recognition accuracy, they may not produce equivalent preparatory states or decision strategies. Prospect Theory proposes that individuals evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point and typically weight losses more heavily than objectively equivalent gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Consistent with this asymmetry, loss contexts have been associated with heightened vigilance, greater attention to potential errors, and more cautious responding, whereas gain contexts have been associated with approach-oriented behavior and goal pursuit (Hochman & Yechiam, 2011; Yechiam & Hochman, 2013). Neuroimaging research further suggests that gains and losses engage partially dissociable neural systems (Palminteri & Pessiglione, 2017; Seymour et al., 2007). Moreover, research on arousal and neuromodulation indicates that motivational significance does not necessarily enhance processing uniformly.

Instead, arousal can amplify the processing of representations that are already prioritized, with these priorities shaped partly by current goals and motivational relevance (Mather et al., 2016).

Together, these findings suggest that gain and loss prospects may establish different motivational priorities before or during retrieval, potentially influencing how individuals process mnemonic evidence and regulate their recognition decisions.

Within this framework, gain prospects, which emphasize obtaining a desirable outcome, may encourage greater engagement with or utilization of mnemonic evidence, potentially improving the ability to distinguish previously studied items from new items. In contrast, loss prospects, which emphasize avoiding an undesirable outcome, may encourage more cautious evaluation of mnemonic evidence, potentially reducing incorrect recognition responses. Thus, even if both incentives improve recognition performance, their effects may arise through different mechanisms. Distinguishing these possibilities requires an approach capable of separating changes in mnemonic evidence from changes in response caution.

Sequential Sampling Models and the Drift Diffusion Model

Sequential sampling models offer a way to distinguish these potential mechanisms by considering not only the accuracy of recognition decisions but also the time required to make them. These models were originally developed to account for the speed-accuracy tradeoff, the observation that faster decisions tend to be less accurate, whereas more accurate decisions often require additional time (Ratcliff & Rouder, 1998; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008). Rather than treating accuracy and response time as separate outcomes, sequential sampling models explain both as consequences of a common decision process in which noisy evidence accumulates over time until a decision boundary is reached.

A central insight of these models is that improved accuracy does not necessarily imply stronger mnemonic evidence. Within this framework, observers can improve performance by requiring more evidence before committing to a response, thereby increasing decision caution at the cost of slower responding. Conversely, observers can prioritize speed by lowering their decision threshold, producing faster but less accurate responses. Thus, changes in measured accuracy need not reflect changes in underlying memory strength; they may instead arise from strategic adjustments in decision strategy.

The most widely used sequential sampling model in recognition memory is the Drift Diffusion Model (DDM) which has been successfully applied to a wide range of recognition memory phenomena, including strength-based recognition effects, response bias manipulations, aging, confidence judgments, and individual differences in memory performance (e.g., Ratcliff et al., 2004; Ratcliff & Starns, 2009 , 2013; Starns et al., 2012; Osth et al., 2018). Within recognition paradigms, the DDM provides a formal framework for decomposing performance into separable mnemonic and decision-related components. Under the model, evidence accumulates towards a decision boundary yielding either an "old" or "new" conclusion, and the timing and accuracy of responding are governed several parameters. Drift rate (v) indexes the average rate of evidence accumulation toward the boundaries and is commonly interpreted as reflecting the quality, strength, or diagnosticity of mnemonic evidence. Boundary separation (a) indexes the distance between the decision boundaries and therefore the amount of evidence required before committing to a response and hence captures the observers degree of caution.

Finally, starting point (z) specifies where the accumulation process begins relative to the two boundaries and is interpreted as reflecting a priori response tendencies or decision bias before evidence accumulation begins (Ratcliff & Rouder, 1998; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Ratcliff & Starns, 2009). By distinguishing these components, the DDM provides a means of examining whether prospective gains and losses influence recognition through changes in mnemonic evidence, decision caution, or response bias. This distinction is particularly important because similar improvements in recognition sensitivity may arise from different patterns of responding.

Indeed, preliminary evidence from our pilot study suggested that gain and loss prospects might improve recognition performance in different ways.

Pilot Findings

In an initial online pilot study, we examined the effects of Gain, Loss, and Neutral prospects introduced during recognition using a repeated-measures design (see Supplementary Materials). Relative to Neutral prospects, Gain prospects increased recognition sensitivity, primarily through an increase in hit rates without a corresponding increase in false alarms. Loss prospects also showed a tendency to improve sensitivity, although this effect was less robust and appeared to arise from a different pattern of responding. Specifically, Loss prospects were associated with a modest reduction in false alarms rather than an increase in hits. Thus, although both types of incentives showed the potential to improve recognition performance, the different patterns of hits and false alarms raised the possibility that gains and losses influence distinct components of the recognition decision process.

Response time patterns further suggested that the two incentive conditions may have operated through different mechanisms. Relative to Neutral trials, Loss prospects were associated with slower responding, particularly for responses involving recognition errors, consistent with the possibility that losses encouraged greater decision caution. In contrast, Gain prospects produced sensitivity improvements without a corresponding slowing of responses.

Thus, although both Gain and Loss prospects appeared to improve recognition performance, they did so under different response-time profiles. Together, these findings raised the possibility that prospective Gains and Losses influence distinct components of the recognition decision process.

Present Study

Although the pilot findings suggested that prospective gains and losses may influence recognition performance in different ways, several questions remained unresolved. First, to our knowledge, the pilot was the first investigation to examine the effects of prospective Gain and Loss incentives introduced specifically at recognition retrieval. The observed dissociation between hit rates and false alarm rates therefore required replication. Moreover, because the pilot was conducted online, it was important to establish whether these effects would generalize to a controlled laboratory setting, particularly given the importance of response times for distinguishing mnemonic and decision-related mechanisms (Van der Stigchel et al., 2026).

An additional concern involved the feedback structure used in the pilot study. The present research was designed to address these issues. Across three experimental groups, we examined whether the asymmetric pattern observed in the pilot would replicate under controlled laboratory conditions and whether it depends on the availability and form of feedback.

Based on the pilot findings, we predicted that Gain prospects would selectively increase hit rates, whereas Loss prospects would selectively reduce false alarm rates, producing dissociable changes in recognition sensitivity not explainable by a simple shift in old/new response bias. If the observed effects primarily reflect feedback-induced learning processes, they should be present only when feedback is available. In contrast, if they more directly stem from motivational reactions to the prospects, then facilitation should emerge even in the absence of feedback.

Finally, performance was analyzed using both signal-detection measures and drift diffusion modeling. Guided by the pilot findings, we predicted that Gain prospects would primarily influence evidence accumulation, reflected in increased drift rates for studied items. This was anticipated by the fact that prospective gains elevated the hit rate (relative to the Neutral prospect) without slowing responding. In contrast, we suspected that Loss prospects would primarily influence decision caution, reflected in changes to boundary separation. This was anticipated by the fact that relative to the Neutral prospect, Loss prospects were associated with slower responding (see Supplementary Materials).

Method Participants

Sample size was determined via power analysis (G*Power 3.1; Faul et al., 2007) using the effect size of the Gain versus Neutral repeated-measures contrast of hit rates from the pilot study (Cohen's d = .44). This yields a power of greater than 96% with 75 participants, and this was our target per group sample size in the three groups. This sample size also yields an acceptable power of 86% to detect between-groups effects at a conventionally medium effect size of .5. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Materials

The experiment was programmed using jsPsych (de Leeuw, Gilbert, & Luchterhandt, 2023) and hosted on Cognition.run although the participants completed the study in person on laboratory computers. Words were selected from the English Lexicon Project database (Balota et al., 2007). The initial pool contained 1, 214 words. From this set, 480 words were randomly sampled without replacement for use in the experiment. In the final selected set, mean word length was 7.03 letters (SD = 1.30), mean log Hyperspace Analogue to Language (HAL) frequency was 7.75 (SD = 1.05), and mean concreteness was 3.90 (SD = 0.88).

Design and Procedure

For each participant, the same pool of 480 words was randomly shuffled and assigned to the experimental lists. Participants completed two study-test cycles, each consisting of a study phase followed by a recognition test. During each study phase, 108 words were presented individually for 3 s each. Participants were informed that their memory for these words would be tested later, without specifying the nature of the upcoming memory test, and were instructed to pay attention to each word. No responses were required during study.

Before each recognition test, participants were informed that previously studied and new words would be intermixed and instructed to indicate whether each word had appeared in the immediately preceding study phase. They were also informed about the incentive contingencies, which were identical to those used in the pilot study. On Gain trials (+100 points), correct recognition decisions earned 100 points, whereas incorrect decisions produced no score change.

On Loss trials (-100 points), incorrect decisions resulted in a deduction of 100 points, whereas correct decisions produced no score change. Neutral trials (0 points) did not affect the score regardless of response accuracy. Participants were instructed to maximize their cumulative score.

Each recognition test consisted of 216 trials, including 108 studied words and 108 new lures. Incentive conditions were equally distributed across studied and new items, yielding 36 Gain, 36 Loss, and 36 Neutral trials within each item type. As illustrated in Figure 1A, each trial began with a centrally presented test word and its corresponding incentive cue (+100, -100, or 0 points) displayed above it. Participants indicated whether the word was OLD by pressing the A key or NEW by pressing the L key. Following the recognition response, the incentive cue disappeared while the test word remained on the screen, and participants rated their confidence using the number keys (1 = low,2 = medium,3 = high). Recognition and confidence responses were self-paced. Feedback was then presented according to the participant's assigned experimental group. Participants were also shown their accumulated points at the end of each study-test cycle.

Feedback Manipulation

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three feedback groups: Score Change (SC), No Feedback (NFB), or Score Change + Accuracy (SC+ACC). The groups differed only in the performance feedback provided during recognition testing; study materials, incentive contingencies, point cues, and trial structure were otherwise identical.

Score Change (SC).

Participants received trial-by-trial feedback through a dynamically updated score bar displayed at the top of the screen, as in the pilot study (Figure 1). A static gray bar remained at the top of the screen without changing position or color, regardless of response accuracy or incentive condition. Thus, participants were informed about the incentive contingencies but received no external information about their performance during individual trials.

Score Change + Accuracy (SC+ACC).

Participants received the same dynamic score feedback as the SC group, supplemented by explicit accuracy feedback on every trial.

Following the recognition and confidence responses, the test word turned green for a correct response or red for an incorrect response, regardless of incentive condition. After 1 s, the cumulative score bar was updated according to the same contingencies used in the SC group.

Surprise Final Recognition Test

Following the two study-test cycles, participants completed an unexpected final recognition test. This test included all 216 words presented during the Cycle 1 recognition test, along with 48 additional new words that had not appeared previously, yielding 264 trials.

Participants were instructed to identify any word they recognized from the earlier portion of the experiment, regardless of whether it had appeared during study or testing.

Unlike the initial recognition tests, the final test included no incentive cues, point changes, or performance feedback. Each trial presented a centrally displayed word, and participants indicated OLD or NEW using the same response keys as before. Following each recognition judgment, they rated their confidence on the same three-point scale.

Individual Differences Questionnaire

After completing the final recognition test, participants completed the Behavioral Inhibition System/Behavioral Activation System (BIS/BAS) Scales (Carver & White, 1994).

This questionnaire assesses individual differences in sensitivity to aversive (BIS) and appetitive (BAS) motivational cues, allowing us to examine whether these traits were associated with responses to Gain and Loss prospects during recognition. Participants were then debriefed and thanked. Illustration of the experimental trial sequence and cumulative score changes.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed in R using the tidyverse (Wickham et al., 2019), rstatix (Kassambara, 2023), lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017), and fddm (Foster et al., 2024) packages.

Recognition sensitivity (d′) was calculated as the difference between the z-transformed hit and false-alarm rates. To accommodate cells with extreme proportions (hit or false-alarm rates of 0 or 1), a log-linear correction was applied (Hautus, 1995).

Data Screening

Reaction time (RT) data were screened prior to analysis because diffusion models are sensitivity to outliers (Ratcliff & Rouder, 1998; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008). Accordingly, trials with RTs shorter than 200 ms or longer than 10,000 ms were removed as absolute outliers, and additional trial-level outliers were identified within each participant using a ±3 SD trimming procedure (see Supplementary Materials).

Drift Diffusion Modelling

The pattern of reaction time and accuracy as a function of feedback groups and value prospects was complex. In the case of accuracy, the initial analysis clearly shows that subjects selectively increase their hit rates under Gain versus Loss and Neutral prospects, and that they do so regardless of the feedback manipulation in place (Figure 2). Hence, recognition sensitivity increases under Gain versus Loss and Neutral prospects in all three groups (Figure 3). This pattern did not result from a more liberal decision bias because false alarm rates did not likewise notably increase under Gain versus other prospects. Aside from a decision bias, one alternative possibility could have been that the benefit of Gain prospects resulted from slower responding (viz. a speed/accuracy tradeoff). The reaction time analysis rules this out, however, because subjects did not demonstrate slower reaction times under Gain versus Neutral prospects during hits (Figure 4, Top Left Panel). Thus, the increased hit rate under Gain relative to Neutral prospects is not the result of a liberal bias or speed/accuracy tradeoff.

To provide a convergent test of the idea that Gain prospects result in the recovery of more, or higher quality, recognition evidence than Loss and Neutral prospects, we next applied the Drift Diffusion Model, which was independently fit via maximum likelihood estimation to each subject in each of the Feedback Groups. For each subject, start point (z) and response caution (a) parameters were free to vary across Value. Evidence accumulation toward the upper boundary produced an 'old' response, whereas accumulation toward the lower boundary produced a 'new' response.

Additionally, we allowed across-trial drift-rate variability to differ for old versus new probes, consistent with behavioral evidence that mnemonic evidence is more variable for studied than novel items (Ratcliff, Sheu, & Gronlund, 1992). Diffusion-modeling studies further indicate that this variance asymmetry is not optional: allowing sv to differ for targets and lures is necessary to jointly account for ROC shape and full RT distributions, whereas constraining evidence variability to be equal across old and new items produces systematic misfit (Starns, Ratcliff, & McKoon, 2012; Starns & Ratcliff, 2014). Recent reviews likewise emphasize that unequal evidence variability between targets and lures is naturally accommodated in diffusion models via the differential drift variability parameter (Ratcliff, 2022). Accordingly, allowing sv to vary by item status was a theoretically and empirically motivated implementation rather than an exploratory modeling choice.

Finally, there were two different variants of this basic model initially considered. In one, the non-decision time (ndt) parameter was a fixed value estimated for each subject. This parameter captures probe decoding and response execution factors unrelated to the decision process. In a second model, we allowed ndt to vary across old and new probes, motivated by the possibility that old items could elicit additional fluency/priming-related processing not intrinsic to explicit recognition. However, this additional flexibility was questionable in this context, because it raised the potential for ndt and sv parameters to compete and is based on the questionable assumption that fluency effects are not part of the evidence used for recognition judgment (Starns, 2014). Additionally, since stimulus types are randomly intermixed, it can be argued that they are fully matched in terms of stimulus-processing and response-output requirements (Starns, 2014). Consequently, any systematic differences observed across item types may be more appropriately attributed to evidence-related parameters (e.g., drift and its variability) rather than to Ter/ndt. Given this, below we focus on the model with ndt fixed across old and new items, although the key patterns remain evident when this was relaxed.

Critically, the goal of the DDM model is to test the main conclusion that Gain prospects increase sensitivity by elevating old item recognition, in a framework that fully accounts for potential speed/accuracy tradeoffs.

For each recovered DDM parameter, standard ANOVAs or t-tests examined whether it was influenced by Value, Feedback Group, or Item Status (old vs. new) where appropriate.

General Discussion

The present study examined whether incentives introduced only at retrieval can alter recognition performance and, critically, whether prospective Gains and Losses might influence retrieval through the different mechanisms. Across three feedback groups, only Gain prospects reliably increased recognition sensitivity by selectively increasing hit rates. This effect was observed not only when participants received trial-by-trial score (SC group), or score plus accuracy feedback (SC+ACC group), but was observed even in the complete absence of feedback (NFB Group). This indicates that the Gain advantage on memory retrieval did not depend on feedback-based learning. In contrast, the Loss prospect did not reliably increase sensitivity, although in the SC feedback group there was modest evidence, consistent with the pilot study, that it mildly reduced false alarm rates.

Together, these findings demonstrate that prospective Gains and Losses are not interchangeable forms of retrieval motivation, a distinction with important theoretical and methodological implications given that previous investigations of retrieval motivation have typically combined the two. Relative to Neutral prospects, Gains consistently facilitated recognition of studied information, selectively increasing hit rates (Figure 2), improving recognition sensitivity (d′; Figure 3), and increasing drift rates for old items in the DDM (Figure 8, left panel). Crucially, these improvements occurred without a corresponding increase in response time (Figure 4), with participants recognizing studied items just as quickly under Gain as under Neutral prospects. Thus, the Gain advantage cannot be adequately explained by a simple liberal shift in the old/new decision criterion or by a speed-accuracy trade-off. In contrast, relative to Neutral prospects, Loss prospects mildly benefitted only the SC group, were reflected in a small decline in false alarm rates for this group (Figure 2) but were also accompanied by the slowest responding under feedback conditions (Figure 3). Hence, to the extent there is any benefit of Loss prospects it appears to be in the slowing of responding and the avoidance of rapidly induced false alarms under the SC feedback regimen.

Gain Selectively Increased Recognition Sensitivity Across Feedback Contexts

Before considering potential mechanisms, it is important to establish that the Gain advantage cannot be explained simply by a greater willingness to endorse items as previously studied. Under a basic detection account, a more liberal response criterion should increase both hit and false alarm rates (Macmillan & Creelman, 2005). Instead, Gain prospects increased hit rates without a corresponding overall increase in false alarms, and drift diffusion modeling provided converging evidence that Gain selectively increased the rate of evidence accumulation for studied items, with no corresponding increase for new items. Importantly, this improvement in recognition sensitivity was observed across all three feedback groups, including participants who received no performance feedback. Thus, the opportunity to earn a reward improved recognition sensitivity even though the incentive was introduced only at retrieval, after the original encoding episode had already occurred. This finding moves beyond the well-established observation that incentives can alter response tendencies: Prospective Gain can improve the accumulation of mnemonic evidence supporting recognition. The critical theoretical question, therefore, is how the prospect of reward enables previously encoded information to contribute more effectively to the recognition decision.

The comparison with Loss provides an important constraint on potential explanations.

One possibility is that Gain simply encouraged participants to devote more attention or time to the recognition decision. However, Loss prospects produced substantially slower hit responses and, particularly in the feedback groups, greater boundary separation than Gain prospects. Despite these increases in decision caution, Loss prospects did not improve hit rates or old-item drift relative to Neutral prospects. Conversely, Gain prospects improved recognition sensitivity without a corresponding slowing of hit responses. Thus, the incentive condition associated with the greatest decision caution was not the condition associated with the greatest improvement in recognition sensitivity. This dissociation is difficult to reconcile with a unitary account in which motivational incentives improve recognition simply by increasing the amount or duration of processing devoted to a decision. Instead, the findings suggest that prospective Gain and Loss influence distinguishable components of the recognition process: Loss primarily increased the amount of evidence required before committing to a response, whereas Gain additionally increased the rate at which diagnostic evidence accumulated for studied items. Critically, increasing the threshold for a recognition decision is not equivalent to improving the evidence on which that decision is based.

One possibility, therefore, is that prospective Gain influences how retrieval processes are configured and directed toward the recovery of diagnostic episodic information. Theories of motivated cognitive control propose that incentives can influence not only the degree of cognitive engagement but also how control is organized and deployed in anticipation of upcoming task demands (Botvinick & Braver, 2015; Braver, 2012; Chiew & Braver, 2014). In particular, reward motivation has been associated with a greater reliance on proactive control, characterized by the anticipatory maintenance of task goals that guide subsequent information processing. Applied to episodic recognition, such motivational influences may facilitate the strategic access to goal-relevant mnemonic information, consistent with accounts emphasizing the role of cognitive control in memory retrieval (Badre & Wagner, 2007). From this perspective, the Gain advantage may reflect a change in how retrieval goals guide mnemonic processing, rather than a nonspecific increase in engagement with the recognition task.

The Readiness-to-Remember (R2R) framework (Madore & Wagner, 2022), introduced earlier, provides a more specific account of how such preparatory control might influence episodic retrieval. According to this framework, fluctuations in preparatory attention influence how effectively retrieval goals are instantiated and maintained within frontoparietal control systems implicated in episodic remembering (Dobbins et al., 2002 (Dobbins et al., , 2003)). These preparatory goal representations may, in turn, influence how effectively subsequent retrieval cues engage mnemonic processes, including hippocampal pattern completion, and how the resulting information is integrated into the recognition decision. Importantly, this framework suggests that retrieval success depends not only on the strength of stored memory representations but also on the cognitive state in which those representations are accessed. Consequently, retrieval failures may arise even when relevant mnemonic information remains available in memory, if the preparatory state is insufficiently configured to support its recovery.

Converging evidence supports the importance of preparatory cognitive states for subsequent remembering. Neural activity preceding retrieval cues has been shown to predict subsequent retrieval success (Otten et al., 2006; Addante et al., 2015). More broadly, evidence reviewed within the R2R framework indicates that fluctuations in preparatory attention, indexed by measures including posterior alpha power and pupil diameter, are associated with subsequent retrieval performance and the fidelity of preparatory goal-state representations (Madore & Wagner, 2022). These findings provide a potential framework for understanding the selective Gain advantage observed in the present study. Because incentive prospects preceded individual recognition decisions, Gain may have promoted a preparatory state that supported more effective instantiation or maintenance of retrieval goals, thereby facilitating the processing of mnemonic information elicited by studied probes. Such an account could explain why Gain selectively increased old-item evidence accumulation without requiring longer decision times or performance feedback. Final Surprise Test: Long-Term Effects of Value at Retrieval

The surprise final test extends the immediate recognition findings by showing that the motivational context associated with an earlier retrieval attempt was also related to subsequent recognition. For items originally presented during the study phase, both Gain and Loss prospects were associated with higher final recognition than Neutral prospects. In contrast, neither incentive significantly improved subsequent recognition of items initially presented as lures.

Although these effects were relatively small, they suggest that the relationship between retrieval incentives and later memory may be more evident for previously studied information.

The Gain advantage on the final test is consistent with its effects during initial recognition, where Gain increased both hit rates and old-item drift. The Loss advantage, however, is more surprising. Despite producing no reliable improvement in initial recognition sensitivity or old-item drift, Loss prospects were associated with better subsequent recognition of previously studied items. One possibility is that the greater decision caution and longer response times associated with Loss provided additional opportunities for processing information during retrieval, potentially benefiting later memory. However, the present findings cannot determine whether this advantage reflects additional processing, re-encoding, or other aspects of the retrieval episode.

Together, these findings suggest that the immediate and subsequent consequences of retrieval incentives may be partly dissociable. Whereas Gain improved the accumulation of mnemonic evidence during initial recognition and Loss primarily increased decision caution, both were associated with modest advantages in subsequent recognition of studied items.

Although these results do not establish that incentives strengthened the underlying memory representations, they suggest that the motivational context surrounding retrieval may have consequences extending beyond the immediate recognition decision.

Mixed Gambles and Prior Weak Support for Incentive Effects

Returning to the literature reviewed in the Introduction, previous investigations have provided limited and inconsistent evidence that incentives introduced at retrieval improve recognition sensitivity. The present findings suggest one possible explanation for this inconsistency: Much of the previous literature has employed mixed gambles, in which correct responses earn rewards while incorrect responses incur losses. However, our findings indicate that these two motivational prospects have different consequences for recognition, with Gain improving sensitivity and Loss primarily increasing decision caution. Interestingly, Yan et al. (2023), which provided the clearest evidence for improved recognition sensitivity among the studies reviewed, also differed from most previous studies in its incentive structure. Specifically, correct responses earned rewards, whereas incorrect responses incurred no penalty, leaving participants' accumulated earnings unchanged. This arrangement corresponds to what we have termed a Gain prospect, consistent with the distinction between gains and losses relative to a reference point emphasized in Prospect Theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Because both hits and correct rejections were rewarded, the gain prospect does not preferentially favor OLD over NEW responses. Thus, the study providing the strongest previous evidence for improved sensitivity employed an incentive structure resembling the condition that selectively enhanced sensitivity in the present experiment. Together, these findings raise the possibility that combining gains and losses within a single incentive condition may obscure sensitivity benefits that emerge when prospective gains are examined separately. Although the present study did not directly test mixed gambles, it demonstrates the importance of distinguishing between the prospect of earning a reward and that of avoiding a loss when investigating motivational influences on memory retrieval.

This distinction may also be relevant to consequential recognition decisions outside the laboratory. For example, eyewitness identification may involve competing motivations to correctly identify a perpetrator and avoid mistakenly identifying an innocent person. The relative importance of these consequences may vary across individuals and situations, potentially influencing both the mnemonic evidence supporting recognition and the caution exercised when making a decision. More broadly, the present findings suggest that understanding how motivation influences remembering requires considering not simply whether incentives are present, but whether they emphasize obtaining gains or avoiding losses.

Limitations and Future Directions

Given the novelty of the present findings, several limitations warrant consideration and suggest directions for future research. The present work focused exclusively on verbal item recognition, a task supported by both familiarity and recollection processes (Yonelinas, 2002; Wixted, 2007). Although diffusion modeling allows decomposition of evidence accumulation and decision processes, standard old/new recognition tasks do not allow a clean separation of recollection-based and familiarity-based influences. Therefore, future work may benefit from manipulating prospects in paradigms that rely more heavily on recollection, such as cued recall and source memory tasks. If Gain prospects enhance reinstatement or retrieval readiness, their benefits may be amplified in recollection-dependent tasks. In contrast, if Loss prospects primarily increase decision caution and reduce familiarity-based errors, their effects should diminish in paradigms where familiarity utility is minimized, such as associative recognition or paired-associate recall.

An important direction for future research is to determine whether the Gain-related improvement in recognition sensitivity originates in preparatory processes preceding the retrieval cue or in mnemonic processing following its presentation. Much of the evidence motivating the R2R framework comes from naturally occurring fluctuations in preparatory neural and attentional states (Madore & Wagner, 2022). The present retrieval-prospect manipulation offers a promising way to extend this work by experimentally varying motivational context on individual retrieval trials. Combined with neural measures, this approach could help determine whether prospective Gain alters the preparatory representations thought to support successful episodic retrieval.

Conclusion

The present findings suggest that understanding how motivation influences remembering requires moving beyond the assumption that incentives simply increase engagement or alter response tendencies. Instead, different motivational prospects may shape how cognitive control is organized during retrieval, potentially influencing both the accessibility of mnemonic information and how cautiously that information is evaluated. Although the proposed distinction between preparatory and post-retrieval control remains to be tested directly, it offers a promising framework for understanding why motivational incentives do not uniformly improve recognition sensitivity. More broadly, our findings highlight that the consequences anticipated during remembering matter: Motivation does not merely influence whether we report a memory, but can also shape how effectively we remember.
