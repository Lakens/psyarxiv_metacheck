Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Generative artificial intelligence (genAI) is increasingly used to support learning and problem solving, but it remains unclear how relying on such support affects people's ability to reason independently afterwards. We investigated whether using genAI to solve a complex reasoning task influences subsequent reasoning, memory, and effort when the technology is no longer available. In a preregistered laboratory experiment with a final sample of 193 participants, we used a three-stage relational reasoning task developed for the study, in which participants learned and reasoned about a network of fictional entities and their relationships. During the first stage, participants completed the task either with or without access to ChatGPT, while performance incentives were independently manipulated. All participants then completed a more complex reasoning task based on the same relational network without genAI support, followed by a memory test. GenAI support improved accuracy during the initial task and reduced perceived difficulty. In the subsequent unaided reasoning task, prior genAI use was associated with some evidence of poorer independent reasoning. GenAI use was also associated with weaker adjustment of effort to incentives and task difficulty once the tool was removed. No clear effects were observed on later memory. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of distinguishing successful AI-assisted task completion from the knowledge construction and effort regulation needed to apply the same information independently.

1. Introduction

Digital technologies increasingly shape how people learn, acquire knowledge, and solve problems. When engaging in these activities, humans frequently reduce cognitive demands by offloading cognitive processes to the environment (Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Traditionally, external tools such as written notes, calculators, and digital devices have supported relatively simple cognitive operations. Recent advances in generative Artificial Intelligence (genAI) offer unprecedented potential to delegate higher-level cognitive functions traditionally associated with humans, such as decision-making, creativity, and reasoning (Chlebowski, Ratajczyk, & Wyrwa, 2026; Makridakis, 2017; Noy & Zhang, 2023). Although cognitive offloading can improve performance by reducing cognitive demands, it may also reduce the internal processing required for learning and knowledge construction (Skulmowski, 2023). Offloading higher-order processes to genAI may therefore have consequences for knowledge acquisition subsequent independent reasoning and the regulation of effort on related tasks.

Cognitive load theory (CLT) describes how the information processing load induced by learning tasks affects the ability to process new information and construct knowledge in long-term memory (Sweller, 2011). A central concept in CLT is element interactivity, referring to the number of interacting elements that must be processed simultaneously in working memory. Organising information into schemas in long-term memory reduces effective element interactivity and the demands placed on working memory. For example, when faced with high element interactivity tasks, learners may resort to rote learning, thereby avoiding the additional information processing but failing to create meaningful understanding, i.e., failing to create organised information schemas in long-term memory.

Cognitive offloading provides another way of reducing processing demands by transferring some cognitive operations to external resources (see Pea, 1985). While this can free internal resources and facilitate immediate performance, offloading operations involved in organising and integrating information may also reduce the processing through which knowledge is constructed.

Consistent with this possibility, cognitive offloading has been associated with memory decay (Kelly & Risko, 2019), reduced effort (Risko, Huh, McLean, & Ferguson, 2017), altered memory strategies (Scarampi & Gilbert, 2020), and decreased acquisition of procedural knowledge (Moritz, Meyerhoff, & Schwan, 2020). Technologies like GPS, for instance, have been shown to impair spatial memory in both short and long terms (Dahmani & Bohbot, 2020; Fenech, Drews, & Bakdash, 2010), which may stem from the reduced engagement with the problem's deeper structures (O'Hara & Payne, 1998) and the divided attention during use (Gardony, Brunyé, & Taylor, 2015).

Decisions about whether and how much to offload depend on metacognitive evaluations of task demands and available cognitive resources. Skulmowski (2023) proposed a cognitive model of digital externalisation based on CLT, according to which lower working-memory capacity or perceived cognitive capacity and higher cognitive load increase offloading, whereas higher perceived value of information decreases it. Thus, reliance on external resources depends not only on task demands but also on individuals' assessment of their own cognitive resources.

1.1. Generative AI and relational reasoning

GenAI systems can generate and transform text, images, code, and other content. Many text-based applications rely on large language models trained to predict linguistic sequences and subsequently fine-tuned using human preference data (Cao et al., 2023; Demszky et al., 2023). Their ability to summarise, compare, transform, and integrate information allows users to offload not only routine operations but also higher-order processes central to reasoning (Baidoo-Anu & Ansah, 2023; Orrù, Piarulli, Conversano, & Gemignani, 2023) This ability makes them a popular tool among learners, who may offload learning-related tasks regardless of the consequences for the learning process. Although offloading may free up cognitive resources, it may also replace processing that is necessary for learning, metacognitive evaluation, and subsequent independent reasoning.

Evidence from empirical studies presents a similarly complex picture. Several findings document clear benefits. AI-based writing tools have been shown to improve output quality for students learning English as a foreign language (Marzuki, Widiati, Rusdin, Darwin, & Indrawati, 2023), while structured uses of genAI, including language-learning chatbots, automated feedback, and personalised recommendations, have been associated with gains in learning performance and higher-order thinking skills (Wang, Cheung, Neitzel, & Chai, 2025). Related studies suggest that active engagement with genAI, particularly when students use it to explore ideas rather than outsource the task, can support knowledge acquisition and application (Jo, 2024), as well as creative, critical, and reflective thinking (Essel, Vlachopoulos, Essuman, & Amankwa, 2024). Benefits have also been demonstrated outside education. For example, Noy and Zhang (2023) found that professionals completing writing tasks with ChatGPT produced higher quality outputs and completed their tasks more quickly.

There are also documented risks. Excessive reliance on genAI has been linked to poorer learning outcomes, memory difficulties, and declines in creativity and problem-solving performance (Abbas, Jam, & Khan, 2024). Fulbright and Morrison (2024), for example, found that although average performance was comparable between participants using ChatGPT and a control group, a subset of ChatGPT users showed poorer problem-solving performance, potentially because the tool directed attention towards irrelevant information. The use of genAI may also affect the accuracy of metacognitive judgments about one's own performance and capabilities (Urban et al., 2024). A closely related concept is metacognitive laziness, introduced by Fan et al. (2025) to describe a pattern in which learners become overly reliant on genAI, shifting both task execution and aspects of cognitive and metacognitive regulation to the tool. In their study, Chat-GPT support improved short-term essay performance, but no significant gains were found for knowledge acquisition or transfer. The authors suggest that this pattern may reflect reduced involvement in self-regulatory processes such as monitoring, evaluation, and deeper reflection.

A similar pattern emerges from neuroimaging research. Kosmyna et al. (2025) reported weaker neural engagement and poorer recall in participants who wrote essays with genAI support compared with those who used search engines or worked without external assistance. Participants in the genAI-assisted condition also showed lower cognitive engagement, frequently relying on copy-and-paste behaviour when incorporating AI-generated content. Reduced neural engagement persisted when participants who had previously used genAI later completed the task independently. Another concern is the potential homogenising effect of LLMs on expression and thought. Sourati, Ziabari, and Dehghani (2026) argue that LLMs may standardise not only language but also perspectives and reasoning strategies because their outputs tend to privilege dominant, frequent, and easily generalisable patterns. Thus, habitual reliance on genAI may reduce engagement in one's own reasoning and narrow the range of linguistic, perspectival, and inferential possibilities that users consider.

The effects of genAI appear to depend on the context and manner of its use. Evidence suggests that benefits tend to emerge when genAI is used for feedback, conceptual scaffolding, guiding reflection, and individualised support, whereas harms are more likely when genAI begins to replace cognitive work that is central to a given task, such as writing, reasoning, or problem solving (Cabitza & Vicente, 2026; Lo, Hew, & Jong, 2024).

Whether genAI supports or replaces task-relevant cognitive processing may be particularly important for relational reasoning, which is central to human learning Dumas, Alexander, and Grossnickle (2013). Reasoning involves integrating internally stored knowledge with newly available information to construct a coherent representation of the problem. Acquiring the representation allows its use to make new inferences in the future (Stenning & Van Lambalgen, 2012). Here, offloading to genAI poses the greatest risk if it disrupts the formation of the representation. For example, learning that a given organism has a particular characteristic (e.g., that amphibians breathe partly through their skin) requires relating that fact to habitat, physiology, and comparison with other classes of animals. Having genAI generate these links can bypass the very processing through which the knowledge is encoded.

Relational knowledge supports reasoning by preserving the structural organisation of information and allowing relations to be applied in novel situations (Halford, Wilson, & Phillips, 2010). According to CLT, constructing such representations becomes difficult when the number of interacting elements exceeds available working-memory resources; organising these elements into schemas in long-term memory reduces their effective complexity and supports subsequent processing (Sweller, 2011). GenAI can reduce these demands by organising and integrating information, but may also allow users to obtain correct answers without constructing the underlying schemas themselves. Consequently, immediate performance may improve at the cost of the relational knowledge required for later independent reasoning.

External access to information may not fully substitute for internally represented relational knowledge. Moreover, when people do not expect information to be useful later, they may be more willing to offload it, potentially impairing memory and increasing future reliance on external sources (Storm, Stone, & Benjamin, 2017). This issue is particularly relevant to genAI and its place in education and the learning process, because it can externalise not only access to individual pieces of information, but also the integration and transformation of those pieces into a coherent relational representation.

1.2. Generative AI, motivation, and effort regulation

Successful learning outcomes are influenced by motivation, which is linked to task engagement and persistence (Howard, Bureau, Guay, Chong, & Ryan, 2021; Reeve & Lee, 2014). Research applying cognitive load theory to task engagement suggests that high cognitive load leads to reduced perception of self-efficacy and hurts motivation (Bandura, 1982; Feldon, Franco, Chao, Peugh, & Maahs-Fladung, 2018), while load reduction leads to higher motivation and higher engagement with the task, along with better outcomes (e.g., Evans, Vansteenkiste, Parker, Kingsford-Smith, & Zhou, 2024; Noy & Zhang, 2023; Skulmowski, Pradel, Kühnert, Brunnett, & Rey, 2016; Wu, Liu, Ruan, Chen, & Xie, 2025). In line with these observations, reducing cognitive load by offloading the task to genAI should lead participants to experience increased self-efficacy beliefs and greater motivation to perform well on the task, suggesting yet another benefit of genAI tools for enhanced performance.

However, theories of motivation paint a more complex picture. The central factor in how much effort is exerted for task performance is task difficulty. Motivation intensity theory (MIT; Brehm & Self, 1989; Brehm, Wright, Solomon, Silka, & Greenberg, 1983; Richter, Gendolla, & Wright, 2016) postulates that the amount of energy deployed for task completion is dependent on task demands and available resources. Challenging tasks "energise" individuals and present higher goal attractiveness, which leads to greater effort. Indeed, higher task difficulty increases the amount of biological and mental resources needed, as evidenced by increased heart rate or blood pressure in preparation for a difficult task (Richter et al., 2016; Wright, Shaw, & Jones, 1990). However, at a very high level of difficulty, when the task starts to seem impossible to perform, the amount of required resources is not proportionate to the task requirements, and therefore people report lower intended effort and lower motivation to engage in the task (Brehm et al., 1983; Silvestrini, Musslick, Berry, & Vassena, 2023).

MIT's account of perceived difficulty relates to the task's objective demands, as well as one's perceived ability to meet those demands, such that lower confidence in one's own capability increases the effort perceived as necessary for a given task (Brehm & Self, 1989; Wright, 1996). This perspective is related to the expected value of control (EVC) theory, which states that individuals decide how much effort to allocate to a task by weighing the potential benefits of allocating control against associated costs (Shenhav, Botvinick, & Cohen, 2013; Silvestrini et al., 2023). When the cost is deemed too high relative to the amount of control required by the task, energy is conserved by allocating less effort to it. Additionally, a promise of a reward leads to increased performance on cognitive control trials (Padmala & Pessoa, 2011; Umemoto & Holroyd, 2015) as a reward increases the attractiveness of the goal and therefore the amount of effort deployed to achieve it. Thus, providing people with additional incentives on top of a relatively challenging task should increase their motivation and the amount of effort exerted in the task.

These theories and their associated predictions lend themselves to address the question of offloading cognitive load to genAI and its implications for learning. Offloading to genAI may reduce task difficulty, which would reduce the amount of effort participants mobilise in the task, compared to a group without access to genAI, as they employ the lowest amount of resources required to perform the task. As per MIT, an incentive could counteract this effect by increasing the amount of effort mobilised. On the other hand, offloading may also disrupt construction of knowledge required for subsequent reasoning, which makes it appear more difficult. As participants' estimate of their own capabilities will be reduced or uncertain, perceived difficulty may increase, which increases the amount of effort mobilised, compared to a group without access to genAI.

Current research on genAI use and effort has not disentangled these predictions. Huffstadt (2026) found that participants who used genAI at work reported reduced mental effort, which in turn predicted increased effort, but other studies report the opposite pattern of results. Dell'Acqua (2022) suggested that high-performing genAI may incentivise humans to decrease effort. He found that HR professionals who were given high quality genAI assistance produced poorer resume evaluations than those who were given low quality assistance. It is possible that the mistaken (or inflated) perception of fluency and competence in human-genAI interaction negatively influenced participants' engagement with the task and the amount of resources they mobilised, in line with motivation theories. Relatedly, Liu, Christian, Dumbalska, Bakker, and Dubey (2026) found that participants who performed maths tasks with genAI were later less likely to complete more maths questions independently, which the authors attributed to reduced persistence. They proposed that after working with genAI, participants' estimate regarding how much time and effort a task should take, is calibrated to genAI-assisted performance and a subsequent task appears to require much more effort and resources, due to a metacognitive decay. This interpretation is in line with theories of motivation, where feeling out of one's depth regarding task difficulty reduces motivation. This effect was not found for participants who were solving maths tasks independently throughout the experiment. Similarly, Wu et al. (2025) examined participants' motivation both after performing a genAI assisted task (or an unassisted control task) and a subsequent independently performed task. They found evidence for increased motivation for the genAI assisted task, compared to a control group who worked independently, but subsequently those who were asked to stop working with genAI reported a larger drop in motivation than those who were working independently throughout the experiment. It appears that genAI tools cause people to overestimate later (independent) task difficulty and perceive it as too difficult to be worth engaging their resources (as per MIT).

However, neither study directly tested how genAI affects motivation when subsequent performance depends on knowledge constructed during the assisted task. In Wu et al. (2025), the successive tasks were unrelated, whereas in Liu et al. (2026), the subsequent task did not depend on knowledge acquired during the genAI-assisted phase. Moreover, participants in the latter study knew in advance that genAI support would later be removed. To our knowledge, no study has orthogonally manipulated genAI access and motivational incentives, leaving their joint effects on knowledge construction and effort regulation unclear.

1.3. The present study

To examine how cognitive offloading to genAI and performance incentives shape subsequent independent reasoning, effort regulation, and knowledge retention, we conducted a three-stage experiment based on a complex relational reasoning task. The study comprised an initial reasoning task (Stage 1), a subsequent independent reasoning task (Stage 2), and a memory test (Stage 3). GenAI access and performance incentives were independently manipulated, with genAI available only during Stage 1. An additional control condition completed a structurally similar but unrelated Stage 1 task without genAI, followed by the same Stage 2 and Stage 3 tasks as the experimental groups.

Based on the theoretical considerations outlined above, we formulated three sets of hypotheses concerning the consequences of genAI assistance, the effects of performance incentives, and the determinants of cognitive offloading behaviour. The hypotheses specified below were preregistered.

1.3.1. GenAI assistance, relational knowledge, and subsequent performance

The first set of hypotheses concerns two competing consequences of cognitive offloading to genAI for subsequent performance. On the one hand, by reducing the cognitive demands of the initial task, genAI assistance may limit the accumulation of cognitive fatigue, leaving more resources available for subsequent independent reasoning. This prediction is consistent with findings from sequentialtask paradigms showing that sustained effortful cognitive control can impair performance on a subsequent demanding task (Hagger, Wood, Stiff, & Chatzisarantis, 2010), as well as research showing that cognitive offloading can benefit performance on concurrent or subsequent cognitively demanding tasks (Runge, Frings, & Tempel, 2019; Storm & Stone, 2015). Accordingly, participants who use genAI during Stage 1 may perform better and more efficiently in Stage 2. On the other hand, offloading the integration of information to genAI may reduce engagement with the underlying relational structure, resulting in weaker internal representations and consequently poorer subsequent reasoning and memory. The control condition allows these accounts to be distinguished by comparing participants who completed a similarly demanding Stage 1 task, and should therefore experience comparable cognitive fatigue, but who did not acquire relational knowledge relevant to the subsequent stages.

H1a: Participants with access to genAI assistance during the chunked reasoning task (Stage 1) will achieve higher accuracy in the subsequent network-level reasoning task (Stage 2) than those without assistance.

H1b: Participants without genAI assistance during the chunked reasoning phase (Stage 1) will achieve higher accuracy in the subsequent network-level reasoning task (Stage 2) than those with assistance.

H1c: Participants in the control condition, who complete an alternative chunked reasoning task in Stage 1, will show lower accuracy in the network-level reasoning task (Stage 2) and the memory task (Stage 3) than participants in the no-genAI-assistance experimental condition.

H2a: Participants with genAI assistance during the chunked reasoning phase (Stage 1) will complete the subsequent network-level reasoning task (Stage 2) faster than those without assistance.

H2b: Participants without genAI assistance during the chunked reasoning phase (Stage 1) will complete the subsequent network-level reasoning task (Stage 2) faster than those with assistance.

H3: Participants without genAI assistance during the chunked reasoning phase (Stage 1) will demonstrate better memory performance in Stage 3 than those with assistance. Participants in the performance-incentive condition will also demonstrate better memory performance than those in the no-incentive condition. The interaction between genAI assistance and performance incentives will be examined on an exploratory basis.

1.3.2. Performance incentives and effort regulation

Performance incentives may influence task performance by changing how much effort individuals are willing to mobilise. According to motivation intensity theory, effort investment depends not only on task demands but also on the importance and attractiveness of successful performance. Incentives can increase the value of success and thereby justify greater investment of cognitive resources, particularly in demanding tasks. Accordingly, participants receiving additional performance incentives may invest more effort in Stage 2, which may translate into higher accuracy and differences in completion time. Because genAI assistance during Stage 1 may alter perceived task demands and subsequent effort regulation, we additionally examine whether the effects of incentives depend on prior access to genAI.

H4: Participants in the performance-incentive condition will achieve higher accuracy in Stage 2 than participants in the no-incentive condition. We will also examine the interaction between performance incentives and prior genAI assistance on an exploratory basis.

H5: Completion time in Stage 2 will differ between participants in the performance-incentive and no-incentive conditions. We will also examine the interaction between performance incentives and prior genAI assistance on an exploratory basis.

1.3.3. Determinants of cognitive offloading behaviour

The extent to which individuals rely on external cognitive support is not uniform, but depends on both taskrelated and individual factors. Cognitive offloading can be understood as a metacognitively regulated strategy in which individuals balance the cognitive costs of performing a task internally against the benefits of using an external resource. Lower working memory capacity is expected to increase reliance on offloading, as fewer internal resources are available for maintaining and integrating task information. In addition, performance incentives may reduce offloading by increasing the perceived importance of engaging with and retaining task-relevant information. We therefore examine whether engaging in the use of genAI support is predicted by both performance incentives and individual working memory capacity.

H6: Within the genAI-assistance condition, participants in the no-incentive condition will engage in more cognitive offloading, operationalised as a greater number of genAI requests, than participants in the performance-incentive condition.

H7: Within the genAI-assistance condition, participants with lower working memory capacity will engage in more cognitive offloading, operationalised as a greater number of genAI requests than participants with higher working memory capacity.

The study employed a three-stage reasoning task (see Materials for details). 2.3.1. 3-stage reasoning task

The study employed a task specifically developed to assess the retention of relational knowledge and the ability to reason on the basis of that knowledge. It comprised three consecutive stages: (1) a chunk-based reasoning task, (2) a network-level reasoning task, and (3) a memory-retention test.

The task was based on a complex network of pseudowords representing entities, their attributes, and the relationships among them. In the main version of the task, the entities were fictitious biological or mythical creatures inhabiting different environments. In the control condition, they were fictitious celestial bodies located in fictitious galaxies. Participants encountered statements defining the entities and their relationships, such as "Kikara lives in the desert and is active only at night", "Bekierc is smaller than Kikara", and "Grzuniak is a type of Hosaluk." 3 The complete main network contained 15 entities and several dozen attributes and relationships. It was described by 82 unique logical propositions, although individual sentences could encode more than one proposition. The network was designed to be too complex to process in its entirety within working memory capacity. Consequently, participants had to construct and retain a structured representation of the network and derive conclusions that were not stated explicitly in the presented descriptions. The reasoning problems were enthymematic, sometimes requiring commonsense assumptions in addition to the explicitly presented information.

Chunk-based reasoning task.

In the first stage, participants were presented with subsets of statements describing relatively small sections of the network. Each subset was followed by questions that required participants to transform and integrate the available information rather than retrieve 3 The task was originally developed in Polish.

an explicitly stated answer. Example questions included "Is Grzuniak bigger than Blusotnia?", "Does Puładra have more legs than Kikara?", and "Are the creatures mentioned above active at the same time of day?" Figure 1 shows an example question.

The information was divided into chunks to limit cognitive load and facilitate the construction of a coherent mental representation of the network. This procedure was consistent with cognitive load theory, as it reduced the number of interacting elements that participants had to process simultaneously. The chunks varied in size and in the difficulty of the corresponding questions. Each question was based on four to eight statements. Participants selected one of three response options: yes, no, or impossible to determine.

Network-level reasoning task.

In the second stage, participants completed a more comprehensive reasoning task involving a substantially larger portion of the network. This stage assessed their ability to integrate and apply relational information encountered during the preceding stage.

Participants had access to additional paper-based materials with 20 information points, each containing one or two statements. The order of these information points was randomised for each participant. Together, they contained 48 logical propositions that had previously appeared in the chunk-based reasoning task. Participants answered 13 questions requiring the integration of information distributed across the 20 information points, which contained all information necessary to answer the questions. Example questions included "Is Blusotnia the largest creature living in the tropics?" and "Are there any herbivorous creatures with antennae in the tropics?". As in the first stage, the available response options were yes, no, and impossible to determine. Three control questions were presented at the end. These were simpler questions which could be answered based on a single proposition and were not included in the analysis.

Memory-retention test.

In the third stage, participants completed a 10-item memory test assessing their retention of information presented in both Stage 1 and Stage 2. Each question had four single-choice response options. 2.3.2. Other measures

Working memory capacity. In addition to the reasoning tasks, we measured working memory capacity with shortened complex span task (Foster et al., 2015), which consisted of two blocks of Symmetry Span (SymSpan) and one block of Operation Span (OSpan) tasks. In SymSpan, participants had to remember locations of red squares in a 4 × 4 grid of potential locations. After each item, a distractor task was presented, where participants were asked to judge symmetry of a displayed shape along its vertical axis. Each block comprised four trials, with set sizes ranging from 2 to 5 locations. Scores were calculated by summing the number of red square locations correctly recalled in the correct order. In OSpan, participants had to remember a series of letters presented one at a time, with the letters randomly selected for each trial. After each letter, participants had to solve a simple maths problem as a distractor task. At the end of the block participants were asked to recall the letters in the correct order. Scores were calculated by summing the number of letters correctly recalled in the correct position. Working memory capacity was calculated as the average of the scores from OSpan and SymSpan tasks.

Perceived task difficulty.

Task difficulty for each reasoning task (chunk-based reasoning, network reasoning and memory test) was self-reported by participants on a 7-point scale, from "very easy" to "very difficult".

Declared effort.

In order to measure perceived importance of the task and how much effort participants put into solving the tasks, we used the Effort/Importance scale of the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (e.g., Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, and Leone (1994) ). The scale was translated to Polish by the authors, with back translations verified by two highly proficient Polish-English bilinguals (including a professional translator).

Attitudes towards and use of genAI. We also collected information regarding participants' attitudes towards genAI using the Polish adaptation of the AI attitude scale (AIAS-4; Grassini, 2023; Juranek & Pochwatko, 2026). Additionally, participants provided information about the frequency of use of genAI tools and the type of tools they use.

GenAI use during the reasoning task.

Based on the conversations with ChatGPT conducted by participants in the genAI condition during the chunked reasoning task, we calculated the number of messages per reasoning question sent to ChatGPT by each participant, as well as the number of unique reasoning questions each participant sent to Chat-GPT. The data was used to analyse the relationship between genAI use frequency and style, and performance on both the chunk reasoning task and the network reasoning task.

2.4. Procedure

The study procedure was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Opinion No. 11/02/202). Participants were tested in a computer laboratory, with up to two participants taking part simultaneously at separate workstations. The workstations were arranged so that participants could not see each other's responses or interact during the tasks. Upon arrival, participants were asked to switch off their mobile phones or activate airplane mode. They then received information about the study, provided written informed consent, and were assigned a pseudonymised participant code. The complete laboratory session lasted approximately one hour.

Participants first completed a shortened complex-span working memory task consisting of Operation Span and Symmetry Span components. They proceeded to the threestage relational reasoning task. Participants received standardised instructions and completed a practice question with feedback to ensure that they understood the task requirements.

Before the reasoning task, participants assigned to the incentive condition received standardised information intended to increase the perceived importance of their performance. They were told that the top-performing third of participants would receive an additional financial bonus, that the Polish results would be compared with results from other European countries, and that they could receive their individual test score. Participants in the no-incentive condition did not receive these instructions.

Participants then completed the first, chunk-based reasoning stage either with or without access to ChatGPT, depending on the experimental condition. In the genAIassistance condition, ChatGPT was available on a separate monitor and participants were informed that they could use it freely to support their performance on the task. At the beginning of the task, participants in this condition were instructed to initiate the ChatGPT conversation by pasting a standardised introductory prompt stating that they were participating in a scientific experiment, and would ask ChatGPT for assistance with various tasks. Access to ChatGPT was removed before the subsequent network-level reasoning and memory test stages.

After completing the reasoning tasks, participants rated perceived task difficulty and the effort invested in completing the tasks. They then completed questionnaires concerning their attitudes toward and previous use of generative AI and provided demographic information. At the end of the session, participants received an initial debriefing explaining the general purpose of the study and the use of partially deceptive instructions. The full explanation of the motivational manipulation was provided after data collection had been completed to reduce the risk that information about the manipulation would reach future participants. See Figure 2 for overview of procedure.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python and R 4 . Prior to analysis, assumptions of the respective statistical tests were evaluated, including normality of residuals and homogeneity of variance. Where necessary, variables were transformed to better satisfy model assumptions; when assumptions remained inadequately met, robust or nonparametric alternatives were used. Multiple-comparison corrections are reported for the respective analyses.

We distinguish between preregistered hypothesis tests, supporting analyses, and exploratory analyses. Preregistered hypothesis tests directly addressed the hypotheses specified above. Supporting analyses were used to characterise task performance, manipulation effects, and other theoretically relevant outcomes that were not direct tests of the preregistered hypotheses. Exploratory analyses examined additional patterns beyond the primary hypothesis tests and are identified as such throughout the Results.

3.6. Patterns of ChatGPT use during the Stage 1 reasoning task

In an exploratory analysis, we examined how participants used ChatGPT during the Stage 1 chunk-based reasoning task and whether different patterns of use were associated with task performance. We classified the conversations conducted during Stage 1. The coding captured two types of information. First, at the item level, we recorded the number of task items for which each participant consulted ChatGPT. Second, at the participant level, we classified each participant according to their overall pattern of ChatGPT use.

The classification scheme was developed inductively based on an initial review of the conversations by three researchers. Subsequently, two other researchers independently classified all conversations into one of four mutually exclusive categories. Inter-rater agreement was Cohen's 𝜅 = 0.85, indicating almost perfect agreement. The coders resolved all disagreements through discussion and assigned a final category by consensus. The four categories were as follows: 1.

No or occasional use: Participants did not use Chat-GPT or exchanged no more than three messages with it. 2. Question copying: Participants copied and pasted task items into ChatGPT without adding substantive comments or follow-up questions. 3. Exploratory use: Participants engaged with ChatGPT beyond merely submitting task items, for example by proposing their own answers, requesting justification, asking follow-up questions, or expressing disagreement with the chatbot's responses. 4. Other use: Participants used ChatGPT primarily for purposes unrelated to obtaining or discussing answers, for example as a notepad.

Of the 79 participants in the genAI condition, one could not be included in the analysis because their conversation was not saved. Among the remaining 78 participants, 31 were classified as showing no or only occasional use, 28 as engaging in question copying, 16 as engaging in exploratory use, and 3 as using ChatGPT in other ways. Because the last category was both small and heterogeneous, these three participants were excluded from subsequent comparisons between patterns of ChatGPT use. Thus, the following analyses compared the three main groups (𝑁 = 75).

The groups differed significantly in Stage 1 accuracy, 𝐹 (2, 72) = 3.15, 𝑝 = .049, 𝜂 2 𝑝 = .080. Mean accuracy was 74.4% in the no-or-occasional-use group, 81.7% in the question-copying group, and 82.3% in the exploratory-use group. Tukey's HSD post hoc tests indicated that none of the pairwise differences reached statistical significance (𝑝s = .079-.990).

Kruskal-Wallis test revealed that the genAI use-type groups also differed significantly in the time required to complete the Stage 1 task, 𝐻(2) = 6.31, 𝑝 = .043, 𝜖2 = .060. The mean completion times were 1148 s in the no-oroccasional-use group, 1137 s in the question-copying group, and 1473 s in the exploratory-use group. Pairwise Mann-Whitney 𝑈 tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction indicated that participants in the exploratory-use group spent more time on the task than participants in both the no-oroccasional-use group (𝑝 adj = .041) and the question-copying group (𝑝 adj = .041). The latter two groups did not differ significantly (𝑝 adj = .897).

The groups also differed in their attitudes toward AI, 𝐹 (2, 72) = 6.27, 𝑝 = .003, 𝜂 2 𝑝 = .148. The mean attitude scores were 4.42 in the no-or-occasional-use group, 6.10 in the question-copying group, and 6.52 in the exploratory-use group. Tukey's HSD post hoc tests showed that participants in the no-or-occasional-use group reported less favorable attitudes toward artificial intelligence than participants in both the question-copying group (𝑝 = .014) and the exploratoryuse group (𝑝 = .009). Participants in the no-or-occasional-use group reported greater task difficulty than those in the question-copying group (𝑝 adj = .035). Finally, at the item level, question difficulty, operationalized as the proportion of incorrect responses to each item, was positively correlated with the number of participants who referred to that item in their conversations with Chat-GPT, 𝑟(16) = .607, 𝑝 = .008. Thus, more difficult items were discussed with ChatGPT by a greater number of participants.

4. Discussion

The present study examined how prior genAI use shapes subsequent independent reasoning within an interconnected network of information. After initially working with or without genAI support, participants were required to reason independently about relations distributed across the same broader knowledge network. Access to genAI provided immediate benefits during the assisted task, including higher aggregate accuracy and lower perceived difficulty. However, these benefits did not clearly transfer to subsequent independent performance: item-level analyses indicated poorer reasoning after prior genAI use, which suggests that it might have altered the process of relational learning. Prior genAI use was also associated with altered effort regulation, as participants showed a weaker increase in effort in response to incentives, particularly for more difficult questions.

4.1. Impact of genAI on relational reasoning

The present findings suggest a trade-off between the immediate benefits of genAI assistance and the subsequent ability to reason independently about the same relational structure. During the initial chunk-based reasoning task, participants with access to genAI achieved higher aggregate accuracy and perceived the task as less difficult than participants who worked independently. Performance incentives, in contrast, increased completion time without producing a corresponding increase in accuracy. Thus, genAI facilitated performance on the immediate task, whereas incentives primarily increased the amount of time participants were willing to invest.

This immediate benefit is consistent with a cognitiveoffloading account, according to which external tools can reduce the internal processing demands of a task (Risko & Gilbert, 2016; Skulmowski, 2023). In the present study, this interpretation is further supported by participants' actual use of genAI: those who submitted more queries to ChatGPT tended to achieve higher Stage 1 accuracy, and more difficult questions were more likely to prompt genAI use. Participants therefore appeared to turn to genAI particularly when cognitive demands were high, and doing so was associated with better immediate performance.

The more important question was whether these benefits transferred to subsequent independent reasoning. However, at the aggregate level, prior genAI access did not significantly affect Stage 2 accuracy. The item-level mixed-effects analysis provided some evidence of poorer subsequent reasoning following genAI use. Within the no-incentive reference condition, prior genAI access was associated with lower odds of answering individual Stage 2 questions correctly. Because the genAI × incentive interaction was not significant, this conditional effect should be interpreted cautiously.

Nevertheless, the direction of this result is consistent with the interpretation that genAI assistance reduced the construction of relational knowledge needed later, that is, it disrupted the knowledge acquisition process. In Stage 1, participants had to identify relevant information and integrate relations across multiple statements. These operations were not only necessary for answering the immediate questions, but also for constructing a representation of the broader relational network. If genAI performed part of this integration, participants could obtain a correct answer while engaging less deeply with the structure underlying it. This interpretation is consistent with previous work showing that more effortful, self-generated processing can support learning of deeper problem structure and later transfer (O'Hara & Payne, 1998), as well as with more recent findings suggesting that genAI assistance can improve immediate performance without necessarily producing equivalent gains in knowledge acquisition or transfer (Fan et al., 2025).

The control condition provides additional context for interpreting the Stage 2 results. In Stage 1, the control task was comparable to the baseline condition (no genAI, no incentive) in accuracy, completion time, and perceived difficulty, but control participants did not acquire prior knowledge of the relational network used in Stage 2. Despite this lack of relevant prior knowledge, their Stage 2 accuracy did not differ significantly from that of the baseline experimental group. Instead, control participants spent significantly more time completing the task. In other words, differences in prior knowledge between the control and baseline conditions were not necessarily reflected in aggregate Stage 2 accuracy, but control participants may have compensated for their lack of prior knowledge by investing more time in reconstructing the relevant relations from the information available during Stage 2. No effects of prior genAI access or incentives emerged in the Stage 3 memory test. This result limits how strongly the Stage 2 findings can be interpreted as evidence of impaired learning. Importantly, the memory test assessed information encountered across both Stage 1 and Stage 2. Participants therefore had an additional opportunity to process the relevant information independently before memory was tested. The absence of group differences in Stage 3 may indicate that subsequent independent exposure reduced earlier differences in knowledge acquisition. It may also reflect a distinction between remembering individual pieces of information and being able to use their relational structure to derive new conclusions.

Taken together, the findings suggest that genAI helped participants solve the immediate reasoning task, particularly when cognitive demands were high, but these benefits did not clearly transfer to later independent reasoning. The results are consistent with the interpretation that when genAI performs some of the integration required by a task, users may engage less deeply in constructing the relational knowledge needed later. More broadly, this supports the distinction between using genAI to support cognitive processing and using it to replace processing that is itself important for learning (Lo et al., 2024). In the present task, information integration was both a source of difficulty and a central part of what participants needed to learn. From the educational perspective, the findings raise the possibility that successful completion of genAI-assisted tasks may not reliably reflect the learners' ability to independently integrate and apply the underlying information.

4.2. Motivational effect

GenAI use altered subsequent effort regulation, particularly by weakening the effect of external incentives when task demands were high. This pattern was already visible at the group level. Participants in the incentive condition without prior genAI access spent more time on Stage 2 than the no-genAI, no-incentive baseline group. Participants in the control condition also spent more time on Stage 2 than the baseline group, despite receiving no incentive and having no prior knowledge of the relevant network from Stage 1. Their longer completion time may therefore reflect compensatory effort: because they could not rely on previously acquired network knowledge, they devoted more time to processing the information available in Stage 2. By contrast, neither group with prior genAI access showed a similar increase in time investment. This suggests that prior genAI use reduced participants' tendency to increase effort when additional effort could have been useful.

The item-level analysis provides the clearest evidence for this interpretation. The interaction between prior genAI use, incentives, and question difficulty showed that differences in time allocation were concentrated among the most difficult questions. In the no-genAI condition, incentives increased the time devoted to difficult questions. This adjustment was much weaker among participants who had previously completed Stage 1 with genAI assistance. Prior genAI use therefore did not reduce time investment equally across all questions. Instead, it weakened incentive-sensitive effort mobilisation when task demands were high.

This pattern cannot be explained by participants experiencing Stage 2 as unusually difficult after losing access to genAI. Although participants with genAI access rated Stage 1 as easier than those who worked independently, perceived difficulty in Stage 2 did not differ across the experimental conditions. The reduced adjustment of effort among prior genAI users therefore occurred without a corresponding increase in their reported task difficulty.

One possible explanation can be derived from motivation intensity theory. According to this framework, effort increases with task difficulty only while success remains possible and the required effort does not exceed the maximum effort justified by the importance of success, referred to as potential motivation (Brehm et al., 1983). Prior genAI use may have lowered the amount of personal effort that participants considered justified for this task. As a result, difficult questions may have reached this subjective limit sooner. Participants without prior genAI access continued to increase their effort when an incentive made successful performance more important, whereas participants with prior genAI access did not show the same increase.

The self-reported effort results were broadly consistent with this account. There was a significant interaction between genAI use and incentives, but none of the simple comparisons were significant. Numerically, incentives were associated with greater declared effort among participants who had worked without genAI, whereas the pattern was reversed among participants with prior genAI access. Thus, the behavioural effect was accompanied by a similar descriptive pattern in participants' reports: those who had used genAI responded faster and reported relatively less effort when an incentive was present.

In simpler terms, successful performance with genAI during Stage 1 may have suggested that the task could be completed with relatively little personal effort. Participants may then have judged a high level of independent effort as less worthwhile when the tool was no longer available. This does not necessarily mean that genAI reduced their general motivation or interest. Rather, it may have lowered the amount of effort they considered reasonable to invest in this particular task.

A second explanation concerns altered effort calibration. From the expected value of control perspective, the amount of cognitive control allocated to a task depends on the expected benefits of control, its expected effect on performance, and its subjective costs (Shenhav et al., 2013). During Stage 1, genAI improved performance and reduced perceived difficulty. Participants may therefore have learned an inaccurate relation between personal effort and successful performance: good performance appeared possible with relatively little internally generated processing. When genAI was removed in Stage 2, they may have failed to adjust this estimate to the greater demands of unaided reasoning and consequently allocated too little time and control, especially to difficult questions.

The two explanations differ in an important respect. Under the potential-motivation account, participants may have recognised that additional effort could improve performance but nevertheless treated that effort as not worth its cost. This judgment need not have been fully conscious. Under the calibration account, they may instead have underestimated how much personal effort was required or how strongly additional effort would improve performance. The interaction in self-reported effort makes the potential-motivation account somewhat more plausible, because participants' reports followed the same general pattern as their behaviour, suggesting that they were at least partly aware of investing less effort. However, because none of the simple effects were significant, the evidence does not clearly distinguish between the two mechanisms. They may also have operated together. Both accounts describe a change in effort regulation rather than a simple decline in reasoning ability.

The findings also do not support a simple explanation in which genAI use made participants overlook or forget the incentive instructions. The incentive increased completion time during Stage 1, indicating that participants responded to the manipulation even when genAI was available. Moreover, participants in the control condition, who received no incentive, nevertheless spent more time on Stage 2 than the baseline group. An explicit reward was therefore not necessary for greater time investment. Instead, prior experience of solving Stage 1 independently may have supported a higher level of later effort mobilisation, whereas genAI-assisted performance may have reduced the amount of independent effort that appeared necessary or justified.

Taken together, these findings suggest that prior genAI use changed how participants allocated effort after the tool was removed. Participants who had previously worked independently increased their effort when incentives and task difficulty made additional investment useful. Participants who had used genAI showed a weaker adjustment. This pattern may reflect a lower level of potential motivation, inaccurate calibration of the effort required for unaided performance, or a combination of both processes. For learning activities that alternate between AI-supported and independent work, this raises the question of whether learners also need support in recalibrating the effort they expect to invest once assistance is withdrawn.

4.3. Determinants of cognitive offloading behaviour

Contrary to our predictions, cognitive offloading was not systematically related to either working memory capacity or performance incentives. Working memory capacity did not predict the number of queries participants submitted to genAI. Similarly, additional performance incentives did not increase or decrease the number of genAI queries. Thus, having fewer internal cognitive resources did not appear to make participants more likely to rely on the external tool, and increasing the importance of successful performance was not sufficient to change the extent to which they used it. Instead, participants' attitudes towards genAI were more clearly associated with whether and how they engaged with the available tool. This pattern was particularly apparent in the qualitative analysis of ChatGPT-use behaviour. Almost 40% of participants with access to genAI either did not use ChatGPT at all or used it only occasionally. These participants reported substantially less favourable attitudes toward genAI than those who used genAI in the task. This pattern was observed despite all participants having the same opportunity to use the tool. A substantial proportion of participants effectively chose to continue solving the task internally.

The behavioural categories further suggest that cognitive offloading should not be treated as a unitary behaviour. Among participants who reported using genAI regularly, 28 primarily copied task questions to ChatGPT, whereas 16 engaged in more exploratory interactions, such as proposing their own answers, requesting explanations, asking followup questions, or challenging the model's responses. These strategies imply different degrees of cognitive delegation. Question copying represents a relatively direct transfer of problem solving to the external system, whereas exploratory use retains greater involvement of the participant in evaluating and integrating the generated information. A simple count of queries therefore captures the amount of interaction with genAI but does not fully characterise the cognitive processes being offloaded.

The exploratory use group also spent substantially more time on Stage 1 than either the question-copying or no-oroccasional-use groups, while the two groups that decided to use ChatGPT achieved numerically higher accuracy. This pattern suggests that genAI use need not necessarily function as an effort-reduction strategy. For some participants, particularly those using the tool exploratorily, genAI became an additional resource with which they actively interacted rather than a substitute for task engagement.

The item-level analysis also showed that participants were more likely to turn to genAI when they encountered more difficult questions. This pattern is consistent with the role of element interactivity proposed by cognitive load theory: questions that require more information to be maintained and integrated simultaneously impose greater demands on working memory and may therefore increase the value of external cognitive support. Interestingly, although working memory capacity did not predict overall genAI use across participants, question difficulty predicted when participants were more likely to seek assistance. This suggests that offloading in the present task may have been more sensitive to the cognitive demands of a particular problem than to stable individual differences in working memory capacity.

4.4. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the evidence that prior genAI use impaired subsequent relational reasoning was not fully consistent across levels of analysis. In the network-level reasoning task, the analysis of aggregated accuracy scores did not reveal a significant main effect of genAI assistance, whereas the negative effect emerged in the item-level mixedeffects analysis. This suggests that the effect may be modest and sensitive to the analytical approach and individual variability between participants and items. Moreover, there was no significant interaction between genAI assistance and performance incentives on accuracy. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and replicated in future studies using larger samples or stronger incentives.

A related limitation concerns variation in actual genAI use. Providing access to ChatGPT did not necessarily result in substantial cognitive offloading, as some participants did not use the tool or used it only occasionally. This may have reduced differences between the genAI and no-genAI conditions. Future studies should therefore distinguish between access to genAI and the extent or type of cognitive offloading.

Second, performance incentives did not increase accuracy, although they affected the time participants devoted to the tasks. This suggests that the manipulation influenced effort allocation without necessarily improving performance. It remains unclear whether this reflects a limitation of the manipulation or of the task itself. Participants may already have been highly motivated in the no-incentive condition, leaving limited room for improvement. In addition, completion time is only an indirect measure of effort. Future studies should therefore include additional behavioural and subjective measures of effort.

A further limitation concerns the operationalisation of question difficulty in the exploratory analyses. Because difficulty was estimated from item-level accuracy in the present sample, the observed moderation effect should be interpreted cautiously and possibly replicated using independently established estimates of question difficulty.

Finally, the present incentive manipulation primarily targeted extrinsic motivation and therefore does not allow conclusions about how genAI interacts with intrinsic motivation. Future research should examine whether factors such as task interest, perceived competence, autonomy, and personal relevance influence cognitive engagement when genAI support is available.

4.5. Conclusion

Overall, the results showed that while use of genAI made participants perform better and perceive the task as less difficult, it also influenced effort regulation on subsequent tasks and dampened the effects of motivational incentives. The findings are in line with previous studies suggesting that relying on genAI for cognitive tasks may have detrimental effect on learning and self-regulation mechanisms. Additionally, we found that participants' choice to rely on genAI depended both on question difficulty as well as their prior frequency of use and attitudes towards genAI. As the genAI technology becomes more prevalent, these findings highlight opportunities for future studies on motivational factors in human-genAI interaction.
