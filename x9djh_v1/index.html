How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. No reminder is given when the paper already cites that replication or reproduction (matched by its DOI).

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the original study was cited for other reasons than the empirical claim tested, or that the replication/reproduction in the FLoRA database is for only one of the studies in the paper, and not the study the authors discuss.

The database can be manually updated with the FLoRA_update() function. For more information, see https://forrt.org/FLoRA/.

Abstract

Background: Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are typically operationalized as binary indicators (present/absent), collapsing heterogeneous exposure levels into a single count. Whether preserving severity gradations improves the prediction of adult health outcomes remains an open empirical question with direct implications for research and clinical screening.

Objective: To systematically compare binary versus severity-weighted ACE operationalizations across multiple levels of data representation (item-level, domain-level, and sum scores), analytic frameworks (sum scores, exploratory factor analysis, item response theory, and penalized regression), and psychosocial outcomes in a perinatal cohort.

Method: Participants were 454 pregnant women recruited within a longitudinal project on early experiences and perinatal mental health. ACEs were assessed using an expanded ACE/ACE-IQ-based instrument capturing both occurrence and severity of 30 adversity items across 17 domains. Five outcomes were measured: depressive symptoms, prenatal psychosocial adjustment, resilience, social support, and benevolent childhood experiences. Ten distinct ACE data representations were constructed. Predictive validity was evaluated using repeated 10-fold cross-validation with honest estimation (factor models refit inside each training fold).

Results: Severity-weighted operationalizations consistently outperformed binary counterparts across all comparisons. The largest advantage emerged when the severity-graded domain scores were retained as separated predictors. Factor-based representations showed comparable predictive performance but poor structural adequacy and computational instability. The severity advantage was consistent at every level of comparison.

Conclusions: Preserving severity information in ACE measurement was associated with greater out-ofsample predictive performance than binary scoring in this perinatal cohort. These findings support considering severity-weighted, domain-specific scoring in future ACE research and warrant further evaluation of its potential utility in clinical screening.

1. Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have been established as one of the most robust predictors of adult physical and mental health outcomes since the landmark study by Felitti et al. (1998). The original ACE Study assessed ten categories of childhood adversity and operationalized each as a binary indicator (present/absent), generating a cumulative count score ranging from 0 to 10. This binary-count approach has become the dominant paradigm in ACE research, adopted in hundreds of subsequent studies and embedded in clinical screening tools worldwide (Hughes et al., 2017; Merrick et al., 2018).

Despite its widespread adoption, the binary operationalization has attracted increasing methodological criticism. Collapsing ordinal or continuous severity information into dichotomous indicators entails a loss of potentially important variance (Lacey & Minnis, 2020; McLennan et al., 2020). A single episode of witnessed domestic conflict receives the same score as years of severe physical abuse. This loss of information is particularly significant because dose-response relationships between severity of adversity and health outcomes have been documented across multiple domains (Gilbert et al., 2009; Norman et al., 2012).

Beyond the binary-versus-severity distinction, the field has debated additional operationalization choices including (a) whether to use the original 10 Felitti categories or expanded taxonomies that include peer victimization, community violence, and other adversities (Finkelhor et al., 2013; WHO, 2018; Mersky et al., 2017); (b) whether to analyze ACEs as a unidimensional construct or as a multidimensional structure; and (c) what level of aggregation is appropriate (individual items, thematic domains, or composite scores) (Briggs et al., 2021; Olofson, 2018). These decisions are not merely academic as they directly affect research conclusions about which ACEs are most strongly associated with health outcomes, how adversity relates to specific outcomes, and how screening instruments should be designed.

From a psychometric perspective, dichotomization of continuous or ordinal variables systematically reduces statistical power and attenuates effect sizes (MacCallum et al., 2002; Royston et al., 2006). From a clinical perspective, ACE severity is known to moderate long-term outcomes: the dose-response gradient observed even within the binary-count framework (Dong et al., 2004; Felitti et al., 1998) suggests that finer gradations would capture additional meaningful variance. Recent instruments such as the ACE International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ; WHO, 2018) and the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ; Bernstein et al., 2003) already collect severity or frequency information. More recently, studies have reported improved psychometric performance when severity or frequency information is retained during ACE scoring (Santelices et al., 2025), and weighted approaches to adversity measurement have been proposed as alternatives to traditional binary scoring (Jacobsen et al., 2022). However, systematic comparisons of these operationalization strategies in terms of predictive validity remain scarce. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses of ACE data yielded inconsistent findings, with studies identifying between one and six factors (Afifi et al., 2020; Ford et al., 2014; Mersky et al., 2017). More recent psychometric work continues to raise questions about the underlying structure and validity of ACE measures, underscoring the importance of evaluating alternative approaches to measuring and scoring childhood adversity (Loncar et al., 2026).

These inconsistencies may partly reflect the use of binary indicators, which constrain the covariance structure and can produce artifactual factors (Rhemtulla et al., 2012). Severity-graded items provide a richer covariance structure that may support more stable factor solutions. However, the practical utility of factorial operationalizations depends not only on model fit statistics alone but also on whether factor scores predict outcomes better than simpler alternatives.

The present study provides a detailed, empirically grounded comparison of ACE operationalization strategies. We systematically vary three dimensions: (a) binary versus severity-weighted scoring, (b) level of aggregation (item, domain, and composite), and (c) analytic framework (sum scores, exploratory factor analysis, item response theory, and penalized regression). All comparisons use a common outcome set and a rigorous honest cross-validation framework designed to prevent information leakage. We hypothesize that severity-weighted operationalizations will show better predictive performance than their binary counterparts at every level of analysis, and that the gains will be most pronounced when individual domains are entered as separate predictors rather than collapsed into a single sum.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Participants and Procedure

The sample comprised 454 pregnant women participating in a broader longitudinal research project [Blinded for review] on early experiences and perinatal mental health in Spain. Initially, 502 women expressed interest and provided informed consent; however, 48 did not meet eligibility criteria or subsequently withdrew, resulting in the final analytic sample of 454. Inclusion criteria were being pregnant at the time of enrolment, being 18 years of age or older, having sufficient proficiency in Spanish, and signing the informed consent form. The exclusion criterion was not residing in Spain, to reduce potential differences related to access to pregnancy-related healthcare.

Data was collected between March 2024 and July 2025. Participants were recruited through the Obstetrics Department of a public hospital in [Blinded for review], as well as through social media and women's associations. In the initial phase, women completed a preliminary form accessible through a QR code, providing contact details. Subsequently, a member of the research team contacted each participant by telephone to explain the study, confidentiality procedures, anonymization, and available support resources.

Women who agreed to participate signed the informed consent form digitally and received a personal code to anonymize their responses to the online questionnaire. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (approval number: PI 203/23) and was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF).

2.2. Instruments

>Instruments

2.2.1. Adverse Childhood Experiences

ACEs were assessed using an expanded version of the Adverse Childhood Experiences questionnaire. The instrument was built upon the original ACE framework proposed by Felitti et al. (1998), which included the 10 classic categories. The item pool was expanded by adding five additional items from Mersky et al. (2017) parental absence, parental or sibling death, homelessness, financial problems, and violent crime victimizationand two items from the WHO ACE-IQ (WHO, 2018)peer bullying and unsafe neighborhoodcollectively designed to address the original instrument's focus on household-level adversity by incorporating peer-and community-level domains. This expanded item pool was further informed by Osofsky et al.'s (2021) conceptual framework emphasizing the distinct relevance of specific ACE subtypes during the perinatal period. The instrument included 30 items organized into 17 thematic domains: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, divorce/separation, exposure to domestic violence, household substance abuse, mental illness in the household, parental incarceration, parental absence, parental or family death, unsafe neighborhood, violent crime victimization, financial problems, homelessness, and bullying. Items 1-9 were scored dichotomously (0 = absent,1 = present), whereas items 10-30 preserved ordinal severity information (0 = never,1 = mild/once,2 = moderate/sometimes,3 = severe/frequently). Because ACEs were conceptualized as a cumulative index of heterogeneous adverse experiences rather than as a unidimensional reflective scale, internal consistency was not used as a primary psychometric criterion. The full domain-item assignment is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

2.2.2. Outcome Measures

Five validated instruments assessed perinatal mental health and psychosocial outcomes.

Depressive symptoms were measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001 Kroenke et al., , 2010)), nine items rated 0-3 (range 0-27), with higher scores indicating greater severity [α = .812].

Prenatal psychosocial adjustment was measured with the PSQ (Lederman & Weis, 2009), using the 42item Spanish adaptation (Armengol et al., 2007), which covers six dimensions (pregnancy acceptance, maternal role, relationship with mother, relationship with partner, childbirth preparation, and worry about own and baby's well-being). Items are rated on a four-point scale and summed after reverse-scoring where required, with higher scores indicating [better / worse] adaptation [α = .898].

Resilience was measured with the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale-21 (CD-RISC-21; Connor & Davidson, 2003 . Spanish version: Márquez-González, Losada, López y Peñacoba (2009), a self-report scale assessing the capacity to cope with adversity. Items are rated on a five-point scale from 0 (not true at all) to 4 (nearly always) (range 0 -84), with higher total scores reflecting greater psychological resilience [α = .921].

Perceived social support was assessed with the Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire (DUKE-UNC-11; Broadhead et al., 1988; Spanish validation by Bellón et al., 1996), an 11-item measure of confidant and affective support rated from 1 (much less than I would like) to 5 (as much as I would like), with total scores ranging from 11 to 55 and higher scores indicating greater perceived support [α = .905].

Benevolent childhood experiences were measured with the BCE scale (Narayan et al., 2018), a ten item self-report measure of positive childhood experiences occurring before age 18, such as feeling safe with a caregiver or having supportive friendship. Items are answered as yes or no and summed (range 0-10), with higher scores indicating a greater number of positive childhood experiences.

T2.2.3. Covariates

Three covariates were included in all predictive models: maternal age, gestational age in weeks at assessment, and educational level. Missing covariate values were imputed to the sample median.

2.3. ACE Operationalization Strategies

Ten distinct data representations were constructed by crossing two scoring approaches (binary vs. severityweighted) with three levels of aggregation (item, domain, and composite). Table 1 summarizes these operationalization strategies. For binary representations, each item was scored as 0 (absent) or 1 (present). For severity-weighted representations, dichotomous items (1-9) retained their 0/1 scoring, whereas ordinal items (10-30) retained their full severity range (0-3). At the domain level, the binary score was defined as the maximum across constituent items, such that any non-zero item score resulted in a domain score of 1; the severity score likewise defined as the maximum severity across constituent items and then rescaled to the 0-1 range.

A clarification is warranted regarding the Felitti-10 severity representations at the domain and composite levels. The severity-weighted versions reported here are possible because six of the ten Felitti domains (corresponding to abuse and neglect subtypes and domestic violence) included items with graduated frequency response options (items 10-30), whereas the remaining four (household substance abuse, mental illness, separation and incarceration) rely exclusively on dichotomous items (items 1-9) and are therefore identical under binary and severity scoring. Consequently, any additional information captured by the severity-weighted Felitti-10 scores derives entirely from these six domains.

2.4. Analytic Framework

>Analytic Framework

2.4.1. Overview

The analytic pipeline proceeded in two stages. Stage A screened candidate EFA solutions across representations, extraction methods, and numbers of factors using cross-validated R² from factor scores estimated on the full sample. Stage B subjected the top candidates to honest cross-validation, refitting the entire factor model and outcome regression within each training fold and scoring held-out folds using training-set parameters only.

2.4.2. Stage A: Screening

For each representation, EFA models were estimated using three extraction methods, minimum residuals (minres), principal axis factoring (PA), and maximum likelihood (ML), and numbers of factors ranging from K = 1 to K = 6 for all representations. Parallel analysis was conducted as a descriptive reference but did not constrain the factor search grid, as it failed to converge for two of the seven representations (Extended-17 domain severity and Item ordinal-severity, both polychoric) and suggested values ranging from K = 4 to K = 7 where it did converge. The appropriate correlation matrix was selected for each representation: tetrachoric for binary items, polychoric for ordinal and mixed binary/ordinal representations, and Pearson for continuous severity scores. All models used oblimin rotation. Items with extreme prevalence (endorsement < 5% or > 98%) were excluded. Factor scores were computed using the regression method (Thurstone, 1934).

2.4.3. Stage B: Honest Cross-Validation

The top 22 candidates from Stage A were subjected to honest cross-validation. In each of 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation, the entire EFA was refit on the training data (including prevalence filtering, correlation matrix estimation, factor extraction, and rotation), and factor scores for the held-out test fold were computed by applying the training-set weight matrix to test data standardized using the corresponding training-set means and standard deviations. For each outcome, a linear regression (including covariates) was fit on the training fold and evaluated on the test fold. This procedure ensures that all factor-model estimation and regression-model estimation occurs strictly within the training partition.

The two-stage screening procedure may introduce modest selection optimism, although all reported estimates derive from the honest cross-validation in Stage B.

2.4.4. Benchmark Models

Simple benchmarks. Covariates only (serving as a lower-bound benchmark); Felitti-10 and extended-17 binary and severity sums; 10 and 17 domain indicators (binary and severity-weighted) entered as separate predictors.

Item response theory (IRT).

Unidimensional 2PL models for binary items and graded response models (GRM) for ordinal severity items, at both item and domain levels. For domain-level severity representations, the GRM was used only when the domain score retained ordered discrete categories; continuous 0-1 severity scores were not treated as ordinal IRT indicators. IRT models were refitted inside each cross-validation fold (honest CV), analogous to the treatment of factor models. Theta scores were estimated using expected posteriori (EAP) scoring.

Penalized regression (LASSO).

LASSO regression was applied to domain-level indicators with nested crossvalidation for regularization parameter selection (Tibshirani, 2011). LASSO serves as a supervised predictive reference representing the best achievable linear prediction with automatic variable selection, rather than as a formal upper bound.

2.4.5. Model Selection and Stability

Among honestly cross-validated factor models, a parsimony rule selected the model with the fewest factors whose mean R² was within Δ = .010 of the best-performing model. Structural stability was assessed via split-half analysis (100 random splits), computing Tucker's congruence coefficient between independently estimated loading matrices.

2.4.6. Confidence Intervals and Software

Confidence intervals for cross-validated R² were computed using the t-distribution (based on the 10 repeatlevel mean R² values, with the standard error calculated across repeats). This procedure was applied identically across factor models, benchmarks, IRT, and LASSO models to ensure comparability of all reported intervals. All analyses were conducted in R using psych (Revelle, 2015), mirt (Chalmers, 2012), glmnet (Friedman, 2001), and custom cross-validation code.

For practical purposes, the computational instability advises against relying on the factor model as the preferred operationalization.

4. Discussion

Consistent with our hypotheses, severity-weighted operationalizations outperformed binary representations across all levels of analysis, and the largest gains emerged when individual domains were retained as separate predictors rather than collapsed into summary scores. This study provides a head-tohead comparison of binary versus severity-weighted ACE operationalizations across multiple levels of analysis, analytic frameworks, and psychosocial outcomes in a perinatal cohort. The central finding is that preserving severity information consistently and meaningfully improves predictive validity. This advantage is held across every comparison, sum scores, domain-level indicators, factor models, IRT latent scores, and penalized regression, and across all five outcomes.

The magnitude of the severity advantage varied systematically with model complexity. At the sumscore level, the gain was modest (ΔR² ≈ .010), reflecting the limited capacity of a single composite to capture differential severity patterns. At the domain level, the gain was substantial (ΔR² ≈ .042), representing to an 31% improvement. The larger differences observed for domain-level representations suggest that preserving severity information may be particularly informative when distinguishing between specific types of adversity. That is, different ACEs, and different degrees of exposure within the same ACE, carry non-equivalent developmental weight rather than functioning as interchangeable units within a single count (Briggs et al., 2021).

Beyond measurement convenience, this finding also has a theoretical explanation, as cumulative stress models propose that adversity affects health through the progressive dysregulation of physiological stressresponse systems, allostatic load, such that the degree and duration of exposure, not merely its presence, determine the biological burden (Danese & McEwen, 2012). Consistent with this dose-graded view, cumulative-risk research has shown that the number and intensity of stressors, rather than any single categorical exposure, best predicts developmental outcomes (Evans & Kim, 2013). A binary scoring scheme is, in effect, a truncation of this underlying dose-response gradient introduced earlier in this paper. It retains information about whether a stressor occurred but discards information about how much. Our results, that severity-weighted scoring outperforms binary scoring, and that the gain increases as more granular domainlevel information is retained, are consistent with what this framework would predict.

This dose-graded logic is particularly pertinent to the perinatal population as pregnancy is increasingly recognized as a period during which a mother's own history of adversity can shape offspring outcomes through cumulative biomedical and psychosocial pathways, including prenatal complications and postpartum maternal mental health (Gomis-Pomares et al., 2026; Rabasa et al., 2026), that appear to scale with the burden of the mother's early adversity rather than with its mere presence or absence (Madigan et al., 2017). If the relevant transmission mechanisms are themselves graded, an ACE measure that discards severity information may systematically understate risk in exactly the population, pregnant women, where capturing that gradient has the most direct downstream relevance for maternal and infant outcomes.

This finding also has direct implications for ACE screening instruments. If severity information is collected but collapsed into binary indicators for scoring, a substantial portion of the assessment's potential value is discarded (McLennan et al., 2020). The additional cost of preserving severity during scoring is minor, no additional items, no additional respondent time, and minimal computational effort. This is consistent with broader methodological evidence that dichotomizing continuous or ordinal variables reduces statistical power and can bias parameter estimates, regardless of the substantive domain (MacCallum et al., 2002; Royston et al., 2006).

Factor-analytic models did not clearly surpass simpler domain-level approaches. The best factor model (R² = .184) only modestly exceeded unreduced 17 severity domains (R² = .175), while LASSO (R² = .191) slightly surpassed both. Moreover, severity-graded factor models exhibited 40-52% failure rates in CV and poor global fit (RMSEA > .50, TLI < 0), and stability could not be verified.

Although the selected exploratory factor solution retained useful predictive information, its poor fit and lack of structural stability suggest that these factors should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as exploratory representations of ACE co-occurrence patterns. This interpretation is broadly consistent with previous Spanish work on the ACE Questionnaire (Villanueva et al., 2026), which approached ACEs as concrete adverse experiences rather than indicators of a common latent construct.

These findings are consistent with growing evidence that ACEs do not conform to a clean latent factor structure (Ford et al., 2014; Olofson, 2018). The heterogeneity of adversity types, the mixture of binary and ordinal response formats, and low base rates of severe adversities combine to produce a correlation matrix poorly approximated by a small number of factors (Rhemtulla et al., 2012). For practical purposes, researchers are better served by entering domain-level severity scores directly, possibly with penalized regression, than by attempting to extract factor scores from a poorly fitting model. Binary/tetrachoric factor models were computationally stable (zero failures) and achieved reasonable predictive validity (R² = .165).

This stability makes binary models attractive where polychoric estimation is not feasible. However, their R² was consistently below severity-weighted counterparts, confirming that computational convenience comes at an informational cost.

4.5. Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample comprised pregnant women from an urban Spanish setting, mostly Spanish nationals and highly educated, limiting generalizability to other populations and to less advantaged sociodemographic groups. Second, the sample size (N = 454), while adequate for cross-validation, constrained the stability of polychoric estimation. Third, all measures were retrospective self-reports. Fourth, the study examined concurrent predictive validity; longitudinal advantages of severity scoring remain to be established. Fifth, the available severity gradations (four levels) represent a coarse approximation; finer gradations might yield further improvements. Finally, candidate factor solutions were initially selected through predictive performance analysis before undergoing rigorous cross-validation. Future studies could extend this approach by implementing fully nested cross-validation procedures, in which model selection and evaluation are repeated together at each resampling iteration and by testing severity-weighted models in independent validation samples.

4.6. Practical Recommendations

For research: When severity information is available, severity-weighted domain-level representations should be preferred over binary ACE scoring, as they consistently showed greater predictive utility in the present study. Researchers are encouraged to examine the robustness of findings across alternative operationalization of ACE exposure.

For clinical screening: Severity-weighted scoring may represent a promising alternative to traditional binary counts, although additional validation across population and settings is needed before clinical recommendation can be made.

5. Conclusions

The binary operationalization of adverse childhood experiences, while historically dominant, discards information about variation in adversity severity. Across five outcomes in this perinatal cohort, severityweighted representations showed higher predictive R² than binary representations, with larger differences observed when individual domains were modeled separately. Factor-analytic and IRT approaches did not consistently improve predictive performance beyond simpler domain-level severity representations. These findings provide a rationale for further evaluating whether severity-weighted scoring could improve the utility of ACE assessment in clinical screening.
