Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Wise reasoning is an adaptive capacity associated with wellbeing and pro-sociality across the lifespan, yet why some individuals reason more wisely than others remains unclear. We examined factors associated with individual differences in wise reasoning across five major psychological domains, and tested whether these associations differ between younger and older adults. A sample of 179 younger and 161 older adults completed a performance-based measure of wise reasoning, two self-report measures of wisdom traits, and a comprehensive battery assessing autobiographical memory, crystallized and fluid cognition, personality, affect, and social functioning. Greater wise reasoning capacity was associated with the richness of autobiographical recollection, better crystallized cognition, greater openness to experience, and better emotion recognition, but not affective dispositions. Cognitive performance accounted for more variance in wise reasoning than any other psychological domain. A data-driven analysis further revealed that wise reasoning capacity is best characterized by a multivariate pattern of covarying factors spanning psychological domains. Although most associations were similar across age groups, some evidence of age-related moderation emerged, including stronger associations with autobiographical memory vividness among younger adults and openness to experience among older adults. The correlates of wise reasoning did not fully generalize to self-reported wisdom traits, which were associated primarily with self-reported personality and affective dispositions. These findings advance an integrated account of the psychological foundations of wise reasoning across adulthood, highlighting their multidimensional nature, relative stability across the adult lifespan, and the key roles of autobiographical memory and crystallized cognition.

[div-01]

Wisdom has long been regarded as a core human virtue and a hallmark of optimal human development 1, 2 . It is ascribed to individuals who demonstrate an ability to successfully navigate life's complexities with insight, balancing their own interests with those of others [3] [4] [5] [6] . A central expression of wisdom is wise reasoning 7 , which involves flexibly drawing on rich knowledge to reason through uncertain life situations, while adopting a metacognitive stance that acknowledges the limits of one's knowledge, considers alternative perspectives, and balances competing interests [8] [9] [10] . Greater capacity for wise reasoning has been associated with higher well-being and more prosocial attitudes and behaviors (Brienza et al., 2021; Grossmann et al., 2013, 2017; Peetz & Grossmann, 2021; Santos & Grossmann, 2021; also see Ardelt, 2016; Ardelt & Jeste, 2018), and interventions designed to enhance wise reasoning have been shown to improve well-being (Grossmann et al., 2021; also see Ardelt, 2020; Jeste et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2020). Therefore, wise reasoning is considered a modifiable capacity that may support adaptive functioning across diverse contexts [22] [23] [24] [25] . Why some people reason more wisely than others remains unclear. The main aim of this study was to systematically examine the psychosocial factors that explain individual differences in wise reasoning.

A central theoretical perspective holds that wise reasoning draws on knowledge and insights gained from personal life experiences (Baltes & Staudinger, 2000; also see Glück, Bluck, et al., 2019; Webster, 2022). Consistent with this view, people often equate wisdom to the accumulation of life experience and, consequently, attribute wisdom to older age (Weststrate et al., 2019). However, empirical findings show inconsistent associations between age and wise reasoning, suggesting that older age is neither necessary nor sufficient 29, 30.

Instead, the nature of life experiences, like formal education and major or adverse life events, and the extent to which people derive insight from them are increasingly recognized as particularly important for cultivating wise reasoning 26, 27.

Autobiographical memory retrieval-the cognitive processes involved in accessing stored knowledge about personally experienced past events-may provide a means through which past experiences inform wise reasoning. Individual differences in autobiographical memory retrieval may therefore account for variation in wise reasoning. Little research has examined this possibility. Existing work has instead focused on the content of wisdomrelated memories specifically, and the ways in which people reflect on past experiences [31] [32] [33] . This gap is particularly notable given evidence that autobiographical memory supports adaptive social functioning [34] [35] [36] and that individuals vary substantially in both the content and phenomenological characteristics of their autobiographical memories 37 . One important dimension of this variation is the extent to which autobiographical recollections contain internal details-referring to episodic, event-specific information-and external detailsreferring to semantic or other information that is not specific to the remembered event.

External details may support wise reasoning by providing relevant general knowledge (cf. 8 ; also see 26, 27 ). It is possible, therefore, that individuals whose memories contain more external details may exhibit wiser reasoning. Internal details could support wise reasoning by providing specific experiential information that informs the imagination of novel situations and potential solutions (cf. 38 ). Consistent with this possibility, greater internal details in autobiographical recollections has been linked to more effective solutions to open-ended social problems [39] [40] [41] [42] . Because these problems share the uncertainty and open-endedness characteristic of situations that benefit from wise reasoning 3, 10 , individuals whose memories contain more internal details may likewise exhibit wiser reasoning. We sought to test the possibility that the richness of internal and external details in autobiographical recollections relates to wise reasoning capacity using well-established and comprehensive interview-based measures of autobiographical memory and wise reasoning.

Beyond life experience and autobiographical memory, research has identified several psychological factors that may support the development and manifestation of wise reasoning. Among these, cognitive abilities show a particularly robust relationship with wise reasoning.

Both crystallized cognition-referring to the ability to store and use general knowledge about the world-and fluid cognition-referring to the ability to reason effectively, solve novel problems and make decisions-are reliably associated with a greater capacity for wise reasoning 43 . Among personality traits, greater openness to experience-referring to the tendency to be open to diverse experiences, ideas, and perspectives-has emerged as a reliable correlate of wise reasoning 43 . In the affective domain, greater well-being has been associated with wiser reasoning 43 . Social functioning has also been proffered as an important contributor to wise reasoning. For instance, it has been proposed that empathy and prosociality are central components of wisdom 3, 4 , to the extent that they have been incorporated into some self-report measures of wisdom-related traits 44 . However, whether individual differences in social functioning are related to the capacity to reason wisely remains less well understood. Another limitation of most existing research into the correlates of wise reasoning is the lack of integrative approaches that examine cognitive, personality, affective, and social factors simultaneously. Consequently, it remains unclear which domains provide the greatest explanatory value and whether their associations with wise reasoning reflect unique contributions or a shared pattern of covariation across domains.

Given growing evidence that the psychological domains previously linked to wise reasoning undergo age-related changes across the adult lifespan, a major unanswered question is whether the factors that explain inter-individual variability in the capacity to reason wisely remain stable or vary across adulthood. The composition of autobiographical memory varies throughout adulthood, with younger adults recollecting more internal details, and older adults recollecting more external details [45] [46] [47] [48] . Reductions in fluid cognition accompanied by stable or increased crystallized cognition are hallmark features of aging 49.

Personality traits also tend to change in later life, with the largest changes sometimes reported for openness to experience 50, 51 . In the socio-affective domain, older compared with younger adults show a greater tendency for prioritized processing of positive information 52 , changes in the size and composition of their social networks 53, 54 and both gains and declines across different aspects of social cognition [55] [56] [57] [58] . Thus, it is possible that the psychological foundations of wise reasoning change across the adult lifespan, such that older and younger adults may draw on different psychological resources to arrive at similarly wise judgments.

We aimed to test this possibility.

Current Study.

In this study, we examined the autobiographical memory, cognitive, personality, affective, and social factors associated with wise reasoning capacity. Our primary aim was to determine whether these psychological domains account for individual differences in wise reasoning scores, including their relative explanatory value and whether their associations with wise reasoning might reflect shared patterns of covariation across domains (Aim 1). Our secondary aim was to examine whether these associations differ between younger and older adults (Aim 2). Finally, given how resource-intensive it is to assess wise reasoning performance, we sought to examine whether its psychological correlates are also evident in self-report measures of wisdom-related traits, which are less demanding to administer (Aim 3). To accomplish these aims, we leveraged a unique cross-sectional dataset in which 179 younger adults and 161 older adults completed the Berlin Wisdom Paradigm 8 , a well-established performance-based measure of wise reasoning; two self-report wisdom scales; and an extensive battery of psychological tests and self-report measures assessing distinct facets of each of the five major psychological domains of interest. We expected that all psychological domains would account for individual differences in wise reasoning, with directional predictions detailed in the results section. Consistent with the conceptualization of wise reasoning as a way of thinking that involves flexibly applying knowledge and insights gained from prior life experiences, we predicted that cognitive abilities and autobiographical memory would show the strongest associations with wise reasoning scores. Given previously observed age-related differences in the psychological domains considered, we further expected that the strength of their associations with wise reasoning would differ between younger and older adults, although we made no directional predictions regarding these agerelated differences.

Wise Reasoning and Psychological Factors as a Function of Age

To characterize age-related differences in the psychological measures of interest, we compared scores on each measure between younger and older adults, and we examined correlations between age and each measure within each age group (Figure 1; Table S3).

Wise Reasoning.

Younger and older adults did not differ significantly in wise reasoning scores (Figure 1; Table S3), a finding consistent with meta-analytic evidence 43 . Within the younger adult group, older age was associated with higher wise reasoning scores (Figure 1).

Education.

Compared to younger adults, older adults reported more years of completed education (Figure 1; Table S3). In both age groups, older age was associated with more years of education completed (Figure 1).

Autobiographical Memory.

Relative to younger adults, older adults exhibited lower internal detail density and greater external detail density in autobiographical recollections (Figure 1; Table S3). Moreover, in older adults, older age was associated with higher external detail density (Figure 1). This pattern of results is consistent with an age-related shift from episodically rich toward more semantically-rich autobiographical memory 46, 59.

Cognition.

In line with an age-related shift from fluid toward more semanticized cognition in later life 49 , older adults exhibited lower fluid cognition scores and higher crystallized cognition scores (Figure 1; Table S3). In older adults, older age was associated with lower fluid cognition scores, but higher crystallized cognition scores (Figure 1), consistent with the possibility that the shift from fluid toward crystallized cognition continues throughout older adulthood.

Personality. Compared to younger adults, older adults exhibited lower neuroticism and higher agreeableness (Figure 1; Table S3), in line with previously observed mean-level agerelated changes in personality 50, 51.

Affect.

Relative to younger adults, older adults showed lower levels of externalized negative affect and higher levels of positive affect (Figure 1; Table S3). In older adults, older age was associated with lower externalized negative affect (Figure 1). These patterns are consistent with previous empirical observations 60 , and with predictions derived from the socioemotional selectivity theory that older adults increasingly prioritize rewarding experiences 61.

Autobiographical Memory.

Both internal and external detail density were expected to be associated with wise reasoning scores. Given that wise reasoning is proposed to draw on knowledge and insights gained from personal life experiences 8 (also see, 26, 27 ), we hypothesized that greater external detail density in autobiographical recollections would be associated with higher wise reasoning scores. Given that internal details in autobiographical recollections are thought to enable imagining hypothetical scenarios 38 , contributing to better social problem-solving (e.g., 42 ), we hypothesized that greater internal detail density would be associated with higher wise reasoning scores. Autobiographical memory detail densities did not explain a statistically significant proportion of variance in wise reasoning scores (Figure 2A, Table S7). Nonetheless, internal detail density showed a significant association with wise reasoning, such that lower internal detail density was associated with wiser reasoning. The direction of this association was opposite to our prediction.

Given the limited research examining how aspects of autobiographical memory retrieval relate to the capacity for wise reasoning, we conducted three follow-up exploratory analyses to further characterize this relationship. Specifically, we examined whether other aspects of autobiographical memory quantified using the Autobiographical Interview were associated with wise reasoning, without having specific a priori predictions. First, given the association between internal detail density and wise reasoning observed in the main analysis, we examined whether specific sub-categories of internal details (e.g., place detail, time details; see Table S1 for a full list and descriptions) were differentially associated with wise reasoning while controlling for overall external detail density. Models including the internal detail sub-categories accounted for a significant proportion of variance in wise reasoning scores (Figure 3, Table S8). Specifically, lower place-detail density and higher perceptualdetail density were associated with wiser reasoning. The finding that these two internal detail subcategories showed opposing associations with wise reasoning scores is notable as it suggests that different aspects of autobiographical recollection can either support or interfere with reasoning depending on the type of information recalled.

Next, we examined whether the identified associations of place and perceptual details in autobiographical recollections with wise reasoning could be replicated using independent scorer ratings. Cognition. Consistent with a role for cognitive abilities in facilitating wise reasoning 70, 71 , we predicted that fluid and crystallized cognition would be positively associated with wise reasoning scores. As expected, cognitive abilities explained a significant proportion of variance in wise reasoning scores (Figure 2, Table S6). Higher crystallized cognition was significantly associated with higher wise reasoning scores, whereas the positive association between fluid cognition and wise reasoning showed a similar direction but did not reach statistical significance.

Personality.

Consistent with perspectives proposing that adaptive personality characteristics contribute to wisdom 44 (see also 43 ), we predicted positive associations between wise reasoning and the more adaptive dimensions of personality (openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, and agreeableness), and a negative association with neuroticism. Personality traits explained a significant proportion of variance in wise reasoning scores (Figure 2, Table S11). At the level of individual traits, only greater openness to experience was positively associated with wiser reasoning, a relationship showing the predicted direction.

We conducted a follow-up exploratory analysis to examine whether the finer-grained intellect and openness aspects of the openness to experience trait (for definitions, see Table S1) showed differential associations with wise reasoning while accounting for the other four personality traits. This analysis revealed that only the intellect aspect of openness to experience was significantly positively associated with wise reasoning (Table S12).

Affect.

A robust link between greater wellbeing and wiser reasoning has been observed 43.

Moreover, the broaden and built theory proposes that positive affect can temporarily broaden one's thought-action repertoire [72] [73] [74] , which may support wise reasoning 15 . Therefore, we predicted that wise reasoning scores would be associated with greater positive affective dispositions and lower negative affective dispositions. Contrary to our predictions, the affective measures did not explain a statistically significant proportion of variance in wise reasoning scores (Figure 2, Table S13).

Social Functioning.

Given the centrality of pro-sociality to theoretical accounts of wise reasoning 4 , we predicted that greater emotion recognition ability and empathy would be associated with wiser reasoning. Given preliminary evidence that social interactions play a role in the development of wise reasoning 75 (also see 76 ), we further predicted that larger social networks and lower loneliness would be associated with wiser reasoning. Social measures explained a statistically significant proportion of variance in wise reasoning scores (Figure 2, Table S14). Only higher emotion recognition ability was significantly associated with wiser reasoning, a relationship showing the predicted direction.

The cognitive, social, internal detail sub-categories and subjective dimensions of autobiographical memory, and, to some extent, personality domains and education improved prediction of wise reasoning scores in unseen data (Figure 2; Table S15), providing evidence for their predictive utility. These results also suggest that the hierarchical regression models were not substantially over-fitted. Although out-of-sample predictive performance was modest in magnitude, it was supported by relatively stable coefficients across cross-validation folds and repetitions (Table S15), suggesting that these associations were robust to sample perturbations. In contrast, affective dispositions and autobiographical memory, when quantified at the broad level of internal versus external detail density, did not improve out-ofsample prediction of wise reasoning scores.

Bayesian model comparisons indicated decisive evidence in favor of including cognitive predictors relative to both the null model and the covariate-only model. Education, internal autobiographical detail sub-categories, and social functioning received moderate evidence of support relative to the covariate-only model. Subjective dimensions of autobiographical memory and openness to experience facets received only anecdotal evidence. In contrast, comparisons provided decisive evidence in favor of the covariate-only model over models including affective dispositions, anecdotal evidence in favor of the covariate-only model over models including personality predictors, and moderate evidence in favour of the covariate-only model over autobiographical internal/external detail predictors.

As expected, cognitive abilities emerged as the strongest predictor domain across analyses, explaining the largest proportion of variance in wise reasoning, demonstrating the strongest out-of-sample predictive performance, and receiving the strongest support from Bayesian model comparisons. In terms of variance explained, cognition was followed by social functioning, autobiographic internal detail sub-categories, personality, and education.

Age-related Differences in the Correlates of Wise Reasoning

Across all predictor domains examined, the addition of age group interactions did not significantly improve model fit or out-of-sample predictive performance (see Figure 2, and Tables S4-S15). Bayesian model comparisons provided evidence in favor of main effects models over models including age group interactions, ranging from anecdotal evidence for the education, autobiographical memory, personality, and affect domains to substantial evidence for the cognitive and social functioning domains (Figure 2, Table S15).

Despite this overall pattern of age group invariance, a small number of individual predictors showed evidence of age-related differences in their associations with wise reasoning. Specifically, in the personality model, we observed a significant interaction between age group and openness to experience (Figure 2, Table S11). Follow-up withingroup analyses indicated that openness to experience was positively associated with wise reasoning in older adults, but not younger adults (Figure S4, Table S11).

We also observed an age group interaction for vividness in the exploratory autobiographical memory analyses focused on subjective characteristics of autobiographical recollections (Figure 2, Table S10). Follow-up within-group analyses indicated that greater vividness of autobiographical recollections was associated with wiser reasoning in younger adults, whereas this association was not significant in older adults (Table S10).

The robustness analyses conducted separately for each group provided limited evidence for within-group age-related variation in the correlates of wise reasoning at the level of domain-specific models (for a detailed description, see Supplementary Results).

Nevertheless, several associations between individual factors and wise reasoning varied as a function of chronological age. Among older adults, a significant sex × age interaction indicated that older age was associated with lower wise reasoning scores among male, but not female, participants (Table S4). Age also moderated the association between place internal details in autobiographical recollection and wise reasoning, such that the negative association was stronger at relatively older ages (Figure S8, Table S8). Likewise, the association between event internal details and wise reasoning was moderated by age, such that a negative association was observed in relatively younger older adults (Figure S8, Table S8). Among younger adults, age moderated the association between perceptual internal details in autobiographical recollection and wise reasoning, such that the positive association was stronger at relatively older ages (Figure S8, Table S8). Additionally, a significant age × conscientiousness interaction indicated an association between higher conscientiousness and wiser reasoning at relatively younger ages (Figure S4, Table S11).

Discussion

Despite growing interest in the empirical study of wisdom, the factors underlying individual differences in the capacity to reason wisely remain poorly understood, potentially reflecting limited integration across psychological domains and insufficient consideration of age-related differences. We addressed these gaps by assessing a broad range of candidate factors spanning five psychological domains in both younger and older adults. Consistent with our predictions, individual differences in wise reasoning were related to autobiographical memory, cognitive performance, personality, and social functioning. Specifically, higher wise reasoning scores were associated with fewer place and more perceptual internal details in autobiographical recollections, better crystallized cognitive ability, greater openness to experience, and better emotion recognition. Contrary to our prediction, affective dispositions were not associated with wise reasoning. As expected, cognitive performance accounted for more variance in wise reasoning scores than any other psychological domain and showed the highest out-of-sample predictive performance and evidence strength. A data-driven multivariate analysis further revealed that individual differences in wise reasoning were best characterized by a configuration of covarying factors spanning psychological domains. Although younger and older adults differed on measures across all five domains, the factors associated with wise reasoning were largely similar between age groups. Nonetheless, some associations were moderated by age, including stronger relationships between greater openness to experience and wiser reasoning among older adults and between more vivid autobiographical recollections and wiser reasoning among younger adults. Finally, the correlates of performance-based wise reasoning differed from those of self-reported wisdom traits, which were more strongly associated with selfreported personality and affective dispositions, and were unrelated to cognitive performance or autobiographical memory. Together, these findings provide an integrated account of the diverse psychological factors associated with the adaptive capacity to reason wisely across adulthood.

Autobiographical Memory Contributions to Wise Reasoning.

Among the psychological domains examined, our findings on autobiographical memory offer a particularly novel perspective on the psychological mechanisms underlying wise reasoning. Research on the relationship between autobiographical memory and wisdom remains limited and has focused on the types of personal experiences in which wisdom manifests and develops, as well as individual differences in self-reported reflection on past experiences 31, 33, 78, 79 . We extend this work by demonstrating that the content of autobiographical recollections is associated with individual differences in wise reasoning. Specifically, higher wise reasoning scores were associated with lower overall internal detail density. This finding indicates that people who recollect more event-specific autobiographical information tend to have a lower capacity for wise reasoning. However, this association obscured opposing relationships among internal detail sub-categories: higher wise reasoning was associated with fewer place details but more perceptual details. This dissociation is consistent with evidence that place and perceptual internal details reflect distinct dimensions of autobiographical memory 47 , and suggests that the relationship between autobiographical memory and wise reasoning depends not simply on how much event-specific detail is recalled, but on the nature of the information retrieved.

Perceptual details capture sensory and experiential aspects (e.g., visual and auditory information) of past experiences that can be flexibly recombined to imagine hypothetical scenarios according to the constructive episodic simulation hypothesis 38 . The observed positive correlation between internal perceptual details and wise reasoning suggests that sensorial aspects of episodic simulation ability might contribute to wise reasoning, perhaps by supporting the consideration of multiple possibilities and perspectives when reasoning though complex, uncertain situations. Indeed, the retrieval of more internal details from autobiographical memory has been associated with more effective solutions to open-ended social problems [39] [40] [41] [42] . Analyses conducted separately in younger and older adults further showed that the association between perceptual internal details and wise reasoning was stronger among relatively older individuals within the younger adult sample but remained relatively stable across age in the older adult sample. This pattern may suggest that the ability to draw on episodic simulation in support of wise reasoning develops across young adulthood.

Place details are closely tied to the specific spatial contexts in which past experiences occurred. Retrieving many such details could make it harder to generalize knowledge from these past experiences to novel contexts that differ from the original, or could introduce contextual information that is incidental to the problem at hand, interfering with the selection and use of relevant information. Either mechanism could help explain the observed association between greater density of internal place details in autobiographical recollections and lower wise reasoning capacity. This interpretation is broadly consistent with evidence that forgetting can be adaptive, allowing individuals to behave flexibly and generalize knowledge from past experiences to new situations 80 . This finding raises the possibility that wise reasoning benefits not simply from access to autobiographical information, but from the ability to flexibly select and apply knowledge from past experiences across different contexts.

Interestingly, robustness analyses indicated that the negative association between internal place details and wise reasoning became stronger with increasing age among older adults. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that context-specific information becomes more disruptive to reasoning when it is more difficult to filter out irrelevant information. Agerelated declines in executive function 49 could contribute to this pattern by making it harder to regulate the influence of contextually specific autobiographical information. Although this interpretation remains tentative, the age-related moderation lends further support to the possibility that the negative association reflects the potential for context-specific autobiographical information to interfere with the flexible use of insights from past experience in support of wise reasoning. More generally, our findings extend previous research on the role of autobiographical memory in social problem solving by examining problem solving quality-specifically, the extent to which responses reflect characteristics of wisdom. This approach differs from the common evaluation of problem-solving performance using quantitative indices of solution generation, such as the number of solutions generated or the number of steps within generated solutions (e.g., 40, 80 ). The opposing associations of internal place and perceptual details with wise reasoning qualifies the previous proposal that greater internal autobiographical detail generally facilitates performance on open-ended problems 40, 41 . Our findings suggest that, rather than being uniformly beneficial, event-specific autobiographical details may support or interfere with reasoning depending on the type of internal details.

Beyond the content of autobiographical recollections, their subjective characteristics were also related to wise reasoning, although the evidence for this association was less consistent across analytic approaches. Specifically, frequentist hierarchical regression analyses indicated that greater self-reported autobiographical memory vividness was associated with higher wise reasoning scores among younger adults, with this association significantly stronger than among older adults. This finding may reflect younger adults' greater reliance on autobiographical memories when reasoning through complex situations, potentially because they have had fewer opportunities to develop a rich store of generalizable factual and practical knowledge through accumulated life experience. Autobiographical memories offer concrete examples of how particular situations have unfolded, which is especially useful when relevant generalizable knowledge is lacking (for a related discussion, see 82 ). However, this account does not explain why the age group moderation emerged for subjective vividness but not for the independently assessed density of autobiographic details.

One possibility is that the subjective sense of re-experiencing, rather than the richness of autobiographical memory more broadly, is particularly relevant to how younger adults draw on past experiences to support wise reasoning. Interestingly, in the within-group robustness analyses, independently rated episodic richness, which captures the extent to which a recollection conveys a sense of re-experiencing to an external observer, showed a similar, albeit weaker, pattern of age group differences in its association with wise reasoning to that observed for vividness. Moreover, among younger adults, the association between greater episodic richness and wiser reasoning tended to be stronger at younger ages. This convergence provides tentative additional support for the possibility that the subjective sense of re-experiencing autobiographical recollections is particularly relevant to wise reasoning in early adulthood.

Cognitive Contributions to Wise Reasoning.

Of all psychological domains examined, performance on cognitive tasks accounted for the greatest proportion of the inter-individual variance in wise reasoning. Broadly consistent with meta-analytic evidence 43 , higher fluid and crystallized cognitive abilities were associated with higher wise reasoning scores, although the association with fluid cognition did not reach statistical significance and was not evident in within-group robustness analyses. This finding is consistent with the view that cognitive abilities support wise reasoning by providing key knowledge and computations required for flexible information processing and problem-solving 4 . The stronger association with crystallized cognition points to a particularly important role for generalized knowledge in wise reasoning. Interestingly, although crystallized cognition was positively associated with external autobiographical details, consistent with the scaffolding role of semantic knowledge in autobiographical memory 83, 84 , external detail density was not associated with wise reasoning. This divergence suggests that crystallized cognition contributes to wise reasoning though aspects of knowledge beyond those involved in supporting autobiographical memory retrieval, potentially including generalizable knowledge not grounded in direct personal experience.

Nevertheless, cognitive performance accounted for only 8% of the variance in wise reasoning scores, underscoring that cognitive abilities alone are insufficient to fully account for individual differences in wise reasoning, consistent with previous perspectives 12, 70, 71, 85, 86.

It is also possible that conventional measures of fluid and crystallized cognition do not fully capture the specific cognitive processes relevant to wise reasoning. For example, these measures do not assess semantic control mechanisms, which underpin the flexible, contextsensitive use of prior knowledge 87, 88 . Future research should therefore examine more finegrained cognitive processes to clarify which aspects of cognition are most relevant to the capacity to reason wisely.

We found no evidence that the associations between fluid and crystallized cognitive abilities and wise reasoning differed between younger and older adults. This finding is not consistent with the recent proposal that fluid cognition plays a more important role in wise reasoning among younger adults, who have had less time to accumulate knowledge, than older adults 85 . Nor was crystallized cognitive ability more strongly associated with wise reasoning in older than younger adults, as might be expected if knowledge played a relatively more important role in wise reasoning in older age, given age-related declines in fluid cognition 49 . Taken together, our findings suggest that cognitive ability, particularly crystallized cognitive ability is a key contributor to wise reasoning across adulthood.

Personality Contributions to Wise Reasoning.

Openness to experience was the only personality trait associated with wise reasoning. Participants higher in openness to experience tended to score higher on wise reasoning, consistent with meta-analytic evidence 43 . The disposition to engage with novel and diverse experiences characteristic of the openness to experience trait may facilitate the acquisition of knowledge and insight into life matters 26 , which supports wise reasoning 8 . Consistent with this possibility, greater openness to experience is associated with greater engagement in information-seeking behaviors, particularly in contexts that provide opportunities for intellectual engagement 89 . Notably, the association with wise reasoning was specific to the Intellect aspect of openness. This finding refines our understanding of the personality correlates of wise reasoning by suggesting that intellectual curiosity and engagement with complex ideas is more relevant than a disposition toward novel sensory and aesthetic experiences. Intellect may promote engagement with educational and intellectually stimulating experiences potentially leading not only to the acquisition of relevant knowledge, but also to critical thinking and consideration of multiple perspectives and alternative possibilities characteristic of wise reasoning. Because Intellect is also more strongly related to objective intelligence than the Openness aspect 90 , the association with wise reasoning could partly reflect overlap with cognitive ability. However, the correlations of the Intellect and Openness aspects with cognitive ability were highly similar in our sample, making differential overlap with cognitive ability an unlikely explanation for the specificity of the association with Intellect.

Despite the absence of age group differences in the level of openness to experience, its relationship with wise reasoning was significantly stronger among older adults. This finding suggests that a disposition towards openness to experiences is more important for wise reasoning in older adulthood, potentially reflecting the cumulative opportunities to engage with diverse experiences and acquire knowledge and perspectives that can be brought to bear on complex situations. Consistent with this possibility, openness was positively correlated with crystallized cognitive ability. Openness to experience has also been associated with greater resilience to age-related cognitive decline [91] [92] [93] , raising the possibility that its characteristic behavioral dispositions contribute to the preservation of cognitive abilities necessary for wise reasoning in later life. Age group did not similarly moderate the association between the Intellect aspect of openness to experience and wise reasoning. This pattern suggests that the stronger association between openness and wise reasoning in older adults reflects aspects of the broader trait beyond intellectual engagement.

Social Contributions to Wise Reasoning.

Among social factors, only emotion recognition ability was associated with wise reasoning. Participants who performed better on the emotion recognition task tended to score higher on wise reasoning. This association is notable given that the two abilities were assessed using different task formats: the Reading the Mind in the Eyes task relied on facial cues, whereas the wise reasoning assessment involved written verbal scenarios. This finding suggests that the ability to reason wisely draws upon the ability to infer others' emotional states. However, performance on the Reading the Mind in the Eyes task has also been linked to vocabulary knowledge 94 , raising the possibility that the observed association between emotion recognition ability and wise reasoning scores partly reflects individual differences in crystallized cognitive ability. Affective empathy was not associated with wise reasoning, contrary to theoretical accounts proposing that empathy is a necessary condition for wisdom 26 . This dissociation suggests that recognizing others' emotional states is more relevant to wise reasoning than the affective tendency to share their emotions.

However, this interpretation warrants caution because emotion recognition and wise reasoning were assessed using performance-based measures, whereas affective empathy was self-reported. Shared method variance may therefore partly account for the observed pattern.

A Multidomain Perspective of Wise Reasoning.

A major contribution of the current work is the identification of a multidomain constellation of psychological characteristics associated with wise reasoning. The multivariate pattern indicated that people who reason more wisely tend to recall autobiographical memories characterized by greater episodic richness, more perceptual detail, and stronger associations with emotional states, thoughts and implications, but fewer event, time and place details. They also report greater emotional change surrounding the recollected events, and tend to experience their autobiographical reminiscences more vividly and as more personally significant. Further, people who reason more wisely tend to have higher educational attainment, report higher Intellect, and demonstrate better crystallized cognitive performance and greater emotion recognition ability. This multidomain profile accounted for 16% of the variance in individual differences in wise reasoning beyond demographic covariates, exceeding the variance explained by any single psychological domain considered separately. This suggests that individual differences in wise reasoning are better understood in terms of a combination of covarying characteristics spanning multiple psychological domains, than through any single domain alone. This finding is consistent with theoretical accounts proposing that wisdom draws on resources form multiple psychological domains, such that no single resource is sufficient for wisdom 3, 95, 96 . The specific multidomain configuration observed further suggests that wise reasoning is not simply associated with greater resources across the board. Rather, it is associated with a differentiated constellation of characteristics, with higher levels of some characteristics and lower levels of others (i.e., internal event, place and time details in autobiographical recollections) conducive to wiser reasoning. Although the present analyses examined a broad range of characteristics across five major psychological domains, substantial variance in wise reasoning remained unexplained by the multivariate profile, suggesting that future research should also explore factors beyond those considered here.

Age (Non)Differences in The Correlates of Wise Reasoning.

Despite age-related differences in many psychological characteristics, the correlates of wise reasoning were largely consistent in younger and older adults. Although age group differences emerged for specific factors, Bayesian analyses favored models without age interactions, suggesting that the psychological underpinnings of wise reasoning are broadly similar across adulthood.

Nevertheless, age interactions involving openness to experience and autobiographical memory vividness in the main analyses, as well as perceptual, event and place internal autobiographical details, and conscientiousness in the within-group robustness analyses, raise the possibility that the contributions of personality dispositions and autobiographical memory to wise reasoning vary with age. This possibility warrants direct investigation using longitudinal designs. The observed age group differences also suggest potential directions for future research into age-tailored interventions aimed at enhancing wise reasoning. For example, interventions encouraging the strategic use of vivid autobiographical memories may warrant investigation among younger adults (cf. 97 ), while approaches designed to promote greater openness to experience and more exploration-based decision-making 49, 98 may be worth targeting among older adults (cf. 99 ).

Distinct Psychological Correlates of Self-Report Wisdom and Performance-Based Wise

Reasoning. The relationship between performance-based and self-report measures of wisdom remains an ongoing topic of debate (see, for example, 3, 4 ), and a detailed consideration of these measurement differences was beyond the scope of the present study. Our aim was specifically to examine whether self-report measures capture the correlates of wise reasoning as assessed using the original performance-based measure, the Berlin Wisdom Paradigm, given that performance-based measures are relatively resource-intensive and may therefore be impractical in non-research settings. Although we observed overlap in the correlates of performance-based and self-report measures, particularly for openness to experience and, to a lesser extent, for emotion recognition, the overall patterns exhibited striking differences. The cognitive and autobiographical memory characteristics associated with performance-based wise reasoning were not similarly associated with self-reported wisdom, which was instead related to self-reported personality and affective dispositions. These findings are consistent with the view that performance-based and self-report measures capture mostly complementary aspects of wisdom 3 . At the same time, they suggest that self-report measures do not capture important psychological correlates of wise reasoning, underscoring the value of performance-based assessment when the construct of interest is specifically the capacity to reason wisely. Making such assessments more practical and scalable is therefore an important direction for future research. Recent work demonstrates the potential of using natural language processing to automate the scoring of details in autobiographical recollections 100 and wisdom-related features in reflections on complex problems 101 , offering promising directions for reducing the resources required to administer and score performance-based measures.

Conclusions.

Our study demonstrates that individual differences in wise reasoning are best characterized by a multidomain constellation of covarying psychological factors, encompassing autobiographical memory characteristics, crystallised cognitive ability, openness to experience, and emotion recognition ability. We provide evidence that autobiographical memory retrieval is a previously unrecognized correlate of individual differences in wise reasoning, and that different aspects of autobiographical memory make dissociable contributions to wise reasoning. Through cross-sectional age group comparisons, we show that the psychological correlates of wise reasoning are largely consistent across younger and older adulthood, while identifying potential age-related differences in how specific autobiographical memory characteristics and personality dispositions contribute to wise reasoning. Together, these findings advance our understanding of the multidomain psychological foundations of wise reasoning across adulthood.

Methods

Participants

Data from 340 participants from a larger participant sample 102 were analyzed in the present study. Inclusion in the study required completion of the performance-based wisdom assessment and all measures within at least one of the six psychological domains of interest.

Ten participants were excluded due to signs of cognitive decline, including confabulation (n = 1), and low scores on the Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) or the NIH Fluid Cognition Composite (n = 7), or extreme scores on the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS > 20, indicative of severe depression, n = 2).

Participants included 179 younger adults (Mage = 22.41 years, SD = 3.31, range = 18-34; 55% female; 45% male) and 161 older adults (Mage = 69.38 years, SD = 6.5, range = 58-92; 57% female; 43% male). The study was granted ethical approval by the Institutional Review Boards of Cornell University and York University. Detailed recruitment information is provided in 102.

Measures

Wise Reasoning. We assessed participants' capacity for wise reasoning using the wellestablished Berlin Wisdom Paradigm (BWP) 103 . BWP is a performance-based measure of general wise reasoning, conceptualized as "expert-level performance in the fundamental pragmatics of life" 103 , where 'fundamental pragmatics' of life refers to the essential principles that govern human existence and interactions 103 . BWP operationalizes wise reasoning as the application of a rich repertoire of relevant knowledge when reviewing, managing and planning life matters. Specifically, wise reasoning is evaluated across five criteria: (i) Rich factual knowledge -general and domain-specific knowledge about human nature and life conditions; (ii) Rich procedural knowledge -knowledge of strategies and heuristics for dealing with life matters; (iii) Lifespan contextualism -consideration of life contexts and their developmental interrelations; (iv) Value relativism -awareness of the diversity and relativity of values and life goals; and (v) Uncertainty -awareness of life's inherent uncertainty and limits of knowledge.

Participants completed an examiner-led semi-structured interview in which they were provided with hypothetical scenarios and asked to verbalize their reasoning about the situation described. The scenarios involved fictional characters facing challenging and ambiguous life circumstances and were divided into two types: life review scenarios and life planning scenarios. Life review scenarios required a retrospective evaluation of a fictional character's life experiences, choices, and accomplishments, thereby engaging the capacity to reflect on and extract insight from lived experiences. Life planning scenarios engaged prospective reasoning about goal setting, decision making, and action planning in support of a fictional character's future-oriented goals. Participants completed one life review scenario and three life planning scenarios. The fictional characters were matched to the age group and gender of the participant. Interviews were audio-recorded, anonymized, and transcribed verbatim by trained research assistants.

Trained raters evaluated the transcribed interviews on each of the five dimensions of wise reasoning outlined above, assigning scores on a seven-point Likert scale. Two groups of English-speaking and neurologically healthy adults were recruited as raters, including 13 older adults (Mage = 67, SD = 3.1; 82% female, 18% male) and 19 younger adults (Mage = 24, SD = 2.2; 77% female; 23% male). All raters underwent a thorough training protocol prior to scoring (see Darboh, 2018) for a detailed description). Each rater scored an average of 109.88 transcripts (SD = 71.73) on a single dimension of wise reasoning, with the number of transcripts per rater ranging from 8 to 324 (median = 100). Each participant's verbal responses were rated by one younger and one older adult to account for potential age-related differences in the evaluation of wisdom characteristics. Although this balance was achieved for most participants, some received a third rating, with two of the three raters belonging to the same age group (6 participants for rich factual knowledge, 2 for lifespan contextualism, 9 for value relativism, and 8 for uncertainty).

One participant received only one rating, from a younger adult, for both value relativism and uncertainty. We retained all available ratings and participants to maximize data use and statistical power.

We assessed reliability at multiple levels. Internal consistency was examined separately for each rater and wisdom dimension using Cronbach's α, with scenarios treated as repeated indicators of the same construct. These analyses revealed moderate to high internal consistency across scenarios (Cronbach's α range = 0.62-0.91). Some variation in scores across scenarios is expected, given that the expression of wise reasoning can depend on the context 105 . To generate more reliable estimates, ratings were averaged across scenarios to obtain rater-specific scores for each wisdom dimension and participant. Inter-rater reliability was then assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC [3,k]), which indicated high reliability of the average ratings (ICC [3,k] range = 0.84-0.91). Ratings were subsequently averaged across raters for each rated dimension (min score = 1, max score = 6).

Finally, because there is little theoretical motivation or empirical evidence that the five dimensions should be related to distinct psychological factors, we used a composite wise reasoning measure computed as the average score across dimensions. Indeed, the scores on the five dimensions were moderately to strongly correlated (Spearman rho range = 0.46-0.64), suggesting that they capture a common underlying construct (Figure 4).

Statistical Analyses

To rigorously assess the relationships between wise reasoning and the psychological domains of interest, we employed a multi-pronged analytic approach. In addition to autobiographical memory, cognition, personality, affect, and social functioning, we examined education as a separate domain.

First, we conducted domain-specific analyses examining associations between variables within each psychological domain and wise reasoning scores. This approach allowed us to test theoretically motivated associations between specific domains and wise reasoning while maximizing statistical power given differences in the number of participants who completed measures across domains. Each analysis included all participants who had completed the relevant measures within that predictor domain, regardless of whether they had completed measures from other domains. Detailed descriptions of the samples included in each analysis are provided in Table S2.

The domain-stratified analyses proceeded in three stages. In the first stage, frequentist ordinary least squares regression models were used to: i) assess whether each domain explained variance in individual differences in wise reasoning beyond demographic covariates, ii) examine associations of individual predictors while accounting for others within the same domain, and iii) test whether these associations differed by age group. Then, cross-validated ridge regression was used to test how well each domain predicts wise reasoning scores in unseen data, and whether considering their interactions with age group improves predictive performance. Lastly, Bayes Factors were computed to quantify the strength of evidence for the association of each predictor domain with wise reasoning, and for age group differences in these associations. Because the above-mentioned analyses serve different inferential purposes, results were interpreted based on convergence across approaches rather than relying on any single statistical criterion (for a similar analytic approach, see 118 ). These analyses were first conducted across age groups and included an assessment of whether the correlates of wise reasoning differed between younger and older adults, with age group modeled as a categorical variable with two levels (younger adults, older adults). We then conducted robustness analyses separately for each age group to examine whether associations observed in the full sample were consistent across groups, and whether the strength of within-group associations varied as a function of continuous age.
