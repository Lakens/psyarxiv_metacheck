Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

Scripture rarely evaluates action apart from the heart that produces it. The Motivation of Moral Action (MoMA) framework represents a moral motivational state as four independent, continuous dimensions formed by crossing motivational direction (approach, avoid) with perceived moral valence (good, bad): Approach Good, Avoid Good, Approach Bad, and Avoid Bad. This article applies MoMA to biblical commands, the teachings of Jesus, parables, and the narratives of prophets, kings, warriors, and disciples. I argue that biblical moral discourse consistently highlights the distinctions MoMA was built to preserve: reluctance versus temptation, conflict versus indifference, identical acts done from different hearts, and repentance as a change in what is perceived as good. The analysis yields integrative contributions including a representation of Gethsemane as conflict without sin, a distinction between sanctification and metanoia, a reading of divine pedagogy as dimensionspecific intervention, and testable predictions for counseling, formation, and the psychology of religion.

Introduction

When Samuel goes to Bethlehem to anoint a king, he is impressed by Eliab's stature and assumes he has found the Lord's choice. The correction he receives is one of the most quoted lines in the Hebrew Bible: "man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart" (1 Sam 16:7, English Standard Version Bible, 2001 Bible, /2016)). The verse states, in theological idiom, a claim that moral psychology has spent decades establishing empirically. Moral evaluation is inferential and goes beyond surface-level observation of behavior. People judge actions by the motives they believe produced them, often more than by the actions themselves (Cushman, 2008; Reeder, 2009). Two people who do the same thing can be judged very differently. Two people who do different things can be judged alike if their motives seem alike. An individual's internal state matters greatly.

Scripture makes this claim repeatedly and in many genres. The Decalogue ends by forbidding not an act but a desire: "You shall not covet" (Exod 20:17). Jesus locates adultery in the look and murder in contempt . He warns that alms, prayer, and fasting done "to be seen" receive no reward from the Father (Matt 6:1-18). Paul insists that giving everything away without love profits nothing (1 Cor 13:3). He also asks that giving be done "not reluctantly or under compulsion" (2 Cor 9:7). The biblical writers did not use the modern jargon of motivation science, but they were consistently concerned with the structure of the motivated heart.

Modern moral psychology has identified many moral motives, such as empathy, fairness, loyalty, purity, and moral identity (Batson, 2011; Graham et al., 2013; Aquino & Reed, 2002). However, it has given less attention to how those motives combine into a single motivational state (McKee et al., 2026). The Motivation of Moral Action (MoMA) framework was developed to fill that gap.

MoMA represents a person's moral motivation as a location in a four-dimensional space. The space is defined by crossing motivational direction (approach versus avoid) with perceived moral valence (good versus bad). The resulting dimensions are Approach Good (ApG), Avoid Good (AvG), Approach Bad (ApB), and Avoid Bad (AvB). Each is continuous and independent of the others. A person may be strongly drawn toward a duty and strongly drawn away from it at the same time. A person may recognize an act as wrong but nonetheless still want to do it. This article brings MoMA into conversation with Scripture. My aim is not to prove the Bible by psychology or psychology by the Bible. Rather, it is to show that the two literatures illuminate one another in representing the moral heart. I pursue three goals in this article. First, I show that biblical commands, teachings, parables, and narratives consistently highlight distinctions that MoMA identifies and that alternative single-dimension models of moral motivation cannot reconcile. Second, I argue that MoMA gives Christian theology and pastoral practice a precise vocabulary for distinctions it already cares about, such as temptation versus sin, reluctance versus rebellion, sanctification versus repentance, and others. Third, I outline testable predictions for counseling, spiritual formation, and the psychology of religion.

To those ends, this article proceeds in four parts. I first summarize MoMA and state its hermeneutical and integrative commitments. I then read five bodies of biblical material through the framework: divine commands, the teachings of Jesus, parables, the prophets, and the narratives of warriors, kings, and disciples, followed by Pauline moral anthropology. I then highlight four theoretical contributions before closing with applications and limitations.

The Motivation of Moral Action (MoMA) Framework Motives, Motivation, and Behavior

MoMA distinguishes three levels of analysis (McKee et al., 2026a). Moral motives are particular reasons, such as compassion, duty, loyalty, fear of punishment, revenge, or self-interest. Moral motivation is the overall subjective state produced by one or more active motives. Moral behavior is what a person actually does with that subjective state given a situation's opportunities and constraints. MoMA is a model focused on the middle level. It makes no descriptive claims about which motives matter or which actions are normatively right. Instead, it specifies how any and all motives combine into a state that can be described, compared, and tracked over time.

Four Independent Dimensions

MoMA crosses two distinctions that are well established in psychology. The first is motivational direction-motivation moves either toward desired states or away from undesired ones (Carver & White, 1994; Elliot, 2006; Higgins, 1997). The second is perceived moral valence-people distinguish actions they regard as morally good from actions they regard as morally bad (Janoff-Bulman et al., 2009). Crossing them yields four dimensions (Table 1). A moral motivational state is therefore a vector, M = (ApG, AvG, ApB, AvB), defined for a particular person at a given moment. Two summary quantities result.

Intensity is the sum of the four components (i.e., how much total motivational pull this person is experiencing, agnostic to direction). Conflict is the degree to which opposing dimensions are active together (i.e., to what degree are there pulls in competing directions): the smaller of ApG and AvG plus the smaller of AvB and ApB. Intensity and conflict are independent. A person can be intensely and wholeheartedly motivated, or intensely and painfully divided.

Reading Scripture With a Motivational Architecture Integrative Stance

Christian psychologists hold several models of how psychology and theology relate (Johnson, 2010).

MoMA aligns with them because it is an architecture, not a restrictive moral theory. MoMA does not say what is good and bad. It accommodates what the agent takes to be good and bad. This leaves the normative question for Christian theology with God's character and revealed will. Theology supplies the content of moral valence. MoMA supplies a way of representing how a person's motives are arranged relative to that context. The relationship, therefore, is complementary rather than competitive. Each discipline answers a question the other isn't asking. This division of labor yields a distinction that will matter throughout. Perceived valence is how the agent evaluates an action. Normative valence is how the action stands before God. The two can come apart. Saul perceived sparing the best livestock as good, a planned sacrifice; Samuel declared it disobedience (1 Sam 15:20-23). MoMA codes Saul's state by his own perception, which is what makes his self-justification intelligible. Scripture locates self-deception, conviction, and repentance in the gap between perceived and normative valence. Hermeneutical Cautions Keep four cautions in mind as you read. First, I make no claim that the biblical authors held anything like MoMA in mind while writing. My claim is narrower in that I identify places where the texts attend to distinctions that MoMA preserves. Second, biblical narrative is famously sparing about inner states. Auerbach & Said (2013) described the Genesis narratives as "fraught with background," and later literary critics have shown how Hebrew narrative withholds motive and invites the reader to infer it (Alter, 2011; Sternberg, 1987).

Where a text is silent about motives, MoMA's profiles are just reasonable interpretive hypotheses. Third, ancient texts do not share our modern introspective habits. Stendahl (1963) warned that

Western readers since Augustine have read Paul's conscience through their own. I try to ground claims in what texts say about desire, fear, and refusal rather than in imagined psychological detail. Fourth, I read the Hebrew Bible as Christian Scripture while acknowledging that it is also the Scripture of Judaism and has its own rich, and often divergent, interpretive traditions.

The second caution, however, is also an opportunity. Because biblical narrative leaves motives implicit, reading it requires the exact kind of motive inference that moral psychology studies (Malle & Hollbrook, 2012). Readers of the binding of Isaac, of Jonah's flight, or of Judas's betrayal have argued for centuries about why these figures acted as they did. MoMA is not positioned to settle those debates. But it can make them more precise by specifying which motivational configuration each reading proposes. It also makes biblical narratives a natural corpus for empirical work on moral motive inference.

Moral motivation inference from narratives is not just a hypothesis. When ordinary readers evaluated real-world narratives that described a difficult moral decision without stating the agent's motives, they reliably inferred all four MoMA dimensions, and the inferred motives predicted their moral judgments (McKee et al., 2026b). Biblical narrative sets readers the same task, albeit in a more extreme form.

The findings suggest that the motive inferences readers have long made about biblical characters draw on the same structured, four-dimensional framework.

Divine Commands: What the Law Addresses Prescription, Proscription, and the Heart

The Decalogue is mostly proscriptive. Eight of its ten words are prohibitions; two (sabbath and honoring parents) are positive commands (Exod 20:1-17). In MoMA terms, the prohibitions primarily target Avoid Bad, and the positive commands target Approach Good. This matches the distinction between proscriptive and prescriptive morality that MoMA extends (Janoff-Bulman et al., 2009).

Proscriptive morality is inhibitory and focused on restraining harm. Prescriptive morality is activating and focused on doing good.

The tenth commandment, however, does something neither category anticipates. "You shall not covet" (Exod 20:17) addresses neither a physical act to perform nor to refrain from. It addresses a subjective motivational state.

Covetousness is Approach Bad directed at what belongs to a neighbor, whether or not any act follows. The Law thus treats Approach Bad as morally significant in itself, even with zero resulting behavior. This anticipates experimental findings that people judge the mere desire to harm as wrong (Cushman, 2008).

The Torah also commands a positive orientation of the heart.

"You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might" (Deut 6:5). "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Lev 19:18). Jesus identifies these as the two greatest commandments and says that all the Law and the Prophets hang on them (Matt 22:37-40). In MoMA terms, these great commandments call for maximal Approach Good directed at both God and neighbor. They are not fulfilled with Avoid Bad alone. A person who scrupulously avoids every prohibited act but feels no pull toward the neighbor's good has faithfully kept the letter of many commands while nonetheless missing their center. Negative and Positive Forms of the Same Rule A well-known contrast illustrates the point. The Babylonian Talmud records Hillel summarizing the entirety of the Torah as "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor" (b. Shabbat 31a; i.e., the Silver Rule). Jesus gives the rule in positive form: "whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them" (Matt 7:12; i.e., the Golden Rule). The difference has often been overdrawn, and sometimes used polemically. Hillel immediately adds that the rest of the Torah is commentary and should still be studied, and the positive love command is already in Leviticus. What MoMA clarifies is that the two forms target different dimensions. The negative form can be satisfied entirely by Avoid Bad. The positive form requires Approach Good. Both are needed because the dimensions are not independent. Strength in one does not supply the other. Not doing wrong to others is not doing right to them. Saul: Incomplete Obedience and Perceived Valence First Samuel 15 shows how commands interact with a divided heart. Saul is told to destroy the Amalekites and their property completely. He spares King Agag and the best of the livestock. Confronted, he first claims success, then says the people kept the animals to sacrifice to God (vv. 13-21). Samuel answers with a principle about motive: "to obey is better than sacrifice" (v. 22). Saul then gives a different account: "I feared the people and obeyed their voice" (v. 24). The text does not ask modern readers to accept the ethics of the ban (herem), which I address later as a hard case. Its focus is Saul's heart. Saul's two explanations correspond to two MoMA configurations. In the first, Saul presents the sparing as Approach Good, a pious act. In the second, he admits Avoid Good driven by social fear. He knew what he had been commanded and shrank from enforcing it against his troops' wishes. The narrative also hints at Approach Bad in the taking of plunder (v. 19). This story shows why perceived valence matters. Saul's self-justification worked by relabeling his motives as Approach Good. Samuel's rebuke worked by refusing that relabeling. The prophet's role here was to close the gap between perceived and normative valence. The Teachings and Example of Jesus The Sermon on the Mount: Evaluating the State, Not Only the Act The six antitheses of Matthew 5:21-48 move moral evaluation from behavior to motivation. Murder is extended to contempt and anger (vv. 21-22). Adultery is extended to the lustful gaze: "everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (v. 28). In MoMA terms, Jesus treats elevated Approach Bad as morally weighty even when Avoid Bad successfully prevents the act. A man who desires another's wife but refrains behaves the same way as a man with no such desire. However, their motivational states differ, and Jesus says the difference matters before God. This is the clearest biblical case of what McKee et al. (2026) call representational compression. A behavioral record or a single "how moral is this person" assigns the tempted man and the untempted man the same value.

The antitheses insist on separating them. Note, however, that Jesus does not say the two are morally identical. The teaching is better read as exposing a dimension that a purely conduct-based reading of the commandments leaves unrepresented, rather than erasing the difference between desire and deed.

Matthew 6: Identical Acts, Different Hearts

Matthew 6:1-18 applied one pattern to three practices: almsgiving, prayer, and fasting. In each, Jesus contrasts two people performing the same act. One acts "to be seen by them" (v. 1); the other acts in secret before the Father. The behavior is identical in both cases, but again the motivational states differ. The first person's Approach Good is mixed with, or replaced by, a motive of reputation. Jesus' verdict is that such people "have received their reward" already (vv. 2, 5, 16).

This teaching parallels a classic distinction in the psychology of religion. Allport and Ross (1967) distinguished intrinsic religious orientation, in which faith is lived as an end in itself, from extrinsic orientation, in which religion serves other ends such as status or comfort. MoMA adds that the difference need not be a single trait.

The same person's motivation for a given act of giving can combine intrinsic Approach Good with a reputational motive, in proportions that shift by occasion.

Gethsemane: Conflict Without Sin

The most theologically significant application concerns Jesus himself. In Gethsemane, Jesus says his soul is "very sorrowful, even to death" (Mark 14:34). He prays, "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39). Luke adds that his sweat became like great drops of blood (Luke 22:44), though I acknowledge the verse's textual status is disputed.

Hebrews says that he "learned obedience through what he suffered" (Heb 5:8).

MoMA represents this scene with incredible precision. Jesus' Approach

Good, directed at fulfilling the Father's will, is maximal. His Avoid Good is also high as he recoils from the suffering that obedience requires, and asks whether it can be avoided. His Approach Bad is absent. Nothing in this text suggests a pull toward an act he regards as wrong (or anywhere else in the Bible). The cup is not evil to be shunned; it is a good to be accepted at terrible personal cost. Gethsemane is therefore reluctance under conflict and not temptation.

This distinction matches a central line of classical Christology. Maximus the Confessor argued, and the Third Council of Constantinople (681) affirmed, that Christ had a genuine human will. That human will naturally shrank from death, yet it was never opposed to the divine will (Bathrellos, 2004). On this account, Christ's recoil from death was not sin but the natural response of human nature that he took on alongside his divine nature. A simpler model of moral motivation cannot adequately express this, and any motivation away from obedience would lower his position, making Gethsemane a moral lapse-an impossibility for a perfect Christ. MoMA allows high Avoid Good to coexist with maximal Approach Good and zero Approach Bad. That is the moral configuration the tradition has always insisted on, but lacked terms for.

The Wilderness Temptation and The Question of Approach Bad

The temptation narratives (Matt 4:1-11; Luke 4:1-13) raise a related question.

Hebrews says Jesus "in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin" (Heb 4:15). Theologians have long debated whether Christ could have sinned, and what his temptation consisted of (Crisp, 2007). MoMA does not dare try to resolve this debate, but it does allow it to be stated cleanly. One position holds that temptations were presented to Jesus externally, with Approach Bad remaining at zero.

A second holds that Jesus felt a real pull toward the offered goods (bread when starving, for example), but that the pull was toward things not wrong in themselves, which would code as approach toward a perceived good under the wrong conditions.

A third allows genuine Approach Bad that was always overcome. Each position specifies a different profile. The framework turns a debate often conducted in abstract terms into a choice among explicit motivational configurations.

Parables: Narratives That Act on the Hearer's Motivation

Parables are not only illustrations. They are, in Snodgrass's (2008) phrase, "stories with intent," designed to provoke a response. Several of the best-known parables work by exposing or changing the hearer's motivational state. MoMA helps specify which dimension each one targets.

The Good Samaritan: Avoid Good and Moral Wiggle Room

The parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) begins with a lawyer who already knows the right answer: love God and neighbor. Luke then adds that he spoke "desiring to justify himself" and asked, "And who is my neighbor? " (v. 29). This is a request to narrow the scope of an acknowledged obligation. It resembles what behavioral economists call exploiting moral wiggle room, or arranging one's knowledge so that a moral demand does not apply (Dana, Weber, & Kuang, 2007).

McKee et al. (2026) cite this behavior as evidence for Avoid Good. The person recognizes the good and is motivated to avoid being bound by it. Within the parable, a priest and a Levite see the wounded man and pass by on the other side (vv. 31-32). Their behavior is identical. The text gives no motives, but interpreters have proposed several, including fear of robbers, ritual purity concerns, and simple haste. MoMA clarifies that these are different motivation profiles. Fear and haste are Avoid Good driven by personal cost. A purity concern, if the priest perceived contact with a possible dead body as wrong for him, would be Avoid Bad in conflict with Approach Good. The parable's silence leaves the reader to supply a motive, and the reader's choice says something about the reader.

A famous experiment tested the parable directly. Darley and Batson (1973) asked seminary students to prepare a talk, some on the Good Samaritan, and then walk to another building. On the way, the students passed a man slumped in a doorway. Students who had been told they were late were far less likely to stop than those who had time. The topic of the talk made little difference. In MoMA terms, lateness increased Avoid Good by raising the cost of helping. It did not need to reduce the students' Approach Good, which their vocation presumably kept high. The study is usually read as showing that situations overpower character. I suggest a more nuanced reading through the lens of MoMA-the situation moved one dimension, not the whole of moral motivation.

Jesus ends the parable by changing the question. The lawyer asked whom he must consider a neighbor. Jesus asks which of the three "proved to be a neighbor" to the wounded man (v. 36), and we all know the answer. Moved by compassion (v. 33), the Samaritan displays strong Approach Good that overrides considerable cost (i.e., Avoid Good). "You go, and do likewise" (v. 37) is an explicit call to this motivational configuration.

The Two Sons: Stated Intention, Enacted Motivation, and Change

In Matthew 21:28-32, a father asks two sons to work in his vineyard. The first says he will not, but later changes his mind and goes. The second says he will go, but does not. Jesus asks which of them did his father's will, and the answer is clearly the first.

The parable separates what a person says from the motivational state that drives what they do. It also depicts motivational change over time. The first son begins with high Avoid Good and then moves; the verb describes regret (metamelomai). MoMA represents this as a trajectory through the state space, not a change of character type per se (McKee et al., 2026). Jesus applies the parable to tax collectors and prostitutes who believed John the Baptist, contrasting them with religious leaders who did not (vv. 31-32). The application concerns repentance, which I argue below is best represented as a change in perceived valence.

The Prodigal Son and the Elder Brother: Reevaluation and Refusal

The parable of the lost son (Luke 15:11-32) contains three motivational portraits. The younger son leaves with his inheritance and wastes it. In the far country, "he came to himself" (v. 17) and resolved to return. The phrase marks a change in how he sees his situation and his father. At first, his return is driven partly by hunger, but his rehearsed speech is modest in its hopes (vv. 18-19). Mixed motives do not disqualify the return. The father runs to him before the speech is finished (vv. McCullough et al. (1998) showed that motivations toward an offender are best measured as separate dimensions: avoidance and revenge, later joined by benevolence (McCullough, Root, & Cohen, 2006). A person can be high on benevolence and on avoidance at once. This same separability logic applies to moral motivation in general.

Nathan's Parable: Changing Perceived Valence From the Outside

The Hebrew Bible's most effective parable is aimed at a single hearer. After David takes Bathsheba and arranges Uriah's death, the prophet Nathan tells him about a rich man who took a poor man's only ewe lamb to feed a guest (2 Sam 12:1-4).

David is furious and declares that the man deserves to die (v. 5). Nathan answers, "You are the man" (v. 7). David responds, "I have sinned against the LORD" (v. 13).

The parable separates judgment from desire. When David evaluates his own conduct, his strong Approach Bad toward Bathsheba, and his interest in concealment can distort how he perceives the act. When he evaluates a stranger's identical structure of conduct, those motives are absent, and his judgment is clear. Nathan then transfers that judgment back to David. In MoMA terms, the parable is an intervention that raises the perceived wrongness of an act.

The Prophets: Reluctance, Compulsion, Exhaustion, and Dissent

The prophetic call narratives form a remarkable body of evidence for MoMAand particularly Avoid Good. Prophets are frequently portrayed as resisting a commission they do not doubt is from God and do not regard as wrong. How God responds to that resistance is equally instructive.

Moses: Reluctance Met With Reduced Cost

At the burning bush, Moses raised five objections in succession (Exod 3:11-4:13). Who is he to go to Pharaoh? What is God's name? What if the people do not believe him? He is not eloquent. Finally, he simply asks God to send someone else. None of these objections claims that delivering Israel is wrong. Each expresses inadequacy, fear, or cost. Moses' state is a clear case of reluctance where Approach Good is present (he does not walk away), and Avoid Good is high.

God's responses are as telling as the objections. To inadequacy, God answers with presence: "But I will be with you" (3:12). To fear of unbelief, God provides signs (4:2-9). To the complaint about speech, God promises help with his mouth (4:11-12). When Moses still refuses, God grows angry, yet appoints Aaron to speak for him (4:14-16). Each response lowers the perceived cost or raises the perceived efficacy of obedience. None of them argues that the task is good, because Moses never disputed that. In MoMA terms, God targets Avoid Good, not Approach Good.

This is precisely the intervention MoMA predicts will be effective for reluctance (McKee et al., 2026).

Elijah: Exhaustion and the Pastoral Response

After his triumph on Mount Carmel, Elijah flees from Jezebel's death threat.

Alone in the wilderness, he sits under a broom tree and asks to die: "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life" (1 Kgs 19:4). His Avoid Good is now overwhelming, driven by fear, exhaustion, and a belief that he alone remains faithful (19:10).

God's first response is not a rebuke or command. An angel wakes him twice and tells him to eat, and he sleeps between meals (19:5-8).

Only after rest and food does God meet him at Horeb and recommission him. God also corrects his belief that he is alone-seven thousand in Israel have not bowed to Baal (19:18). And God provides Elisha as a companion and successor (19:16-21). Each step addresses a source of Avoid Good: depletion, isolation, and the felt burden of sole responsibility. The sequence is a pastoral template. It suggests that reluctance rooted in exhaustion is first met with care for the body and companionship, not exhortation. Readers have long noted the parallels between Elijah's state and modern descriptions of burnout and depression. I do not diagnose the prophet, but the parallel strengthens the clinical relevance of the pattern. Jonah: When Perceived Valence Diverges From God's Jonah is the prophet whose case most requires MoMA's emphasis on perceived valence. Commanded to preach against Nineveh, the Assyrian capital, he boards a ship in the opposite direction (Jonah 1:3). Only at the end of the book does he explain why: "I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and relenting from disaster" (4:2). Jonah did not flee because he feared failure. He fled because he feared success and the sparing of Israel's enemy. Jonah's flight is therefore not Avoid Good in his own perception. He did not regard Nineveh's deliverance as good. By his valuation, the sparing of a violent empire was something to avoid bringing about, which gives his flight the structure of Avoid Bad. God's valuation differs. MoMA's insistence on coding perceived valence makes Jonah's motivation intelligible without making it right. It also identifies the book's real subject. The narrative is less about Jonah's obedience than about the gap between Jonah's valuation and God's. The book ends, like the parable of the elder brother, with God's unanswered question about whether he should not pity the great city (4:11). Both texts end with an invitation to revalue.

Warriors, Rulers, and Disciples

Gideon and Esther: Courage as Approach Good Under High Avoid Good

When the angel finds Gideon, he is threshing wheat in a winepress to hide it from the Midianites (Judg 6:11). He objects that his clan is the weakest and he is the least in his family (6:15). When he obeys the command to tear down his father's altar to Baal, he does it at night, "because he was too afraid of his family and the men of the town to do it by day" (6:27). He then asks for two signs with a fleece (6:36-40).

Gideon obeys throughout, but his Avoid Good never disappears. He adapts his obedience to it. God's responses again target fear rather than conviction. Before the battle, God tells him, "if you are afraid to go down," to take his servant and overhear the enemy camp, where he hears a dream foretelling Midian's defeat (7:10-15). The narrative portrays courage not as the absence of fear but as Approach Good sufficient to act while Avoid Good remains high.

Esther faces a similar choice. Mordecai asks her to plead with the king for her people. She replies that anyone who approaches the king unsummoned may be put to death (Esth 4:11). Mordecai's answer works on several fronts at once. He warns that silence will not save her, and he asks, "who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (4:14). The first appeal raises the cost of inaction.

The second strengthens Approach Good by giving the act meaning and a providential frame. Esther responds by asking the Jews of Sus to fast with her, and declares, "if I perish, I perish" (4:16). Communal fasting adds social support to the decision. The resulting state is high Approach Good, still-high Avoid Good, and action.

Joseph: Avoid Bad Grounded in Relationship

Joseph's refusal of Potiphar's wife is the Hebrew Bible's paradigm of Avoid Bad. His stated reasons are relational: his master's trust and God. "How then can I do this great wickedness and sin against God?" (Gen 39:9). The pressure is daily (39:10), and when she seizes his garment, he flees, leaving it in her hand (39:12). Flight is avoidance in its most literal behavioral form. The text says nothing about whether Joseph felt any attraction. Jewish and Christian interpreters have filled this gap in opposite ways. Some portray Joseph as untempted; others imagine a hard-won struggle. MoMA shows that these are different moral configurations resulting in the same behavior: high Avoid Bad with low Approach Bad, versus high Avoid Bad overcoming high Approach Bad. The distinction matters for how Joseph is used as a moral exemplar. A Joseph who felt no pull represents a settled character. A Joseph who resisted a strong pull models selfcontrol. Both readings are possible, and each yields a different lesson. Joseph's later reconciliation with his brothers adds a further example. He tests them at length before revealing himself (Gen 42-44), weeps repeatedly, and finally says, "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (50:20). Interpreters disagree about whether the tests express lingering resentment, a desire to know whether his brothers have changed, or both. The forgiveness literature's separable dimensions of revenge, avoidance, and benevolence ( McCullough et al., 1998 McCullough et al., , 2006) ) allow each reading to be stated precisely. Joseph may have been high on benevolence and avoidance at once for a long time before the avoidance resolved.

Achan and David: The Anatomy of Approach Bad

Achan, a soldier at Jericho, took forbidden punder and hid it. His confession gives a compact sequence: "when I saw among the spoil a beautiful cloak from Shinar… then I coveted them and took them" (Josh 7:21). David's sin follows the same order: he saw Bathsheba, sent for her, and took her (2 Sam 11:2-4). James later generalizes the pattern: "each person is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desire" (Jas 1:14). Desire, once conceived, gives birth to sin (1:15). These texts describe Approach Bad rising from perception through desire to action. They also identify the early phase of seeing and desiring as the point for intervention. This matches MoMA's claim that motivational states should be distinguished from the behavior they might eventually produce (McKee et al., 2026).

Peter and Judas: Two Trajectories After Failure

Peter promises, "Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away" (Matt 26:33). Hours later, he denies Jesus three times (26:69-74). His Approach Good is sincere, but in the high priest's courtyard, fear raises his Avoid Good above it. The rooster crows, and he "wept bitterly" (26:75). The weeping again marks conflict rather than indifference. After the resurrection, Jesus asks him three times whether her loves him and three times recommissions him (John 21:15-17). The threefold restoration mirrors the threefold denial. MoMA represents this as a trajectory where the state changes over time while the person and the framework remain the same.

Judas offers a contrast. John's Gospel says he was a thief who stole from the common purse (John 12:6), indicating Approach Bad toward money. After the betrayal, Matthew says he "changed his mind" and returned the silver (Matt 27:3).

The verb is metamelomai, the same verb used of the first son in the parable of the two sons, who "changed his mind and went" (Matt 21:29). Both Peter and Judas regretted.

Their trajectories diverged afterward. I do not presume to explain why, but the comparison shows that a regretful state is not an endpoint. What follows depends on further movement through the moral motivational space, and the Gospels place restoration at the center of that movement.

Hard Cases: Abraham and the Conquest Narratives

Any honest reading must face texts in which God commands acts that readers perceive as gravely wrong.

The binding of Isaac (Gen 22) is the classic case and was Auerbach & Said's (2013) central example of narrative silence about inner states. The text says nothing about what Abraham felt. Kierkegaard (2013) made that silence the subject of Fear and Trembling. At least three readings are available, and MoMA states each as a distinct profile. On the first, Abraham perceives obedience as good and is torn by love for Isaac: very high Approach Good and very high Avoid Good. On the second, Abraham perceives the act as both commanded and wrong: high Approach Good toward obedience in conflict with high Avoid Bad toward killing his son. On the third, Abraham's statement that "God will provide for himself the lamb" (Gen 22:8) and Hebrews 11:19 lead him to trust that God will preserve Isaac, and the conflict is lower than either alternative suggests. MoMA does not choose among those readings. It makes clear that they are psychologically different claims. The conquest narratives, including commands to devote whole populations to destruction (Deut 7:1-2; Josh 6:21; 1 Sam 15:3), raise moral and theological questions that Christian scholars answer in sharply different ways (Copan & Flannagain, 2014; Seibert, 2009).

MoMA is not designed to adjudicate those questions, and I do not attempt to. Because valence is perceived, MoMA can describe the motivational states of the narratives' agents as the texts present them, without endorsing the acts. It can also identify motivational content the texts themselves condemn, such as Achan's covetousness and Saul's fear and appetite for plunder. Readers who find these texts troubling and readers who defend them can use the same framework to say what they are reading. Pauline Moral Anthropology and Its Augustinian Reception Romans 7: The Locus Classicus of Approach Bad No biblical text describes Approach Bad more directly than Romans 7. "For I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate" (Rom 7:15). "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing" (7:19). The speaker approves the good (7:16, 22) and is drawn toward the evil he hates. In MoMA terms, Approach Good and Approach Bad are both high, and Approach Bad often wins. This is temptation as McKee et al. (2026) define it and not reluctance. The speaker is not shrinking from a costly good.

He is pulled by a perceived wrong.

The identity of the "I" in Romans 7 is one of the most contested questions in New Testament scholarship. Interpreters disagree about whether Paul describes his pre-Christian life, his ongoing struggle as a believer, Israel under the Law, or a rhetorical figure (Moo, 1996). Stendahl (1963) argued that readers have read the chapter too introspectively since Augustine. Again, MoMA need not settle these debates. On any reading, the text depicts a state in which a person perceives an act as wrong and is drawn toward it anyway. That the text depicts such a state at all supports the psychological reality of Approach Bad. The independence of the dimesnions also explains the passage's peculiar anguish. If moral motivation lay on one continuum, approving the good and wanting the evil would cancel each other out. Paul's "I", however, experiences them as simultaneous.

Galatians 5:17 describes the same structure in communal and pneumatological terms-the desires of the flesh are against the Spirit and the desire of the Spirit against the flesh, "to keep you from doing the things you want to do."

The fruit of the Spirit (5:22-24) is not simply the suppression of fleshly desire. It names positive orientations-love, joy, peace, and kindness-that correspond to Approach Good. Paul's ethic requires both reducing Approach Bad and growing Approach Good. Because these are independent dimensions, progress on one does not guarantee progress on the other. The Cheerful Giver: Reluctance as a Distinct Moral Category Paul's instructions about the collection for Jerusalem make a distinction that no simpler model of moral motivation can represent adequately. "Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Cor 9:7). The ESV's adverb is apt as Paul names reluctance itself. The reluctant giver and the cheerful giver perform the same act. Both have Approach Good sufficient to give. The critical difference is Avoid Good, where the reluctant giver feels a strong pull away from the gift, while the cheerful giver does not. Paul makes a parallel request in Philemon 14, asking that Philemon's goodness "might not be by compulsion but of your own accord." Paul does not call the reluctant giver wicked. He calls the cheerful giver beloved of God. In MoMA terms, Scripture treats Avoid Good as morally relevant, worth reducing, but categorically different from Approach Bad.

Ordinary moral judgment draws the same distinction. When human raters inferred Avoid Good in an agent, they judged the agent more blameworthy. When they inferred Approach Bad, the increase in blame was nearly twice as large (McKee et al., 2026). Lay observers, like Paul, treat a pull away from the good as a real moral failing, but a lesser one than a pull toward the bad.

First Corinthians 13:3 makes a related point about motive. Giving away all one has and delivering up one's body to be burned, "but have not love," gains nothing. The act is maximal by definition-giving up everything one has, even your own physical body-but the motivation is wrong. Combined with Matthew 6, these texts show that the New Testament consistently separates motivation from behavior.

Perfect Love Casts Out Fear: Changing the Motives Behind a Dimension

First John 4:18 says that "perfect love casts out fear. For fear has to do with punishment." The verse describes a change not in direction but in the motives that feed a direction. Avoid Bad can be driven by fear of punishment or by love for the person one would wrong. Joseph's refusal (Gen 39:9) is driven by loyalty to his master and to God. MoMA's formal structure accommodates this. Each dimension draws on many motives, and the same dimension can be fed by different motives at different times (McKee et al., 2026a). On this reading, Christian formation includes not only changing how strongly one avoids sin but also why. Moving from feardriven to love-driven Avoid Bad may leave behavior unchanged while transforming the person's relationship with God.

Augustine: The Pear Theft and the Divided Will

Augustine's Confessions gave Western Christianity its most influential account of Approach Bad and moral conflict. In Book 2, he recounts stealing pears as an adolescent. He was not hungry, he had better pears at home, and he threw most of the fruit to pigs. He concludes that he loved the wrongdoing itself, not the fruit (Conf.

2.4.9). Attraction to an act because it is forbidden is Approach Bad in its purest form.

Over 1,500 years later, Ruedy et al. (2013) found that people who cheat often feel better afterward than they predicted, and McKee et al. (2026a) cite this finding as evidence that perceived wrongdoing can carry positive incentive value. Augustine diagnosed the same phenomenon through self-examination. In Book 8, Augustine describes his inability to commit fully to the life he already believed was right. He speaks of two wills in himself, one old and one new, at war (Conf. 8.5.10). He wanted to change and also did not want to. MoMA can represent this state as high Approach Good toward conversion, high Avoid Good toward the cost of giving up his former life, and Approach Bad toward the habits he could not yet leave. Augustine's account of the divided will anticipates the ambivalence-indifference problem by centuries. He knew that being torn is not the same as not caring. Religion and Self-Regulation Empirical work suggests that religious commitment strengthens self-control (McCullough & Willoughby, 2009). MoMA raises a question this literature has not asked: through which moral motivational dimension does religion work? Religion might strengthen Avoid Bad, through moral norms and accountability. It might reduce Approach Bad, through practices that reshape desire. It might strengthen Approach Good, through love and purpose. It might reduce Avoid Good, through community support that lowers the cost of good acts.

The biblical texts reviewed here imply that all four are in view. They also imply that the practices targeting each are different, an empirical claim that can be tested.

Theoretical Contributions

Table 2 gathers the major cases into illustrative profiles. Four contributions follow from the readings above, two for psychology and two for theology. Long Witness to Four Dimensions The two MoMA dimensions most likely to draw skepticism are Avoid Good and Approach Bad (McKee et al., 2026a). Scripture gives both sustained attention. Avoid Good appears in nearly every prophetic call, in the rich young ruler, in Peter's denial, and in Paul's reluctant giver. Approach Bad appears in the tenth commandment, in Achan and David, in Romans 7, in James 1, and in Augustine's pear theft. Biblical moral discourse does not confine itself to approaching good and avoiding evil. It repeatedly identifies all four moral motivations MoMA defines. This is not empirical evidence in the sense of a controlled study. Rather, it is a historical and phenomenological witness. A moral tradition that has shaped billions of people over three millennia has found it necessary to describe reluctance and temptation as distinct states. That convergence supports the claim that these dimensions are part of ordinary human moral experience, and not artifacts of laboratory paradigms, or simply modern manifestations. It also suggests that a model lacking these dimensions would fail to represent the moral experience of religious people, a population that psychology has often understudied.

This witness now has an empirical counterpart. In real-world narratives, ordinary observers inferred Avoid Good and Approach Bad in a majority of evaluations (McKee et al., 2026b). Human raters also linked these two dimensions more strongly than any other pair, treating active wrongdoing and the deliberate withholding of good as frequent companions, but clearly distinct from one another.

Scripture pairs them the same way. James warns that "whoever knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, for him it is sin" (Jas 4:17), naming Avoid Good as a sin alongside the sins of commission the letter condemns elsewhere.

Contribution 2: Reluctance and Temptation Are Different States

Gethsemane and Romans 7 are both scenes of intense moral conflict. In a single-dimension model, they are hard to tell apart because each shows a person pulled away from doing what they judge right. MoMA separates them. Gethsemane combines maximal Approach Good with high Avoid Good and no Approach Bad.

Romans 7 combines Approach Good with high Approach Bad. The first is reluctance; the second is temptation.

The distinction has important theological weight. It allows Christ's agony to be real without being sinful, as the dyothelite tradition requires (Bathrellos, 2004). It also has pastoral weight. Believers who feel reluctant toward costly obedience may conclude they are sinning, even though Scripture's own exemplar of obedience felt the same recoil. Conversely, believers who experience temptation need not conclude that their faith is false, since Paul describes the same conflict. MoMA gives counselors and pastors a vocabulary for helping people describe their experience accurately.

Contribution 3: Sanctification and Metanoia as Two Kinds of Moral Change

MoMA's formal structure distinguishes two ways a moral motivational state can change. In the first, the person's evaluations stay fixed, and the strengths of the dimensions shift. Approach Bad weakens, Approach Good grows, Avoid Good diminishes, or the motives feeding a dimension change, as when love replaces fear (1

John 4:18). I propose that this corresponds to what Christian theology calls sanctification, or the gradual reshaping of desire within a settled commitment. In the second, the person's perceived valence changes. An act once seen as good is now seen as wrong, or the reverse. Formally, a motive's contribution moves from one valence column to the other: Saul's sparing, which he coded as Approach Good, becomes Approach Bad once he accepts Samuel's verdict. David's desire, which he had rationalized, becomes recognized sin after Nathan's parable. I propose that this corresponds to metanoia, the New Testament's word for repentance, which literally means a change of mind. Jonah and the elder brother are invited to exactly this kind of change. Their stories end before readers learn whether it happens. The two kinds of change make different predictions. Sanctification should typically be gradual and dimension-specific. Metanoia should often produce correlated, abrupt shifts across two dimensions at once, as a motive moves from one valence column to the other. Sanctification should be supported by practices that reshape desire and lower costs. Metanoia should be supported by interventions that alter perception, such as confrontation, testimony, or narrative. These predictions can be tested in studies of conversion, recovery, and moral growth.

Implications for Christian Counseling and Formation

Locating the Struggle Accurately

Many Christian clients bring moral distress into counseling, and the distress often depends on how they interpret their own motivation. MoMA offers a short set of diagnostic questions. Is the client drawn away from a good they affirm (Avoid Good)? Drawn toward something they regard as wrong (Approach Bad)? Torn, or simply unmoved? Has their evaluation of the act itself changed? Each answer points to a different kind of help.

This matters especially for scrupulosity, a form of obsessive-compulsive presentation centered on religious and moral fears (Abramowitz et al., 2002).

Scrupulous clients often treat any reluctance or intrusive, unwanted thought as evidence of sin. MoMA distinguishes a thought's content from the motivation behind it. An unwanted intrusive image accompanied by distress and no pull toward acting has a high Avoid Bad and a negligible Approach Bad. Gethsemane provides a theological anchor here. Reluctance toward a costly good is not sin, since Christ experienced it. Counselors working within a Christian framework can use this distinction to reduce misplaced guilt without minimizing real moral concerns. The distinction between regret and guilt also follows from MoMA. Omissions driven by Avoid Good tend to produce regret, while commissions driven by Approach Bad tend to produce guilt (McKee et al., 2026a). Peter's bitter weeping and David's confession in Psalm 51, traditionally linked to the Bathsheba episode, represent different emotional aftermaths. They may call for different pastoral responses, with encouragement and restoration in the first case, and confession and assurance of forgiveness in the second.

Forgiveness Interventions

Forgiveness interventions developed by Christian psychologists, such as

Worthington's (2013) REACH model, have shown meaningful effects in metaanalysis (Wade et al., 2014). MoMA is consistent with the separable-dimension measurement that already underlies this work (McCullough et al., 1998). It adds a moral layer. A client may perceive forgiving as good (Approach Good), shrink from it because of the cost of letting go (Avoid Good), and feel drawn toward revenge they regard as wrong (Approach Bad). Each of these may require different therapeutic work. Joseph's long path to reconciliation and the elder brother's unresolved anger can serve as narrative resources for clients who find that forgiveness does not arrive all at once.

Preaching, Discipleship, and Spiritual Direction

Sermons and discipleship materials often treat moral motivation as a single quantity, urging listeners to commit more or try harder. The biblical material reviewed here suggests a more differentiated approach. Listeners whose main obstacle is Avoid

Good may need what God gave Moses and Elijah: presence, help, rest, and companions. Listeners whose main obstacle is Approach Bad may need practices that reshape desire and accountability that limits opportunity. Listeners whose perceived valence differs from Scripture's may need what Nathan gave David: a story that lets them see the act clearly. A single exhortation cannot meet all three needs.

AI Tools in Christian Care and Ministry

Christian counselors, pastors, and ministries increasingly use large language models to draft sermons, answer pastoral questions, and support counseling. The narrative study described above also compared human raters with 23 frontier language models evaluating the same narratives (McKee et al., 2026b). The models recovered the broad four-dimensional structure and largely agree with humans about which narratives showed which motivations. But they inferred Approach Bad less than half as often as humans did and confined it to a narrower set of stories. The models also treated good-and bad-directed motivations as more opposed than humans did, behaving more like the single-continuum representations MoMA was designed to replace. Their ratings were compressed, with lower variance and entropy than human ratings, especially for Approach Bad.

From a Christian anthropological standpoint, this pattern matters. Scripture is unsentimental about the human heart: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" (Jer 17:9). A tool that under-detects the pull toward wrong will tend to offer a gentler reading of a person's struggle than a wise human counselor would, and it may miss the conflict of temptation altogether.

Charitable interpretation has pastoral value, and the study's authors note that it may be desirable in some settings. But counselors who use these tools should know that the models' default reading of moral motivation is flatter and more benign than the human one. AI summaries of a client's motives are best treated as one perspective, not a substitute for discernment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, any reading of ancient texts through a modern framework risks anachronism. I have tried to limit this risk by grounding claims in what the texts say and by flagging rival readings, but readers should weigh these profiles accordingly. Second, I selected cases that illustrate the framework. Some texts sit uneasily with a motive-centered approach. In the story of Uzzah, for example, a man who reaches out to steady the ark is struck down despite what appears to be a good intention (2 Sam 6:6-7). Biblical ethics also include corporate dimensions, such as the punishment of Achan's household (Josh 7:24-26), that an individual-level architecture does not capture. The narrative study cited above measured observers' inferences about motivation, not agents' actual motives, and its raters were U.S. adults. Readers' inferences about biblical characters carry the same limits. Whether readers from other cultures and traditions infer the same structure from biblical text remains an open question.

Some readers may worry that coding perceived valence relativizes sin. It does not. MoMA describes how an agent perceives an act; theology declares how the act stands before God. Keeping these apart allows a framework to describe selfdeception, conviction, and repentance.

Conclusion

Samuel learned at Jesse's house that the Lord looks at the heart (1 Sam 16:7).

The texts reviewed here show that Scripture's account of the heart is not simple. Its commands address desire as well as deed. Its parables target distinct motivations. Its prophets show reluctance under a sure call, its warriors show courage while afraid, its apostle names temptation he cannot shake, and its Savior accepts a cup he asked to pass. The MoMA framework provides a representational architecture that can hold these distinctions without collapsing them. In return, Scripture and the tradition it shaped offer the framework a long human witness to the four dimensions it proposes.

It also offers hypotheses about how moral motivation changes and how it can be helped to change. The integration of psychology and Christian faith is served when each discipline helps the other see what it already cares about more precisely.

When the problem is Avoid Good, the responses lower costs and raise efficacy. They include presence (Exod 3:12), signs (Exod 4:2-9; Judg 6:36-40), a helper (Exod 4:14-16), rest and food (1 Kgs 19:5-8), companionship (1 Kgs 19:16-21), and reassurance against fear (Judg 7:10-15).
