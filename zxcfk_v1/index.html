Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Why are some people more committed than others to protecting human rights worldwide? McFarland (2010) proposed that people high in Right-Wing Authoritarianism (RWA) and Social Dominance Orientation (SDO) are less committed to universal human rights partly because they are more ethnocentric and identify less with humanity as a whole. We tested this model in a larger sample of US adults (N = 512). The core model was supported: RWA and SDO were linked to greater ethnocentrism, which was associated with weaker identification with all humanity (IWAH) and human-rights commitment, whereas IWAH was associated with stronger commitment. As McFarland found, SDO also had a direct negative association with commitment that empathy did not explain. However, McFarland's original hypothesized model fit significantly better than his final trimmed model. Two previously excluded paths emerged in our larger sample, linking SDO with lower IWAH and RWA with weaker human-rights commitment. Extending the model to environmentalism revealed several similar associations, but poor overall model fit. Our findings support McFarland's framework while refining its specification and suggesting limits to its generalizability across domains and national contexts.

Introduction

Since the adoption of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (United Nations, 1948), human rights have become a central organizing principle of international law and global politics (Lauren, 2003). Human rights are not only a moral ideal but also matter for human welfare: the protection of civil and political rights is associated with higher national income, greater societal equality, and higher subjective well-being (Diener et al., 1995). Supporting human rights in principle, however, is not necessarily the same as being committed to them. General statements of human-rights principles are difficult to oppose-for example, few people would openly disagree with the statement that "everyone deserves basic human rights"-but say little about what individuals will prioritize when human rights conflict with other goals. In one US sample, for example, three-quarters of respondents strongly endorsed abstract human-rights principles, yet few supported committing troops to stop the Rwandan genocide or ranked human rights above national self-interest goals (McFarland & Mathews, 2005). Accordingly, McFarland and Mathews (2005) distinguished agreement with human-rights principles from human-rights commitment: the willingness to prioritize international human rights when doing so conflicts with national interests. Notably, individual differences such as ethnocentrism and authoritarianism predict human-rights commitment considerably better than agreement with it (McFarland & Mathews, 2005; see also Cohrs et al., 2007).

McFarland (2010) proposed identification with all humanity (IWAH), universalism, principled moral reasoning, benevolence, and dispositional empathy as positive correlates of human-rights commitment, and ethnocentrism and its ideological roots, Right-Wing Authoritarianism (RWA; Altemeyer, 1998) and Social Dominance Orientation (SDO; Pratto et al., 1994; see Duckitt, 2001; Duckitt & Sibley, 2010), as among its strongest negative correlates.

McFarland proposed a structural model in which RWA and SDO predict weaker human-rights commitment largely through greater ethnocentrism, which in turn reduces IWAH and commitment, whereas IWAH increases human-rights commitment. Across two US samples, ethnocentrism and IWAH predicted human-rights commitment with roughly equal strength; RWA was associated with commitment only indirectly, whereas SDO retained a direct negative association that lower dispositional empathy did not explain.

A key feature of the model is the distinction between ethnocentrism and IWAH.

Theoretical and empirical evidence indicates that IWAH is related to low ethnocentrism but is more than its absence: individuals may be relatively free of prejudice while caring little about humanity as a whole (McFarland, 2010; McFarland et al., 2012). IWAH predicts concern for global human rights and humanitarian needs (McFarland et al., 2012) and, in a 44-country study, was moderately associated with concern for global issues including climate change and human rights violations (Penczek et al., 2026). It is related negatively to ethnocentrism, RWA, and SDO, and positively to empathy and universalism (McFarland et al., 2019).

Although McFarland (2010) provides a novel structural model for understanding humanrights commitment, there are good reasons to revisit the model. McFarland tested it in two relatively small US samples (Ns = 218 and 102) from a geographically restricted area (i.e., Kentucky, Tennessee, Indiana), and his final model was obtained by trimming nonsignificant paths in the first study and retesting it in only one further sample. Data-driven trimming of this kind can capitalize on chance characteristics of a sample, so that the resulting model may not generalize (MacCallum et al., 1992). McFarland himself noted this risk in earlier path-modelling attempts (McFarland & Mathews, 2005).

The model may also have implications beyond human rights. Responding to climate change requires concern and cooperation beyond immediate ingroups. Climate change threatens many rights set out in the Universal Declaration, including the rights to life, health, and an adequate standard of living, and its impacts fall most heavily on poorer and marginalized communities that have contributed least to the problem (Feygina et al., 2020). In 2022, the United Nations General Assembly recognized the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment as a human right (United Nations General Assembly, 2022), and in a New Zealand national panel, support for human rights (in particular the right to food, clothing, housing, and medicine) and climate change beliefs and concern predicted each other over one year (Athy et al., 2022). Moreover, RWA and SDO are established predictors of environmental attitudes (Milfont et al., 2013 (Milfont et al., , 2018 (Milfont et al., , 2025;; Stanley & Wilson, 2019), but whether ethnocentrism and IWAH, the proximal orientations in McFarland's model, are also implicated is less clear.

IWAH may promote environmental concern by broadening the circle of people whose welfare matters, whereas ethnocentrism may constrain it by prioritizing national and ingroup interests. Global and superordinate identities are consistently associated with stronger proenvironmental views and climate-change mitigation (Lalot et al., 2022; Loy et al., 2022; Pong & Tam, 2023; see also Reese, 2016). Indeed, early work found that human-rights commitment was strongly associated with globalism, a preference for global goals such as protecting the natural environment over nationalistic goals (McFarland & Mathews, 2005). Conversely, climate threat can heighten ethnocentrism while reducing pro-environmental intentions (Uhl et al., 2018), and national narcissism is associated with lower environmental concern (Cislak et al., 2024). To our knowledge, however, no study has examined ethnocentrism and IWAH within a single model of environmentalism.

The present study had two aims. First, we replicated McFarland's (2010) model in a larger US sample. We expected RWA and SDO to predict greater ethnocentrism, ethnocentrism to predict lower IWAH, and ethnocentrism and IWAH to predict lower and higher human-rights commitment, respectively. Following McFarland's final model, we expected RWA to relate to commitment only through ethnocentrism and IWAH, whereas SDO would retain a direct association. Second, we tested whether the model extends to generalized environmentalism.

Results from a supplementary New Zealand sample, which completed an unadapted version of the survey, are summarized here and reported in full in the Supplementary Material.

Method

Participants and Procedure

Data came from a cross-sectional Qualtrics survey approved by the University of Waikato Human Research Ethics Committee (FS2021-48). CloudResearch recruited adults through SENTRY; participants needed ≥100 completed tasks with ≥95% approval. As detailed in Milfont and Osborne (2024), responses were excluded with failed attention checks (e.g., "Please select 'Neither agree nor disagree' for this item"), participation outside the target country, substantial missingness, or completion times < 10 min or > 1.5 hr. The US sample (Amazon Mechanical Turk) comprised N = 512 adults after 37 exclusions (age M = 42.40, SD = 12.66; 52.9% male, 45.5% female, 0.6% non-binary/third gender). As part of the same project, the survey was also administered to a New Zealand sample (Prime Panels; N = 447 after exclusions).

Due to a data collection error, however, the national identification items referred to "Americans" rather than "New Zealanders", and most human rights items referred to the United States rather than New Zealand. We therefore report these data as a supplementary generalization check in Section S5 of the Supplementary Material. Sample size was determined a priori based on available funding, with the goal of recruiting 500 adults. A sensitivity power analysis (Lakens, 2022) indicated that the US sample provided 80% power (α = .05, two-tailed) to detect bivariate associations as small as r = .12, compared with r = .19 and r = .27 for McFarland's (2010) samples (Ns = 218 and 102). The present sample could therefore detect effects about one third to one half smaller than those detectable in the original studies. Power for specific structural paths is reported with the corresponding results.

Measures

Following McFarland (2010), items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) unless noted and averaged after reverse-scoring. We used the 12-item RWA scale and 12item Manitoba Ethnocentrism Scale (Altemeyer, 1998), the 16-item SDO6 scale (Pratto et al., 1994), and 14 dispositional empathy items (Davis, 1983). IWAH was measured with McFarland et al.'s (2012) nine items, each answered for the local community, the national group, and all humanity. Following McFarland, identification with all humanity was residualized on community and national identification in the human-rights models. The environmental extension used raw IWAH, and the environmental results were unchanged when IWAH was adjusted for community and national identification across US and New Zealand samples (Supplementary Material, Section S4).

Human-rights commitment was a latent factor indicated by the 12-item Human Rights Choices Questionnaire (HRCQ), which pits international human-rights goals against national Generalized environmentalism was a latent factor indicated by climate reality, human causation, and climate concern (seven belief items) and by 12 private-sphere and 12 environmental-citizenship behavioral intentions (1 = not at all likely,5 = very likely), with correlated residuals between the two intention indicators. These measures and their measurement model, which showed acceptable fit and metric invariance across the US and New Zealand samples, were previously reported by Milfont and Osborne (2024). Further measurement details are provided in Section S1 of the Supplementary Material.

Analytic Strategy

Models were estimated in Mplus 8.11 with robust maximum likelihood (MLR). Fit was evaluated with χ², CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR, using CFI/TLI ≥ .95, RMSEA ≤ .06, and SRMR ≤ .08 as heuristic indicators of good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999); nested models were compared with the scaled difference test (Satorra & Bentler, 2001). Indirect associations were estimated with 5,000 bootstrap resamples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals

Results

Descriptive statistics, reliabilities, and correlations are reported in Table S1, fit statistics for all models in Table S2, and all bootstrapped indirect associations in Table S3 of the Supplementary Material.

Replication of the Structural Model

McFarland's final model fit the data acceptably, χ²(6, N = 512) = 16.43, p = .012, CFI = .989, TLI = .975, RMSEA = .058, SRMR = .023. As depicted in Figure 1, RWA and SDO were positively associated with ethnocentrism (βs = .44), which was negatively associated with residualized IWAH (β = -.50) and human-rights commitment (β = -.39). IWAH was positively associated with commitment (β = .26), and SDO retained a direct negative association (β = -.27; all ps < .001). The indirect associations of RWA and SDO with commitment through ethnocentrism, and serially through ethnocentrism and IWAH, were all supported (Table S3).

Overall, most estimates were similar to McFarland's (see Table S4). Two notable differences emerged, however. First, ethnocentrism predicted commitment more strongly than IWAH, whereas McFarland found these associations to be roughly equal in magnitude. Second, RWA, SDO, and ethnocentrism were more strongly intercorrelated here (rs = .63-.72) than in his samples (rs = .23-.61).

McFarland's (2010) hypothesized model depicted in his Figure 1 fit our data significantly better than his final empirical model depicted in his Figures 2 and 3, robust Δχ²(3) = 13.80, p = .003. Two trimmed paths were statistically significant: SDO was negatively associated with IWAH (β = -.14, p = .018), and RWA with human-rights commitment (β = -.14, p = .009). However, because the reciprocal paths are identified largely through the assumption of uncorrelated disturbances, this finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Finally, we examined McFarland's (2010) extended empathy model depicted in his figure 4; that is, a model in which dispositional empathy was added as a mediator of the association between SDO and human-rights commitment. As in McFarland's Study 2, SDO predicted lower empathy (β = -.39, p < .001), but empathy was not associated with human-rights commitment (β = .01, p = .809), and the indirect association through empathy was not supported. Adding empathy reduced model fit (CFI = .939, RMSEA = .117), as it also did in McFarland's study.

Extension to Environmentalism

We then extended the model to environmentalism by replacing the latent human-rights commitment outcome with latent generalized environmentalism. In the model paralleling

McFarland's final model, ethnocentrism was negatively (β = -.26, p < .001) and IWAH positively (β = .11, p = .004) associated with generalized environmentalism, but fit was poor (CFI = .927, RMSEA = .108), partly reflecting the moderate fit of the measurement model itself.

The full model fit better, though still suboptimally, χ²(20, N = 512) = 122.31, CFI = .946, TLI = .905, RMSEA = .100, SRMR = .059. As depicted in Figure 2, RWA (β = -.25) and SDO (β = -.41) were associated with lower environmentalism, whereas IWAH retained a positive association (β = .14; all ps < .001). In contrast, ethnocentrism was no longer independently associated with environmentalism (β = -.11, p = .077).

The indirect associations further qualified these findings (Table S3). For SDO, the indirect associations through IWAH alone (B = -.021, 95% BC CI [-.045, -.007]) and serially through ethnocentrism and IWAH (B = -.017, 95% BC CI [-.034, -.007]) were both negative, as was the total indirect association (B = -.075, 95% BC CI [-.130, -.032]); the association through ethnocentrism alone was not supported. For RWA, the two IWAH routes ran in opposite directions and offset each other: the association through IWAH alone was positive (B = .016, 95% BC CI [.004, .037]), whereas the serial association through ethnocentrism and IWAH was negative (B = -.016, 95% BC CI [-.031, -.007]). Neither the association through ethnocentrism alone nor the total indirect association was supported. The positive IWAH-only route for RWA depends on a positive conditional RWA → IWAH path (β = .17, p = .007) that was absent when IWAH was adjusted for community and national identification (Supplementary Material, Section S4). Because RWA was negatively correlated with IWAH at the zero-order level (r = -.24; Table S1), this path likely reflects suppression arising from the strong RWA-SDO correlation and should be interpreted with caution.

Overall, IWAH carried part of the association between SDO and generalized environmentalism, paralleling its role in the human-rights model. For RWA, the indirect associations through IWAH offset each other and rested partly on a likely suppression effect, so the model provides no clear evidence that RWA relates to environmentalism through ethnocentrism or IWAH; instead, RWA was associated with lower environmentalism directly (β = -.25). Thus, both ideological dispositions were associated with lower environmentalism, but only for SDO was this association also consistently via IWAH.

Discussion

More than a decade after McFarland's (2010) studies, the core of his model was reproduced in a substantially larger US sample: RWA and SDO were associated with greater ethnocentrism, which was associated with weaker IWAH and weaker human-rights commitment, whereas IWAH was associated with stronger commitment (see Figure 1). His empathy result also replicated: SDO predicted lower empathy, but empathy did not explain SDO's remaining association with commitment.

The replication was not exact, however. McFarland trimmed three nonsignificant paths, none of which reached significance in his second sample. In our larger sample only one exclusion held; SDO was also associated with lower IWAH, and RWA with weaker commitment. Our power estimates suggest that McFarland's samples had little chance of detecting paths of this size (power ≈ .18-.40), so their exclusion more likely reflects limited power than true null associations, consistent with the observation that models refined through data-driven trimming in modest samples often fail to reproduce exactly (MacCallum et al., 1992). Ethnocentrism was also a stronger predictor of commitment than IWAH. Both differences may partly reflect the much stronger intercorrelation among RWA, SDO, and ethnocentrism in our sample, which changes how shared variance is distributed across direct and indirect paths.

The persistence of a direct SDO association is consistent with evidence that SDO, which reflects valuing group dominance and a lack of universal concern, relates more strongly to human-rights commitment than RWA does, whereas RWA relates more strongly to restricting the rights of unpopular groups (McFarland & Mathews, 2005). The indirect associations are consistent with

McFarland's ordering but do not uniquely establish it: the reversed model fit poorly, as in McFarland, but a small reciprocal path emerged. Given the cross-sectional design and the negative zero-order association between ethnocentrism and IWAH (see Table S1), this coefficient should not be read as evidence that IWAH increases ethnocentrism. The empathy emerged, but the model extended only partly. In the full model (see Figure 2), RWA and SDO retained substantial direct associations with environmentalism, IWAH retained a smaller positive association, and the independent association of ethnocentrism became nonsignificant, suggesting that it overlaps substantially with ideological orientations. To our knowledge, these results provide the first test of ethnocentrism and IWAH as joint predictors of environmentalism, and the IWAH association is consistent with the broader global-identity literature (Pong & Tam, 2023). The prominence of SDO is consistent with evidence that SDO is among the most robust ideological correlates of environmentalism across cultures (Milfont et al., 2018; Stanley & Wilson, 2019) and that, over time, within-person increases in SDO precede declines in climate change beliefs more consistently than increases in RWA do (Milfont et al., 2025), possibly because approval of group-based hierarchy extends to support for human dominance over nature (Milfont et al., 2013 (Milfont et al., , 2018)). Because RWA and SDO associations with environmentalism in these samples have been reported previously (Milfont & Osborne, 2024), the contribution here lies in placing ethnocentrism and IWAH within the framework used to understand human-rights commitment. This complements longitudinal evidence that support for human rights and climate change beliefs are reciprocally related (Athy et al., 2022), while indicating that processes identified for human rights should not be assumed to generalize unchanged to environmental protection.

Although the New Zealand sample could not serve as a direct replication, its results provide qualified further support for the model. Even though participants completed a survey that referred to "Americans" and to US foreign policy, the ideological core of the model reappeared: RWA and SDO were associated with greater ethnocentrism, which was associated with weaker human-rights commitment and environmentalism, and SDO retained a direct association with commitment, with estimates of broadly similar size to those in the US (Supplementary Material, Table S5). That these paths emerged in a measurement context poorly matched to the sample suggests that they are robust. The IWAH paths, however, did not generalize. The humanity measure itself appeared to function: in New Zealand, as in the US, IWAH correlated positively with empathy and environmental behavioral intentions (Table S1). One plausible explanation for its null association with commitment is that the human-rights items pitted global concerns against US rather than New Zealand interests, and thus did not engage the trade-off between national and global identification that IWAH captures. Human-rights commitment was also higher and less variable in New Zealand (Table S1), consistent with uniformly high support for human rights among New Zealanders (Athy et al., 2022), which may have attenuated associations. Item wording cannot explain the null IWAH-environmentalism association, however, because the environmental measures were not US-specific; in New Zealand, IWAH was related to environmental intentions but only weakly to climate-change beliefs (Table S1).

Although identification with all humanity is associated with concern for global issues in most countries (Penczek et al., 2026), the strength of global-identity associations varies across national samples (Pong & Tam, 2023). Replication with culturally adapted measures is therefore needed to determine whether the weaker IWAH findings reflect measurement features, restricted variability, or genuine cross-national differences.

Limitations and Future Directions

The data are cross-sectional, so the indirect associations cannot establish mediation or temporal order, and cross-sectional estimates of indirect effects can be substantially biased (Maxwell & Cole, 2007)

Conclusion

McFarland's (2010) model remains a useful account of who is committed to universal human rights: ethnocentrism and identification with all humanity were again central. However, his final trimmed model omitted two detectable paths, and the model extended only partly to environmentalism and to a supplementary New Zealand sample that completed an unadapted survey. Understanding commitment to global concerns requires attention to both the boundaries and the breadth of social identification.

S2. Detailed Results for the US Sample

Tables S2 and S3 provide detailed information about model fit and indirect paths, respectively. We provide a narrative overview of the findings below.

Replication of the Structural Model

McFarland's (2010) final model showed acceptable fit to our US data, χ²(6, N = 512) = 16.43, p = .012, CFI = .989, TLI = .975, RMSEA = .058, 90% CI [.025, .093], SRMR = .023.

RWA and SDO (β = .44, p < .001 for both) were positively associated with ethnocentrism, which was negatively associated with residualized IWAH (β = -.50, p < .001) and human-rights commitment (β = -.39, p < .001). IWAH was positively associated with human-rights commitment (β = .26, p < .001), whereas SDO showed an additional negative association (β = -.27, p < .001). HRCQ and Human Rights Scenarios loaded .95 and .77, respectively, on humanrights commitment. We next estimated McFarland's original hypothesized model by restoring all three paths that had been removed in his Study 1. This model showed excellent fit, χ²(3, N = 512) = 2.58, p = .461, CFI = 1.000, TLI = 1.000, RMSEA = .000, 90% CI [.000, .070], SRMR = .006, and significantly improved fit relative to McFarland's final trimmed model, robust Δχ²(3) = 13.80, p = .003. Two of the three paths that McFarland had removed were statistically significant: SDO was negatively associated with IWAH (β = -.14, p = .018), and RWA was negatively associated with human-rights commitment (β = -.14, p = .009). In contrast, the RWA → IWAH path remained nonsignificant (β = -.05, p = .444), reproducing McFarland's original finding for this path. Thus, the present results supported one of McFarland's three path exclusions but not the other two. A detailed comparison with McFarland's (2010) estimates is provided below and in Table S4.

Following McFarland (2010), we also examined alternative specifications of the association between ethnocentrism and IWAH. Reversing the proposed ordering, such that IWAH predicted ethnocentrism rather than ethnocentrism predicting IWAH, produced poor fit, χ²(6, N = 512) = 109.16, p < .001, CFI = .881, TLI = .762, RMSEA = .183, 90% CI [.154, .214], SRMR = .186. Thus, as in McFarland, the reversed specification was not supported. A bidirectional model showed good fit, χ²(5, N = 512) = 10.87, p = .054, CFI = .994, TLI = .983, RMSEA = .048, 90% CI [.000, .087], SRMR = .014, and it also improved fit relative to the final trimmed model, robust Δχ²(1) = 5.65, p = .017. Unlike McFarland, however, the additional IWAH → ethnocentrism path was small but statistically significant (β = .11, p = .039), while the ethnocentrism → IWAH path remained substantial (β = -.58, p < .001). Because no variable in the model predicts IWAH without also predicting ethnocentrism, the reciprocal paths are identified mainly through the assumption of uncorrelated disturbances, and the IWAH → ethnocentrism estimate should be interpreted cautiously.

Replication of the Empathy Model

In a supplementary replication of McFarland's (2010) Study 2, SDO was negatively associated with dispositional empathy (β = -.39, p < .001). Empathy, however, was not independently associated with human-rights commitment after accounting for ethnocentrism, SDO, and IWAH (β = .01, p = .809). Consistent with this result, the bootstrapped indirect association from SDO through empathy was B = -.004, 95% BC CI [-.037, .027]. Ethnocentrism (β = -.39), SDO (β = -.27), and IWAH (β = .26) retained independent associations with humanrights commitment (all ps < .001). Thus, as in McFarland's Study 2, lower empathy did not account for the remaining association between SDO and human-rights commitment. The full empathy model itself fit poorly, χ²(10, N = 512) = 80.05, p < .001, CFI = .939, TLI = .878, RMSEA = .117, 90% CI [.094, .141], SRMR = .055. McFarland's empathy model likewise fit less well than his model without empathy (CFI = .967, RMSEA = .091).

The conclusion was unchanged when identification with all humanity was modeled directly while controlling for community and national identification within these SEM models.

SDO was negatively associated with empathy (β = -.39, p < .001), but empathy was unrelated to human-rights commitment (β = -.02, p = .684), and the bias-corrected bootstrap interval for the indirect association through empathy included zero (B = .007, 95% BC CI [-.026, .042]). In contrast, the serial indirect association through ethnocentrism and identification with all humanity remained supported (B = -.041, 95% BC CI [-.064, -.021]).

Extension to Environmentalism

We next tested whether the McFarland model extended to generalized environmentalism.

The five environmental indicators loaded positively on the latent factor (standardized loadings = .56-.93). In the initial model paralleling McFarland's final human-rights model, RWA and SDO predicted greater ethnocentrism (βs = .44), ethnocentrism predicted lower raw IWAH (β = -.42), and SDO predicted lower environmentalism (β = -.48). Ethnocentrism was also negatively associated with environmentalism (β = -.26, p < .001), whereas IWAH showed a smaller positive independent association (β = .11, p = .004). This initial model fit poorly, χ²(23, N = 512) = 159.70, p < .001, CFI = .927, TLI = .889, RMSEA = .108, 90% CI [.092, .124], SRMR = .067. Because the environmental measurement model alone showed only moderate fit (RMSEA = .079, SRMR = .089), part of this misfit may reflect the measurement component rather than the structural paths.

A full environmental model additionally allowed RWA and SDO to predict IWAH directly and RWA to predict environmentalism directly. This model fit better than the initial model on all descriptive indices, although fit remained suboptimal, χ²(20, N = 512) = 122.31, p < .001, CFI = .946, TLI = .905, RMSEA = .100, 90% CI [.083, .117], SRMR = .059. RWA (β = -.25, p < .001) and SDO (β = -.41, p < .001) were negatively associated with environmentalism, whereas IWAH retained a smaller positive association (β = .14, p < .001). The independent association between ethnocentrism and environmentalism was reduced and was not statistically significant (β = -.11, p = .077). Ethnocentrism remained negatively associated with IWAH (β = -.39, p < .001), and SDO showed an additional negative association with IWAH (β = -.21, p < .001). RWA showed a positive conditional association with IWAH (β = .17, p = .007). This association was not evident when IWAH was adjusted for community and national identification (β = -.07, p = .219; see Section S4) and, given that RWA was negatively correlated with IWAH at the zero-order level, likely reflects suppression arising from the strong correlation between RWA and SDO.

Bootstrap analyses of the full environmental model further qualified the indirect associations. For RWA, the total indirect association with environmentalism was not supported (B = -.033, 95% BC CI [-.076, .004]), nor was the specific association through ethnocentrism alone (B = -.033, 95% BC CI [-.075, .003]). However, the specific association through IWAH alone was positive (B = .016, 95% BC CI [.004, .037]), whereas the serial association through ethnocentrism and IWAH was negative (B = -.016, 95% BC CI [-.031, -.007]). For SDO, the total indirect association was supported (B = -.075, 95% BC CI [-.130, -.032]). The ethnocentrism-only association was not supported (B = -.037, 95% BC CI [-.083, .004]), whereas both the association through IWAH alone (B = -.021, 95% BC CI [-.045, -.007]) and the serial association through ethnocentrism and IWAH (B = -.017, 95% BC CI [-.034, -.007]) were supported. The focal environmental associations were substantively unchanged when IWAH was simultaneously adjusted for community and national identification within the SEM (see details below). associations through ethnocentrism and IWAH were supported for both RWA (B = -.036, 95% BC CI [-.059, -.018]) and SDO (B = -.039, 95% BC CI [-.062, -.020]).

A two-path integrated sensitivity model, freeing the SDO → IWAH and RWA → humanrights commitment paths that were statistically significant in the original hypothesized model, also showed poor overall fit, χ²(14) = 124.83, p < .001, CFI = .932, TLI = .893, RMSEA = .124, 90% CI [.105, .145], SRMR = .082. Ethnocentrism remained negatively associated with IWAH (β = -.36, p < .001), whereas SDO showed an additional negative association with IWAH (β = -.13, p = .010). IWAH remained positively associated with human-rights commitment (β = .20, p < .001). The additional indirect association from SDO through IWAH was also supported (B = -.028, 95% BC CI [-.059, -.008]).

Finally, the empathy model was re-estimated using the same integrated adjustment of IWAH. Overall model fit was poor, χ²(22) = 341.57, p < .001, CFI = .831, TLI = .770, RMSEA = .168, 90% CI [.153, .184], SRMR = .129. SDO was negatively associated with empathy (β = -.39, p < .001), but empathy was not associated with human-rights commitment (β = -.02, p = .684), and the corresponding indirect association was not supported (B = .007, 95% BC CI [-.026, .042]). In contrast, the serial indirect association from SDO through ethnocentrism and IWAH remained supported (B = -.041, 95% BC CI [-.064, -.021]). Thus, although the integrated models showed poor overall fit, the focal results involving IWAH and empathy were substantively consistent with those obtained using the residualized IWAH measure.

Environmental Extension

As a robustness check, the full US environmental model was re-estimated while simultaneously adjusting raw IWAH for community and national identification. The model showed poor overall fit, χ²(36) = 236.37, p < .001, CFI = .907, TLI = .874, RMSEA = .104, 90% CI [.092, .117], SRMR = .092. Nevertheless, the focal environmental associations were essentially unchanged. IWAH remained positively associated with environmentalism (β = .14, p < .001), whereas RWA (β = -.25, p < .001) and SDO (β = -.41, p < .001) remained negatively associated with environmentalism. Ethnocentrism was not independently associated with environmentalism (β = -.11, p = .077). Thus, the principal conclusions of the environmental extension were robust to adjusting IWAH for community and national identification within the SEM.

S5. Supplementary New Zealand Sample

Participants and Survey

The New Zealand sample was recruited through Prime Panels (CloudResearch) using the same procedure and exclusion criteria as the US sample and comprised N = 447 adults after 208 exclusions (age M = 46.60, SD = 16.94; 46.5% male, 52.8% female, 0.7% non-binary/third gender). Participants completed the original US-worded survey, which affected two sets of measures (see Appendix). First, the national-identification target of the IWAH measure was "Americans" rather than "New Zealanders." For New Zealand participants, this target is a foreign outgroup rather than the national ingroup, so identification with all humanity could not be residualized on national identification as in the US analyses. Because the nine humanity items were identical in both samples, the New Zealand analyses used raw IWAH. Second, the humanrights commitment measures referred to the United States. Of the 12 HRCQ items, only Items 1 and 2 contain no US referent, and all 10 Human Rights Scenarios ask what the United States, its President, or its Senate should do. Removing the US-referent items would leave too few indicators for a meaningful commitment factor. For New Zealand participants, these measures therefore assessed support for prioritizing human rights in US foreign policy rather than over their own country's interests. The RWA, SDO, empathy, and environmental measures did not refer to the United States, and the ethnocentrism items referred to "our country," although some reflect US group references (e.g., "Indians").

Human-Rights Model

The final (trimmed) human-rights model showed acceptable fit, χ²(6, N = 447) = 14.55, p = .024, CFI = .982, TLI = .957, RMSEA = .056, 90% CI [.019, .094], SRMR = .025. RWA (β = .39) and SDO (β = .42) were positively associated with ethnocentrism, and ethnocentrism predicted lower raw IWAH (β = -.22; all ps < .001). Ethnocentrism (β = -.31, p < .001) and SDO (β = -.41, p < .001) were negatively associated with human-rights commitment, but raw IWAH was not independently associated with commitment (β = .01, p = .883). Consistent with this, bootstrap serial associations through ethnocentrism and IWAH included zero for both RWA (B = .000, 95% BC CI [-.007, .006]) and SDO (B = .000, 95% BC CI [-.007, .006]), whereas indirect associations through ethnocentrism alone were supported (RWA: B = -.088, 95% BC CI [-.141, -.047]; SDO: B = -.090, 95% BC CI [-.142, -.048]; Table S3).

Environmental Model

In the environmental model paralleling the final human-rights model, RWA and SDO again predicted greater ethnocentrism (βs = .39 and .42), and ethnocentrism predicted lower IWAH (β = -.22). Ethnocentrism (β = -.27, p < .001) and SDO (β = -.22, p = .005) predicted lower environmentalism, whereas IWAH was not independently associated with environmentalism (β = .03, p = .522). The corresponding serial bootstrap associations again included zero (RWA: B = -.003, 95% BC CI [-.014, .006]; SDO: B = -.003, 95% BC CI [-.014, .006]), whereas the ethnocentrism-only routes were supported (RWA: B = -.112, 95% BC CI [-.193, -.050]; SDO: B = -.114, 95% BC CI [-.197, -.054]). As in the US sample (CFI = .927, RMSEA = .108), this model fit poorly, χ²(23, N = 447) = 136.24, p < .001, CFI = .904, TLI = .854, RMSEA = .105, 90% CI [.088, .122], SRMR = .073.

Comparison With the US Estimates

Table S5 compares the New Zealand and US estimates. Because the New Zealand models used raw IWAH, whereas the primary US human-rights model used residualized IWAH, we also re-estimated the US final human-rights model with raw IWAH, χ²(6, N = 512) = 30.21, p < .001, CFI = .974, TLI = .940, RMSEA = .089, 90% CI [.059, .121], SRMR = .027. The paths linking ideology, ethnocentrism, and the outcomes were similar across samples. RWA and SDO predicted ethnocentrism with similar strength in both samples (US βs = .44 and .44; New Zealand βs = .39 and .42). Ethnocentrism predicted lower human-rights commitment (US β = -.45 with raw IWAH; New Zealand β = -.31) and lower environmentalism (US β = -.26; New Zealand β = -.27), and SDO retained a direct negative association with commitment in both samples (US β = -.28; New Zealand β = -.41). In contrast, the IWAH paths differed.

Ethnocentrism was more weakly associated with IWAH in New Zealand than in the US (β = -.22 vs. -.42), and raw IWAH predicted human-rights commitment in the US (β = .18, p < .001) but not in New Zealand (β = .01, p = .883). The IWAH → environmentalism path was small in both samples (US β = .11; New Zealand β = .03).

Interpretation

The New Zealand results reproduce the ideological core of McFarland's (2010) model (RWA and SDO predicting ethnocentrism, which in turn predicts weaker commitment to human rights and environmentalism, with an additional direct association between SDO and commitment) despite a survey that was poorly matched to the sample. This consistency provides further, if qualified, support for these paths. The IWAH paths did not replicate, and several observations suggest that measurement features contributed to this difference. First, raw IWAH showed expected associations with other constructs in New Zealand: it correlated with empathy (r = .47; US r = .53) and with individual and collective environmental intentions (rs = .34 and .42; US rs = .52 and .52), indicating that the humanity items functioned similarly in both samples. Second, IWAH was nearly uncorrelated with the HRCQ (r = .11) and Human Rights Scenarios (r = .10) in New Zealand, compared with rs = .44 and .39 in the US. Because the commitment items asked whether the United States should prioritize human rights over American interests, they did not pit New Zealand participants' own national interests against those of humanity, which is the conflict McFarland's commitment measures were designed to create. Third, human-rights commitment was higher and less variable in New Zealand (HRCQ M = 4.26, SD = 0.62, vs. US M = 3.85, SD = 0.94), which may have attenuated associations. The null IWAH-environmentalism association is less easily attributed to item wording, because the environmental items did not refer to the United States: in New Zealand, IWAH correlated with environmental intentions but only weakly with climate-change beliefs (rs = .04-.10, vs. .28-.36 in the US). Whether these differences reflect measurement, restricted variance, or genuine crossnational differences requires replication with culturally adapted measures.

S1. Measurement Details Right-Wing Authoritarianism (RWA). RWA was assessed with the 12-item measure (Altemeyer, 1998) used by McFarland (2010). Items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with negatively keyed items reverse-scored and averaged so that higher scores indicated greater RWA. Social Dominance Orientation (SDO). SDO was assessed with the 16-item SDO6 scale (Pratto et al., 1994), as used by McFarland (2010). Items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree); egalitarianism-keyed items were reverse-scored before averaging. Ethnocentrism. Ethnocentrism was assessed with the 12-item version of the Manitoba Ethnocentrism Scale (Altemeyer, 1998) used by McFarland (2010). Items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), reverse-keyed as appropriate, and averaged so that higher scores indicated greater ethnocentrism. Identification With All Humanity (IWAH). IWAH was assessed with McFarland et al.'s (2012) nine-item measure.

McFarland's (2010) studies used a nine-item version of this measure; the present version included ten scenarios used in McFarland et al. (2012). Following McFarland (2010), HRCQ and Human Rights Scenario scores were specified as indicators of a latent human-rights commitment factor. In the New Zealand sample, both measures retained their US referents (see Section S5). Dispositional Empathy. Dispositional empathy was assessed with the same Davis' (1983) 14 items used in McFarland's Study 2. Items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), reverse-keyed as appropriate, and averaged. Empathy was examined in a supplementary replication of McFarland's Study 2 results. Generalized Environmentalism. The environmental measures were previously reported by Milfont and Osborne (2024), who examined associations with left-and right-wing authoritarianism and SDO. Climate-change beliefs were assessed with seven items forming three indicators (climate reality, human causation, and climate concern), and behavioral intentions for the next 12 months were assessed with 12 private-sphere (individual) and 12 environmentalcitizenship (collective) items rated from 1 (not at all likely) to 5 (very likely); full item details are reported by Milfont and Osborne (2024). The present extension addresses a distinct question: whether ethnocentrism and IWAH, central to McFarland's human-rights model, are also implicated in generalized environmentalism. Consistent with Milfont and Osborne (2024), climate reality, human causation, climate concern, individual behavioral intentions, and collective behavioral intentions were specified as indicators of a latent generalized environmentalism factor, with correlated residuals between the two behavioral indicators.
