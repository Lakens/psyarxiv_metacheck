Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Virtual reality enables studying active visual search and gaze behavior under naturalistic, free-viewing conditions while preserving experimental control. However, free head-eye coordination, extended 3D space, dynamic target trajectories, and headset-specific field of view and eye-tracking constraints mean that findings from traditional 2D paradigms cannot be assumed to generalize to immersive environments. Interpreting gaze-based outcomes in VR requires benchmarks that establish how basic target properties influence detection. We used target eccentricity, size, and speed as benchmark predictors of visual detection in an immersive VR coin-foraging task. Using in-headset eye-tracking, participants guided a gaze-controlled avatar to collect coins that varied in gaze-relative eccentricity, size, and speed. We modeled gaze-based observation probability, observation latency, dwell time, gaze-coin angular distance, and saccade dynamics. Observation probability was analyzed using mixed-effects logistic regression, whereas latency was analyzed with a censoring-aware Cox framework that retained neverdetected coins. Eccentricity and size showed the clearest benchmark effects. Peripheral and small coins were less likely to be detected. Speed's role was more nuanced. Although slow coins were detected more often in a binary model, this advantage did not persist in a censoring-aware survival analysis, suggesting that speed influenced the opportunity for observation more than momentary salience. Consistent with this interpretation, slow coins produced longer dwell times, and gaze-coin angular distance and saccade measures indicated that target properties shaped both the geometry and effort for acquisition. These findings establish a feature-based benchmark for gaze-based detection in immersive visual search and demonstrate the importance of modeling missed targets as censored detection opportunities.

[div-01]

Visual search depends on the ability to detect relevant objects across the visual field. In active, free-viewing search, observers must detect objects outside of fixation and decide whether they are task-relevant and orient their gaze for further inspection. Eye movements, head movements, body orientation, and task goals jointly determine what information becomes available and when it is sampled (Land & Hayhoe, 2001). Peripheral vision contributes critically to this process by enabling scene monitoring and guiding orienting responses to objects beyond the fovea. However, these representations are spatially constrained. Visual acuity and pattern recognition decline with eccentricity; crowding and reduced representational precision outside central vision make object identification increasingly more difficult (Levi, 2008; Rosenholtz, 2016). Based on these constraints, visual detection is also a spatial sampling problem. Three factors are particularly relevant to the spatial sampling problem: eccentricity, target size, and motion. Eccentricity specifies the angular displacement of a target from the fovea, size constrains the spatial information available for its identification, and motion influences both its attentional priority and the temporal window within which it can be detected.

In traditional visual-search paradigms, task performance declines as target eccentricity increases. Carrasco et al. (1995) showed how target eccentricity affects conjunction-search performance. Across experiments, both reaction times and errors increased as target eccentricity increased. Subsequent research showed that eccentricity effects arise not only from peripheral visual limitations, crowding, and cortical magnification, but also from central attentional biases (Wolfe et al., 1998; Carrasco & Frieder, 1997). However, detectability in visual search is not governed solely by eccentricity. The amount of spatial information a target provides plays a key role in visibility, especially in peripheral vision.

Target size is therefore another physical constraint for detection, and it interacts with eccentricity closely. The larger the target, the more spatial information it provides, partially compensating for the loss of resolu-tion that occurs as eccentricity increases. Cortical-magnification and eccentricity-scaling accounts have shown that peripheral performance is jointly dependent on retinal location and stimulus scale (Carrasco & Frieder, 1997; Rovamo & Virsu, 1979; Strasburger et al., 2011.; Virsu & Rovamo, 1979). However, conventional 2D displays typically constrain targets to a relatively limited region around fixation, leaving the sizeeccentricity relationship less well characterized at the range of eccentricities experienced in natural viewing.

Over time, targets can change positions and visibility. Therefore motion likely shapes visual detection, but interpreting its role is difficult to isolate. Salience models identify early visual features as a source of bottomup attentional guidance (Itti & Koch, 2001), and dynamic features are among the strongest triggers of attentional shifts. Importantly, target speed is not equivalent to motion onset or looming. Faster targets may produce stronger momentary dynamic signals, but they can also be more difficult to track, and may remain available for a shorter period of time (providing fewer opportunities to be acquired), and be more difficult to stabilize with gaze. In contrast, slower targets may be less dynamically salient at any given point, but they may allow more opportunities to be detected by remaining visible for a longer period of time. Therefore, speed is better treated as a predictor that can affect both visual salience and temporal availability.

Considering the components discussed above, immersive virtual reality provides a uniquely suited environment for examining these canonical determinants of detection. Compared to traditional 2D desktop paradigms, immersive VR allows observers to view a larger spatial environment using coordinated eye and head movements, while preserving experimental control over timing, target properties, and scene geometries (Clay et al., 2019). VR is thus well suited to studying active visual behavior, but at the price of a more complex measurement problem. Gaze direction, head orientation, world position, and headset field of view all help determine what visual information is available at any given moment. Consequently, it is not just a question of whether classical visual-search effects can be reproduced in VR, but whether they also remain measurable when detection is affected by all of these factors jointly.

In VR, eye movements can occur along with head and body movements, and observers differ in the relative contribution of these movements (Sidenmark & Gellersen, 2019). Analyses comparing gaze behavior during locomotion in virtual reality and the real world suggest that VR can reproduce many aspects of real-world gaze control while maintaining experimental control (Drewes et al., 2021). However, this does not mean that VR is perceptually equivalent to either 2D desktop displays or real-world natural vision. Instead, VR requires benchmark studies that establish which visual and oculomotor regularities remain observable under immersive, free-viewing conditions.

Recent studies have begun to address this challenge by examining visual search and attention in immersive VR. Gaze-contingent visual search experiments have shown that not only does central vision play a minor role in object identification in natural scenes, but also that peripheral vision contributes to search in a manner that has been underestimated by 2D experiments (David et al., 2021). Similarly, studies of extended fields of view have shown that saliency advantages for target detection occurred only when the target was within the initial field of view; when head movements were needed, observers used the same strategy as when no target was visible. These finding suggest that stimulus saliency functions differently in active search across an extended field compared to a fixed 2D display (Stein et al., 2024). Aivar et al. (2024) showed that spatial memory guides eye and body movements during naturalistic search. Dozio et al. (2022) further demonstrated that eccentricity and distance affect localization of static visual targets in immersive VR, arguing that perceptual parameters must be characterized within VR environments specifically because perception differs both from reality and across VR systems. Together, these studies establish that VR is a productive platform for visualsearch research while also demonstrating that immersive search is dependent on variables that are difficult to capture in conventional 2D paradigms.

The outstanding need, therefore, is not a further demonstration that visual search is feasible in VR, but a methodological benchmark establishing whether the core physical determinants of detection remain measurable under dynamic, three-dimensional presentation and unconstrained gaze and head movements. A benchmark should therefore (1) jointly model eccentricity, size, and speed; (2) distinguish detection probability from detection latency; and (3) quantify gaze dynamics in a way that is reproducible across participants and trials. The present study addresses this benchmark gap using an immersive VR coin-foraging task with head-mounted eye tracking. Participants used a gazecontrolled interface to collect moving coins that varied in gaze-relative eccentricity, size, and speed.

Establishing such a benchmark also requires demonstrating that observed effects reflect genuine perceptual effects rather than properties of the apparatus. Built-in eye tracking in head-mounted displays (HMDs) has enabled object-level gaze analysis in immersive environments, but gaze estimates from HMDs are not uniform across various devices, and sometimes not even across users with the same HMD. Accuracy and precision can vary with headset fit, calibration quality, use of glasses or contact lenses, gaze angle, and scene geometry. These characteristics are directly relevant when detection is defined as gaze proximity to a target. An observed eccentricity effect may therefore reflect a combination of perceptual constraints, orienting demands, and measurement properties. Therefore, eye-tracking quality should be included as part of the benchmark, rather than as a background technical detail.

These difficulties are the focus of an emerging body of methodological work on gaze-to-object analysis in immersive environments. Researchers have developed toolkits and workflows for virtual scene preparation, assigning gaze to dynamic objects, correcting and aggregating raw gaze data from HMDs, and visualizing gaze behavior in interactive environments (Kapp et al., 2021; Ryabinin & Belousov, 2021; Ugwitz et al., 2022). Such developments do indeed increase the feasibility of using HMD eye tracking as a behavioral research method, but they also highlight a methodological requirement: studies should clearly specify how gaze-defined events are operationalized, how undetected objects are handled, and how device limitations may affect the dependent measures.

Beyond gaze assignment, the benchmark requires distinguishing detection probability from detection latency. In dynamic search tasks, some targets are detected while others are never detected before they disappear or the trial ends. A binary model will certainly show if one class of targets are detected more often overall, but it will not be able to show if an advantage is reflective of increased momentary detectability or more opportunity for detection. Similarly, an observedonly latency analysis answers a conditional question: among targets that were successfully detected, how long did detection take? This approach, however, excludes the most difficult or the least available targets, precisely the observations that are most informative about detection failure.

A target that is not detected is not missing latency data; rather, it represents an unresolved event. Detection simply did not occur during the interval in which the target was available. Time-to-event models provide a framework for retaining these cases as right-censored observations. Event-history approaches have also been recommended for response-time and visual-search data because they retain the time course of responding, which is obscured by analyses restricted to mean latencies on successful trials (Panis, Moran, et al., 2020; Panis, Schmidt, et al., 2020). By treating never-detected targets as censored observations, the quantity being estimated changes from latency among successes to the conditional rate of detection across all eligible targets. This framework is important for a VR paradigm because it demonstrates a core component of active visual search specifically in VR. Targets are not just available or unavailable; their perceptual availability evolves over time in tandem with gaze, head position, and target motion.

The primary benchmark analyses focused on equalvalue conditions, where all coins equaled one point, allowing the physical determinants of detection to be estimated without a reward-value confound. Detection probability was modeled using mixed-effects logistic regression, and detection latency was modeled using a censoring-aware time-to-event framework in which never-detected coins remained in the risk set as rightcensored observations. Supporting analyses examined dwell time and gaze dynamics to characterize how target properties influenced visual behavior beyond initial detection.

We predicted that coins appearing at a greater eccentricity would be less likely to be detected, due to the combined perceptual and orienting costs of peripheral target placement. We also predicted that small coins would be less likely to be detected and would show lower detection hazard than large coins, consistent with the role of stimulus scale in peripheral visibility. For speed, rather than committing to a single directional prediction, we contrasted two accounts. Under a salience account, greater speed should enhances dynamic salience and therefore facilitate detection. Under an availability account, in which speed governs tracking stability and exposure duration, slower coins should then accrue higher cumulative detection. Together, these analyses provide a reproducible benchmark for gaze-based detection in immersive VR while also evaluating the methodological value of treating never-detected targets as censored events rather than discarded observations.

Method Participants

Forty-eight participants (20 Male) were recruited from the University of Oregon Psychology and Linguistics Departments' Human Subjects Pool. Ages ranged from 18-27 years (M= 19.7, Md= 19). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision via contact lenses. Each study visit took approximately 45 minutes, and participants were compensated for their time. All procedures were approved by the University of Oregon Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all methods adhered to institutional and federal guidelines.

The target of 30 usable datasets follows within-subject norms in vision science, where high trial density per participant provides statistical sensitivity; we overrecruited and retained 48 usable participants after exclusions. Prior VR experience was required to reduce attrition, facilitate eye-tracker calibration, and minimize potential novelty effects associated with first-time VR exposure. This criterion necessarily constrains the sample to VR-experienced observers rather than firsttime users, a limitation we return to in the discussion. Participants with physical glasses were excluded from this study due to poor eye-tracker calibration and reports of physical discomfort while wearing both the HMD and glasses. Participants with contact lenses were included.

Apparatus

Stimuli were presented in the HTC Focus Vision headset, with an embedded binocular eye tracker featuring a 90Hz sampling rate. The experiment was developed and deployed using Unity game engine. Gaze and event data were logged through Cognitive3D; Unity ground-truth metrics were logged in parallel to the desktop (AMD Ryzen CPU, 64GB RAM).

Measures, Stimuli & Task

Participants performed a gaze-controlled coin-foraging task in a navigable 3D virtual environment. Using eye and head movements to steer a spaceship avatar, participants collected approaching coins that varied in size, speed, and initial gaze-relative eccentricity. Coins spawned across a wide range of retinal eccentricities and were defined by two crossed physical attributes -size (large vs. small) and speed (slow vs. fast) -together with a continuously varying spawn eccentricity.

Size was manipulated in world-space units by scaling a coin model to one of two values (scale factors of 4 and 8); because objects were viewed in depth and approached the observer, angular (retinal) size varied continuously over each coin's trajectory rather than being fixed by condition. Speed was manipulated as constant world-space translational velocity. Each coin advanced toward the observer along the depth axis at a fixed rate, set to one of two values for the slow and fast conditions. As with size, speed was defined in worldspace rather than retinal terms; because coins moved in depth, their angular velocity and rate of angular expansion increased over the approach rather than remaining fixed by condition.

Participants used a gaze-controlled avatar (a spaceship) to collect as many coins as possible. Each trial was 90 seconds long. Participants completed a total of 18 trials. Total points (1 point per coin collected) earned was shown after every trial. To motivate participants, the highest scoring participant received a $100 Visa gift card.

Because participants were untethered and free to move their head throughout the experiment, without a chin rest or central fixation constraint, eccentricity at spawn was intentionally computed as the angular distance between the coin's spawn position, relative to the participant's live gaze direction (rather than the coin's absolute position in the scene). This gaze-relative definition reflects how far into the participant's actual current periphery each coin appeared, consistent with the study's aim of capturing attention under naturalistic, unconstrained viewing rather than fixed-gaze laboratory conditions. A coin was logged as observed when gaze fell within 5° of the coin center at any point during the coin's lifetime. A coin collection was triggered by physical contact between the coin and the gaze-steered spaceship. Each coin yields a binary observation outcome, a binary collection outcome, and, for observed coins, a latency to first observation and a total observation dwell time. Two gaze-derived oculomotor measures were computed per coin lifetime: mean saccade amplitude and mean gaze-coin angular separation.

Analytic Strategy

Models were fit in R with lme4 and lmerTest (GLMMs/LMMs, Satterthwaite df), coxme (mixed-effects Cox), and survival (proportional-hazards diagnostics); marginal predictions used ggeffects. Unless noted, models included nested random intercepts for participant and session (1 | participant_id / session_id). Continuous eccentricity was standardized (ecc_z) for the full-data interaction models, where the unscaled predictor produced ill-conditioning; benchmark models retained eccentricity in degrees for interpretability. GLMMs used the bobyqa optimizer; residual adequacy was assessed with DHARMa. Convergence and singularity are reported per model.

Latency: Cox proportional-hazards models were formulated for censored event-time data (Cox, 1972), and frailty extensions allow clustered or repeated event histories to be modeled when observations are nested within participants or sessions (Grambsch & Therneau, 1994). Never-observed coins were not treated as "missing" data but as right-censored observations. Detection latency was therefore modeled with a mixed-effects Cox proportional-hazards model treating never-observed coins as right-censored over the full corpus, retaining participant and session random intercepts, with a Gamma GLMM on observed coins as a secondary, observed-only comparison. Since a hazard ratio is interpretable only under proportional hazards, we tested this assumption for every predictor using scaled Schoenfeld residuals (cox.zph) on a fixed-effects refit, since the mixed-effects estimator does not directly support the diagnostic. When the assumption was violated, we characterized the time course of the effect by re-estimating hazard ratios within latency windows. Because a single model with a covariate-by-window interaction failed to converge, we fit separate mixed-effects models per window, letting baseline and covariate effects vary across windows. Instead of defending a timeaveraged effect, we used a Cox model to recover detection dynamics that an observed-only latency model cannot represent.

Results

Across 48 participants and 852 trials, observation and collection rates were computed at the trial-level as the proportion of spawned coins per session. Mean observation probability was 0.50 (SD = 0.18) and mean collection probability was 0.28 (SD = 0.18). Among observed coins with a measurable latency (N = 47,600 coin-observations), the median time to first observation was 2.1 s.

Analyses focused on the conditions with no value manipulation (c1-c4), in which every coin was worth one point. This sub-dataset serves as the benchmark for this study. Detection probability, saccade amplitude, and gaze-target distance in relation to coin eccentricity, size, and speed were the three canonical detection laws modeled in these conditions. Detection latency was analyzed across all conditions using a censor-aware survival model, with session value included as a covariate. Post-detection dwell time and coin collection were recorded but are reported elsewhere. Odds ratios, hazard ratios, and dwell ratios are reported as exp(b), with 95% confidence intervals.

Detection probability.

In this benchmark dataset (n = 65,759 observations, 567 trials), observation probability decreased with eccentricity, was approximately halved for small targets, and was higher for slow than fast targets. Diagnostics for the benchmark model were adequate (DHARMa dispersion ≈ 1.01; the uniformity KS test reaches significance only because trivial deviations do so at N in the tens of thousands).

In order to determine whether eccentricity, size, and speed predicted observation probability, we used a logistic GLMM. This showed that each additional degree of eccentricity reduced the odds of observation by ~1% (b = -0.013, OR = 0.99, 95% CI [-0.015, -0.011], z = -14.71, p < .001) (Fig. 1); small coins were about half as likely to be observed as large (OR = 0.50 [0.458, 0.543], z = -15.91, p < .001), and slow coins 1.70× as likely as fast (OR = 1.70 [1.557, 1.856], z = 11.81, p < .001) (Fig. 2). Although the per-degree odds change is small, it compounds across the scene: the odds of observation were roughly halved across 45° of eccentricity and fell by about 70% across 90°, producing the steep cumulative gradient (Fig. 1). The effect of eccentricity also depended on target size. The gradient was steeper for small than large coins (eccentricity × size: b = -0.005, z = -3.55, p < .001), so peripheral placement penalized small targets the most. The eccentricity × speed, size × speed, and three-way interaction were not significant (ps = .38, .47, .30), indicating that size and speed act as largely additive shifts on the eccentricity function, rather than reshaping it. Relation to the observed-only model.

To understand if among observed coins, physical properties predict how quickly they are first noticed, we used a Gamma GLMM. The model (n = 47,600) showed the familiar patternlonger latencies for slow than fast coins (ratio = 1.71, p < .001) and shorter latencies for small than large coins (ratio = 0.90, p < .001; Fig. 5). Because the model includes only successfully observed coins, it primarily characterizes the early portion of the detection process, where most observations occur. It therefore cannot capture the late reversal, which emerges only once never-observed coins are censored, and the hazard is allowed to vary over time. The observed fast-coin sample is truncated toward short latencies because fast coins that are not detected quickly are often never detected at all. The difference between the observed-only and the censoring-aware models with respect to speed is not a contradiction, but rather a consequence of censoring and non-proportional hazards. The main effects of size (p = .173) and speed (p = .048) were non-significant and marginal, respectively.

Discussion

This study provides a benchmark for measuring gaze-based target detection in immersive virtual reality. Consistent with findings from traditional 2D visual search paradigms, the canonical predictors of detection-eccentricity, size, and motion-were all reliable predictors of gaze-based target detection. The present findings indicate, however, that these determinants do not behave equivalently when search is embedded in an active, dynamic three-dimensional task and modeled as an event process. Eccentricity and target size had the strongest effect on target detection. The likelihood of coin detection decreased as eccentricity increased; larger coins were more likely to be detected than smaller coins. Speed, however, produced a more informative diagnostic pattern: a dissociation between cumulative and instantaneous detection. Overall, slow coins were detected more often in the binary model, but speed had no robust effect on the instantaneous hazard in the censoring-aware latency model. This pattern is more consistent with an availability account, as opposed to a salience account. This finding signifies that cumulative detection and detection dynamics are not interchangeable outcomes when studying immersive visual search.

The eccentricity gradient was the clearest validation result in this study, establishing that active viewing does not eliminate the cost of peripheral target placement. Even with free viewing, targets at greater eccentricities were less likely to be detected. This finding is consistent with prior work that has shown that eccentricity restricts object recognition and detection (Carrasco et al., 1995; Rosenholtz, 2016; Strasburger et al., 2011.) This study is not a replication of fixed-display eccentricity effects. In immersive VR, initial eccentricity is best understood as a composite of several correlated task demands rather than a single perceptual quantity. In addition to reflecting a target's gaze-relative position, it may also determine whether: the target falls within the headset's field of view, whether a head movement is required, how quickly the target can be brought into central vision, and how reliably gaze proximity can be estimated. The implication is that gaze-relative eccentricity functions as a task-level determinant of detection. As gaze-relative eccentricity increases, the likelihood of target detection declines.

Target size also showed a strong benchmark effect. As expected, large coins were more likely to be detected, and the censoring-aware model indicated that size influenced detection hazard. In other words, size affected not only whether a coin was eventually detected, but also how quickly it was detected when it was still available. Although they point to the same conclusion, these are two distinct characterizations of this data. The effect exists under both outcome definitions, making it unlikely to be an artifact of outcome definition (binary vs. latency), supporting the interpretation of size as a robust determinant of detection in this task. The gaze-coin angular-distance result offers nuance to the size effects observed. Gaze remained closer to small coins than large coins across the eccentricity range, despite small coins being less likely to be detected overall. This measure should not be interpreted as a fixation-duration measure, as it was averaged over each coin's lifetime, but it does suggest that smaller targets need more precise gaze alignment in the search process. This pattern is consistent with the idea that target size affects not only visibility, but also the spatial precision required for acquisition. This interpretation is consistent with findings from previous target-acquisition studies, where smaller targets require greater precision demands (MacKenzie, 1992; Namnakani et al., 2025). Overall, this result illustrates why object-level gaze measures are advantageous in VR benchmarks. While detection probability indicates if a target was acquired, gaze-target distance characterizes the geometry of acquisition. This distinction has significant consequences for immersive environments, where gaze is mapped onto objects in three-dimensional scenes (Clay et al., 2019; Kapp et al., 2021; Ugwitz et al., 2022) Speed was more instructive methodologically than substantively. The value of the findings lie less in characterizing physical salience than on the consequences of how detection is operationalized. A salience account would predict that motion helps capture attention, and therefore faster targets would be more easily detected (Franconeri & Simons, 2003; Itti & Koch, 2001). A central methodological implication of this benchmark study is that detection probability and detection hazard can support different conclusions about the same predictor. According to the binary detection model, slower coins were detected more often. In isolation, this tells us that slower targets are easier to detect. But the Cox model adds more nuance. Instead of asking "Among the coins that were seen, how fast were they noticed?" it asks, "Across every coin, including those that were never seen, what was the rate of being noticed at each moment it was available?" In the Cox model, coins that were never detected are no longer censored observations, and speed no longer showed the same robust effect on detection hazard. Reframing the question around the rate of detection per unit of available time, instead of whether detection happened or not, changes the impact of speed's effect. The effect largely disappears, suggesting that speed is operating on the opportunity for detection, not the rate of detection given the opportunity. There are several reasons why slow coins may have been detected more often that are not due to permoment salience. Perhaps they were available longer, moved through the field of view more slowly, and/or were easier to stabilize with gaze. Regardless, slow coins were not necessarily detected more often because they were more salient at any given moment. The Cox model therefore prevents the binary model from being overinterpreted, shifting the interpretation from a salience to an opportunity account.

The dwell-time results further support the idea that speed affected post-detection gaze behavior rather than simply increasing momentary salience before detection. Slow coins produced longer dwell times than fast coins, indicating that once a coin was noticed, gaze remained on or near slow-moving targets for longer. This pattern is consistent with the task dynamics: a slow coin would remain within a useful gaze region longer before drifting away, whereas a fast coin may be acquired but leave the gaze region more quickly. Size also independently predicted dwell time, with large coins holding gaze slightly longer than small coins. Thus, dwell time provides a post-acquisition complement to the detection and Cox results. Speed and size influenced not only whether coins were detected, but also how long gaze remained engaged with them after detection. Importantly, the model-level significance tests should be interpreted as evidence that speed and size independently predicted dwell time overall, rather than as pairwise tests of any single plotted group.

This benchmark did not only capture whether participants detected targets, but also how they searched for them. Saccade amplitude increased with target eccentricity, showing that not only did these targets produce lower detection rates, but they also required larger orienting responses. Part of the challenge associated with eccentricity in immersive VR is the acquisition problem. Peripheral targets are not just more difficult to see; they require larger gaze shifts to acquire. This interpretation is consistent with the literature on saccadic targeting (Kowler, 2011), as well as VR-specific findings demonstrating that gaze shifts are impacted by eye, head, and body movements (Sidenmark & Gellersen, 2019). The saccade-count result furthers this interpretation. Slow coins elicited substantially more saccades than fast coins,, suggesting that even when the censoring-aware latency model did not support a speed effect on detection hazard, motion still altered search effort. This may explain why the speed effect cannot be reduced to either "motion salience" or "no effect". The search process was structured by motion, but its effect was dependent on the outcome being modeled.

Together, the detection and oculomotor results jointly show that this benchmark captured more than a binary detection outcome. The results characterize how canonical determinants of visual search function in immersive VR across different layers of behavior, including detection probability, gaze-target geometry, and oculomotor sampling. Eccentricity and size influenced whether coins were detected, gaze-coin distance characterized the spatial precision of acquisition, and saccade measures showed that target properties affected active orienting and search effort. The study's second contribution is methodological. The speed analysis, via the censoring-aware Cox model, revealed a more nuanced pattern than the binary model alone suggested.

5.1 Implications

The distinction between cumulative detection success and detection dynamics is broadly relevant for VR eye-tracking methods because immersive tasks often contain unresolved events. Objects may be missed, disappear, leave the field of view, or fail to meet a gaze-defined event threshold before the trial ends. When analyses include only successful detections, detection difficulty is effectively converted into missing latency data. A censoring-aware framework avoids that problem by modeling both acquisition and failure-to-acquire within the same event process. Thus, the Cox model is not simply a statistical addon, it reframes what "detection" means in immersive environments. It changes the question being asked and enables researchers to determine whether a predictor influences the likelihood of eventual detection, the temporal rate of detection, or both.

The results of this study do not show that immersive VR is equivalent to a 2D desktop display or to real-world vision, nor that every canonical visual search effect transfers exactly into immersive environments. Rather, they demonstrate that two core physical constraints -eccentricity and size -remain strongly quantifiable in a free-viewing dynamic 3D task, behaving much as the classical literature would predict. Speed is the more instructive case. Rather than replicating a familiar effect, it showed how the same predictor can take on a different role in an active task. Because a moving coin's speed shapes where and possibly how long it remains available for detection, its influence in this paradigm operated through opportunity for detection more than through moment-to-moment salience. Whereas motion is often treated as a cue that guides attention, here its effect was better characterized as one of availability. Immersive VR, then, preserves the canonical spatial constraints of visual search while also revealing how they become entangled with the timing and structure of an active task -something that cannot be exposed by a 2D display.

These results carry practical implications for both measurement and design. Small targets at high eccentricity are the most likely to be missed, and faster motion should not be assumed to rescue a peripherally placed element. These findings speak most directly to immersive experiences that depend on a user noticing targets in a wide, free-viewing field. In attentiontraining and focus-based applications, the strong, quantifiable roles of eccentricity and size offer principled levers for calibrating difficulty -making detection harder by moving a target to the periphery rather than by speeding it up, since speed appears to change the window of opportunity more than the moment-to-moment demand on attention. The same logic extends to training simulations that hinge on peripheral detection, such as aviation, where a safetyrelevant cue placed in the far field should not be assumed detectable simply because it is large or in motion. In these settings, placement is the more dependable design constraint. Prior work on immersive attention guidance suggests that peripheral or nonvisual cues can improve detection and orienting under some conditions (Meghanathan et al., 2021; Schmitz et al., 2020). The present benchmark complements that work by quantifying how physical target properties structure baseline detection performance in a targetdetection task.

Limitations

This benchmark's scope is bounded by several limitations. First and foremost, this study does not include a matched desktop or real-world comparison condition. While the results show that eccentricity and size effects are observable in this task, they do not estimate whether the magnitude of these effects is equivalent across platforms. Second, both the size and speed manipulations varied world-space object properties rather than their retinal counterparts. In a dynamic 3D scene, both a target's angular size and angular velocity change continuously with its distance and trajectory. While the results from this study establish that the scale and speed manipulations affected gaze-based detection, they cannot isolate the world-space property from the continuous change in its retinal projection. To distinguish them, angular size and angular velocity would need to be logged frame-by-frame, which is an informative direction for future work. This limitation is most consequential for interpreting the effects in perceptual terms, that is, for attributing detection directly to angular size or velocity. It is far less consequential for the applied implications developed above. In practice, XR designers specify an element's physical scale and motion in world coordinates, not the retinal projection it will produce for a given viewer at a given moment. Consequently, the finding that world-space scale and speed reliably shape detection is directly relevant for XR design, even though the underlying retinal quantities were not isolated. Finally, because prior VR experience was an inclusion criterion, the present results characterize detection in VR-experienced observers. Whether the same benchmark results hold for firsttime users, whose viewing behavior may be shaped by novelty effects associated with immersive VR environments, remains open.

Conclusion

This study establishes a reproducible benchmark for gaze-based target detection in immersive VR. Consistent with findings from traditional 2D visual-search paradigms, eccentricity and size strongly govern target detection, whereas the influence of speed is more nuanced and can support various interpretations. The key methodological takeaway is the demonstration that missed targets contain meaningful information and should be retained as censored events in latency analyses. By pairing object-level gaze measures with time-to-event modeling, this study offers a more comprehensive approach in which detection is treated as an event process rather than a binary outcome.

Figure 1. Observation probability as a function of eccentricity, by target speed: Points show raw binned rates (points, ±95% CI) with the fitted model prediction (dashed). Detection declines steeply with eccentricity; slow coins are observed more often than fast throughout.

Figure 2.

Figure2. Fixed-effect odds ratios (±95% CI) for the physical determinants of observation probability. Increasing eccentricity (per SD) and small size reduced the odds of observation while slow speed increased them. Each estimate is the unique effect controlling for the others.

Figure 3.

Figure 3. Scaled Schoenfeld residuals for the speed term (slow vs. fast) plotted against time since spawn. Under proportional hazards, the fitted spline would remain approximately flat around the dashed zero reference. Instead, the spline (blue) is below zero early in the trial and rises above it later, crossing zero at approximately 4-5s. This pattern indicates that the effect of speed on detection hazard reverses direction over the trial rather than remaining constant, violating the proportional hazards assumption. Beyond ~10s few coins remain at risk of first detection (most fast coins have already been collected, removed, or censored), so the widening confidence band in the right portion of the plot reflects sparse data and should not be interpreted as a strengthening late effect.

Figure 4.

Figure4. Cumulative proportion of coins detected over time, by speed and size. A higher proportion of slow coins were eventually detected, and large coins were detected more often overall. The separation reflects differences in how many coins were ultimately found, not in how quickly -consistent with the crossing-hazards pattern in Fig.3, where the speed effect reverses direction over the trial rather than favoring one speed throughout.

Figure 5.

Figure 5. Latency to first observation among detected coins, by speed and size (observed-only sample). Slow coins show longer observed latencies; the observed fast-coin sample is truncated toward short latencies by selection.

Figure 6 :

Figure 6: Slow coins held gaze longer once detected; size was a secondary but significant effect, with large coins held slightly longer than small coins.

Figure 7.

Figure 7. Predicted mean saccade amplitude as a function of spawn eccentricity, value-free benchmark conditions. Amplitude scales with eccentricity; the absolute effect is small.

Figure 8.

Figure 8. Mean gaze-coin angular distance by spawn eccentricity and coin size. Distance is averaged over each coin's on-screen lifetime; lower values indicate gaze positioned closer to the coin. Gaze remained closer to small coins than large across the eccentricity range.

Figure 9.

Figure 9. Saccade count by speed: slow-coin trials trigger far more saccades than fast-coin trials, consistent with the greater tracking effort of moving-target search.
