Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Full text

Abstract

Artificial intelligence increasingly influences high-stakes decisions, from employment and education to healthcare and public services. When such judgments are disputed, affected people may receive an explanation without being able to reach an authority empowered to change the outcome or remedy its consequences. We introduce cultural repairability: the institutional capacity to review an AI-mediated decision, correct the outcome and downstream records, provide meaningful restoration, and learn from failure through procedures people can understand and use. The framework addresses a visibilityaccountability asymmetry: people are exposed to judgments about their standing but lack effective means of obtaining an answer or remedy. Culture matters because exposure, legitimate authority, and appropriate restoration may be understood differently across settings. Language accessibility also determines whether review mechanisms are usable in practice. We identify three measurable cultural mechanisms: the meaning of exposure, the credibility of review, and the meaning of restoration. We further distinguish affected people's appraisals from institutional actors' responsibility and capacity to respond. Four falsifiable propositions specify how reviewer authority and audience shape responses to repair, and how these effects vary with cultural orientation, institutional history, and the form of restoration. They predict whether people appeal or withdraw, how they recalibrate trust, and whether they remain willing to engage. The resulting Repairability Principle establishes a common floor of substantive review, correction, restoration, and prevention of recurrence, while allowing people to choose how restoration is delivered. Cultural repairability therefore shifts global AI accountability from whether procedures appear acceptable to whether institutions can correct harm in practice.

1. Why AI accountability must be culturally repairable

Artificial intelligence increasingly informs decisions that shape people's opportunities and well-being, including decisions about employment, education, healthcare, benefits, and access to services (1, 2). Yet a fundamental question remains: when an AI-mediated judgment is disputed, can the affected person reach an identifiable authority that is both obliged and empowered to review the decision, correct its consequences, and prevent recurrence? Transparency or explanation without the capacity to change an outcome leaves this central accountability problem unresolved.

We call the required institutional capacity cultural repairability: the ability to review a disputed AImediated decision, correct both the decision and its downstream effects, provide meaningful restoration, and learn from failure through procedures that affected people can understand, trust, and use. A welfarerisk label, for example, may be removed from one record while continuing to influence a person's treatment, opportunities, or reputation. Accountability therefore requires more than explaining or reversing a judgment; it requires repairing the material and social consequences that the judgment has produced.

This problem is culturally situated because exposure, authority, and restoration do not necessarily have the same meaning across settings. Cross-linguistic studies show that LLM behavior can vary with prompt language (3, 4), while experiments with human participants suggest that responses to artificial partners may differ across samples (5). These findings do not establish fixed national dispositions. Rather, they show why neither AI behavior nor human responses observed in one linguistic or social setting can be assumed to generalize universally. Cultural repairability addresses the institutional implication: accountability must remain substantively effective when AI systems operate across languages, cultural orientations, and historically different relationships with authority.

The consequences of an AI-mediated judgment depend on the institutional system in which the model operates. Although a model may generate a classification or recommendation, the deploying organization determines how that output is used, how widely and persistently it is recorded, and whether affected people can challenge it. Institutions also decide who has authority to overturn a decision, correct downstream records, and provide meaningful redress. Yet responsibility is often dispersed among technology vendors, managers, and frontline staff, making it difficult to identify a person capable of changing the outcome (6, 7). This fragmentation is especially consequential because automated classifications can circulate across organizational systems and shape opportunities while appearing to provide neutral measurements of social worth (8). The same model may therefore have very different effects depending on how authority, responsibility, review, and remedy are organized. The appropriate unit of analysis is the institution that defines the system's purpose, acts on its outputs, and determines whether resulting harms can be effectively repaired.

Existing frameworks identify necessary parts of this problem but stop short of repair. Transparency can reveal a process without creating a route to action, and explanations may be technically accurate yet unintelligible to their audience (9) (10) (11). Contestability and procedural justice address challenge, voice, and outcome control, but a fair procedure does not establish that downstream harm was corrected (12) (13) (14) (15). Responsibility-gap research explains dispersed control (16) (17) (18), and trust-repair research examines restored relationships after organizational failure (19). Cultural repairability integrates these functions and adds a missing criterion: whether substantive correction and restoration remain usable and meaningful across linguistic, cultural, and institutional settings.

The visibility-accountability asymmetry names the motivating configuration. Perceived evaluative visibility concerns whether an institutional representation appears consequential for a person's opportunities, treatment, or standing. Effective answerability requires an identifiable authority that is obliged to respond and empowered to reconsider the decision (20). Full accountability additionally requires correction, remedy, and institutional learning. Answerability therefore makes repair possible, but neither an available appeal nor an accepted explanation demonstrates that repair occurred.

Culture shapes repair through the audiences before whom evaluation matters, the credibility of reviewing authorities, and the meaning of restoration (19, (21) (22) (23) (24) (25) (26) (27). These mechanisms should be measured through orientations, meanings, language accessibility, and institutional experience rather than inferred from nationality. Repeated institutional responses may also change expectations about whether authorities can be challenged.

Cultural repairability applies to consequential decisions in which an institution acts on an AI-generated output and has the authority to review or change the resulting outcome. It does not assume that every AI error reflects cultural differences, nor does it imply that legal rights or minimum protections should vary across groups. All institutions should provide substantive review, effective correction, and meaningful remedy. Cultural variation concerns how people experience exposure, whether they regard review as credible and accessible, and what form of restoration adequately addresses the harm. The following sections distinguish an institution's capacity to provide repair from an affected person's evaluation of that process. They then identify the relevant cultural mechanisms, derive falsifiable propositions, and show how these principles can be implemented and audited.

2. The visibility-answerability configuration

Accountability unfolds across recognition, review, correction, restoration, and learning rather than through a single disclosure or appeal. A failure must be recognized and attributed; an empowered authority must then reconsider the decision, correct its consequences, and reduce recurrence. People evaluate each stage through prior expectations about institutional legitimacy and efficacy (19, 20, 26). This sequence distinguishes a formally available procedure from a repair process that can change outcomes.

The framework distinguishes four related constructs. Perceived evaluative visibility concerns exposure before a consequential audience and differs from actual profiling: people may be extensively profiled without knowing it, whereas a single public label can feel highly exposing (28, 29). Effective answerability refers to the institution's capacity to review and change an outcome. Perceived credibility is the expectation that the responsible authority can and will act fairly. Repair occurs when the decision and its material or social consequences have actually been corrected. An appeal process may therefore have genuine authority yet appear futile to those affected. Conversely, it may appear reassuring while lacking the power to reverse an outcome. Credibility and explanation can support repair, but neither demonstrates that repair has occurred.

Figure 1A crosses perceived evaluative visibility with effective institutional answerability. Unanswerable invisibility combines low perceived visibility with low answerability, whereas low-visibility answerability combines low perceived visibility with high answerability. High perceived visibility with low answerability defines the visibility-accountability asymmetry; high levels of both define accountable legibility. These labels describe configurations of perceived exposure and reviewer capacity, not completed repair. Low perceived visibility may coexist with hidden profiling and does not establish privacy. High answerability creates a route to correction without guaranteeing remedy or restored trust. Figure 1B connects this configuration to appraisal, valuation, action, and institutional repair. Its arrows summarize proposed relationships rather than an established or obligatory sequence: cultural meanings can shape both the anticipated value of appeal and evaluation of a completed response, and appeal or withdrawal does not guarantee repair. This distinction also explains why a nominal human-in-the-loop is insufficient. Reviewers may defer to an algorithm, lack relevant evidence, or have no discretion to reverse an outcome (30, 31). Formal oversight can then legitimate a faulty process rather than correct it (32). Effective answerability requires an identifiable reviewer with access to the case and real decision authority. Logs and audit trails matter only when such a role can use them (6, 33). The difference between nominal and effective review provides the institutional basis for the psychological account that follows.

Institutional arrangements change the expected benefits and costs of responding to a decision. Exposure may increase anticipated devaluation, while weak answerability lowers the expected efficacy of appeal. Procedural-justice research supports the importance of voice and process control (34), but the proposed interaction remains to be tested. The prediction concerns behavior as well as judgments: whether people appeal, disclose information, continue engaging, or withdraw depends on how they appraise both the threatened harm and the available response.

Social emotions offer one route through which these appraisals may influence behavior. Perceived devaluation before a relevant audience can evoke shame or humiliation, whereas perceived injustice may elicit anger. Institutional actors face a different problem: guilt becomes relevant when they accept responsibility and believe they can repair the harm. Research on self-conscious and moral emotions emphasizes appraisals of agency, audience, and relationship rather than fixed responses to events (35) (36) (37). The framework therefore does not assume that AI replaces guilt with shame. It asks how institutional arrangements alter the interpretations from which several emotional responses can arise.

Institutional procedures determine whether a harmful decision can be corrected, but cognitive processes shape how people respond to that correction. Affected individuals must evaluate how the decision changed their social standing, identify who is responsible, estimate whether an appeal can succeed, and update their beliefs about the institution after observing its response. Research on the cognitive processes underlying social valuation, responsibility, and moral learning specifies candidate mechanisms for these evaluations. Moral choices integrate anticipated social evaluation with self-interest and moral cost (38). Attributions of responsibility can elicit guilt and compensatory behavior (39, 40), while social learning changes the value assigned to moral actions (41, 42). These findings motivate examining how people anticipate social devaluation, assign responsibility, judge whether repair will be effective, and revise their beliefs as the process unfolds. They help explain why the same institutional response may restore trust for one person but appear ineffective or insincere to another. However, they provide evidence for component cognitive processes rather than direct evidence for cultural repairability, an AIspecific neural signature, or the proposed institutional pathways. A parsimonious valuation account makes the framework testable. The expected utility of appealing a decision should increase when correction is considered both likely and valuable, and decrease when an appeal requires substantial effort or creates further social exposure. Reviewer authority should mainly affect whether people expect the appeal to succeed, whereas the relevance of the audience should shape its anticipated social costs and benefits. Feelings such as shame, humiliation, or anger may connect these appraisals to behavior, as may the motivation to restore one's social standing. Their effects should not be assumed to produce withdrawal or protest in a fixed way. Competing models should instead be compared by how well they predict choices and response times in held-out data, rather than by whether a single emotion correlates with the decision to appeal.

The affected person and the institutional actor must therefore remain analytically separate. A person can experience devaluation or futility without believing that a particular employee intended harm. An employee may recognize a failure yet lack the authority to remedy it. For this reason, a model-generated explanation or apology cannot carry accountability on its own: language does not create institutional duties or the power to alter consequences. Figure 1B primarily depicts the affected person's appraisaland-action pathway; institutional actors' responsibility, guilt, and repair motivation are discussed here but are not separately depicted. Because judgments of devaluation, authority, and repair all depend on social meaning, the psychological account leads directly to culture.

3. How culture enters the visibility-answerability configuration

Culture comprises socially learned meanings, practices, and institutional histories within as well as between societies (21, 27, 43). People participate in overlapping traditions, models of self shape cognition and motivation, and institutions can change those patterns over time (23, 24, 44, 45). The framework therefore treats national residence as a sampling context, not an individual cultural profile, and requires direct measures of relevant orientations and experience.

Language accessibility enables all three cultural mechanisms. A person must understand what is being judged, who can reconsider it, and what a remedy would change. An explanation may be formally available yet unusable because of mistranslation, unfamiliar administrative terms, or a missing route to action. Uneven multilingual performance and alignment effects make these failures plausible (46) (47) (48). Language can also alter model behavior itself, as the opening game studies illustrate. Institutions must therefore test both decision behavior across languages and whether affected people can understand and use the repair process.

3.1. Meaning of exposure

The first mechanism is the meaning of exposure. A classification threatens standing through a particular audience and relationship. Dignity, face, and honor describe orientations that vary within populations (21). Concern about relational exposure may increase the anticipated cost of a public appeal; concern about public standing may increase the value of visible vindication. These are hypotheses about appraisals, not assignments of preferred remedies. Treating LLM-generated group profiles as evidence about individuals would risk cultural flattening (49).

Normative climate adds a second layer. Tighter cultural settings tend to enforce social norms more strongly, whereas looser settings permit wider behavioral variation (50, 51). In a seven-country vignette study, managers in tighter contexts relied more on AI advice, particularly when it carried government endorsement (52). This does not demonstrate a repair mechanism. It does suggest, however, that institutional authorization and normative expectations jointly shape reliance. The framework extends that observation by asking whether tightness also changes the social cost of classification and the legitimacy of contesting it.

3.2. Credibility of review

The second mechanism is the credibility of review. Legitimacy depends partly on perceived fairness and the rightfulness of authority (26). AI enters institutions with histories of surveillance, discrimination, colonial administration, or reliable provision (53), and historical experience can leave durable differences in trust (25). We hypothesize that these experiences shape the expected efficacy and legitimacy of appeal even when reviewer powers are identical. Evidence on source trust in misinformation interventions offers an adjacent comparison, not a direct test of institutional review (54).

The categories embedded in AI systems raise a related problem. Systems such as Delphi attempt to predict whether ordinary situations are morally acceptable, but performance for one population cannot stand in for human morality as a whole (55). NormBank makes context explicit, while EgoNormia documents continuing limitations in physical-social norm understanding (56, 57). LLMs also make systematic errors when estimating moral values across nations (58). Together, these findings support a limited conclusion: model-generated judgments about blame or appropriate response should remain open to institutional challenge rather than being treated as moral authority (59).

Broader representation in training data can improve performance without settling whose values should govern a decision. Resources such as CultureBank and Culture Cartography reveal knowledge that standard corpora may miss, and research on global representation shows how alignment redistributes performance across populations (47, 60, 61). Cultural accountability therefore concerns more than representativeness. It concerns who can question the values encoded in practice and how those values can be revised.

3.3. Meaning of repair

The third mechanism is the meaning of repair. An apology may communicate responsibility in one context and relational concern in another (22, 62). Public correction may restore standing after visible injury but aggravate unwanted exposure. Work on norm violation and cooperation with artificial agents likewise cautions against assuming invariant responses (5, 63). The proposed mechanism is the recipient's appraisal that a response addresses the actual harm. After material correction is held constant, audience and measured orientation should help explain whether a form of restoration reduces threatened devaluation and supports renewed engagement.

3.4. How repair can change cultural expectations

Repair may also change cultural expectations. Legal-socialization research links encounters with authorities to developing views of legitimacy, and organizational research examines how responses to failure rebuild trust (19, 64). AI strategy-transmission studies and work on homogenization provide analogies rather than direct evidence about appeals (65, 66). We propose that successful repair can teach that institutions are challengeable, whereas repeated futile appeals can teach the opposite. Evidence for cultural change requires more than individual updating: revised expectations must spread to peers, new members, or shared practice, and institutional learning must alter procedures rather than merely perceptions.

Cultural repairability is not cultural relativism. Consequential decisions must remain reviewable and correctable even when explanations and preferred forms of restoration vary. Ethical principles require institutions able to implement them (67), and apparently shared principles can differ in meaning across contexts (68). Cultural adaptation cannot justify discrimination, weaken rights, or substitute a locally acceptable gesture for substantive remedy. It should make a common institutional floor effective by ensuring that review and restoration are understandable, credible, and usable.

4. Testable propositions and a cumulative research program

The four propositions in Table 1 separate institutional manipulations, proposed psychological processes, moderators, and outcomes. Future experiments should vary evaluative exposure, reviewer authority, and repair form while holding the disputed decision constant. Candidate mediators include threatened devaluation, shame or humiliation, anger, expected appeal efficacy, perceived legitimacy, and motivation to seek restoration. Measured cultural orientations and institutional history are moderators; appeal, withdrawal, correction acceptance, calibrated trust, and future engagement are outcomes. Crosslanguage comparisons require measurement invariance (69), and mediation requires temporal separation and defensible identification assumptions. The propositions are supported only if the specified interactions and pathways improve out-of-sample prediction over simpler models.

Proposition 1: culturally interpreted visibility and effective answerability interact. High visibility should reduce procedural justice and trust most strongly when answerability is weak. Threatened devaluation and shame or humiliation are proposed pathways to withdrawal when appeal seems futile; anger and motivation to restore standing may instead support challenge when effective action seems possible. Audience relevance and measured orientations should moderate these pathways. Existing work supports their components (12, 14, 15, 34), not the complete AI-specific model. Additive institutional effects or absent predicted indirect pathways would challenge the corresponding claims.

Proposition 2: responses to answerability depend on power and historical credibility. An empowered reviewer should influence judgments and behavior more than a nominal human-in-the-loop label, although the advantage should be smaller where authorities have rarely corrected past errors. Existing research supports the distinction between formal and effective oversight (30) (31) (32). Its interaction with institutional history is under-tested. Expected appeal efficacy and perceived legitimacy are candidate mediators, distinct from actual reviewer capacity.

Proposition 3: cultural fit shapes the effectiveness of substantive repair. After the same error is corrected, measured orientations and audience should help predict which response best restores standing and calibrated trust. Research on honor, face, dignity, apology, and norm violation supports variation in response (21, 22, 62, 63). Direct tests in AI-mediated decisions should compare matched material remedies and test whether perceived restoration explains subsequent correction acceptance and engagement. A private injury need not benefit from public vindication.

Proposition 4: repair practices update collective expectations. Repeated substantive correction should increase later expected contestability and engagement more than explanation or apology alone. Futile appeals should decrease them. Institutional trust repair and legal socialization motivate this prediction (19, 64); AI cultural-transmission studies supply an analogy (65, 66). Tests must distinguish individual learning from transmission to peers or new institutional members. Without transmission or convergence beyond directly exposed people, the evidence would support personal updating rather than cultural change. A cumulative research program should combine controlled experiments, multilingual field studies, and longitudinal institutional data. Multilevel models can separate individual orientations from organizational and national context. Preregistered interactions, measurement-invariance tests, and outof-sample prediction can establish whether cultural measures explain behavior beyond country membership. LLM-generated participants are not an adequate substitute because they may reproduce uniformity and distort cultural variation (70, 71). Field studies should additionally measure whether correction reaches downstream records and whether expectations of contestability spread beyond directly affected individuals.

Candidate computational mechanisms should be evaluated by comparing how well they explain observed behavior and predict choices and response times in held-out data (38, 41, 42, (72) (73) (74) (75) (76) (77) (78) (79) (80). Repeated appeal tasks could compare models in which reviewer authority changes the expected efficacy of an appeal, audience composition alters its anticipated social value, and previous repair outcomes update beliefs about the institution. These models should be compared with simpler alternatives that omit one or more of these computations. Parameter recovery and prediction of held-out choices and response times are necessary before the inferred latent variables can be interpreted. Separate tasks for institutional reviewers could examine how perceived responsibility produces guilt or motivation to repair, and whether these responses lead to corrective action when the reviewer has sufficient authority. Neural measurements could subsequently investigate how validated computational variables are implemented in the brain. Emotion ratings or regional activation alone, however, cannot identify the computations underlying AI accountability.

5. From cultural repairability to institutional design

AI-mediated decisions distribute knowledge and control across developers, vendors, deploying organizations, and reviewers (81, 82). Distribution is not itself a responsibility gap. The gap becomes actionable when no role is expected to understand the decision, empowered to intervene, and obliged to repair its consequences. Blaming the most visible operator can then create a moral crumple zone while leaving the wider system unchanged (16). Similar attribution patterns appear when people blame human drivers more than automated vehicles for comparable mistakes (83).

AI can deepen this problem through technical opacity, contractual separation from vendors, and routine claims that an outcome was determined automatically. Delegating decisions to an algorithm may also create moral distance and normalize misconduct when similar judgments are repeated (84). Accountability should therefore not depend on whichever staff member happens to encounter the affected person or receive the complaint. Before deployment, the institution should designate a role with clear authority to preserve relevant evidence, reopen disputed cases, correct decisions and resulting records, and ensure that failures inform subsequent practice.

Effective answerability assigns distributed participation to a role that is both obliged to respond and empowered to act (20, 32). That role must be able to inspect the record, suspend or reverse the decision, propagate corrections to downstream uses, authorize remedy, and initiate organizational learning. A model-generated explanation or apology cannot provide these capacities.

The Repairability Principle requires any institution using AI to inform consequential decisions to retain the capacity to reconsider disputed outcomes and correct both the original decision and its wider effects. Institutions must also address resulting material and social harm and learn from failures to reduce recurrence. Cultural repairability extends this principle by requiring pathways to review and redress to remain understandable, credible, and accessible across languages and institutional contexts. Its success is judged by whether correction and restoration occur in practice, rather than by procedural compliance or perceived acceptability alone.

Consider an erroneous AI-generated welfare-risk classification. A person who values individual autonomy may regard individualized reasons and independent appeal as especially important. Someone whose concern is unwanted relational exposure may prefer a discreet correction. When standing has been damaged publicly, visible vindication may be necessary. The examples do not assign preferences by nationality. They illustrate how the same institutional error can require different forms of restoration while leaving the underlying right to review and correction unchanged.

Institutions should ask affected people which available form of restoration addresses their harm. They should not use AI, nationality, or demographic proxies to infer that preference. Meaningful options may include private correction, public vindication, independent appeal, relational mediation, or compensation, where appropriate. A person's choice about the form of restoration must not reduce the underlying right to review and remedy. Language provides a second example. A notice does not create answerability merely because it exists.

Poor translation, unfamiliar administrative language, or the absence of a clear next step can make a technically accurate explanation unusable (11). Institutions should therefore test whether people can understand the reason for a decision, locate the review process, and reach someone with authority to act.

Human review provides a third example. A reviewer who cannot access the record or reverse the outcome offers symbolic oversight rather than answerability (32). An empowered reviewer can connect explanation to correction and remedy. Table 2 translates this distinction into six institutional functions. The point is practical: formal disclosure does little if people do not notice it or cannot act on it. In one four-country experiment, only three of 3,226 participants who were shown a privacy-notice link opened it (85).

Table 2. Institutional repairability architecture

Repairability function Observable institutional capacity

1. Identifiable accountable deployer

Affected people can name an institution with authority and duty to answer, reconsider, and remedy.

2. Actionable repair notice

The notice explains the decision and AI's role, identifies relevant reasons and data, and provides an accessible route and deadline for review.

3. Institution-in-the-loop

A reviewer has the record, competence, protected discretion, time, duty, and authority to suspend or reverse the decision.

4. Propagating correction

The outcome, relevant source records, and downstream uses are corrected, and the affected person is told what changed.

5. Proportionate restoration

Where warranted, correction is paired with reversal, reinstatement, compensation, restoration of standing, or institutional acknowledgment.

6. Non-recurrence Serious or repeated failures trigger root-cause analysis, system or process change, vendor remediation, and oversight reporting.

The functions in Table 2 can be audited through the experience of a disputed case. Can the affected person identify the responsible institution, reach a competent reviewer, obtain correction, and see that correction carried through to downstream records? Outcomes such as reversal, response time, recurrence, and restored trust should be examined across languages and relevant cultural orientations.

Procurement contracts should also ensure that deployers can access the records and vendor cooperation needed for repair. A system that cannot support necessary individual correction is not suitable for consequential use.

The emotional account also informs how these functions are audited. Confidential review can reduce further exposure; acknowledgment can address experienced devaluation; and reviewer authority can turn repair motivation into action. Distress or anger should never determine eligibility for correction, and expressed institutional remorse should never substitute for a completed remedy.

Implementation should involve affected communities without treating participation as a remedy in itself. Participation can become symbolic when it leaves institutional power unchanged (86), whereas codesigned procedures can translate fairness concerns into repeatable practice (87). Cultural responsiveness must therefore be paired with an independent route of challenge, an empowered reviewer, and substantive remedy. Its purpose is to make accountability work across settings while preserving the same minimum rights to review, correction, restoration, and non-recurrence.

6. Conclusion

AI systems can scale judgment without scaling answerability. Across linguistic and social settings, people may be classified in ways that affect their opportunities while remaining unable to reach an authority empowered and required to change the outcome. Cultural repairability shifts attention from documenting variation in AI behavior or public acceptance to a more demanding question: what happens when an AI-mediated judgment causes harm and is challenged?

The framework links people's cognitive and behavioral responses to the institutional capacity to correct that harm. Its propositions examine how authority, audience, cultural orientation, institutional history, and restorative practices influence whether people appeal, withdraw, or re-engage. The Repairability Principle establishes a common requirement: institutions must provide effective review, correct decisions and their consequences, offer meaningful restoration, and learn from failure. Cultural responsiveness determines whether people can understand and use these capacities and whether restoration addresses the harm they experienced. It does not justify weaker protections or remedies assigned by nationality.

The next empirical challenge is to test repair across languages, institutions, and time. The critical outcome is whether disputed judgments are actually corrected and whether trust becomes better calibrated-not whether an unrepairable system has been made easier to accept.
