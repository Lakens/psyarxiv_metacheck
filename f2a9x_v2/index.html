Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Research on predictive processing has shown that listeners anticipate upcoming linguistic information using linguistic cues, such as verb meaning. Beyond information in the input, listeners' prediction can also be influenced by top-down control, such as goals and strategies developed in response to a given task. However, it is unclear to what extent evaluations of task relevance modulate top-down control of prediction. We present evidence from two eye-tracking studies in the Visual World Paradigm that associative predictions are automatic and not influenced by implicit shifts in the task relevance of a predictive verb. However, when a predictive verb's task relevance is made explicit in the form of an instruction to predict, prediction is upregulated and initiated earlier irrespective of the verb's predictiveness, likely due to an enhancement of predictive visual search. Task relevance is thus modulatory for associative prediction when it is specified for listeners.

Introduction 1

Prediction, or the "preactivation of various aspects of upcoming [linguistic] information," (Kaan & Grüter, 2021, p. 3) is a well-attested mechanism undergirding language comprehension, with listeners pre-activating upcoming linguistic input on the basis of incrementally processed cues (for overviews, see Ferreira & Chantavarin, 2018; Huettig, 2015 Huettig, , 2025;; Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Magnuson, 2019). These cues are present at multiple levels of representation (i.e., syntactic, lexical, phonetic, suprasegmental, discursive) and are reliably and rapidly used by comprehenders to guide predictions about upcoming input. For example, listeners may use verb subcategorization information to predict the referent they are likely to hear next, as demonstrated in Altmann and Kamide (1999). Using the Visual World Paradigm (VWP), Altmann and Kamide (1999) found that first language (L1) English speakers rapidly pre-activated semantic information about an upcoming object (e.g., cake) after hearing a verb (e.g., eat) which constrained for the target object, as compared to when they saw the same object with a sentence containing a non-constraining verb (e.g., move). Similar effects have also been observed for a variety of other linguistic cues, such as verb transitivity (Arai & Keller, 2013); morphosyntactic markers of case (Mitsugi & Macwhinney, 2016), number (Koch et al., 2021), or gender (Lew-Williams & Fernald, 2007); and prosody (Weber et al., 2006). Given the widespread and variegated use of these predictive cues, some have endorsed a view of human brains as "predictive machines" (Clark, 2013, p. 181). However, this strong view of prediction as a fundamental component of human cognition and language processing is at odds with evidence that top-down factors, such as the context of prediction or a comprehender's goals and strategies, may modulate the time-course and extent of predictive behaviour (Huettig, 2015). Indeed, there is ample evidence that prediction is neither wholly pervasive or homogeneous, nor is it likely to be an optimal processing strategy for all populations (Federmeier, 2007; Huettig, 2015; Huettig & Mani, 2016; Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Mani & Huettig, 2012; Wlotko et al., 2012). These findings motivate a top-down and forward-modeling conceptualisation of the predictive mechanism which responds to context and resource-limitations, as opposed to a ballistic, 'all or nothing' mechanism (Huettig, 2015; Wlotko & Federmeier, 2015). These models of prediction center flexibility as a key feature of predictive processing while integrating resource limitations into the dynamics thereof; such resource-rational or 'resource-conscious' models (e.g., Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Ryskin & Nieuwland, 2023) are moreover in harmony with recent proposals of the role of utility in guiding efficient predictive processing (Kaan & Grüter, 2021).

Since prediction may not always be necessary or cost-effective given extant resource-limitations, cue reliability, or strategies (Huettig, 2015 (Huettig, , 2025;; Huettig & Mani, 2016), considerations of utility are expected to contribute substantially to the timing and extent of predictive behaviour (Kaan & Grüter, 2021).

To explain why predictive processing displays disparate time-courses across cue types and contexts, some proposals have sought to pair resource-constrained, utility-oriented views of prediction with observable prediction behaviour under a multi-stage, multi-representational, and hierarchical architecture of language-mediated prediction (e.g., Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Ryskin & Nieuwland, 2023; Salverda et al., 2011). One influential proposal by Pickering and Gambi (2018) argues for a two-stage model of prediction distinguishing between 'prediction-byassociation' and 'prediction-by-production' pathways (see also Pickering & Garrod, 2013).

During online processing, listeners may initially utilise associations between linguistic items (i.e., functional co-occurrences) to pre-activate upcoming representations; this "general-purpose" process is presumably cost-free and requires few cognitive resources to deploy, though this may come at the expense of prediction accuracy (Pickering & Gambi, 2018 , p. 1034). These predictions thus tend to occur earlier and may be outside of the conscious control of a comprehender to use strategically. Prediction-by-production, by contrast, is an optional route for comprehension which is resource-intensive and involves the covert activation of mechanisms of language production (see also Pickering & Strijkers, 2025). Germane to the arguments presented in this paper is the extent to which top-down control guides predictive processing within these bifurcated models. Under Pickering and Gambi (2018)'s proposed model, prediction-byassociation provides listeners with a rapid and cognitively 'cheap' track for prediction on the basis of extant semantic, functional, and phonological relations between words, with this 'fast track' supplemented by more conscious, effortful, and engaged prediction-by-production.

Prediction-by-association is moreover indifferent to global top-down factors such as whether prediction is required or viable as a processing strategy, since these predictions are generated automatically and so are ostensibly beyond the scope of a comprehender's conscious control.

However, this conceptualisation of associative prediction has yet to receive a comprehensive validation, though some work has shown that associative prediction effects may be attenuated or outright inhibited when input is perceived as unreliable (Brothers et al., 2017 (Brothers et al., , 2019;; Henry et al., 2017; Hopp, 2016; Kim et al., 2026). To clarify the dynamics of prediction-by-association with respect to top-down control via strategising, we turn to studies which manipulate the task relevance of associative prediction.

We define task relevance, as it pertains to prediction, as whether linguistic information is considered relevant and consequently used for predictive processing, especially given a listener's task demands and goal. This 'utility-driven' definition assumes linguistic representations are similarly activated but differentially used when they are task-(ir)relevant. We distinguish this working definition from a 'mechanistic' account, which defines task relevance in terms of activation. 2 Under a mechanistic definition, task-irrelevant information (e.g., a verb and its potential associated complements) is less activated during comprehension and so is less available for prediction, even if the overall goals and strategies of the comprehender have not changed (i.e., prediction is still a goal). Because this distinction is not a primary focus of this study, we use the phrase 'task relevance of prediction' and 'task relevance of the predictive cue' interchangeably.

Task relevance in prediction research has been most often manipulated via instructions that either explicitly upregulate prediction or which do not offer information about the viability of prediction for comprehension. By and large, prior studies supply evidence that associative predictions proceed automatically even when it is not clear if prediction is an optimal processing strategy, with the possibility of upregulation if prediction's task relevance is clarified. Altmann and Kamide (1999) provided early evidence of this latter possibility in the VWP: participants were asked to passively listen to sentences containing verbs which occasionally constrained for an upcoming direct object. Participants in the passive listening group (Experiment 2) as well as those of a group asked to judge if each sentence corresponded with the visual tableau (Experiment 1) displayed prediction effects by pre-activating target information after hearing a constraining verb, suggesting that listeners generated predictions to the verbal cue irrespective of their task. However, despite this apparent automaticity, predictive effects emerged earlier when participants had received the metalinguistic judgement task. Although associative prediction may occur automatically or by default, additional tasks which require attending to or engaging with stimuli are likely to upregulate attention and prediction. Importantly, this finding does not provide a complete picture of the scope of up-and downregulation possible under different task conditions and so leaves open relevant questions about the modulatory effect of task relevance.

More recent work has built on this initial finding and highlighted the influence of task relevance on the extent and time-course of predictive processing for both L1 and second language (L2) populations. Kim et al. (2026) presented 32 L1 Korean speakers with Korean sentences, some of which contained an honorific marking, while looking at a dual-image display.

Using the honorific marker, Kim et al. (2026) argued that listeners could generate predictions about the upcoming referent (e.g., teacher in the honorific condition, young man in the nonhonorific condition) and so would display earlier and more frequent anticipatory fixations to the relevant referent (see Kim et al., 2024, for proof of concept). Kim et al. (2026) also manipulated the task relevance of the honorific cue: some participants were asked to listen to each sentence and respond to a question about the referent while others were told to actively predict the upcoming referent. Thus, the predictive utility of the honorific cue was established as explicitly relevant to the participants' task in the latter condition, whereas it was unspecified in the former.

Both groups ultimately used the honorific cue to predict the upcoming referents, but participants who were given an explicit prediction task engaged in more robust and earlier predictive processing relative to the participants in the comprehension group. Kim et al. (2026) interpreted these results as indicative that prediction is modulated by top-down control in the form of processing goals and strategies, with listeners strategically integrating information about the relevance of the honorific cue into the predictive mechanism. Since use of the honorific marker to predict upcoming referents necessitates Korean listeners drawing on statistical regularities and thus likely engaging in prediction-by-association (Pickering & Gambi, 2018), we take these data as evidence that associative predictions can be influenced by top-down goals and strategies so long as listeners are made explicitly aware of how a cue bears on their goals during listening. We also note a similar study with a L1 Dutch population by Huettig and Guerra (2019) that included an explicit prediction task and reported a minor anticipatory effect when predictive, gendermarked determiner cues were presented at a naturalistic speech rate and with reduced visual preview. The authors conclude that predictive processing may be scaffolded by non-linguistic factors, such as task, but do not elaborate further on the specific role of task relevance. Moreover, both Huettig and Guerra (2019) and Kim et al. (2026) interrogate the effect of task relevance on prediction via explicit manipulations of prediction's importance to participants' task. Thus, current work on prediction leaves unclear whether these shifts in predictive processing also emerge when the task relevance of prediction is not explicitly clarified for listeners (i.e., under a subtle manipulation of task relevance). Do comprehenders adjust their predictive processing as a function of task relevance without prompting or instruction, such as when task relevance is implied?

Research on L2 predictive processing has also presented evidence that language-mediated predictions are influenced by the task relevance of prediction. Ito (2025) presented 48 L2 English speakers across two studies with sentences containing objects (e.g., newspaper) that were (un)predictable based on the main verb's lexical semantics (e.g., read or buy). In the first study, some participants were given an explicit prediction task ("predict how the sentence [is] likely to continue", p. 811) before receiving a comprehension task for a different set of stimuli. Other participants received the opposite order. Based on anticipatory fixations, Ito (2025) found that participants were more likely to fixate an upcoming target object which was constrained for by the verb, and that they were more likely to do so when there was an explicit prediction task.

Despite this interaction, Ito (2025) did not report a temporal benefit for explicit prediction.

Additionally, the effects of the prediction task were likely sensitive to the order in which tasks were presented, with a potential carry-over effect of the prediction task on the comprehension block. A follow-up experiment measured predictive fixations when participants received a comprehension task prior to the prediction task but failed to find an interaction of task and condition or any temporal difference in prediction across task conditions. However, there was a main effect of task such that participants were more likely to fixate a target in the explicit prediction condition even when the verb was not predictive of the target. Ito (2025) thus argued that the benefit of explicit prediction tasks for predictive processing is general, with listeners using top-down strategies to upregulate predictive visual search rather than to "enhance prediction at their will" (p. 822). A similar finding was reported in Kim et al. (2024), which utilised the same materials as Kim et al. (2026) but focused on L2 Korean speakers. When L2 listeners were given an explicit prediction task, their predictive processing was comparable to that of an L1 Korean control group who had only received a comprehension task with the same stimuli.

Taken together, these two L2 prediction studies corroborate results from L1 populations (e.g., Kim et al., 2026) in suggesting that predictive processing can be upregulated when the task relevance of prediction is made explicit to comprehenders. However, it is unclear whether these task relevance effects require an explicit task in order to modulate predictive processing in the first place. Additionally, we know of no study which has considered whether task relevance effects can downregulate associative prediction if listeners perceive prediction as a non-optimal strategy given an assessment of task relevance. In addition to these questions, we consider the divergences between work on L2 and L1 prediction with respect to task relevance effects, most notably with respect to the interaction of task and predictive condition. Ito (2025) found a more general effect of task such that predictive visual search is likely upregulated, whereas Kim et al. (2026) reported an interaction of a prediction task and predictiveness of a cue which would suggest L1 listeners can consciously and strategically enhance their use of a given cue for prediction; we return to this disparity in our overall discussion.

Synthesizing these results, we advance that there is sufficient evidence to support the conclusion that predictive processing can be upregulated when the task relevance of prediction is made explicit to listeners, such as when they are given a prediction task which clarifies the relevance of predictive processing for the task at hand. Whether this effect is concomitant with a broader boost of predictive visual search or reflects an enhancement of more accurate prediction remains unclear, though this dichotomy may itself be affected by predictive processing in a first (or dominant) v. second (or non-dominant) language. Importantly, prior literature corroborates a picture of predictive processing as broadly automatic (at least for associative prediction) but nevertheless situationally sensitive to top-down information (e.g., whether prediction is an ideal processing tool). Both views endorse dynamic, resource-constrained models of prediction, which characterize prediction as malleable, resource-sensitive, and task-contingent. Still, there is a need to qualify the pervasiveness of associative prediction and its susceptibility to top-down control.

Specifically, the current literature has not considered whether task relevance effects can emerge from listeners developing their own understanding of the relevance of prediction given a task that does not specify for them a role for prediction. Additionally, it is unclear whether task relevance effects can work to disincentivize prediction, especially types of predictions which have traditionally been assumed to occur automatically. That is, if a listener perceives prediction to be task-irrelevant, is there a corresponding downregulation of their predictive processing? Such a question would also bear on resource-limitation debates as they pertain to prediction, since engagement in task-irrelevant prediction would be unexpected if anticipatory processing is costly.

To answer these questions, we present results from two experiments using eye-tracking in the VWP in which the task relevance of prediction was manipulated. In Experiment 1, we presented participants with sentences consisting of two clauses, with one (Male or Female) referent mentioned per clause. Experimental items contained either a predictive or non-predictive first-clause verb which constrained for one of three displayed objects and was associated with the Male referent. Thus, by manipulating the referent to whom participants were instructed to attend, we also established a proxy manipulation of task relevance, with the predictive cue being taskirrelevant for participants attending to the Female referent. We reasoned that if participants across attention groups demonstrated comparable predictive processing even when prediction is task-irrelevant, such findings would not support top-down modulation of prediction (e.g., resource-rational approaches). However, if prediction effects differ across attention groups, this would provide evidence that task relevance effects can emerge under more subtle designs relative to those in past research and that prediction is sensitive to top-down mechanisms. Experiment 2 featured the same stimuli but gave an explicit task to engage in prediction rather than an implicit manipulation of task relevance via an attentional direction. This study acts as a direct comparison to the results of Experiment 1. We state the following two research questions, corresponding to Experiments 1 and 2 respectively:

RQ1:

To what extent are predictive eye-movements affected by the implicit manipulation of the task relevance of a predictive cue, such as via an attention demand? Are the extent and onset (e.g., increased proportions of predictive fixations, earlier predictive looks) of predictive processing sensitive to whether the predictive verb is relevant to the participant's task?

RQ2:

To what extent are predictive eye-movements influenced by explicit task instructions to engage in prediction? Do listeners engage differently in predicting an upcoming target noun in terms of extent and onset when the relevance of prediction to the task is made explicit?

Experiment 1

To respond to RQ1, we conducted an experiment with eye-tracking in the VWP in which L1 English speakers heard some sentences containing a verb constraining for an upcoming target word (a predictive verbal cue) and some sentences containing a verb which did not constrain for the target (a neutral or non-predictive verbal cue). This target word was pictorialised alongside images of a non-target object heard in the sentence and an unrelated distractor. To manipulate the task relevance of the predictive verbal cue, we instructed participants to pay attention to the actions of one of two referents, a Male or a Female, in each sentence for comprehension. For Male-attending participants, their referent's actions were described by the predictive verb; for Female-attending participants, their referent's actions were not. Thus, the prediction of the information cued by the predictive verb was always relevant to the attention task of the Maleattending group but task-irrelevant for the Female-attending group. To ensure the task relevance manipulation was implicit to the attentional demand, both groups were only instructed to listen to each sentence and answer questions about their respective referents' actions, and neither was given specific instructions regarding prediction or was otherwise made explicitly aware of the predictive nature of the stimuli. Given that prediction is resource-contingent (Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016) and moreover sensitive to task relevance effects in explicit instruction designs (Kim et al., 2026), we expected listeners to display prediction effects distinct to their task demands. Specifically, we predicted that when a predictive cue was not task-relevant, participants would show reduced and delayed prediction of the target image before processing of the target word in predictive sentences 3 , as well as a reduced number of target fixations while processing the target word in both predictive and non-predictive sentences. We preregistered this experiment and its analysis on Open Science Framework (OSF) at osf.io/6qfcg.

Materials and methods

Participants

We recruited 135 participants by word-of-mouth and from the departments of Spanish and Portuguese Studies; Speech, Language, and Hearing Sciences; and Linguistics at the University of Florida. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 28. Participants were required to have no issues with hearing, seeing, learning, or reading. Participants must have also begun learning English before 6 years of age, grown up speaking English in a predominantly English-speaking country, and be comfortable speaking and reading English. Eight participants were excluded because their eye movements could not be tracked or their data could not be extracted. Two participants were excluded from analysis due to not fitting the language background criteria of the experiment.

Three participants were dropped for poor-quality of eye-tracking data resulting in fewer than six usable trials in either the predictive or non-predictive conditions, 1 participant was excluded for a lack of fixations in the interest areas during the predictive window, and 1 participant was removed for failing to meet the preregistered minimum for comprehension accuracy (80%). Our preregistered analysis called for 120 participants; this number was chosen on the basis of similar sample sizes in adjacent research, as well as a preregistered power analysis estimate necessary to reach 80% power for an interaction of our factors. Ultimately, 120 participants (age: M = 20.1, SD = 1.36) were included in the final analysis, with 57 in the Male-attending (33 self-identified as female; age: M = 20, SD = 1.1) and 63 in the Female-attending (38 self-identified as female; 2 self-identified as nonbinary; age: M = 20.1, SD = 1.56) condition. All participants received compensation in the form of either course credit or cash at a rate of $5.00 USD per 0.5 hours.

Sessions lasted between 30 and 40 minutes.

Materials

The methods of sentence construction and image selection used in both Experiments 1 and 2 were preregistered. Due to copyright, we are unable to share the specific images used; however, all other materials are publicly available on OSF (osf.io/63xr2) or are available upon request.

Sentences

Experiment 1 followed a 2 (Condition) by 2 (Attention Group) factor design. Sentences contained a verb whose meaning was either exclusively compatible with the target/first-clause noun (predictive) or compatible with all image-objects shown, including the second-clause noun and distractor (non-predictive). Additionally, participants were grouped according to a betweengroup attention task. Participants were instructed to attend to either the actions of the Male (Male-attending) or Female referent (Female-attending) and answer referent-specific questions for comprehension. As will be discussed in greater detail below, the Attention Group manipulation serves as a proxy for a task relevance manipulation, with predictive verbs in the experimental items being task-relevant for Male-attending participants and task-irrelevant for Female-attending participants.

Sentences were bi-clausal and contained both a Male (Peter, Gary, or John) and a Female referent (Sarah, Jane, or Mary), with one referent per clause (e.g., After Gary answered his phone, Mary sighed and produced her wallet.). In the experimental items, the Male referent always appeared in the first clause. This first-clause verb could also be either predictive or nonpredictive of the target noun (the object of the first-clause verb), following a similar manipulation in Altmann and Kamide (1999). Thus, the target noun was always the first-clause noun in the experimental items (see Stimulus Organization). Male-attending participants were instructed to listen to the actions of the Male referent and answer a corresponding comprehension question after each trial (e.g., What did Gary answer?) ; Female-attending participants were similarly instructed and received relevant post-trial questions (e.g., What did Mary produce?).

Since both the predictive verb and the Male referent appeared together in the first clause of the experimental items, we consider the Male-attending group the group for whom prediction was always task-relevant. For Female-attending participants, the predictive verb (and so prediction overall) was task-irrelevant, since the Female referent was in the second clause of the experimental items and therefore not associated with the first-clause verb.

All of the images participants saw were coloured, cartoon-style clipart selected from open sources and from a set of images downloaded in 2014 from ThinkStock Photos (https://nam10.safelinks.protection.outlook.com/?url=http%3A%2F%2Fwww.thinkstockphotos.com%2F&data=05%7C02%7Cjsingleton2@ufl.edu%7Cdb3aee8dfcde4505adf408de0dc16fe7%7C0d4da0f84a314d76ace60a62331e1b84%7C0%7C0%7C638963321606780961%7CUnknown%7CTWFpbGZsb3d8eyJFbXB0eU1hcGkiOnRydWUsIlYiOiIwLjAuMDAwMCIsIlAiOiJXaW4zMiIsIkFOIjoiTWFpbCIsIldUIjoyfQ%3D%3D%7C0%7C%7C%7C&sdata=lwsNCPrVCMKRtJWSYbsv17QzUIvBZnxCPIkP5G2aiuE%3D&reserved=0) under a license held by one of the authors. The objects were chosen such that the name of the target object did not share the initial phoneme of the non-target objects. We did not control for any other measures of relatedness or similarity (e.g., semantic closeness, visual similarity) due to the difficulty inherent to balancing the target object being solely compatible with the predictive verb against all objects needing to be compatible with the non-predictive verb. Note that since the displays were the same for the predictive and nonpredictive conditions, as well as for the attention groups, any difference between the groups cannot be due to the materials. All images were adjusted to a size of 302 x 302 pixels on the computer display.

Norming study

To ensure that the verbs in the predictive condition indeed elicited a preference for the target image and the non-predictive did not, we conducted a norming study. We recruited 48 participants (age: 19 to 66, M = 33.9) on Prolific (https://www.prolific.com/) with a minimum approval rate of 80%. This approval rate was chosen to screen for participant quality and excluded participants rejected from more than 20% of Prolific studies they have participated in.

These participants then completed the norming study in Qualtrics (https://www.qualtrics.com/).

All participants indicated that they were native English speakers, had learned English before the age of 4, and currently resided in the United States of America. For the norming study, only the subject, verb and pre-nominal determiner of the first clause of the materials were presented alongside three pictures (target, second-clause object, unrelated distractor). For instance, participants read Peter answered his … (predictive) or Peter lost his … (non-predictive) while viewing a picture of a phone, wallet, and glasses. Participants were then asked to select which picture was most likely to be mentioned next in the sentence fragment. Forty items were tested; each participant saw 20 items each in the predictive and non-predictive conditions. Items and conditions were randomized for each participant, and three attention checks (e.g., Please select the fridge) were presented randomly. All participants passed the attention checks. The selection task was followed by a short questionnaire about participants' language backgrounds and whether they had been paying attention. Based on the results of this norming study, 15 items were slightly revised and re-normed in a follow-up study with a different group of 48 raters (age: 19 to 54, M = 33.2) drawn from the same population as above. Thirty-two pairs of items were ultimately selected for the eye-tracking study. In these items, the target picture was selected in the predictive condition between 96% and 100% of the time (M = 99.2%), and in the nonpredictive version between 0% and 64% (M = 32.1%). In the non-predictive condition, the second-clause object and unrelated distractor images were selected, on average, 32.1% and 35.8% of the time, respectively (for details, see osf.io/63xr2).

Sentence recording

Sentences were recorded by a female native speaker of American English at a rate of 2.9 syllables per second using a Marantz Ò Model PMD661MKII Handheld Solid-State Recorder in a quiet room. Audio files were subsequently edited in PRAAT (Boersma & Weenink, 2022) such that all stimuli were preceded by 100 ms of silence and the duration between first-clause verb onset and target noun onset in each item was at least 500 ms, with an overall item average between 650 and 750 ms. This was done by lengthening the pause between the verb offset and noun onset. The time frame from the onset of the verb to the onset of the target noun onset was longer in the non-predictive (M = 723 ms) than the predictive condition (M = 655 ms). For this reason, we analysed our data time locked to the onset of the noun rather than the verb. We did not specifically control for sentence length, with non-predictive sentences being slightly longer (M = 5,492 ms) than predictive ones (M = 5,129 ms). Overall sentence length was, on average, 5,149 ms. All sentences were normalized to 68 dB in PRAAT using a script provided by Winn (2020).

Stimulus organization

Participants were shown 68 trials in total. Of these, 32 were experimental trials (Male referent in the first clause, Female referent in the second clause): 16 with a predictive first-clause verb, 16 with a non-predictive first-clause verb. Participants also received 32 filler trials. These fillers were similar to the experimental trials but crucially always contained the Female referent in the first clause. 16 of these filler trials began with a subordinating clause (e.g., While Jane was looking for a saltshaker…) while the remaining 16 contained coordinating clauses with either while, and, or but. Sixteen of the 32 filler trials across both filler types also used a predictive verb with the Female character. Participants also received four practice trials prior to beginning the main experimental block. These trials were structured similarly to the experimental and filler trials but did not contain predictive verbs in either clause so as to avoid cluing participants in to the manipulation. Experimental trials were Latin-squared over two lists per attention group such that each list contained 16 trials per condition. Trial order was pseudorandomized.

In each trial, a sentence was coupled with a 1920 x 1200-pixel visual display containing three images (the target/first-clause, non-target/second-clause, and unrelated distractor objects) arranged around a 62 x 126-pixel centre fixation point. Each image was positioned in one of three unique, 302 x 302-pixel interest areas and was rotated between these areas between trials to encourage eye-movement and prevent strategising. The position of the centre fixation point was fixed and image distance from the centre was controlled to be equidistant. Procedure

Participants were seated approximately 60 cm away from a 27-inch computer monitor with their forehead and chin on a desk-mounted headrest to minimize movement. Eye movement data were recorded using an EyeLink 1000 desktop-mounted eye-tracker (SR Research Ltd., 2005) interfaced with an IBM-compatible PC. Monocular tracking of the right pupil and corneal reflection was performed at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Participants completed a 9-point calibration and validation procedure for the collection of accurate horizontal and vertical eyemovements. Eye position errors were less than 0.5 degrees. In the event of increased drift values or a change in the participant's posture, eye movements were recalibrated and revalidated between trials, and the reset was recorded in an experimental log.

Prior to the experiment, participants were given oral and written instructions that they would be listening to sentences while looking at objects displayed on the screen in front of them. Participants were also instructed to attend to the actions of either the Male or the Female referent in each sentence and answer a corresponding comprehension question after each trial. 4 In between each trial, participants saw a centre fixation point. When a fixation was detected with a drift under 0.3 degrees, the images appeared on the screen for 1,250 ms, after which the sentence was played through a pair of 5W desk-mounted Logitech Z130 PC speakers. Answers to comprehension questions were made via mouse-click. Prior to the main experimental block, participants saw four practice trials and could ask clarifying questions. Participants were also able to take a break halfway through the experiment. After the experiment, participants completed a debriefing survey and language background questionnaire via Google Forms (responses to both forms for Experiments 1 and 2 can be found on OSF; osf.io/63xr2). All participants gave written informed consent; per regulations and policies of the Institutional Review Board of the University of Florida, the experimental protocol was exempt from review.

Analysis

Prior to analysis, data for 127 participants (excluding eight participants whose data were either unextractable or not successfully collected) were subset to include only experimental trials (4,064 trials, or 7,067,296 datapoints; 2,176, 53.54%, Female-attending trials and 1,888, 46.46%, Male-attending trials). Data were aggregated by subject and item and analysed across two separate windows: the predictive window and the target window, both time-locked to the noun onset. We did not pre-register our analysis in the target window and present it as exploratory. The average interval between the verb onset and the noun was 750 ms. The predictive time window therefore spanned from 750 ms before the noun to the noun onset, offset by 200 ms (from 550 ms pre-onset to 200 ms after the noun). We offset the window by 200 ms because planned eyemovements take approximately 200 ms to program and launch (Travis, 1936). The target window extended from 200 ms past the noun onset to 1,000 ms post-onset. Data were subsequently cleaned to remove 39 trials (0.95%) containing incorrect comprehension question responses (11 of which ( 28.21%) were for Female-attending trials and 28 (71.79%) Male-attending; 17 of the 39 (43.59%) were for predictive trials and 22 ( 56.41%) were for non-predictive trials). As well, 520 trials (12.7%) were removed for containing less than 50% usable data in the predictive window (291 ( 55.96%) Female-attending and 229 ( 44.04%) Male-attending; 257 (49.42%) had predictive verbs and 263 (50.58%) non-predictive verbs). At the 1000 Hz sample level, we excluded blinks, saccades, and sample points suffering tracker-loss from these cleaned data (1,142,137 datapoints, (16.16%); 586,341 (51.34%) Female-attending and 555,796 (48.66%) Male-attending; 572,283 (50.11%) predictive and 569,854 (49.89%) non-predictive). Finally, we excluded participants who had fewer than six usable trials across either level of Condition (three participants, all of whom were Female-attending), participants who had less than 80% accuracy on the comprehension questions (one participant, Male-attending), as well as participants who did not meet our inclusion criteria (two participants, both Female-attending). One Male-attending participant was also excluded for having no looks within any interest area during the predictive window. In total, 120 unique participants were included in the final analysis. Pre-processed data represented 81.96% (3,331 trials) of the raw experimental subset, and data loss was approximately equivalent across conditions: Female-attending, predictive: 880 trials preserved in the pre-processed subset, 80.88% of the corresponding subcondition in the raw subset; Femaleattending, non-predictive: 869 trials, 79.87%; Male-attending, predictive: 791 trials, 83.79%; and Male-attending, non-predictive: 791 trials, 83.79%).

A generalised linear mixed effects model with family specified as binomial was run in each window using the lme4 package version 1.1-7 (Bates et al., 2014) in the R Environment for Statistical Computing program, version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). For the dependent variable (DV), fixation data were time aligned and separated into 20 ms bins. The total number of bins with looks to the target object versus the total number bins with looks to the secondclause and distractor objects was included as the DV in both models, with Attention Group, Condition, and their interaction serving as fixed effects. Participant ID and Item were included as random intercepts. We included a maximal random effects structure as justified by the design (Barr et al., 2013). Thus, Condition was included as a by-subject random slope with the main effects and interaction of Attention Group and Condition as by-item random slopes in both models. All factors were deviation coded, with predictive coded as +0.5 and Male-attending as +0.5.

We also conducted an exploratory Divergence Point Analysis (DPA) using a script provided by Stone et al. (2021) and adjusted by Lago et al. (2023) to determine when looks to the target between the predictive and non-predictive conditions began to differ as a function of task relevance. Two generalised logistic mixed effects models with binomial distributions were fit at every sampled 20 ms bin for both attention groups with the number of bins containing target fixations versus the number of bins with non-target fixations as the DV. To calculate the onset of group and condition-level differences, divergence points were defined as the first timepoint at which the difference in looks to the target for the predictive versus non-predictive conditions in each attention group was significant in a minimum of ten consecutive significant points (bins), or every 200 ms. Data were sampled for N = 2000 iterations and aggregated over items and grouped by participants, bins, Attention Group, and Condition using a bootstrap function. We then compared group onsets of target looks in the predictive condition and calculated a betweengroup prediction onset difference. We also calculated 95% confidence intervals for the onset in both attention groups and their difference. The DPA script, as well as the raw data and analysis pipeline, are available on OSF (osf.io/63xr2).

Discussion

The main aim of Experiment 1 was to determine whether the onset and extent of predictive eyemovements in the visual world were sensitive to a subtle or implicit manipulation of prediction's task relevance via an attention task (RQ1). Participants were given an attention task that determined whether a predictive verbal cue was task-relevant (Male-attending) or task-irrelevant (Female-attending). Based on fixations to the target object in the predictive window, we found evidence that all participants made use of the first-clause verb to predict the upcoming noun. However, we did not find evidence of downregulated predictive processing under task-irrelevant contexts, since both attention groups were equally likely to fixate the target throughout the predictive window and began predicting at similar times. Task relevance effects only emerged in the target window, with higher probabilities of visual attention to the target when prediction was task-relevant and regardless of whether the verb was predictive or not.

Overall, listeners engaged in prediction even when it was not required or expected of them to do so given their task-demands. This is consistent with prior work showing predictionby-association is automatic and not influenced by top-down factors. One potential reason for the lack of difference between the attention groups is that Female-attending participants were strategically attending to the Male referent in order to rule out the target object as a valid comprehension question answer and so converge more rapidly on the relevant object. If Femaleattending participants began strategising during the experiment, this would imply that early in the experiment, this group would initially show predictive fixation patterns distinct from those of the Male-attending group. However, we did not find evidence that the groups differed in their predictive processing when analysis was restricted to the first half of the data (trials one through 34; see OSF). Alternatively, Male-attending participants may have not been aware that prediction was relevant to their task due to the instructions they received (see Footnote 4), which did not specify prediction as a task-demand or goal. We addressed this concern in RQ2 and conducted a second experiment using the same materials in which participants received an explicit task to predict an upcoming target. We compared these data to those of the Male-attending group in Experiment 1.

Experiment 2

Experiment 2 presented 63 participants with the same materials and design, excepting the attention groupings, described in Experiment 1. Crucially, these participants were given an explicit instruction task to predict, rather than simply pay attention to, the actions of the Male referent; thus, the task relevance of prediction was made explicit in Experiment 2. To further ensure participants were maximally aware of prediction's task relevance, we also asked participants to report the accuracy and confidence of their trial-by-trial predictions. In line with research indicating that explicit prediction tasks can upregulate predictive eye-movements (Ito, 2025; Kim et al., 2024 Kim et al., , 2026)), we expected an explicit prediction task would lead to more frequent and rapid predictive fixations to the target after hearing a predictive verb as compared to the attention task in Experiment 1. Discussion

The main aim of Experiment 2 was to test whether emphasizing the task relevance of a predictive cue through an explicit instructional demand to predict modulated the extent and time-course of predictive processing. Experiment 1 did not find evidence of attention manipulation of task-relevance on predictive eye-movements, though this may have been due to our Male-attending participants not being aware that prediction was task-relevant. Hence, participants in Experiment 2 were told to listen to each sentence and predict what the Male referent was doing or acting upon. Since prediction is immediately task-relevant within this design, we predicted that participants would display faster and more frequent predictive eyemovements to the target noun compared to the Male-attending group in Experiment 1, as in Kim et al. (2026). In line with these predictions, we found that an explicit prediction task encouraged more frequent and rapid anticipatory fixations in the predictive window. However, in contrast to Kim et al. (2026), this upregulation in the number of predictive fixations was indifferent to the predictability of the target, suggesting that the benefits of an explicit task are limited to an upregulation of predictive visual search and are not attributable to an at-will enhancement of prediction, as is argued in Ito (2025). Importantly, these results are in spite of an effort to maximize awareness of prediction's task relevance via post-trial questions about prediction. The DPA results, however, showed that predictive eye-movements in response to a predictive verb were initiated earlier with an explicit prediction task, corroborating findings from Kim et al. (2026). Altogether, we broadly corroborate extant findings for L1 prediction, though our results suggest a more general benefit for specifying the task relevance of prediction than has been previously reported.

General discussion

Across two eye-tracking experiments, we examined whether the extent and onset of predictive processing is sensitive to the task relevance of a predictive cue when participants are given an attention task that renders said cue relevant or irrelevant (RQ1) or an explicit task to engage in prediction (RQ2). Broadly, we found that prediction was sensitive to explicit manipulations of task relevance, whereas more subtle manipulations failed to modulate predictive eye-movements.

In this section, we present and interpret the overall results of Experiments 1 and 2, beginning with a corroboration of evidence that associative prediction is automatic and followed by a qualification in light of task relevance effects observed in Experiment 2. We then motivate the findings of the explicit prediction task and offer two potential accounts favoring either a working memory-oriented or awareness-oriented explanation. Finally, we consider limitations of the present design and avenues for future research.

We investigated effects of task relevance on the onset and extent of predictive processing in two eye-tracking experiments in the VWP. Specifically, we tested for effects of subtle (Experiment 1, RQ1) and explicit (Experiment 2, RQ2) manipulations of task relevance while participants heard sentences containing predictive or non-predictive verbs. In Experiment 1, the task relevance of a predictive verbal cue varied based on the referent participants were instructed to attend to. We expected a downregulation of predictive processing when the predictive verb was task-irrelevant; however, task relevance did not influence the onset of extent of participants' associative predictions. To test whether task relevance effects require explicit awareness, we instructed participants in Experiment 2 to actively predict the upcoming target. In line with adjacent research (Ito, 2025; Kim et al., 2026), we found that when task relevance was specified for listeners in the form of an unambiguous prediction goal, predictive processing was upregulated. Critically, however, this upregulation was observed for both predictive and nonpredictive sentences, though looks in the predictive condition were initiated earlier. We conclude from these results that associative predictions are automatic and insensitive to subtle manipulations of task-relevance, whereas specifying task relevance enhances prediction generally, possibly as a result of increased awareness, attention, or working memory resources.

Prediction is thus sensitive to top-down control, but in a limited sense.

Automaticity of associative predictions

We interpret the lack of a main effect of attended gender in the predictive window or an interaction of attended gender and condition in Experiment 1 as evidence that verbal semanticsbased predictions are likely automatic and thus outside of the strategic control of the comprehender, at least when prediction is not specifically called for. Instead of strategically adjusting to prediction's task relevance to most efficiently process each sentence, Female-results of Experiment 2 as evidence of this initiation process, with the explicit task expediting the beginning of predictive eye-movements mostly clearly in the DPA. Taken together, our effects in Experiments 1 and 2 provide strong evidence for the automaticity of semantic predictions and so clarify the dynamics of so-called "dumb" prediction (Huettig, 2015). As a necessary qualifier for how these results relate to two-stage models of prediction (Pickering & Gambi, 2018), we did not test for other kinds of prediction and so cannot characterize the role of task relevance in the unfolding of more effortful prediction (i.e., syntactic, phonological, perspective-based). Our support for two-stage models of prediction is therefore an explication of early-stage prediction rather than a holistic analysis.

The present analysis does not offer a means to identify whether the persistence of associative prediction in Experiment 1 is directly attributable to the prediction-by-association pathway being insensitive to task relevance. Indeed, task relevance may be defined in one of two ways, with distinct implications for our interpretation. Under a utility-driven definition, task relevance is a measure of how integral similarly activated linguistic information is to online processing strategies. In contrast, a mechanistic definition assumes that task-irrelevant information is under-activated and so less available for the same online processing strategies.

For the results herein, under a mechanistic definition, associative prediction may still be taskrelevant for the Female-attending group. That is, the predictive cue may be less accessible as a result of the attention manipulation, but enough to trigger predictive eye-movements indistinguishable from those in the Male-attending group. Our current interpretation of the results of Experiments 1 and 2 relies on a utility-driven definition of task relevance, but we are currently unable to determine whether there are in fact underlying differences in activation at the verb between the attention groups and so tease apart these two accounts of task relevance. However, we recommend future research to more closely examine how (implicit) task relevance manipulations may influence activation during processing.

Effects of explicit manipulations of task relevance

Our results from Experiment 2, when compared with those of Experiment 1, suggest that there is a general promotion of predictive processing when task-relevance is explicitly clarified. We qualify this finding with two comments. Firstly, explicit tasks appear to confer a general benefit for prediction which is irrespective of the cue's predictiveness, with instructed listeners deploying more frequent and earlier eye-movements to the target across predictive and nonpredictive stimuli. These results diverge from Kim et al. (2026), who reported an interaction of predictability and task for a L1 Korean population. However, since Kim et al. (2026) relied on a relatively simple dual image display, their reported interaction of task and predictability may have facilitated participants' predictions by reducing the domain of reference for their sentences.

We also note that the sample size (N = 32) in Kim et al. (2026) limits the generalisability of their findings, despite their effort to accommodate by presenting more items per condition. Moreover, our non-predictive sentences may have also slightly benefited prediction, since some of these were slightly biased in favor of the target (see Images). We thus cannot conclusively determine why our results diverge from those of Kim et al. (2026), but it is apparent across both studies that explicit prediction tasks can enhance predictive processing. Conversely, our findings align with those in Ito (2025), who used a similar task manipulation and similar materials containing predictive verbs on a L2 English population. We do note, however, a key difference across these two studies: we observe a temporal benefit for prediction, with explicitly instructed participants showing earlier anticipatory fixations to the target in the predictive versus non-predictive conditions, whereas Ito (2025) did not report significant differences in prediction onsets across task or predictability conditions. One potential explanation is that L1/L2 predictive processing differs insofar as the former benefits more from an explicit instruction to predict. Prediction in one's L2 is likely to be more inefficient and may be initiated later (Kaan & Grüter, 2021); an explicit prediction task thus may not suffice to overcome the gap in predictive processing ability between one's L1 and L2. However, prediction-by-association specifically is less likely to be hindered by broader resource limitations, and so one might still expect an interaction between task and predictability even in an L2 population (e.g., Kim et al., 2024; Pickering & Gambi, 2018). A more likely explanation for the contrast in prediction onsets with Ito (2025) is the wording of our instructions, which were more specific about where and what listeners should predict. Ito (2025) instructed participants to "predict how the sentence was likely to continue," whereas our instructions made clear that the target was a noun serving as the direct object of a verb (see Footnote 4) (p. 11).

Consequently, our participants may have been uniquely advantaged in knowing where and when to predict and so may have been able to initiate prediction earlier.

Enhanced visual search

Given that participants in Experiment 2 showed a main effect of Task Group, with the explicitly instructed participants showing more eye-movements to the target in the predictive time window than the Male-attending participants, in addition to an earlier onset of prediction for explicitly instructed listeners, we argue that these are downstream effects of enhanced predictive visual search, echoing similar arguments by Ito (2025). That is, under an explicit demand to predict, participants begin searching for an upcoming target earlier and more widely and are thus more likely to fixate it in the predictive window. Though we are unable to comment on the specific mechanisms powering this upregulation, one convincing account proposed by Ito (2025) is that an explicit task instruction leads to the recruitment of more working memory (WM) resources for prediction. WM has been previously linked to predictive eye-movements and visual search (e.g., Huettig, Olivers, & Hartsuiker, 2011; Huettig, Rommers, & Meyer, 2011; Salverda & Altmann, 2011; Salverda et al., 2011), with listeners likely storing object-image associations in the visuospatial sketchpad to then access linguistic representations in long-term memory. WM resources have also been shown to influence the time-course and extent of prediction during online processing, with reduced WM capacity associated with reduced predictive processing (Huettig & Janse, 2016) and increased memory load associated with a downturn in prediction (Allison et al., 2025; Ito et al., 2018; Li & Qu, 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Thus, an explicit prediction task, by making prediction immediately task-relevant, may promote an increase in available WM resources and subsequently enhance predictive processing.

Awareness

An alternative explanation of our results is that a specification of task relevance engenders a domain-general increase in awareness which is not specific to prediction (Lai et al., 2023).

Hence, an explicit task encourages more comprehensive and thorough utterance parsing, and so prediction may be upregulated along with other processes (e.g., integration, encoding). This approach may explain why our effects are much more general than expected, and is moreover in harmony with findings establishing a benefit for prediction when listeners are aware, such as in learning paradigms (Andringa, 2020; Curcic et al., 2019; Koch et al., 2021). Relatedly, our comparison of Experiments 1 and 2 suggests that task relevance effects may require explicit awareness to be observable, at least for associative predictions. This finding is at least superficially at odds with descriptions of prediction as continuously updating or forwardmodeling (e.g., Brothers et al., 2017 Brothers et al., , 2019)), since we would expect listeners to adjust their predictive behaviour to how relevant prediction is at any given time-point. However, we qualify this contradiction by pointing out that the kind of prediction under study may be a poor diagnostic tool for whether listeners dynamically adjust predictive processing on account of its aforementioned automaticity. Thus, it may appear that listeners are not sensitive to implicit or subtle task-demands only because prediction is minimally resource-intensive and so proceeds automatically. Extending from this logic, the absence of task relevance effects in Experiment 1 as compared to Experiment 2 may implicate a level of difficulty in downregulating, rather than upregulating, associative predictions. Such a difficulty would be expected for associative predictions if they are truly automatic, though we note the possibility that an initial goal-state could be a desire to inhibit prediction. Some studies (Brothers et al., 2017 (Brothers et al., , 2019;; Henry et al., 2017; Hopp, 2016) have already demonstrated that prediction can be disengaged when global reliability of a cue is low, but these do not clarify whether top-down inhibition can be selfinitiated as a strategy in direct response to a task. Future work might clarify this dimension of associative prediction by explicitly asking participants to inhibit prediction by providing instructions similar to those in Experiment 2.

Limitations and future directions

We would like to address various limitations of the present studies and suggest avenues for future research. Firstly, as has been suggested throughout, the lack of task relevance effects in Experiment 1 may be due to the relative ease of associative predictions to the verb (Corps et al., 2022; Pickering & Gambi, 2018). If the type of prediction listeners are performing is cognitively cheap, there may be no need to adjust cognitive resource distributions in response to prediction being task-irrelevant and, consequently, no need for a distinct processing strategy sans prediction. Simply put, prediction in these two studies may be too easy. We expect that more resource-intensive types of prediction will experience a task relevance effect, even under implicit manipulations. Recent work by Corps et al. (2022), Corps, Liao, & Pickering (2023) and Corps, Yang, & Pickering (2023) has examined how listeners predict when they are given instructions prompting them to adopt a perspective other than their own (see also Nota et al., 2026). Such non-egocentric predictions have been classified as evidence of 'consistent' prediction and are generally understood to be resource-intensive. If participants are given an attentional demand akin to Experiment 1 but are expected to predict from the perspective of a referent within the stimuli, task relevance effects may emerge in the form of reduced and delayed predictive fixations. Such results would suggest that task relevance is involved in processing during later stages of prediction and thus align with current models of predictions. Future research might also diversify the type of predictive cue in pursuit of more subtle and potentially effortful flavors of prediction, such as verbal cues for implicit causality (Kim & Grüter, 2021).

Additionally, we acknowledge that the nature of our instructions in Experiment 2 may have inadvertently biased participants to predict the target noun, potentially explaining the main effect of Task Group. Similar studies to our own have typically presented participants with instructions that tell them to predict what will come next in an utterance without a clear indication of what participants could expect to hear or where prediction must occur (Ito, 2025; Lai et al., 2023). By contrast, our instructions may have clued participants in to the fact that the item to be predicted was the direct object of the verb. Thus, participants may have been better able to predict from the verb during the experiment, potentially explaining the temporal effect in Experiment 2. Such subtle differences in instruction have yet to receive attention in the prediction literature, and future research should take efforts to investigate how instructional wording informs predictive processing.

Lastly, we should be reasonably skeptical of how informative explicit instruction designs (Experiment 2) are of processing dynamics given that naturalistic language processing is unlikely to feature overt demands to predict (Brothers et al., 2015; Huettig & Tanenhaus, 2025; Kim et al., 2026). Considerations of task relevance may instead be pertinent at later stages of processing, such as integration. In support of this, we point to the main effect of task relevance in the target window of Experiment 1, with Male-attending participants directing more fixations to the target noun regardless of whether it was cued for by the verb. Since this target noun was also the answer to the post-trial comprehension question for Male-attending participants, this increase in target window fixations may reflect a strategy to integrate target information and aid comprehension. However, the opposite argument is also possible: Female-attending participants may have been quicker to integrate the target because of its task irrelevance for following comprehension questions. We are unable to tease these accounts apart under the current analysis but emphasize that task relevance motivates both. Similarly, target window effects from Experiment 2, where explicitly instructed participants were less likely to fixate the target noun when it was not cued for by the verb, suggest that specifying task relevance can have downstream consequences for the integration of unpredictable information or stimuli which do not immediately fit into the task-demands (cf. Ito, 2025).

Conclusion

We conducted two eye-tracking studies in the VWP investigating the effect of task relevance on predictive eye-movements during online sentence processing. We found that when task relevance was modulated via an attentional demand so that prediction was task-irrelevant for certain participants, predictive processing was unhampered and proceeded similarly across attention groups. However, when an explicit prediction task was introduced in a follow-up study, we found that participants who were given an explicit prediction task moved their eyes to the target more frequently and displayed earlier prediction effects. From these data, we conclude that predictions based on verbal semantics are automatic and insensitive to task relevance when it is not specified and are thus best categorized as part of prediction-by-association under a two-stage model of prediction. This type of prediction is still sensitive to top-down factors, such as goals and strategies, but only when listeners are made explicitly aware of how prediction relates to their task-demands. Future research should investigate the extent to which this top-down control of prediction is sensitive to information about task relevance with more costly, non-automatic types of prediction.
