Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Full text

Abstract

Learned helplessness (LH) theory proposes that uncontrollable stress, but not otherwise identical controllable stress, produces behavioral consequences relevant to depression. Although extensively validated in animal models, the extent to which experimentally induced uncontrollability in humans reproduces depression-related behavioral alterations remains unclear.

Here, we conducted one of the largest experimental investigations of human LH to date, using a preregistered yoked-control design in which participants were exposed to identical aversive stimuli but differed in their ability to control stimulus termination. We first examined whether uncontrollability impaired performance on a classic LH outcome measure, the anagram task. We then assessed its effects across multiple depression-relevant domains, including reward learning, effort-based reward motivation, and social behavior. Uncontrollability did not produce the broad behavioral impairments predicted by classical LH theory. Anagram performance showed effects in the expected direction but did not meet preregistered criteria for successful LH induction.

Similarly, uncontrollability did not alter reward learning or social behavior, including social submissiveness and social avoidance. In contrast, uncontrollability selectively affected effortbased decision-making by reducing sensitivity to reward magnitude and probability when participants evaluated whether to exert effort for potential rewards. These findings suggest that acute experimentally induced uncontrollability in healthy adults does not produce a generalized depression-like behavioral syndrome but may selectively influence reward valuation processes underlying motivated behavior. More broadly, our results highlight the importance of identifying which components of depression-related dysfunction are reliably captured by laboratory models of stress controllability.

Introduction

Depression is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide and remains a major public health challenge. Animal models have been instrumental in advancing our understanding of depression 1 . Among these, the learned helplessness (LH) model is particularly notable from a mechanistic perspective because it emphasizes stressor controllability 2 as a key determinant of whether stress leads to depressive symptoms. Specifically, LH theory proposes that uncontrollable stress promotes depressive-like behaviors, whereas behavioral control over stress protects against these outcomes 3 . In the seminal animal studies, dogs exposed to inescapable shocks subsequently failed to escape the shocks when escape became possible, whereas those exposed to escapable shocks did not show this deficit despite identical stress exposure, implicating controllability as the critical factor underlying helpless behavior 4 . Because this behavioral passivity resembles core features of depression, LH became an influential experimental model of the disorder. Following the initial discovery of LH in animals, the paradigm was adapted for use in humans, where analogous findings were demonstrated, for example, by using loud noises rather than electric shocks to induce LH and a cognitive anagram task to assess its consequences 5 . Using distinct induction and assessment tasks was intended to establish that helplessness generalized beyond the induction context rather than reflecting task-specific learning 5 . More recent attempts to replicate the LH effect in humans have, however, yielded mixed results [6] [7] [8] [9].

Several behavioral domains are consistently disrupted in major depressive disorder (MDD). Among the best characterized are reward processing and social functioning. Individuals with MDD show impairments in reward learning 10, 11 and in the motivation to exert effort for reward 12, 13 . Depression is also associated with impaired social functioning, including increased social avoidance [14] [15] [16] and alterations in social-hierarchy-related behaviors, such as increased submissive behavior 17, 18 . The validity of animal models of depression depends, in part, on their ability to reproduce these behavioral features. Consistent with this, animal models of depression, including the LH model, exhibit alterations in reward-related processes [19] [20] [21] as well as increased social avoidance [22] [23] [24] and submissive behaviors 25, 26 . These parallels support the use of LH as a model for investigating mechanisms underlying depressive behaviors.

However, despite extensive evidence for the role of stressor controllability in animal models 3 , it remains unclear whether experimentally induced uncontrollability in humans produces behavioral alterations relevant to MDD. Establishing whether LH induction in humans can reproduce behavioral features of depression is therefore critical for assessing the potential clinical utility of animal research based on this model, as well as the extent to which mechanistic insights derived from it can inform our understanding and treatment of human depression. Addressing this question is particularly important given that findings from animal models of psychiatric disorders often fail to translate to the clinical setting 27, 28 . Thus, the objective of the present preregistered study is to assess the translational validity of the LH model by determining whether experimentally induced uncontrollability in humans recapitulates the broader behavioral phenotype described in animal models. We therefore examine three domains that are relevant to the translational characterization of the model: (1) the classic LH effect (i.e., impaired anagram performance following uncontrollable stress), (2) reward learning and motivational behavior, and (3) social functioning, including social avoidance and social hierarchy-related behavior. First, we aim to replicate the classic LH effect in one of the largest human samples to date. Second, we investigate whether exposure to uncontrollable, compared with controllable, stress alters reward learning and motivational behavior. Third, we examine whether the LH manipulation influences social functioning, specifically social avoidance and social hierarchy-related behavior. We prioritize implicit behavioral measures of social behavior, such as posture and interpersonal distance, to reduce potential biases associated with self-report measures.

Hypotheses

Main hypothesis

Participants in the uncontrollable LH group will perform worse on the anagram task than those in the controllable group.

Secondary hypotheses

1. Participants in the uncontrollable group will show impaired non-social probabilistic reward learning relative to the controllable group. Analyses of the social version of the task were preregistered as exploratory.

2. Participants in the uncontrollable group will show lower preference for High-Cost/High-Reward (HC/HR) options on the Effort Expenditure for Rewards Task (EEfRT), reflecting reduced effort-based motivation.

3. Participants in the uncontrollable group will show greater forward head tilt, reflecting increased submissiveness 29 . 4. Participants in the uncontrollable group will position their chair farther from the interviewer's chair, reflecting increased social avoidance. 5. Participants in the uncontrollable group will show reduced signature size, which is interpreted as decreased self-esteem 30 and social dominance 31.

Methods

This study was pre-registered on OSF: https://osf.io/f6jq9/overview.

Experimental manipulation

Participants completed a computerized LH task adapted from Song and Vilares 32 , programmed in PsychoPy (v2023.2.3) 33 . The aversive stimulus was a 3000-Hz pure tone (90 dB) delivered through headphones. Participants were informed that an aversive sound would be presented intermittently and that they could terminate it by discovering a fixed sequence of mouse clicks on colored squares presented on the computer screen. After each trial, visual feedback indicated whether the sound was successfully terminated (green) or ended automatically after the maximum presentation duration (red).

Participants were assigned to either the controllable or uncontrollable group using an alternating assignment procedure. The first participant was assigned to the controllable group, the second to the uncontrollable group, and so forth, in order to maintain the yoked design. In the controllable group, participants could terminate the aversive tone by performing the correct response sequence, whereas responses had no effect on tone termination in the uncontrollable group. Participants were unaware of the two conditions and, unbeknownst to them, participants in the uncontrollable group were yoked to participants in the controllable group such that tone durations on each trial were identical across matched pairs. Thus, both groups received equivalent aversive stimulation but differed in control over its termination.

The task consisted of 35 trials separated by intertrial intervals of 10-20 seconds (mean = 15 seconds), during which the screen remained black and no sound was presented. Each trial began with a 5-second inescapable presentation of the aversive tone, after which two blue squares appeared on the left and right sides of the screen (Figure 1). Participants in the controllable group could then terminate the tone by discovering the correct four-click mouse sequence (right-leftright-left). If unsuccessful, the tone continued for up to 10 additional seconds, resulting in a maximum trial duration of 15 seconds.

Mastery was defined as successfully terminating the tone on three consecutive trials and was achieved after an average of 8.94 (out of 35). Participants in the controllable group who did not meet this criterion, along with their yoked counterparts, were excluded from subsequent analyses to preserve the matched-pairs design.

Manipulation check

To assess whether the LH manipulation influenced participants' subjective sense of control, participants completed a single-item self-report measure following the task. They were asked, "How much did you feel you were in control during the task in which you were exposed to the loud sounds?" Responses were recorded on a 10-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (I felt that I had no control at all) to 10 (I felt that I was completely in control), with higher scores indicating greater perceived control during the task.

Anagram task

The anagram task was adapted from Hiroto and Seligman 5 . This task has been used historically to assess the effect of LH in human studies 5, 34, 35 . Anagrams required participants to rearrange letters to form French words using each letter exactly once (e.g., noyade from ynedao).

Participants were instructed to solve 20 anagrams, with a maximum of 140 seconds per anagram and could give up at any time and proceed to the next anagram, as in Taylor et al. 9 . Performance was assessed using: 1) mean response time, with unsolved or abandoned trials assigned a value of 140 seconds, and (2) number of failures, defined as the number of anagrams not solved due to giving up or exceeding the time limit. The task was programmed in PsychoPy (v2023.2.3) 33 and administered on a computer.

Probabilistic non-social reward learning task

The non-social reward learning task was adapted from Guitart-Masip et al. 36 and programmed in PsychoPy (v2023.2.3) 33 using the original code as a basis. Participants were instructed to maximize points, with each point converted into a 4-cent bonus. On each trial, participants identified which of three columns contained a target image within a 2000-ms response window. Correct responses were probabilistically rewarded, meaning that selecting the column containing the current target would result in a reward with a probability of 80% and no reward with a probability of 20%, whereas selecting a column that did not contain the target would result in a reward with a probability of 20% and no reward with a probability of 80%. Participants first completed a signalled version of the task as practice, which was not analyzed, followed by the reversal learning task. During the reversal phase (150 trials), the target image changed unpredictably five times, requiring participants to adapt their choices based on changing reward contingencies. Participants also completed a social reward version of the task, where monetary rewards were replaced by social rewards; a more detailed description of these tasks is provided in the Supplementary Information.

Effort expenditure for rewards task (EEfRT)

The EEfRT 37 task assessed willingness to expend physical effort to obtain monetary rewards. On each trial, participants chose between an easy task (30 dominant index-finger presses on a keyboard within 7 s) and a difficult task (100 non-dominant little-finger presses within 21 s).

Successful completion provided eligibility for monetary rewards, with the easy task always yielding $1.00 and the difficult task offering variable rewards ($1.24-$4.21). For both tasks, reward probabilities varied across trials (88%, 50%, or 12%). The probability of choosing the HC/HR option during the task was used as the outcome measure. As in the original task, participants were informed that the task would last exactly 20 minutes and that only randomly selected completed trials would contribute to their bonus compensation. For details, see Supplementary Information.

Head posture

Head posture, an index of postural submissiveness, was assessed from pre-and post-LH videos using OpenPose 38 to identify anatomical landmarks. Head angle was calculated between the ear-shoulder line and the image's vertical axis and averaged across left-and right-profile images 29 . For details, see Supplementary Information.

Interpersonal distance task

Interpersonal distance, an index of social avoidance, was assessed using a task adapted from Givon-Benjio et al. 39 in which participants placed a chair in front of an unfamiliar interviewer's chair, with preferred interpersonal distance operationalized as the mean distance (cm) between the front legs of the two chairs. For details, see Supplementary Information.

Signature

A handwritten signature was collected both before and after the LH task, following the procedure described by Shin et al. 30 to investigate changes in signature size. Signature size has been interpreted as an index of self-esteem 30 and social dominance 31 . Results for this measure are presented in the Supplementary Information, as well as additional methodological details.

Results

Sample characteristics

The final sample included 170 participants (85 per group; M age = 28.65 years, SD = 10.62; 36 males) with usable anagram data. Sociodemographic and psychological characteristics are presented in Table 1. The groups did not significantly differ on any tested variables.

Manipulation check

As expected, participants in the uncontrollable group reported significantly lower perceived control during the LH task (M = 3.80, SD = 2.78) than those in the controllable group (M = 7.64, SD = 1.88), t(147) = 10.54, p < .001 (one-tailed), d = 1.62. These results indicate that the experimental manipulation successfully manipulated participants' subjective sense of control.

Anagrams

A robust one-way MANOVA was conducted to examine whether the LH manipulation influenced mean response time and the number of failed anagrams. The multivariate effect of group was not statistically significant, MATS = 7.21, p = .059 (parametric bootstrap, 10,000 resamples), indicating that the manipulation did not produce a statistically significant difference across the combined dependent variables. However, the effects were in the hypothesized direction (see Table 2).

We then conducted preregistered exploratory analyses on two additional measures of anagram performance. We conducted a two-tailed Welch t-test on the number of times participants gave up. Participants in the uncontrollable group gave up on significantly more anagrams (M = 4.35, SD = 4.51) than participants in the controllable group (M = 3.02, SD=3.28), t(153.43) = -2.20, p = .029, d = 0.34, 95% [0.03, 0.64]. We also analyzed differences in time before giving up using a two-tailed t-test. To preserve the yoked-pair design, this analysis included only complete yoked pairs in which both participants gave up on at least one anagram (n = 40 per group). Among these participants, there was no significant difference in mean time before giving up between the controllable (M = 76.90, SD = 32.45) and uncontrollable (M = 74.67, SD = 32.80) groups, Welch's t(77.99) = 0.30, p = .762, d = 0.07, 95% [-0.38, 0.51].

Non-social reward learning

A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) was used to examine whether accuracy (correct = 1, incorrect = 0) changed differently across trials in the controllable and uncontrollable groups (Table 3). Specifically, the Trial × Group interaction tested whether participants in the two groups differed in their rate of improvement in accuracy across the task-that is, whether exposure to controllable versus uncontrollable outcomes led to different learning trajectories. The preregistered baseline model included Group, Trial, and their interaction as fixed effects. None of the tested individual-difference variables significantly improved model fit beyond this baseline model (Table S2); therefore, the final model was identical to the preregistered baseline model.

The analysis included 170 participants (85 per group). Contrary to our hypothesis, the Trial × Group interaction was not significant, OR= 0.99, 95% CI [0.91, 1.08], z = -0.24, p = 0.813, indicating that learning trajectories did not differ between the controllable and uncontrollable groups. There was no significant main effect of Group, OR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.84, 1.02], z = -1.63, p = 0.103. However, there was a significant main effect of Trial, indicating that accuracy increased over the course of the task, OR = 1.26, 95% CI [1.19, 1.34], z = 7.61, p < .001, demonstrating that participants learned across trials.

A GLMM was used to test whether Group affected the probability of choosing HC/HR options during the EEfRT (Table S5), using our preregistered baseline model (148 participants; 74 per group; Table S5). Group did not significantly affect HC/HR choice, OR = 0.85, 95% CI [0.54, 1.33], SE = 0.23, z = -0.72, p = .470. The odds of selecting HC/HR options were approximately 15% lower in the uncontrollable group, but this difference was not statistically significant.

As preregistered exploratory analyses, we evaluated whether adding interactions between Group and several key variables improved model fit. Likelihood ratio tests indicated that including the Group × Reward interaction significantly improved model fit, χ²(1) = 27.18, p < .001, as did inclusion of the Group × Probability interaction, χ²(2) = 23.92, p < .001 (Table S6). In contrast, neither the Group × Trial interaction, χ²(1) = 0.17, p = .68, nor the Group × Sex interaction, χ²(1) = 0.16, p = .69, improved model fit. Thus, participants exposed to uncontrollable outcomes were less sensitive to increases in reward magnitude when deciding whether to exert effort.

Discussion

The present study first sought to replicate the original human LH effect using a protocol similar to those used historically 5, 34, 35 . We then examined whether experimentally manipulating controllability over an aversive stimulus in humans would produce changes in reward processing and social behavior paralleling alterations observed in depression and in animal models of depression. This approach allowed us to test the translational relevance of experimentally induced uncontrollability by examining whether behavioral consequences observed in preclinical models extend to humans and map onto behavioral domains implicated in depression. As expected, the manipulation substantially reduced participants' subjective sense of control, confirming successful induction of the intended psychological state. However, contrary to most preregistered hypotheses, uncontrollability did not produce widespread behavioral impairments.

Anagrams

Regarding the replication of the original LH effect, findings were in the hypothesized direction but did not reach statistical significance, although results from the MANOVA approached significance (p = .059). Preregistered exploratory analyses nevertheless showed that participants in the uncontrollable group gave up on significantly more anagrams, suggesting that uncontrollability may have reduced persistence despite the absence of a robust overall LH effect.

Recent attempts to replicate human LH effects have produced mixed results. Song et al. 6 largely replicated the original LH effect, using a procedure very similar to ours to induce LH with loud noises. However, the tasks used during the induction procedure and outcome measure were highly similar, limiting conclusions about whether LH effects generalize beyond closely related tasks. This distinction is important because a behavioral change that emerges primarily on tasks resembling the induction procedure may reflect task-specific learning rather than a broader consequence of experiencing uncontrollability. Another study using a similar task for both induction and outcome assessment found no significant group differences, although effects were directionally consistent with LH theory 7 . Notably, this study assessed outcomes 24-48 h after induction, unlike the immediate assessment in the original studies.

Two other studies used distinct induction and assessment tasks, providing a stronger test of generalization: one replicated the effect using an unsolvable concept-formation task 8 , whereas the other failed to replicate it using thermal pain 9 . Overall, findings provide no clear pattern linking LH replication to either the induction procedure or the similarity between induction and assessment tasks. Overall, no clear pattern emerged linking LH replication to the type of induction procedure or the similarity between induction and assessment tasks. The number of participants per group ranged between 23 8 and 59 7 in these studies. These findings from the recent replication attempts appear inconsistent with the initial human LH studies, which reported quite robust effects despite using relatively small samples (e.g., 8 participants per condition 5, 34, 35 ). Our study was designed to provide a rigorous test of original human LH findings. Specifically, we used completely different induction tasks for induction and assessment of the affect, which allowed us to assess the generalizability of the effect. Moreover, our substantially larger sample (i.e., 85 participants per group) provides greater statistical power to detect effects of the magnitude reported in the original studies, while our preregistered analyses and comprehensive assessment of behavioral outcomes reduce the potential for selective reporting and allow for a broader evaluation of the consequences of experimentally induced LH. Given these methodological features, the absence of clear evidence for an LH effect in our study is therefore informative. Together with the findings of recent replication attempts, our results raise questions about the robustness and generalizability of experimentally induced human LH across behavioral domains following acute laboratory induction. Although such effects may exist, their magnitude may be considerably smaller than originally suggested. More broadly, these findings are consistent with concerns regarding the reproducibility of psychological research, including evidence that historically reported effects may have been inflated by factors such as reporting or publication bias 40.

Reward processing

Regarding reward learning, the absence of effects on probabilistic reward-learning tasks suggests that experimentally induced uncontrollability did not impair participants' ability to learn reward contingencies. This contrasts with evidence that reward learning is disrupted in depression 10, 11, [41] [42] [43] [44] . Consistent with these clinical findings, reward learning seems to be impaired in animal models of depression 19, 45 . However, our findings are consistent with a recent human study using a similar loud-noise LH induction, which likewise found no effect of uncontrollability on probabilistic reward-learning accuracy 6 . Together, these findings suggest that acute laboratoryinduced uncontrollability may be insufficient to disrupt reward learning in healthy individuals, despite the impairments observed in depression and following chronic stress in animal models.

Contrary to our hypothesis, there was no significant effect of group on HC/HR choice in the EEfRT, indicating that uncontrollability did not reduce overall willingness to exert effort for reward. This finding contrasts with evidence from both human depression research and animal models. In humans, reduced motivation to exert effort for rewards on the EEfRT has been observed in individuals with MDD 12, 13 . Similar, although less pronounced, impairments have also been reported in individuals with subclinical depression 13 . In animal models, tasks analogous to the EEfRT have shown that uncontrollable stress decreases motivation to exert effort 20, 46 , and studies directly manipulating stress controllability have demonstrated that exposure to inescapable shocks reduces effortful responding relative to exposure to an identical amount of escapable shocks.

Nevertheless, our results are consistent with those of Bukowski and colleagues 47 , who found in two experiments that experimentally induced uncontrollability did not reduce participants' willingness to invest cognitive effort. Together, our findings and those of Bukowski and colleagues 47 suggest that acute laboratory-induced uncontrollability may not reliably impair effortbased reward motivation in humans, despite robust associations observed in depression and in animal models.

Interestingly, although uncontrollable stress did not reduce overall HC/HR choice, it altered how participants integrated reward-related information when making effort-based decisions.

Reward magnitude positively predicted HC/HR choices in both groups, but more strongly in the controllable group. Thus, participants exposed to uncontrollable outcomes appeared to be less sensitive to increases in potential reward magnitude when deciding whether to exert effort.

Similarly, the effect of reward probability differed between groups, such that participants in the controllable group were less likely than those in the uncontrollable group to select the high-effort option when the probability of receiving the reward was low (12%). Moreover, participants exposed to uncontrollable outcomes showed reduced sensitivity to increases in expected value (EV) -defined as reward magnitude multiplied by the probability of receiving the reward -when deciding whether to exert effort (Table S9). This pattern aligns with results from Treadway and colleagues who found that individuals with MDD based their effort allocation less on reward magnitude and reward probability compared with healthy controls 12 . Although the effect of expected value itself did not reach statistical significance in their study, the direction of the findings was consistent with reduced integration of reward-related information in depression. A recent study also found that experimentally induced low perceived control attenuated sensitivity to rewards compared with a high-control condition 48 . Overall, our findings and those from this recent study suggest that uncontrollability may not necessarily impair motivation to exert effort for rewards itself, but rather alter the evaluation of reward information underlying effort-based decisions.

Specifically, individuals exposed to uncontrollable outcomes may be less efficient at integrating reward magnitude and probability to compute the expected value of an action, leading to altered decisions about whether effort investment is worthwhile.

Social behavior

Similarly, we found no evidence that experimentally induced uncontrollability altered measures of social avoidance or hierarchy, including preferred interpersonal distance, head posture, or signature size. This contrasts with evidence linking uncontrollable stress in animals and depression in humans to social withdrawal and reduced social status. Individuals with MDD report greater social avoidance 14 and maintain greater interpersonal distance 16 , while animal models show reduced social interaction following inescapable versus escapable shocks 22, 23.

Depression has also been linked to alterations in social hierarchy and dominance-related behaviors. Evidence indicates that depressive symptoms are associated with lower perceived social rank 49 , submissive behaviors, and feelings of inferiority 50, 51 . It has also been shown that depressed patients have a more slumped posture 17 . In particular, depression is associated with a more forward head inclination 18, 52 , and this disappears during remission, where participants' head angle becomes comparable to healthy controls 53 . Similarly, rodents exposed to inescapable stress show reduced social competitiveness and increased submissive behavior compared with animals exposed to controllable stressors 25, 26, 54, 55 . Taken together, our findings suggest that a single laboratory induction of uncontrollability may be insufficient to produce measurable changes in social behavior in healthy humans, in contrast to findings from animal models.

Limitations

One limitation of our study is the unequal sex distribution, with substantially more females than males (120 vs. 36). This is particularly relevant given emerging evidence that the effect of stressor controllability may differ between sexes. For example, one study found that controllability did not buffer against the behavioral consequences of stress in female rats, suggesting reduced or absent protective effects in females 56 . In contrast, another study found that inescapable stress impaired escape learning in male rats, whereas most females acquired escape responses regardless of stressor controllability 57 . Together, these findings suggest that the relationship between stressor controllability and stress-induced behavioral outcomes may differ across sexes, highlighting the importance of considering sex as a potential moderator in LH research. Although sex did not moderate group effects in our study, the small number of males may have limited our ability to detect such effects.

Another limitation concerns the time elapsed between LH induction and subsequent behavioral measures. Because the effects of uncontrollability may be transient, greater confidence may be placed in findings from measures administered immediately following the induction, such as the anagram task. Findings from measures administered later should be interpreted with greater caution. In our study, approximately one hour elapsed between the LH induction and the EEfRT, raising the possibility that any effects on reward-related effort allocation had diminished by the time of assessment.

Our study was also limited by ethical constraints, which limit the intensity and duration of aversive stimulation that can be administered in human studies. Thus, the aversive experience induced by loud noise in our paradigm may have differed in intensity from that produced by electric shocks in animal models, potentially contributing to the absence of detectable behavioral effects in humans. However, our 90 db sound stimulation was within the range previously shown to induce behavioral effects in human LH paradigms 5, 35, 58 . Nevertheless, differences in the nature, intensity, and duration of aversive stimulation across species and experimental protocols remain a potential factor contributing to differences in the behavioral consequences of uncontrollability.

Lastly, another limitation of the study regards our measures of social behavior. We focused on implicit behavioral measures of social behavior, such as posture and interpersonal distance, to reduce potential biases associated with self-report measures, including social desirability effects.

However, these measures may not have been sensitive enough to capture subtle changes in social behavior.

Conclusion

Overall, this preregistered study, one of the largest experimental investigations of human LH date, provides a comprehensive assessment of the behavioral consequences of acute uncontrollable stress across multiple domains, including reward learning, effort-based decision making, and social behavior. Acute experimentally induced uncontrollability reliably reduced subjective feelings of control but produced surprisingly selective behavioral consequences. We could not replicate the classic LH effect in humans using the anagram test. Moreover, neither reward processing nor social behavior was significantly affected by our LH paradigm. The strongest evidence for an effect of stressor controllability emerged from the EEfRT, where uncontrollability reduced sensitivity to reward magnitude and expected value without altering overall willingness to exert effort.

Overall, these findings provide limited evidence that acute stressor controllability produces a broad depression-like behavioral phenotype in humans, as observed in animal models, and call into question the robustness and generalizability of findings from early experimental studies of LH in humans. Together, these findings suggest that the behavioral effects of acute LH in healthy adults may be more specific than classical theories propose, highlighting altered reward valuation as a promising mechanism through which uncontrollability influences motivated behavior while indicating that broader behavioral impairments may require more prolonged or repeated exposure to uncontrollable stress or additional vulnerability factors.
