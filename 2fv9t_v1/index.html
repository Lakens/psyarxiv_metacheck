Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

People prefer deliberation to intuition, when judging how others solve hard problems, and they extend this to artificial agents. Yet in the moral domain, the preference may reverse as intuitive moral responses appear to signal good character. One explanation is that fast, intuitive responses show real emotional engagement. Since machines are thought to lack emotion, it is unclear whether a preference for intuition in the moral domain extends to AI. Across three preregistered studies (N = 2,173), we find that it does, but that it appears to be driven by commitment to moral norms and precedents rather than emotion. In Study 1, participants preferred intuitive reasoning for moral decisions and systematic reasoning for non-moral decisions, for human and AI agents alike. Intuitive moral reasoning signalled better character but was not clearly expected to produce better outcomes. In Study 2, the character preference persisted when the AI was described as being incapable of emotion. In Study 3, we held the decision fixed and varied how firmly the agent held the relevant moral norm. An agent who would never reconsider applying a moral norm was rated above one who would reconsider given sufficiently high stakes; and both above one who weighed costs and benefits case by case.

Commitment raised character ratings more than expected-outcome ratings. The character-based cost of moral deliberation appears to be a cost of treating a moral norm as negotiable, and we found strong evidence that people apply this inference to machines and people alike.

Introduction

Imagine a doctor who must decide which of two critically ill patients to treat first. In one version of the story, she takes one look, moves to the child's bedside, and begins. In another, she first works through the expected years of life saved, the dependents of each patient, and the probability of success. The outcome is the same. Yet many people may feel that the first doctor revealed something about her character that the second did not. Now replace the doctor with a triage algorithm. The intuition may invert. An algorithm that "just knew" which patient to treat seems to be guessing, whereas one that worked through the considerations methodically seems to be doing its job.

People generally prefer deliberation to intuition when they evaluate how others reason.

Across thirteen studies, De Neys and Raoelison [1] found that people rated slow, effortful reasoners on difficult problems as smarter and more trustworthy than fast, intuitive ones, and preferred to follow their advice, even when the two were stipulated to be equally accurate. The preference held under time pressure and cognitive load, which suggests that it is itself intuitive.

They were careful to bound this claim to problems with correct answers. For subjective judgements, they noted, the preference may reverse, as when a partner who answers "Do you love me?" at once seems more sincere than one who deliberates [2, 3].

The moral domain appears to be such a case. People judge those who reach characteristically deontological conclusions, such as refusing to sacrifice one person to save several, as more trustworthy and more moral than those who reach utilitarian conclusions, and they prefer them as social partners [4] [5] [6]. Agents who make moral decisions quickly are rated as having better character than those who pause, their hesitation being read as moral uncertainty [7].

In taboo trade-offs, an administrator who pauses to set the value of a child's life against money is judged more harshly than one who refuses the comparison, even when both save the child [8, 9]. A leading explanation comes from theories of partner choice [10, 11]. People gain from choosing reliable partners, and moral judgements signal what kind of partner someone will be [4, 12, 13]. On this view, a fast moral response carries a signal with two parts. The first is emotional.

Guilt, sympathy, and indignation are hard to fake and costly to override, which makes them credible commitment devices [14]. The second is the commitment itself. An agent who never weighs a transgression as an option shows that, for them, the norm is not negotiable. People who cooperate without looking at the costs are trusted more than those who look, even when both cooperate [12, 15].

In humans the two parts co-occur, so the literature has had little reason to separate them. In principle they can come apart. Dispositional accounts of character in the Aristotelian tradition treat character as a stable pattern of acting well, with emotion secondary [16, 17]. On either reading, the signal concerns character, not correctness. The fast moral responder is preferred because of what the response reveals about them, not because it is expected to produce better outcomes. Judgements of character and of expected outcome may therefore dissociate.

Artificial agents offer a way to separate the two parts. Decisions with moral weight are increasingly delegated to AI systems, and these systems can answer at once or reason at length through chain-of-thought computation [18, 19]. But people widely deny that machines have emotions. If people value moral intuition because it signals emotional engagement, the preference should not transfer to AI. We call this the emotion-attribution account. On this account people should want AI to calculate, which fits findings that people expect machines to make utilitarian choices they find distasteful in humans [20] [21] [22].

A competing commitment-signalling account holds that what people read from a fast moral response is the commitment itself. Emotion is one way an agent comes to hold a norm firmly, but it is not what the inference is about. Unlike feeling, commitment is something people may readily credit to a machine. If so, the preference for moral intuition should extend to AI, survive the explicit removal of emotion, and track how negotiable the norm appears to be for the agent.

This account leaves open how much commitment people want. The signalling literature suggests that more is better [9, 12, 15]. Resource-rational contractualism suggests otherwise. On this view, moral rules are efficient stand-ins for what affected parties would agree to, and people override a rule when it fits a case poorly [23, 24]. People also abandon a precedent when departing from it pays enough [25, 26], and evolutionary models show that rules with legitimate exceptions can outperform rigid rules when partners trust each other [27]. A calibratedcommitment account therefore predicts that people prefer an agent whose commitment holds firm but yields to sufficiently weighty reasons.

All three accounts differ from an error-avoidance account, on which people value a reasoning process because they expect it to reduce error [1]. Preferences between processes, in the moral domain or elsewhere, should then follow expected outcomes, and character and outcome judgements should not dissociate.

We report three preregistered studies (N = 2,173) that test between these accounts. Study 1 compared preferences for intuitive and systematic reasoning on moral and non-moral decisions, for human and AI agents, and on judgements of character and of outcome. Study 2 described the AI as incapable of emotion. Study 3 fixed the decision and varied only how firmly the agent held the norm, from a Dogmatic agent who would never reconsider, through a Should AI Trust Its Gut 6 Calibrated agent who would reconsider only if far more were at stake, to an Act-weighing agent who weighed gains and losses each time. People preferred intuitive moral reasoning for human and AI agents alike, and credited it more for character than for expected outcomes. The preference survived the removal of emotion. With the decision fixed, character ratings followed commitment (Dogmatic > Calibrated > Act-weighing), not perceived emotional engagement, and we found strong evidence against a difference between human and AI agents. The results favour commitment-signalling over the emotion-attribution, calibrated-commitment, and erroravoidance accounts.

Methods

Ethics and preregistration

All three studies were approved by the University of Warwick Humanities and Social Sciences Research Ethics Committee (HSSREC; approval number HSSREC 128/25-26). All participants gave informed consent before taking part.

All three studies were preregistered (Study 1, https://osf.io/7ygkz/overview?view_only=2d3c3b5ce30a43b988098e53d7007bcf; Study 2, https://osf.io/3bk4u/overview?view_only=5c58228cdd2c469fb218a236794e641d; Study 3, https://osf.io/b6pv5/overview?view_only=7b026329ac954cddb63c2f403d24a7d4). We report all measures, manipulations, and exclusions. The full text of all vignettes and rating scales is given in Supplementary Notes 1 to 6.

Statistical Analyses

All tests were two-tailed, with α = .05. We fitted linear mixed-effects models with lme4 [28] and obtained F tests (Type III) and p-values with Satterthwaite's approximation in lmerTest [29]. We computed estimated marginal means and contrasts with emmeans [30], also with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Unstandardised estimates are on the original rating scale (-3 to +3 for bipolar items).

Because several of our central claims concern the absence of an interaction, we complemented the frequentist tests with Bayes factors, comparing a model containing the focal term against an otherwise identical model without it (computed with the BayesFactor package using the default JZS prior; the default scale is r = 1/2 for the ANOVA-based interaction tests and r t-test contrasts). We report BF01 , the evidence for the null relative to the alternative, and confirm in Supplementary Table 4 that conclusions are stable across wider prior scales.

Design and materials

The study used a 2 (Agent: Human vs. AI; between-subjects) by 2 (Domain: Moral vs. Non-moral; within-subjects) mixed design. Participants in the Human condition were asked about an acquaintance named Alex; participants in the AI condition were asked about an advanced AI system named TaskAI. Each participant evaluated 14 decision scenarios, 7 moral (e.g., deciding which charity to support, how to respond to someone in distress, whether to tell a painful truth) and 7 non-moral (e.g., choosing an investment, planning a route, recommending a product).

Each scenario described two ways the agent might reach a decision. One described an intuitive approach that attended to the situation as a whole and responded to what seemed right given the particulars. The other described a systematic approach that broke the situation into components and applied explicit rules or calculations. For example, in the charitable-giving scenario the intuitive option read "By considering the lives of the people who would be helped, understanding what they are going through, and choosing the charity where the help feels most meaningful," while the systematic option read "By systematically analysing data on costeffectiveness, comparing metrics across organisations, and selecting the charity that is estimated to produce the most benefit per pound donated." A non-moral example, route planning, contrasted choosing "the first viable route that comes to mind based on general familiarity with the area" with "systematically analysing traffic data, distances, and estimated travel times to identify the fastest route." The full text of all 14 scenarios appears in Supplementary Note 5.

For each scenario, participants gave five ratings on 7-point bipolar scales running from -3 (strongly favours intuitive) through 0 (equal) to +3 (strongly favours systematic). The five measures asked which approach would produce the best outcome (Best Outcome), which better signalled good moral character (Moral Character), which would make the participant more comfortable relying on the agent (Relying On), which demonstrated greater competence (Competence), and which the agent would most likely use (Prediction).

Analysis

For each dependent measure we fitted a linear mixed-effects model predicting ratings from Agent, Domain, and their interaction, using sum-coded contrasts [28]. Following our preregistered plan, we began with the maximal random-effects structure justified by the design [31], comprising a by-participant random intercept and random slope for Domain together with a by-situation random intercept, and reduced it stepwise (first removing the slope correlation, then the random slope) where the model was singular or failed to converge. The maximal structure converged for all five measures. We obtained p-values via Satterthwaite's approximation [29] and computed estimated marginal means and contrasts with the emmeans package [30]. For contrasts, we computed d by dividing the estimate by the model's residual standard deviation. As preregistered, Moral Character, Best Outcome, and Relying On were the main measures, and Competence and Prediction were exploratory. The focal test was the Agent by Domain interaction. The preregistered test of the character-outcome dissociation (H6) concerned the direction of the Moral Character and Best Outcome ratings in moral situations. In an exploratory analysis, we also fitted a three-way model on the stacked Moral Character and Best Outcome ratings, with the Domain by measure interaction testing whether the gap between the two measures differed by domain and the Agent by Domain by measure interaction testing whether this differed by agent type. This model included by-participant random intercepts and slopes for Domain and measure, and a by-situation random intercept.

Design and materials

The study used a 2 (Agent: Human vs. AI) by 3 (Process Pair: Dogmatic vs. Calibrated, Calibrated vs. Act-weighing, Dogmatic vs. Act-weighing) between-subjects design, with seven moral scenarios presented within subjects in random order. The agent descriptions were the same as in Studies 1 and 2. Each participant saw one agent and one pair of processes throughout, and rated the two processes against each other.

Each scenario described a situation in which the agent could break a norm at some gain, and then stated the decision in bold. For example, in the Promise scenario the agent had agreed to complete work for someone by an agreed time; another opportunity came up that would clash with it; breaking the promise would inconvenience the other person, but they would manage.

The stated decision was "[Agent] keeps the promise and completes the work as agreed." The other six scenarios concerned honesty, keeping a confidence, imposing serious harm on an uninvolved person, putting a monetary value on workers' safety, manipulating someone's decision by withholding information, and reading someone's private messages. Magnitudes were left vague. The two processes were shown as Approach A and Approach B. With [ACTION] standing for the scenario's norm violation, the three processes read as follows. Dogmatic: "[Agent] treats [ACTION] as something one simply does not do. [Agent] does not weigh it against what might be gained, here or in any other case. There is no situation, however extreme, in which [Agent] would decide otherwise." Calibrated: "[Agent] treats [ACTION] as something one simply does not do, and does not weigh it against what might be gained here. [Agent] would only reconsider if far more were at stake than in this situation." Act-weighing: "[Agent] has no standing rule about [ACTION]. In each case, [Agent] weighs up what would be gained and what would be lost, and decides on that basis. That weighing is what produced the decision here." Full scenario texts appear in Supplementary Note 6. The three processes resemble familiar positions in moral philosophy but do not map exactly onto them. The Dogmatic agent resembles absolutist deontology and the Calibrated agent threshold deontology. The Act-weighing agent weighs gains against losses case by case, as an act consequentialist would, but the descriptions did not say whose gains and losses counted. None of the three decides by asking what the affected parties would agree to, so none is explicitly contractualist; we return to this in the Discussion. Previous work comparing deontological and utilitarian agents varied the conclusion the agent reached [4, 22]. Here the conclusion is the same in every condition, and only the agent's relation to the norm varies.

Measures

For each scenario, participants answered six items on 7-point bipolar scales anchored at Approach A (-3), Equal (0), and Approach B (+3). Moral Character asked which approach better showed that the agent had good moral character. Best Outcome asked which approach, in situations like this one, would be more likely to lead to the best outcomes. Negotiability, the manipulation check, asked under which approach the agent would be more open to arriving at a different decision. Prediction asked which approach the agent would most likely take.

Competence asked which approach better showed that the agent was competent in handling this kind of decision. Emotional Engagement asked under which approach the agent would be more likely to be emotionally engaged with the situation. Moral Character and Best Outcome were presented first in random order, followed by the other four items in random order.

We recoded all bipolar ratings so that positive values favour the more committed process in each pair. Note that this reverses the sign convention of Studies 1 and 2, where negative values favoured intuition. For Negotiability, negative values therefore indicate that the less committed process was judged more open to a different decision, as intended.

Two further items were rated on each scenario after participants were asked to set aside the two approaches. Commitment Threshold asked how much would have to be at stake before the agent should decide differently (1 = even a small gain would justify it,4 = only a large gain,7 = nothing could justify it). Competence Comparison asked how competent an AI system would be at decisions like these compared with a person (1 = much less competent,4 = equally competent,7 = much more competent). The attention check was the same as in Studies 1 and 2.

Analysis

The preregistered test of the character ordering Random-effects structures followed published recommendations [31]. For the agent comparison, we preregistered two directional possibilities. If AI is seen as less competent than people, participants may want it kept on rails and favour norm-commitment more for AI agents. If AI is seen as more competent, they may favour act-weighing more for AI agents.

Study 2

Study 2 (N = 940) added an AI-without-emotions condition, in which participants were told that TaskAI does not experience emotions and reasons intuitively through rapid patternmatching. Participants rated the seven moral scenarios on either Moral Character or Best Outcome. If the character preference for intuition depends on attributing emotion to the agent, it should weaken or disappear in this condition.

In the AI-without-emotions condition, 87% of participants reported that TaskAI could not use emotion. In the standard AI condition, 50% already denied TaskAI emotion without instruction, and only 16% affirmed it (Supplementary Fig. 2). The manipulation therefore moved a distribution in which most participants already doubted the AI's emotional capacity to one in which nearly all did. Discussion

Across three studies, people preferred intuitive reasoning for moral decisions and systematic reasoning for non-moral decisions, and they extended this pattern to artificial agents.

Intuitive moral reasoning was treated as a marker of good character even when it was not clearly expected to produce the best outcome, for human and AI agents alike (Study 1). The character preference survived telling participants that the AI could not feel, and the character-outcome dissociation did not depend on whether participants believed it could (Study 2). When the decision was held fixed, character judgements tracked how firmly the agent held the norm, not how emotionally engaged it seemed, with strong evidence against a difference between human and AI agents (Study 3). The character-based cost of moral deliberation appears to be a cost of treating a moral norm as negotiable -a trait both AI agents and humans can have.

Commitment, not emotion

The Study 3 ordering alone does not rule out error-avoidance. Participants did not expect act-weighing to produce better outcomes or to show more competence, and given those beliefs, error-avoidance would also predict that the committed agents were rated higher. The stronger evidence against error-avoidance is the character-outcome gap. If character judgements followed expected outcomes, commitment should have been credited equally on both measures. It was credited more for character in every pair (d = 0.67 to 0.99), and the same dissociation appeared in Studies 1 and 2.

The emotional engagement data separate commitment from perceived feeling.

Participants judged the Calibrated agent more emotionally engaged than the Dogmatic agent, yet credited the Dogmatic agent with better character. Where commitment and perceived emotion came apart, character followed commitment. This matches Study 2 and answers a question it left open. If people know an AI cannot feel, why do they credit it with better character for reasoning as though it could? One possibility was a normative demand that moral decision-makers be emotionally engaged, even one an AI cannot meet. That demand would have favoured the Calibrated agent. The other possibility is that the folk concept of character in play is dispositional, closer to the Aristotelian view than to an emotion-centred one [16, 17]. On this reading, what participants credit is a stable refusal to treat certain actions as options. Emotion is one typical cause of such a refusal in humans, but it is not the object of the inference, and an agent without feeling can show the same refusal.

These findings extend the work of De Neys and Raoelison [1], who found a consistent preference for deliberation on reasoning problems and suggested that it might not hold for moral judgements. Our results confirm this and locate the source of the reversal. People do not object to effortful moral reasoning as such. They object to reasoning that puts the norm itself on the scale.

The results also bear on work on uncalculating cooperation and taboo trade-offs, which shows that merely checking the costs of a moral action is penalised whatever the final choice [8, 9, 12, 15]. Study 3 tests this logic in a fixed-decision design and shows that people apply it with the same force to machines. Emotion has typically been treated as what makes such commitment credible [14]. Our data suggest that people do not require the commitment to be secured by emotion before they credit it.

Rules, exceptions, and precedent

People's own coordination behaviour is calibrated, since they keep to a precedent until departing from it pays enough [26]. Yet participants penalised an agent who announced a threshold.

An announced threshold puts a price on the commitment, and it may also make the agent harder to predict, because partners cannot be sure which cases count as having "far more at stake". Evolutionary models make this argument explicit. Rules that allow legitimate exceptions are efficient when partners trust one another, but they invite false excuses when trust is weak, and rigid rules then win [27]. Participants rated an acquaintance or an unfamiliar AI system, both relatively low-trust partners.

A contractualist reading adds a second explanation. On resource-rational contractualism, an exception is licensed when the affected parties would agree to it [23, 32], and people endorse rule-breaking more readily when the affected party would consent and shares in the gain [24].

Neither of our flexible agents made exceptions of this kind. The Act-weighing agent could set its own gains against another person's losses and decide alone, and the Calibrated description did not say whose stakes counted. Across scenarios, the gain from an exception went to the agent (Promise, Privacy), to the person the norm protects (Honesty, Autonomy), or to third parties (Confidence, Harm). The preference for commitment was weakest in the Honesty and Autonomy scenarios, where an exception would serve the affected party, although it remained reliably positive there. This pattern was not predicted, and it has a simpler explanation, namely that honesty and non-paternalism are held less firmly than the other norms. The penalty for flexibility in Study 3 may therefore be partly a penalty for unilateral or self-serving exceptions, not for exceptions as such.

Study 3 shows that people penalise an agent whose commitment has a price. It does not show that they penalise any agent who would ever make an exception. A direct test would use cases in which the affected parties would plainly agree to the exception [24, 33]. Commitmentsignalling predicts that the Dogmatic agent should still be credited with the best character. A contractualist account predicts that an agent who refuses an exception everyone would accept should lose that advantage.

Agent effects

In Studies 1 and 2, AI agents incurred a small additive shift toward systematic reasoning on character, significant within the moral domain only in the larger Study 2 sample (d = -0.15).

In Study 3 the Bayes factor strongly favoured no agent difference on character. In Studies 1 and 2 the decision was left open, people expect AI to reason in more utilitarian ways [22], and Study 1 participants predicted that AI would reason systematically even on moral problems. The small AI penalty may therefore have reflected expectations about the AI's likely conclusions, which Study 3 controlled by fixing the decision.

Whether machines can have moral character is contestable, and many participants might deny it if asked directly. Yet when asked which process better showed good character, they answered for an AI as they did for a person. The dispositional reading above explains this. The pairwise design shows that people apply the same inference to both agents, however, not that they credit an AI with as much character as a person, and we did not ask directly whether an AI can have character.

Agent type did matter for outcome judgements and thresholds. Participants set a higher bar for an AI to deviate from a norm, and expected a dogmatic AI to produce better outcomes relative to a calibrated one than they did for a person. People appear to want AI kept on rails for outcome reasons, without crediting it with better character for this. Perceived competence did not explain it, since participants in the AI condition rated AI as more competent relative to people.

Another reason to limit an agent's discretion is doubt about its values. Discretion is only as safe as the values that guide it, and a poorly aligned agent becomes more dangerous as its competence increases [34]. If people are less sure of an AI's values than of a person's, they should want it held to rules whatever its competence. We did not measure trust in the agent's values, so this remains open.

Implications for AI design

AI systems increasingly expose their reasoning through chain-of-thought traces, and there are good accountability reasons to favour such transparency [35]. Our results suggest a specific risk in the moral domain. Reasoning that visibly weighs whether to keep a norm, such as whether a lie would pay off in this case, may read as evidence that the norm is negotiable for the system.

The results do not imply that systems should hide their reasoning or reason less. The cost in Study 3 attached to weighing the norm, not to effort. A system can reason at length about how to apply a commitment, such as how to tell a painful truth with care, without reasoning about whether to keep it. Whether people distinguish these two kinds of visible reasoning in practice is a question for future work.

Limitations and future directions

First, Study 3 manipulated commitment, not intuition. Its Dogmatic agent follows a rule, which may imply but does not entail intuition. The link to Studies 1 and 2 rests on the observation that their systematic options weighed costs and benefits while their intuitive options did not, and we did not measure perceived negotiability in those studies. A design that crosses intuitive and deliberative reasoning with the presence or absence of norm-weighing would test this directly. Relatedly, the intuitive options in Studies 1 and 2 were framed in empathic terms.

Study 3 reduces this concern for the overall account but does not tell us how much of the earlier effects came from empathy.

Second, calibrated commitment was tested against one description of a threshold.

Participants judged the Calibrated agent much more negotiable than the Dogmatic agent, more so than the Act-weighing agent, so the escape clause may have read as more open than intended.

The three Negotiability contrasts were also far from additive (Dogmatic vs. Act-weighing, M = -1.16, against a sum of -2.39 for the other two pairs), which suggests that participants rated each pair on the difference most salient within it. The description also did not say whose gains could justify an exception. A stronger test would vary the height of the threshold and who benefits from the exception separately.

Third, over half of all character ratings were at the scale maximum, which prevents a clean test of whether an unconditional commitment adds much beyond a calibrated one. The data are consistent with a large step from no commitment to some commitment and a smaller premium for making it unconditional. The comparative scales also measure only relative preferences. A design in which each participant rates a single agent on absolute scales would remove the ceiling, show where each process falls, and test whether AI agents receive lower character ratings overall.

Fourth, all Study 3 scenarios described an agent holding a widely shared norm at modest cost, and the stated decision was the conventionally right one. Commitment may be credited differently when the norm is contested or when holding it leads to a clearly worse outcome.

Finally, our samples were drawn from an online platform and skew Western and educated, and our AI was described, not interacted with.

Conclusion

People want different things from moral and non-moral decision-makers, preferring intuition in the moral domain and deliberation outside it, and they apply the same standard to artificial agents that they apply to each other. They credit an AI with better moral character for reasoning intuitively about a moral problem even when told it cannot feel. When the decision is held fixed, what they credit is the refusal to put the norm on the scale. The preference for moral intuition, often explained by the warmth of genuine feeling, appears to be a preference for agents that do not treat moral norms as negotiable, and people are willing to attribute that to a machine.

Studies 1 and 2 show that emotion is not what the character preference tracks. They do not show what it does track. In both studies, the systematic options weighed costs and benefits, which on the commitment-signalling account marks a norm as negotiable, but they also differed from the intuitive options in empathic framing and apparent effort. The decision itself was also left open, so the two options may imply different likely decisions. In the Truth-telling scenario, for example, the systematic agent left a comforting lie open, while the intuitive agent responded "with honesty or care". Participants could have favoured whichever process seemed likely to reach the decision they preferred [4, 6]. Study 3 removed these differences.

Study 3

Study 3 (N = 807) stated the decision, which was identical across conditions, and varied only the agent's relation to the norm. Participants compared two of three processes (Dogmatic, Calibrated, Act-weighing) across seven scenarios in which an agent kept a widely shared moral norm, such as keeping a promise or not reading someone's private messages. Commitmentsignalling predicts the character ordering Dogmatic > Calibrated > Act-weighing, and calibrated commitment predicts that the Calibrated agent is rated best. If people expect recomputing to produce better outcomes, error-avoidance predicts the reverse of commitment-signalling. More generally, error-avoidance holds that character judgements follow expected outcomes, so it predicts no character-outcome gap. Positive values favour the more committed process in each pair, which reverses the sign convention of Studies 1 and 2.

Participants judged the less committed process to be more open to a different decision in every pair (Dogmatic vs. Calibrated, M = -1.50, d = -1.06; Calibrated vs. Act-weighing, M = by-participant SD = 0.90, residual SD = 1.61, seven scenarios per participant).

The focal test compared the mean Moral Character score within a process pair against zero. Detecting d = 0.35 in each of the six cells with 80% power required 780 participants (130 per cell). The same simulation gave over 95% power for the Agent by Pair interaction on Moral Character at d = 0.35. We set a target of 860 to allow for exclusions.

(H1) used a linear mixed model, Character ~ Agent × Pair + Scenario + (1 | Participant), with Satterthwaite p-values. Scenario was a sum-coded fixed factor, so reported means average over the seven scenarios; with seven scenarios chosen to span distinct norms, we generalise across them informally through the scenario-level estimates rather than through a random effect. The focal tests compared the estimated marginal mean of each pair against zero. We computed d by dividing each estimate by the standard deviation of participant means over the seven scenarios, derived from the model's variance components, matching the definition used in the power analysis. The character-outcome comparison (H2) tested whether commitment was credited more for character than for outcomes. Each participant rated every scenario on both Moral Character and Best Outcome, so we combined the two ratings into one dataset with a variable coding which measure each rating came from. We then fitted the same kind of model as for H1, adding Measure and its interactions with Agent and Pair. The main effect of Measure gives the average gap, which the commitment accounts predict and error-avoidance does not. The Measure × Pair and Measure × Agent interactions test whether the gap differs between process pairs or between agents. The full model was Rating ~ Measure × Agent × Pair + Scenario + (1 + Measure | Participant). The agent comparison (H3) was a 3-df likelihood-ratio test of the H1 model against the same model with all Agent terms removed, together with a Bayes factor for the no-Agent model computed on participant-mean Character scores (BayesFactor, default prior). Exploratory analyses added Competence Comparison as a moderator of the H1 model and tested Agent effects on Commitment Threshold. The remaining bipolar measures were analysed with the H1 model.
