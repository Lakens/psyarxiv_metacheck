Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

How a language divides the color spectrum into named categories is increasingly explained as efficient compression: a vocabulary is a code that names colors as accurately as possible with as few words as possible. Every such model needs a description of how similar colors look, and nearly all use the same one: a standard, evenly spaced color space (CIE L*a*b*, or CIELAB) in which each color carries a fixed, symmetric blur of perceptual uncertainty. Here I argue that this assumption is wrong in a way that matters. When people with normal color vision judge hues they disagree in systematic ways, and the pattern of that disagreement, summarized by factor analysis, points to roughly eight narrow, largely independent regions that tile the color circle and lie near category boundaries rather than their best examples. I show how to build this measured structure into the standard model by stretching the circle at the category boundaries. A full test substitutes the reshaped space into the information-bottleneck model and rescores all 110 World Color Survey languages. Under the model's near-uniform default prior the warp is close to a wash; but under a realistic, measured communicative need a modest warp improves fit for most languages, a small but systematic benefit the uniform prior masked, although the predicted optimal category systems themselves barely change. The effect is small and conditional, marking when a non-uniform perceptual space matters. The proposal yields a prediction testable with public data and code, informative whether it succeeds or fails.

1. Introduction

Color is the classic test case for a deep question: when a language sorts a smooth, continuous range of experience into a handful of named categories, what decides where the categories go? The question has deep roots in psychology: Brown and Lenneberg (1954) tied how easily a color is named to how well it is remembered, and Eleanor Rosch (publishing as Heider, 1972) showed that focal colors are named and remembered consistently even across languages with very different color vocabularies. For more than half a century the leading answers have been organized around the basic color terms that Berlin and Kay (1969) catalogued across languages and that the World Color Survey later documented in detail. In the past two decades those answers have become quantitative. The dominant idea is that a color vocabulary is an efficient code: it should let a listener recover the color a speaker has in mind as accurately as possible, while keeping the number of color words small (Regier, Kay, & Khetarpal, 2007; Zaslavsky, Kemp, Regier, & Tishby, 2018). Models built on this idea reproduce many regularities seen across the world's languages (Zaslavsky et al., 2018). They explain much of the rest by noting that some colors simply need to be talked about more often than others (Gibson et al., 2017; Twomey, Roberts, Brainard, & Plotkin, 2021).

Every model of this kind rests on three ingredients: a description of how similar colors look to one another, a measure of how often each color needs to be communicated, and a rule that trades the accuracy of the vocabulary against its size. The second and third ingredients have been studied closely. The first, the perceptual part, has been treated as settled background. In practice it is almost always the same: each color sits in a standard color space, wrapped in a small, symmetric cloud of uncertainty of fixed size and shape. Two colors are confusable to the degree that they sit close together. This amounts to assuming that the perceptual map of color is smooth and even, with no special regions. In calling this the perceptual ingredient I mean the input the model takes as given, not a claim that perceptual similarity is logically prior to, or independent of, communicative use; whether the perceptual map is itself partly shaped by use is a separate question, and one the present test leaves open.

My argument is that this assumption is incorrect, and incorrect in a way that bears on the central question of the vision-language interface: how much of the shape of color vocabularies comes from perception, and how much from the pressures of communication. If the perceptual map is not smooth, if perception itself already divides the color circle into category-sized regions, then some of the work the efficiency models credit to communication may instead be done, quietly, by perception. The evidence that the map is uneven in just this way comes from individual differences in how people judge color, and the rest of this paper draws out what that evidence implies for the models and turns it into a testable prediction.

A note on sources. The perceptual measurements this paper builds on were reported in earlier studies of hue scaling and color naming (Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017a , 2017b , 2023), of which I am a co-author. The step taken here, treating that structure as a perceptual prior for models of color naming, and specifying and testing how to build it into them, extends that work rather than restating it (see the Author Note).

2. The perceptual ingredient that efficiency models assume

The reliance on a smooth perceptual space is explicit in the founding work. Regier, Kay, and Khetarpal (2007) pointed out that the color chips used in cross-linguistic surveys are catalogued in a system (the Munsell system) that does not come with a meaningful way to measure how different two colors look. To supply one, they placed every chip in CIELAB.

CIELAB (short for the CIE L*a*b* space, defined by the International Commission on

Illumination) is a standardized color space built so that the straight-line distance between two colors corresponds, at least roughly, to how different they appear. It has three axes: lightness, a red-to-green axis, and a yellow-to-blue axis. Treating CIELAB as the perceptual space is convenient and reasonable as a first pass, but it carries a strong hidden assumption: that a given step in any direction, anywhere in the space, looks like the same-sized change in color. Regier and colleagues asked which ways of dividing this space into categories are most efficient, and found that the efficient divisions resemble the color systems languages actually use. They framed their account as a middle path between two older views, one in which categories form around universal best examples, and one in which their boundaries are set by linguistic convention. This optimal-partition idea had an earlier origin in a proposal by Jameson and D'Andrade (1997) that it is the irregular shape of perceptual color space, rather than communicative convention, that governs where categories fall. Zaslavsky et al. (2018) turned this into a precise theory of compression, the information bottleneck, the specific model tested throughout this paper. A speaker has a color in mind, holds a slightly uncertain mental image of it, and sends a single word; a good vocabulary keeps the listener's reconstruction accurate while keeping the number of words small. The trade-off is governed by a single knob (written β): turn it up and the most efficient vocabulary splits into more categories, in an order that echoes the sequence Berlin and Kay described. Here too, the mental image of a color is a bell-shaped "blur" centered on its location in CIELAB, with one fixed width meant to capture how far apart two colors must be before they are easily told apart.

The model was fit to the standard 330-chip color array and to naming data from 110 languages.

Later work has made the inputs to these models more realistic, but only on one side. Zaslavsky, Kemp, Tishby, and Regier (2019) separated the influence of "perceptual structure" from that of "communicative need," yet the perceptual structure was still just distances in CIELAB. Gibson et al. (2017) and Twomey et al. (2021) showed that need is highly uneven: some regions of color space are talked about far more than others; a recent extension even absorbs a language's history as a further non-perceptual constraint (Twomey, Brainard, & Plotkin, 2024). But that unevenness is in how often colors come up, not in how colors look. The part of the model that describes how similar two nearby colors look has stayed smooth and even everywhere (see also Chaabouni, Kharitonov, Dupoux, & Baroni, 2021).

It is worth being exact about the claim, because these models are not naïve. They already allow the color chips to be spread unevenly through CIELAB (Regier et al., 2007), and they already allow some colors to matter more than others (Twomey et al., 2021). What they assume to be uniform is something narrower: the local sense of how different two neighboring colors look, a single blur width, the same in every direction and at every location, with no built-in division into category-sized regions. That is the assumption the individual-differences data contradict.

3. What individual differences reveal about that ingredient

People with normal color vision do not see color identically. They disagree, in stable and measurable ways, about questions like which exact shade looks like pure green, or where red ends and orange begins. These disagreements are not merely noise: the pattern of who agrees with whom, across many colors and many observers, carries information about the hidden machinery of color perception. Factor analysis is the standard statistical tool for extracting that information: it finds the smallest set of independent "dials" whose differing settings from person to person could produce the observed pattern of agreement and disagreement (Webster & MacLeod, 1988). The relevant task asks observers to judge how much red, green, blue, and yellow each of a ring of hues contains. The hues are spaced around the cone-opponent plane, the circle of equally light colors defined by the early visual system's comparisons among cone types (MacLeod & Boynton, 1979; the task and stimuli follow Webster, Miyahara, Malkoc, & Raker, 2000, and Malkoc, Kay, & Webster, 2005). Applied to these judgments, factor analysis reveals a perceptual map that is anything but smooth.

In two independent datasets, Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, and Webster (2017a) found that the disagreements among observers were captured not by two broad red-green and blueyellow dimensions, as classical color theory would predict, but by seven or eight narrow dials, each affecting only a small stretch of the color circle and acting largely on its own. A companion study (Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017b) extended this to color naming and found the same picture: the boundaries people draw between color words vary largely independently of one another, again pointing to seven or eight separate influences, some shared between naming and perceptual judgments. A later study with a matched task (Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2023) sharpened the result to nine narrow factors, each a single bump over a small range of hues, together accounting for about three-quarters of the variation among observers and best described as roughly eight localized sources. Tellingly, observers' judgments were correlated only for nearby hues, never across the circle, which is the opposite of what a smooth two-axis representation predicts, and unlike the broad, circle-spanning pattern the same authors found for the perception of motion direction. They summarized the difference by saying that hues behave less like points along a few smooth dimensions and more like a set of qualitatively distinct things. (A similar picture, many narrowly tuned color processes rather than a few broad ones, has recently emerged from cortical recordings; Kaneko, Kuriki, Andersen, & Peterzell, 2025.) Two features of this picture matter for models of naming. First, the perceptual map of hue is divided into about eight regions, close to the number of basic color categories a language like English distinguishes (the four primary hues red, green, blue, and yellow, plus the four inbetween hues orange, purple, blue-green, and yellow-green; Emery & Webster, 2019). Second, and more important, the divisions fall at the boundaries between categories rather than at their centers. It helps to keep two ideas apart: the focus of a category is its best example, the most typical red, whereas its boundary is where one category turns into the next, where red becomes orange. The individual-differences data show that observers vary most, and that the hidden dials act most strongly, near the boundaries, not at the foci (Emery et al., 2017b (Emery et al., , 2023;; cf. Webster & Kay, 2012). This connects to an old debate about color words : Regier, Kay, and Cook (2005) argued that the best examples of categories are shared across languages while their boundaries vary more, and Regier et al. (2007) explicitly set focus-based against boundary-based accounts.

The hue-scaling results give that debate a perceptual anchor on the boundary side: the places where individual differences in hue scaling are most pronounced, and which I take as candidate sites of sharper perceptual differentiation, are the category edges. In the public hue-scaling data this is directly visible: cross-observer disagreement is largest about 10° from the factor loci and smallest near the unique hues, some 32° away (Figure 1). Open symbols mark the measured best examples (foci) of the four unique hues, red, blue, green, and yellow, which fall between, and are not evenly spaced around, the cardinal axes. Filled symbols mark the peak angles of the nine systematic factors recovered from hue scaling (Emery et al., 2023); the factors tile the circle, fall in regions English has no basic word for (cyan, lime, magenta), and tend to cluster near the boundaries between categories rather than at the unique-hue foci. The contrast between this uneven, boundary-aligned structure and a smooth, even space over the circle is what the reshaped model of Equations 2-3 exploits.

Three threads of independent work make this more than a quirk of one task. First, the idea that color is carried by more than the two cardinal cone-opponent axes is long-standing, if still contested. Higher-order mechanisms tuned to intermediate directions have been reported psychophysically (Krauskopf, Williams, & Heeley, 1982; Krauskopf, Williams, Mandler, & Brown, 1986; Webster & Mollon, 1994; see also Bosten & Boehm, 2014, on how variable the unique hues are across observers), though a careful review cautions their number and status remain unsettled (Eskew, 2009). Second, cortical measurements point the same way, with responses selective for intermediate hues (Kuriki, Sun, Ueno, Tanaka, & Cheng, 2015) and a neural geometry of color that is itself non-uniform, expanding near the unique hues (Chauhan, Jakovljev, Thompson, Wuerger, & Martinovic, 2023). Third, within a single language the boundaries people draw are not shared but fall into several distinct patterns, or motifs (Lindsey & Brown, 2009 , 2014). I do not claim that the factors are literal early-cortical channels: the safer reading, and the one Emery et al. favor, is that they describe how color appears and is decoded at a later stage. For the argument that follows, what matters is only that the perceptual representation relevant to naming is measurably uneven, whatever its mechanism.

4.1. Reshaping the perceptual space

To state the proposal precisely I focus on the hue circle alone, the colors at a fixed lightness, each identified by its angle θ around the cone-opponent plane, and set aside lightness and saturation. This means setting aside categories such as brown that depend on lightness; extending the idea to the full three dimensions of color is left for later. In the informationbottleneck model, a color u is represented by a blur m that captures how easily it is confused with its neighbors. The standard choice is a bell curve of a single fixed width, centered on the color's position in CIELAB:

Here ‖x -u‖ is the ordinary straight-line distance in CIELAB and σ is the width of the blur. Because the width is the same everywhere and in every direction, the model treats every part of the color circle alike: the perceptual landscape is flat.

The factor analysis tells us the landscape is not flat. Each of the K narrow factors (about eight or nine) is summarized by three numbers measured directly from the data (Emery et al., 2023): where on the circle it sits (its peak angle μ ), how wide it is ( ₖ σ ), and how much of the ₖ between-observer variation it accounts for (its weight v ). Because the factors are strongest ₖ where observers disagree, at the category boundaries, I use them to build a measure of how sharply perception changes from place to place around the circle: large at the boundaries, gentle within categories. The factor loadings index where observers differ most in their hue scaling; I take this between-observer structure as a candidate marker of sharper within-observer differentiation, a hypothesis the model then tests, not a direct measurement of discriminability.

The proposal is simply to run the same information-bottleneck model in this stretched coordinate instead of in flat CIELAB; the standard model is the special case in which nothing is stretched (g ≡ 1). The two models differ in exactly one thing, the shape of the perceptual space, so any difference in their predictions isolates the contribution of perceptual structure. Because the stretching never reorders the hues, it changes only where the boundaries and best examples fall, which is exactly what cross-linguistic naming data can check.

4.2. What the reshaped model predicts

The flat model and the stretched model disagree in ways that can be measured. First, the stretched model puts category boundaries where perception changes most sharply, at the factor peaks μ , and the best examples in the calm regions between them; the flat model instead places ₖ boundaries wherever they happen to make categories most compact in CIELAB. Second, the two make different predictions about languages that agree on their best examples but differ on where they draw the lines between categories; such languages are the cleanest test (Regier et al., 2005 (Regier et al., , 2007)). Third, the order in which categories appear as vocabularies grow should shift, including cases where the flat model produces a category only because the color chips happen to bunch up there. In each case the question is how closely the world's actual color vocabularies follow the model's predicted best-possible trade-off, scored with the same measures already used in this literature (Zaslavsky et al., 2018).

The test is within reach: the model's code and the perceptual measurements are both publicly available, so the only new ingredient is the stretching defined above. And it can fail informatively in either direction. If the stretched perceptual space is a better representation for modeling how languages partition color, it should predict their category boundaries better than the flat one; if instead the predictions barely change, then this particular perceptual structure does not provide a better account, and CIELAB has been standing in for a perceptual map the model does not really depend on, which would itself be worth knowing. The test holds the communicative ingredients fixed and varies only the perceptual space, so it speaks to the adequacy of the perceptual representation, not to any division of labor between perception and communicative need. The broad prediction, boundaries at the factor peaks, best examples between them, does not depend on the exact formula chosen for g; it follows from stretching the circle at those peaks. The precise numbers will depend on modeling choices that should be reported and varied. The sensitivity of these models to the perceptual space has been examined before, but only while retaining local uniformity: Zaslavsky et al.

(2018, SI §6) compared CIELAB with CIELUV and varied the perceptual-uncertainty parameter, and related results are known to shift when one standard space is swapped for another (CIELUV; Jraissati & Douven, 2017). What has not been tested is an empirically measured, structurally non-uniform metric of the kind proposed here.

What the present test settles is narrower but real: the procedure runs on the published numbers, and whether the perceptual structure earns its keep is now a quantitative question with a definite answer waiting to be computed.

4.4. Running the real experiment: the factor-warped space inside the information bottleneck

I carried out that experiment directly. Substituting the factor-warped meaning space for CIELAB inside the published information-bottleneck model (Zaslavsky et al., 2018), I recomputed the model's theoretical limit for the warped space and rescored all 110 World Color Survey languages against it. The bound for each space was recomputed from scratch by reverse deterministic annealing (a Blahut-Arimoto iteration over sixty values of the trade-off parameter), using the same perceptual σ and the same optimizer for CIELAB and for every warped space, so the two are compared on identical footing; the Supplementary Methods give the procedure in full, and two built-in checks confirm it, reproducing the CIELAB bound exactly at zero warp and an independently computed baseline at unit warp. Each language was scored by its inefficiency, its distance from the optimal accuracy-complexity trade-off, relative to the same quantity under plain CIELAB (mean inefficiency 0.2555). Because the strength of the reshaping is itself a modeling choice, I swept it with a single gain parameter, from zero (no warp, exactly CIELAB) upward, and compared the per-language inefficiencies with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Applied as the proposal specifies, a uniform hue reshaping at the factor loci, the warp neither clearly beat nor was beaten by CIELAB. Fit improved only marginally as the warp strengthened, reaching a shallow best near twice the nominal strength (mean inefficiency 0.2550, an improvement of about 0.2 percent; 66 of 110 languages better; p = 0.009). A split-half check, choosing the gain on a random half of the languages and testing it on the held-out half, reproduced the direction of this improvement but not its significance (held-out p ≈ 0.06). Pushed further, the warp became significantly harmful, which confirms that the model is not indifferent to the perceptual space: it rewards a particular, modest amount of reshaping and penalizes too much. The full optimization thus returns the same verdict as the boundary-placement test of the preceding section: at the factors' published widths, reshaping the equiluminant hue circle is close to a wash. How the hue warp should extend across lightness is not, in fact, a free choice; the relevant measurement has been made. Extending the hue-scaling analysis outside the equiluminant plane, to increments and decrements at five lightness levels, Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, and Webster (2017c) recovered the same roughly seven narrowly hue-tuned factors and found their loadings consistent across lightness, with no factor specific to increments or decrements: the hue dials operate similarly at every lightness. The principled extension of the warp into the solid is therefore the uniform one already tested above, in which the same hue reshaping applies at every lightness, and that configuration is the near-wash just reported. For completeness I also tested non-uniform extensions that have no warrant in the measured structure. Concentrating the reshaping on the lightest and darkest chips did improve fit, for roughly nine of every ten languages and surviving cross-validation (held-out p ≈ 10 ⁶), while ⁻ concentrating it at mid-lightness consistently worsened it; but precisely because the factor structure is lightness-invariant, this pattern cannot be a signature of that structure. A further test sharpens this picture and partly qualifies it. The near-wash just reported was obtained under the model's default, nearly uniform prior over colors, the least-informative prior of Zaslavsky et al. (2018). Communicative need in natural language is far from uniform, however (Gibson et al., 2017; Twomey et al., 2021), and the perceptual warp might matter more once a realistic need is in place. 4.6. Do the optimal systems themselves change?

Inefficiency measures how far a language sits from the model's optimal frontier; it does not by itself say whether the warp changes which naming systems the model deems optimal. To check this I compared, for every language, its similarity to the matched information-bottleneckoptimal system under CIELAB and under the in-scope warp, using the generalized normalized information distance (gNID; Zaslavsky et al., 2018), computed with the published package's own routine. The predicted category structure barely moves. Mean gNID to the matched optimal system is 0.274 under CIELAB and 0.272 under the warp, and 106 of the 110 languages shift by less than 0.02 on the zero-to-one gNID scale; four languages move substantially closer, but this does not generalize. The optimal systems themselves tell the same story (Figures 7 and 8): along each bound the most efficient systems are near-identical under CIELAB and the warp at every level of complexity, and the system matched to a given language is, in each case, almost the same whichever space is used. The small changes in efficiency reported above therefore correspond to equally small changes in the category structure the model predicts. Because this comparison runs through a different computation than the inefficiency sweep, it also serves as an independent check on those results.

5. Discussion

The proposal fits a recent shift within the efficiency camp itself. Conway, Malik-Moraleda, and Gibson (2023) argued that the long-standing "opponent" theory of color appearance, the idea that all colors are built from red-versus-green and blue-versus-yellow, is mistaken on both its perceptual and its physiological sides, and that color appearance is instead shaped by flexible, use-driven processing. This challenge to opponent organization did not originate with them: cross-cultural and individual-differences studies had already found the Hering primaries far less universal and stable than the theory requires (Lindsey, Brown, & Lange, 2020; Bosten & Boehm, 2014; see also Lindsey & Brown, 2021). If so, then the smooth opponent geometry that CIELAB quietly inherits is exactly the thing to discard, and the uneven, non-opponent map recovered from individual differences is a natural replacement.

For the vision-language interface, the point is conceptual as much as technical. The efficiency program is often read as showing that the broad shape of color vocabularies follows from the pressure to communicate efficiently over a generic perceptual space; an older view held instead that the bumps and seams of perception themselves shape how colors are named (Jameson & D'Andrade, 1997). If perception already divides the color circle into category-sized pieces, the line between what perception contributes and what communication contributes moves: regularities credited to efficient communication may be, in part, regularities of perception, and the apparent efficiency of color vocabularies may partly reflect a perceptual map that is already organized for naming. Reviews of the field have long said that perception and communication together shape color words (Lindsey & Brown, 2021; Witzel & Gegenfurtner, 2018); what is new here is giving the perceptual half a specific, measured, uneven shape rather than a smooth default.

Two clarifications place the contribution. The first is about novelty. The idea that an uneven perceptual space shapes naming is not new; it goes back at least to Jameson and D'Andrade (1997). Non-uniform discriminability has already been built into models of how color categories emerge among populations of communicating agents (Komarova, Jameson, & Narens, 2007; Jameson & Komarova, 2009; Baronchelli, Gong, Puglisi, & Loreto, 2010, who showed that a shared just-noticeable-difference function alone can seed universal naming patterns). What is new here is the specific source and use of the unevenness: a factor structure recovered from individual differences in hue scaling, inserted as a warped metric into the information-bottleneck model, and turned into a prediction about where category boundaries fall.

The second clarification is about the direction of causation, and about what the proposal does not claim. It claims that perceptual structure helps fix where languages place category boundaries; it does not claim that color categories warp perception, the separate and muchdebated phenomenon of categorical perception (Witzel & Gegenfurtner, 2013 , 2018). The two are easy to conflate, and the distinction matters here. The strongest recent evidence runs against the top-down, Whorfian reading: replicating and extending the most-cited neural marker of language-on-perception effects, Martinovic, Delov, Tomastikova, Martin, Paramei, and Griber (2026) found the early brain responses once attributed to color categories are driven by contrast and adaptation, not category membership, echoing earlier behavioral limits on language's influence on color discrimination (Martinovic, Paramei, & MacInnes, 2020). If language is not carving early perception, then an alignment between perceptual structure and category boundaries is most naturally read in the bottom-up direction proposed here. That reading fits evidence that color categories have partly perceptual or biological origins, present in infants before language (Skelton, Catchpole, Abbott, Bosten, & Franklin, 2017; Garside, Chang, Selwyn, & Conway, 2025) and reflected in a non-uniform neural geometry of color (Rosenthal, Singh, Hermann, Pantazis, & Conway, 2021; Chauhan et al., 2023). The computational test now sharpens what can and cannot be claimed. The experiment Section 4.3 called for, placing the factor-warped space inside the information-bottleneck model and rescoring the World Color Survey, has been carried out (Section 4.4, Figure 5), and its verdict is sobering for the strong form of the proposal and clarifying for the weak one. Reshaping the equiluminant hue circle as the factors prescribe does not measurably improve the efficiency with which the world's languages name color: at plausible strengths, and under the model's nearuniform default need, the warped space and plain CIELAB are statistically indistinguishable and the model's optimum is not moved; under an independently measured, non-uniform need the same warp improves fit for 89 of 110 languages (Section 4.5). That verdict, however, holds under the model's near-uniform default need; once a realistic, measured need is supplied (Section 4.5), the same modest warp improves fit for the large majority of languages, a small but systematic benefit that the flat need had masked. The reshaping is therefore not inert, but its value is conditional on a realistic account of communicative need, itself a statement about the perception-communication interface. This is the outcome the introduction allowed for, that the choice of perceptual space may "never have been doing much" on this ring, and it is worth knowing on its own terms. A non-uniform extension concentrated at the extremes of lightness did improve fit and survived cross-validation, but the measured factor structure is itself lightnessinvariant (Emery et al., 2017c), so that gain cannot be credited to the perceptual prior and is set aside as a property of the stimulus geometry. What remains is a narrow but clear conclusion: on the equiluminant hue circle where the factor structure actually lives, building it into the model neither clearly improves nor harms its fit to the World Color Survey at plausible warp strengths.

That is not what the strong form of the proposal hoped for, but it is a definite answer to a question not previously asked of the data. It places the burden where Section 4.3 already located it: on richer perceptual measures, the underlying scaling functions rather than their Gaussian summaries, and a test on the full color solid rather than a single ring. The information-bottleneck framework of Zaslavsky, Kemp, Regier, and Tishby (2018, 2019) provides the template for all of this, for the test reported here and for the fuller one it points toward; the elegance and ingenuity of their analyses are what make a question of this kind precise enough to put to the data at all. Several limits should be stated plainly. The perceptual structure was measured for hues at a single lightness and saturation, in people with normal color vision, and the naming comparisons rest on one language; whether the same structure holds across the full range of colors and across diverse languages is open. Turning the measured factors into a perceptual blur (Equations 2-3) involves choices that must be spelled out and tested, not assumed, as the first computational test makes clear. And the central claim, in its full form over the complete color solid and using the underlying scaling functions rather than their Gaussian summaries, remains a prediction rather than a finished result: its value lies precisely in the fact that it can be carried out and could come out wrong. What the individual-differences data establish is not the answer but the starting point: that the perceptual ingredient in models of color naming is something to be measured and tested, not taken for granted, and that the evidence about its shape points away from the smooth, even space these models currently assume.
