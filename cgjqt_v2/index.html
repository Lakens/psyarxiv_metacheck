Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

SARS-CoV-2, the virus responsible for the Covid-19 pandemic, has been shown to have an impact on cognitive function, but the specific areas affected remain unclear. In this preregistered study, we present findings on the effects of Covid-19 on cognition using UK Biobank data.

Cognitive changes in participants who recovered from Covid-19 (N=1085, 51-82 years) were compared to those of control participants tested before the pandemic and after the pandemic outbreak (pandemic control group, N=1011, 51-82 years) and to those of a second control group that underwent both testing sessions before the pandemic (pre-pandemic control group, N= 2360, 49-82 years). In accordance with our predictions, we found an effect of Covid-19 on associative memory, such that participants who underwent Covid-19 infection showed a decrease in performance compared to both control groups, conceptually replicating our previous results obtained with another dataset (Weinerova et al., 2025). Contrary to our predictions, however, we did not find an effect of Covid-19 on executive function, problem solving and language. This study provides valuable insights into the nature of cognitive changes after Covid-19 infection which may inform public health professionals and policy makers.

the UK Biobank dataset.

1 Introduction

It has become increasingly apparent that SARS-CoV-2 infection has several long-lasting effects on the brain and cognition (Douaud et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2022; Hampshire et al., 2021; Wild et al., 2022). These are part of a multi-systemic condition referred to as Long Covid or Post Covid Syndrome, with previous studies showing that up to 50% of people with Long Covid reported having problems with memory, cognition or concentration, along with other symptoms (Dennis et al., 2023). The current study sheds light on the effects of Covid-19 on cognition using sive data collected from a large sample of participants, offers unique and highly valuable resource for studying this topic. The linked medical records include specific dates of Covid-19 infection and vaccination. Moreover, the same standardized data collection procedure was used before and after the pandemic outbreak, enabling the inclusion of a pre-pandemic control group to test for the effect of the pandemic itself.

In addition to the effects caused by Covid-19 infection alone, the Covid-19 pandemic led to the implementation of various distancing measures including lockdowns, resulting in physical and social isolation across different countries (Koh, 2020), possibly affecting cognition beyond the effect of Covid-19 infection itself. Ingram, Hand, and Maciejewski (2021) measured participants' cognitive function over 13 weeks as lockdown restrictions were gradually lifted and found a pattern of gradual improvement across most tasks, although pre-lockdown cognitive function was not measured. Additionally, Nogueira et al. (2022) found that while post-lockdown cognitive functioning was comparable to pre-pandemic performance, participants nevertheless self-reported more memory complaints, higher rates of depressive symptoms, and lower quality of life. Notably, in that study infection itself was not taken into account (i.e., the study does not specify whether participants were infected with . Crucially, to disentangle potential effects of the Covid-19 infection from those of the Covid-19 pandemic (i.e. the environmental conditions imposed during the period following the onset of the pandemic, such as social distancing, isolation, fear of infection and others), these two sources should be compared within one design. To our knowledge, only one study to date considered both within the same design, (Mohammadi-Nejad et al., 2025). In that study, a subset of data from the UK Biobank was used to model "brain age" of participants from 3 groups: those who had Covid-19 infection, a pandemic control group of participants tested at the same time as the Covid-19 group but were not previously infected, and another pre-pandemic group in which participants were tested twice prior to the pandemic. In the second measurement, the deviation between the modeled 'brain age' and real age in both the infection and the non-infection groups was about 5.5 month larger compared to the pre-pandemic group, suggesting an effect of pandemic rather than Covid-19 infection. The study also investigated cognitive functions following the infection and the pandemic (albeit using a different set of measures from those investigated here), revealing an effect of a Covid-19 infection on the Trail Making Test (Mohammadi-Nejad et al., 2025). These outcomes informed the development of the models used in the current study (see Methods section below).

In the current study, we tested whether Covid-19 infection and/or the pandemic affect cognitive performance.

To this end, we analysed cognitive data obtained from UK Biobank, to examine cognitive change in participants who experienced Covid-19, tested both before and after the outbreak of the pandemic, against a control group of participants who did not experience Covid-19 but were tested during the same time periods ("pandemic control group") and a second control group of participants who were tested twice before the outbreak of the pandemic ("pre-pandemic control group). This design allows us to distinguish between the effect of Covid-19 (i.e., the Covid-19 group shows a different trajectory compared to both control groups) and the general effect of the pandemic (i.e., the Covid-19 group and the pandemic control group show similar trajectories which are different compared to the pre-pandemic control group).

Our main aim was to establish the effects of Covid-19 on various cognitive domains. Based on previous literature, we predicted an effect on long-term memory (Guo et al., 2022; Trender et al., 2024; Weinerova et al., 2025), language (Weinerova et al., 2025; Wild et al., 2022), executive function, and problem-solving (Hadad et al., 2022; Hampshire et al., 2021). The hypotheses and analysis plan for this study were preregistered, and are accessible on OSF at https://osf.io/r4efm.

Data

Figure 1 shows the timeline of data collection for the various groups. The UK Biobank Covid-19 dataset contains data from 2,096 participants who have undergone an assessment visit that included cognitive testing (among other measurements), between 2014 and the start of the pandemic (here we use 31 December 2019 as a cutoff date for pre-pandemic scans, though first cases of Covid-19 were detected in the United Kingdom at the end of January 2020 (UK Health Security Agency, n.d.)), and again in 2021, during the pandemic. Testing took place in one of the UK Biobank centres in Cheadle (Stockport), Newcastle, Reading, or Bristol. Out of those participants, 1085 were infected with SARS-CoV-2 before their second visit, and 1011 were not. In the preregistration, we indicated an overall sample size of 2092, which we thought was case-control matched. In practice, however, a) 4 participants were added to the dataset, and b) within the dataset there were 1620 matched participants (forming 810 pairs), with additional unmatched participants. To maximise the statistical power of our design, and given that we already control for the matching criteria in our statistical models, we have decided to also include the unmatched participants in our study.

We included a second control group of participants from within UK Biobank who were tested twice prior to the pandemic (pre-pandemic control group; N=2360, 49-82 years, 49.9% female), and conform to the same selection criteria. The original recruitment into UK Biobank relied on posted invitations to approximately 9 million people whose contact details were obtained from the National Health Service central registers. The age range for inclusion in the original assessment was 40-69 years. (Sudlow et al., 2015).

The selection criteria for participation in the repeat assessment included: not moving out of the catchment area of the clinic in which they attended their first assessment; no incidental findings identified in their scans at first assessment; had not withdrawn or died; had valid email and postal address; high quality scans were obtained during the first assessment (further details are available in the online documentationfoot_0 ). Additionally, for the pandemic groups only, further criteria included: not having attended a second assessment already prior to the pandemic, and when tested during travel restriction periods, living within the allowed travel range.

Across all groups, we have excluded participants who had any neurodegenerative conditions (such as dementia), psychiatric (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder) or neurological (e.g., epilepsy) disorders prior to the first testing instance. Our final sample therefore included N = 1071 participants in the Covid-19 group (46-80 years, mean age = 60.11, 54% female), N = 995 participants in the pandemic control group (46-80 years, mean age = 60.29, 53% female) and N = 2321 participants in the pre-pandemic control group (46-80 years, mean age = 62.86, 49% female).

Study design

UK Biobank's Covid-19 case control dataset was used in our study to form two groups of participants: the Covid-19 group and the pandemic control group. Data were collected by UK Biobank to explore the potential impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on internal organs. To this end, pre-and post-infection brain, heart, and abdomen MRI scans, together with a variety of cognitive measurements, were collected from individuals who were infected.

The dataset further includes measurements from comparable individuals (on age, gender, ethnicity, location of baseline assessment clinic, and date of baseline imaging assessment) who have not been infected, but were tested at two time points: one preceding the pandemic, and another post-outbreak. Between 2014 and 2021, approximately 49,000 UK Biobank participants have undergone a first assessment (Instance 1). Approximately 3,200 of these participants also returned for a repeat assessment (Instance 2). Due to the pandemic, all imaging clinics had to temporarily close and then reopened in February 2021. Our study included those participants who had repeat assessment after the outbreak of the pandemic and also a second control group: the pre-pandemic control group, which underwent the same testing procedures, but with both instances taking place prior to the outbreak of the pandemic.

Analysis of vaccination effects was done with participants for whom dates of vaccination were accessible and who either had at least one dose of vaccination prior to infection or were only vaccinated after the infection (more details included in the section below).

Group definition

The positive infection status of participants in the Covid-19 group was determined based on a record of a positive PCR antigen test result, a clinical code identified in the electronic health record data, or through the home-based lateral flow SARS-CoV-2 antibody test sent to participants.

Participants were eligible to enter the pandemic control group if they met the main selection criteria and had no record of confirmed or suspected Covid-19 in their linked health records.

Data on vaccination status and date of infection was only available for a subset of participants. In our preregistration, we stated that for relevant analyses we will only consider participants who received 2 doses of vaccination at least two weeks prior to the infection date. However, when applying this approach, the vaccinated group only included a negligible number of participants (N=8). Therefore, to allow interpretable results, and as previous studies have shown that even one dose provides partial immunity (Berec et al., 2022; Hill & Keeling, 2021), we have also included participants with one dose of vaccination prior to infection, to form the vaccination group (overall N = 45, mean age = 65.97, range = 53-81). Participants in this group were compared to participants who had been vaccinated for the first time after infection (N = 438, mean age = 64.1, range = 51-82).

Date of infection was based on dates of positive test results for SARS-CoV-2. If multiple positive tests were recorded, we categorized them as being part of one infection episode if they were taken within one month of each other. If longer time elapsed between positive tests, they were categorized as multiple infections. The start of infection was taken as the date of the first test. Following the approach used in our previous study (Weinerova et al., 2025), for the analysis of vaccination effect we only selected participants who underwent one infection prior to the second instance, to avoid interpretation issues caused by participants who were vaccinated after one infection but before another. We then calculated the time difference between the estimated date of infection and the vaccination date.

Measurements

Participants completed a series of cognitive tasks, fully detailed in UK Biobank's documentationfoot_1 , and briefly described below. The reliability and validity of cognitive tests in UK Biobank was previously reported in Fawns-Ritchie and Deary (2020). Each task was completed twice during two separate testing instances, as shown in Figure 1. The tests were presented on a touch screen at the testing location, and a trained member of staff was present during the testing. Variable ID numbers of each test, as appear in UK Biobank, are shown in Table 1.

Fluid Intelligence.

Fluid intelligence is the capacity to solve problems that require logic and reasoning abilities, independent of acquired knowledge. In this test, participants had 2 minutes to complete as many problem-solving questions as they could out of 13 questions in total (e.g., "If Truda's mother's brother is Tim's sister's father what relation is Truda to Tim?"). Participants had to select the correct answer out of 7 options including "Don't know"

and "Prefer not to answer". The fluid intelligence score (number of correctly answered questions) was used as dependent variable.

Reaction Time. Participants' reaction times were tested with a Snap test 3 ), in which they had to press a button as soon as two identical cards were seen on the touchscreen. Participants were instructed to use their dominant hand and hold it ready over the button box. 12 pairs of cards were presented. Previous studies testing the reliability of this test reported Cronbach's alpha of 0.85 (Hagenaars et al., 2016). The dependent variable was the average time to correctly identified matches. Rounds 0-4 were not used in this calculation as they are regarded as training rounds by the UK Biobank pre-processing team.

Trail Making Test.

The Trail Making Test (Oosterman et al., 2010) includes a numeric (TMT A) and alphanumeric (TMT B) versions, designed to evaluate processing speed, attention, and visuospatial ability (both versions), and mental flexibility (TMT B). In TMT A, participants are presented with digits scattered around on the screen and asked to click on them sequentially. In TMT B, participants are presented with digits and letters and asked to click on them sequentially in alternating order. If participants touched the wrong item, the screen flashed red to inform them that they made a mistake, and the participant had to touch the correct number or letter before continuing the task. The duration to complete the paths, separately for TMT A and TMT B, was used as the dependent variable.

Symbol Digit Substitution: This is a test of motor speed, attention, and visuoperceptual functions (Jaeger, 2018).

Participants are presented with two grids, one linking symbols to single digit integers and another containing only the symbols. They are asked to indicate the numbers attached to each symbol in the second grid using the first grid as a key. Participants had 1 minute to complete as many matches as possible. The dependent variable was the number of correct matches made. In the preregistration, we stated that we would also include the duration to entering symbol choice as a dependent variable. However, since no participant completed the whole task within the 1 minute time limit, the two dependent variables were redundant (i.e., fewer correctly identified symbol-digit pairs also means longer time taken to fill them into the grid). We therefore only used the first dependent variable.

Picture Vocabulary. This test was adapted from Gershon et al. (2014) and vocabulary difficulty level scores were recalibrated based on initial UK Biobank data (Giunchiglia, Curtis, Smith, Allen, & Hampshire, 2024). Participants were shown a word accompanied by four pictures (e.g., the word silhouette, accompanied by 4 pictures), and were asked to choose the picture that best matches the word. In each round, the word presented was selected based on the participant's performance in previous rounds. Words varied in difficulty, and the selection at each round was based on the participant's estimated vocabulary level, calculated using maximum likelihood estimation from their responses up to that point.. If a participant correctly responded to all the words presented so far, the algorithm selected a more difficult word. Otherwise, the participant's vocabulary level was calculated and a word in a next level of difficulty that was not yet presented was chosen. Each participant was shown at least 20 words. The test ended after the estimate of the participant's vocabulary was either within a standard error of <0.5 or when 30 words were seen. The dependent variable was the participant's vocabulary level based on a maximum likelihood estimate of their answers across all seen words.

Paired Associate Learning.

In this test of associative memory, during the learning phase participants were shown 12 word pairs, all presented simultaneously for 30 seconds in 2 columns, such that each row contains 4 words (2 pairs). The test phase followed an interval during which participants completed the Matrix Pattern Completion task (see below). In the test phase, the first word of 10 of the pairs were presented sequentially, and participants were asked to select the matching word from a choice of four alternatives, all presented during the learning phase, or select "Prefer not to answer". The dependent variable was the number of correctly paired words.

Matrix Pattern Completion.

Participants were presented with a series of matrix designs, each with one missing element. Each design had a logical structure, enabling participants to work out what the missing element should look like. Participants were presented with 6-8 possible options for the missing element, depending on the specific trial, and asked to choose which element correctly completes the design. The number of options presented for each item was fixed across participants. Participants were instructed that there is only one piece that can correctly complete the design both horizontally and vertically. There were 16 trials in total. This task was used as a distractor task between the phases of the Paired Associate Learning test described above, but similar tests are commonly used to test fluid intelligence (Cattell & Cattell, 1960). The dependent variables were the number of puzzles correctly solved and the average duration time spent solving each puzzle, which were analysed separately.

Questionnaires

Data on participants' general characteristics (age, date of birth, sex, and Townsend deprivation index) have been collected as part of the first assessment. The Townsend index is a measure of material deprivation (Townsend, 1987). It was calculated based on the participants' post code and data on the output area from the previous census immediately before participants joined UK Biobank.

Vaccination. Since December 2022, UK Biobank included data on whether participants have received a Covid-19 vaccine and when. The vaccination record data are provided to the UK Biobank database by NHS England and are therefore available for only a subsample of the overall participants. The sample size for this analysis is described in the Group definition section above.

Analytical approach

The Effect of Covid-19/pandemic Linear mixed effects models, fit with the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova, Brockhoff, & Christensen, 2017) in R (R Core Team, 2023), were used to examine the effect of group (Covid-19, pandemic control, pre-pandemic control) and testing instance (first and second testing visit) on cognitive performance. We used treatment contrast coding for the group and instance variables, with the Covid-19 group serving as the reference level for the variable group, and instance 1 as the reference for the variable instance. Thus, the main effect of group represents the difference between each control group and the Covid-19 group at Instance 1, while the main effect of instance represents the change from Instance 1 to Instance 2. The model further includes an interaction term, representing the difference in slope between the Covid-19 group and the pandemic and pre-pandemic controls. This allows us to answer our main research question: whether cognitive scores in the Covid-19 group show greater decline from Instance 1 to Instance 2 compared to both (or either) control groups. Based on previous work (Douaud et al., 2022), and on variables originally used for matching of the case-control matched sample (UK Biobank, 2023), age, sex, ethnicity, and Townsend index were used as covariates in our analyses.

The analytical model used was as follows:

Cognitive outcome ∼ Group + Instance + Group * Instance + Duration between Instance 1 and 2 + (Length of time between Instance 1 and 2) 2 + Age at T2

All independent continuous variables were z-scored prior to data analysis. In the preregistration, we stated that dependent variables will also be z-scored. However, such standardisation would have limited our ability to interpret the effect of the predictors on the unstandardized scale. We therefore present the results obtained with unstandardised dependent measures, and then also present standardised beta coefficients separately using the effectsize package (Ben-Shachar et al., 2025).

To develop the mixed effects models used in the current study, we analysed data from the TMT task described above. Notably, data from this task were already analysed in two previous published studies, both using a subset of UK Biobank data (Douaud et al., 2022; Mohammadi-Nejad et al., 2025). These studies reported significant differences in TMT A and B scores between the Covid-19 and control groups. While these prior analyses allowed us to develop the methods that were used here, we note that the effect of Covid-19 previously reported by Douaud et al. (2022) was absent when the full sample was used, even with models identical to those used in the previous study. These results of our preliminary analyses are fully described in Supplementary Material 1 and included only the Covid-19 and pandemic control group. Note also that these analyses were done prior to the preregistration in order to establish methodologies based on (what we thought was) a known effect. We do include analyses of these variables in the full model with pre-pandemic control group, as this was not run prior to the preregistration.

The effect of vaccination

The model used for the analysis of the effect of vaccination was the same as the one used above, but instead of Covid-19 status, vaccination status was included as the grouping variable. The main effect of interest was therefore the interaction between vaccination group and instance, which would indicate different change in performance in individuals who were and were not vaccinated with 1 dose prior to infection. While analysis of vaccination effects was preregistered, the expected outcome for the effect of interest was not specified in the preregistration as there is currently not enough evidence to support specific predictions of the effects of vaccination prior to infection on long-term cognitive function.

Correction for multiple comparisons was applied using false discovery rate correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) across p-values obtained from all models which included the same predictors and contrasts using the p.adjust function (R Core Team, 2023).

Predicted outcomes:

As summarised in the introduction, previously reported effects of Covid-19 on cognition vary across studies. In our previous study (Weinerova et al., 2025), we have replicated a global effect of Covid-19 infection on long-term memory and additionally found an effect on a test of semantic and syntactical understanding. We therefore hypothesised that we would find deficits in tasks associated with long-term memory function and language (Paired Learning task and Picture Vocabulary test). Given that some studies have also reported decline in the domain of executive function and problem-solving, we also expected to find a significant difference in the Fluid Intelligence, Matrix Pattern Completion and Symbol Digit Substitution tests (Hadad et al., 2022; Hampshire et al., 2021). Specifically, in all of these cases, we expected to find a significant interaction between Covid-19 status and testing instances, such that there will be a significant difference in the effect of time on each group, with the Covid-19 group showing greater decline compared to both control groups.

Exploratory analysis: Time since infection

In this exploratory analysis, we examined whether the time elapsed between infection and the second testing session has an effect on the cognitive performance. Some previous studies have reported an alleviation of symptoms over time (Ballouz et al., 2023; Del Brutto, Rumbea, Recalde, & Mera, 2022), while other studies report deterioration (Berec et al., 2022; Hill & Keeling, 2021). In our previous study (Weinerova et al., 2025), we found a tentative deterioration effect but only on one variable (reaction time in a verbal item memory task). Therefore, we explored this further in the current study, although this analysis was not preregistered. To do so, we subset the participants to include only those who had Covid-19 prior to the second testing instance and whose data included a date of a positive Covid-19 test (N=700, mean age at 2nd instance = 62.96, age range = 51 -82, 53% female). The time since infection was calculated from the date of the most recent positive test to the date of the second assessment in days, and data were scaled and centered (using z-scores) prior to the analysis. The mean time since infection was 177.7 days, with a range of 15 to 693 days.

Since time since infection is not a meaningful predictor for participants' scores at first instance, we have run this analysis using a regression model with time since infection and score at time 1 as predictors (together with all the covariates included in our previous models), and score at instance 2 as outcome. This analysis did not yield any significant effects of the time since infection.

4 Discussion

Our aim in this study was to evaluate the cognitive performance of individuals who underwent Covid-19 infection, tested before and after the pandemic, and compare them to two control groups: a group of individuals who were also tested before and after the pandemic outbreak, but did undergo Covid-19 infection (pandemic control group), and a group of individuals for which two testing instances occurred before the pandemic (pre-pandemic control group). This longitudinal approach supports the evaluation of the effects of infection, above and beyond other potential cross-sectional or group-related confounds, thereby expanding beyond most previous research (Guo et al., 2022; Hampshire et al., 2021; Trender et al., 2024; Wild et al., 2022), including our own study (Weinerova et al., 2025), which mainly relies on cross-sectional data collected after the pandemic. To our knowledge, only three other studies to date have employed a longitudinal approach, but using different measures or subsamples of our sample (Trender et al. (2024), Douaud et al. (2022); Mohammadi-Nejad et al. ( 2025)).

Based on our previous findings (Weinerova et al., 2025), we predicted a negative effect of Covid-19 on long-term memory and language. However, we also set out to evaluate other cognitive domains (executive function and problem-solving) detected as impacted by Covid-19 by other studies (Hampshire et al., 2021; Wild et al., 2022). We therefore also predicted that Covid-19 would reduce performance in the Fluid Intelligence, Matrix Pattern Completion, Symbol Digit Substitution and Picture Vocabulary tests, such that Covid-19 group will experience more deterioration over time, compared to both controls.

Our preregistered analyses revealed the predicted effect of Covid-19 only on the Paired Associate Learning test which is a test of associative long-term memory. In this task, the effect of time differed between the Covid-19 group and both control groups. This finding converges with our previous study (Weinerova et al., 2025) and other studies (Guo et al., 2022; Trender et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2022) to suggest that the cognitive effect of Covid-19 is fairly specific to long-term memory. Importantly, the inclusion of two different control groups allowed us to disentangle effects that are due to Covid-19 infection from those that are broadly related to the pandemic (e.g., result from extreme conditions of social isolation), but are not related to the infection per-se. For the Paired Associative Learning test, we did not observe a significant differences in trajectory between the two control groups, suggesting that the decrease in memory performance observed in the Covid-19 group was comparable when contrasted against the pandemic and the pre-pandemic control groups, which did not differ from each other. This suggests that the reduction in memory performance observed here is due to Covid-19 infection rather than the pandemic in itself. Our study thus crucially shows that the effect of Covid-19 on long-term memory is replicable and consistent. Indeed, this effect was detected in different samples (e.g. (Guo et al., 2022; Trender et al., 2024; Weinerova et al., 2025)), including a direct replication (Weinerova et al., 2025), and with different memory tests.

An equally important question beyond the replicability of the effect is whether the effect is of theoretical and practical significance. Across the samples and tasks that we have studied, the magnitude of the effect was small to medium. As larger samples usually provide more stable results, the more likely estimates are those obtained with the UK Biobank data, as reported here, in which a small effect was observed. Nevertheless, we argue that given its stability, even a relatively small effect is still of theoretical and practical interest. Namely, with over 778 million cases of Covid-19 infection worldwide to date, even small effects affecting such a large population are of academic and potentially clinical and policy interest. In other words, given its prevalence, a minor average change in cognition could have a large impact when affecting millions of individuals.

The Paired Associate Learning task was the only task where we detected a significant difference in trajectories between the Covid-19 group and both pandemic control group and pre-pandemic control group. Nevertheless, there were other tasks in which the Covid-19 group showed a different trajectory compared to the pre-pandemic control group (albeit not compared to the pandemic control group), including the Trail Making Test A (TMT A). Notably, a previous study reported an effect of Covid-19 infection in this task compared to a pandemic control group with subset of the data that were analysed here (Douaud et al., 2022). We were unable to replicate this with the current (full) sample. While we have detected a difference in trajectory between the Covid-19 group and the pre-pandemic control group, there was no significant difference between the two control groups. Therefore, this does not suggest an effect of pandemic as this would be manifested in different trajectories between the pre-pandemic and pandemic control groups.

Additionally, we found significant difference in trajectory between the Covid-19 group and the pre-pandemic control group in the average time to complete the Matrix Pattern Completion task (a test of fluid intelligence).

Surprisingly, the direction of this effect was of improved performance in the Covid-19 group compared to the prepandemic control group. This effect was only observed for reaction times (time taken to complete the puzzles) but not for number of puzzles completed in total. In addition, we found a significant difference between the trajectories of the two control groups, with faster times in the pandemic control group compared to the pre-pandemic control group. These results are therefore contrary to our predictions, both in direction and also in the trajectory difference being found only between the Covid-19 group and the pre-pandemic control group. This may indicate that participants in the pandemic groups were more rushed and impulsive in their responses. However, this interpretation would also predict a trade-off between speed and accuracy, which was not observed. Therefore, this explanation remains speculative.

Given that a key aim of the study was to replicate and further establish previously detected effects of Covid-19 on cognitive outcomes, it is important to also consider cases where the expected effects did not emerge. Based on prior research, we have predicted an effect on executive function, problem-solving and language (Hadad et al., 2022; Hampshire et al., 2021; Weinerova et al., 2025). However, in the current study, the predicted pattern was not observed in any of the four tests tapping into these areas (Fluid Intelligence, Matrix Pattern Completion, Symbol Digit Substitution and Picture Vocabulary), and in fact, as described above, the effect that was observed for reaction times in the Matrix Pattern Completion task was in the opposite direction. Our failure to conceptually replicate effects in these areas generally aligns with our findings described in (Weinerova et al., 2025), where the effect of Covid-19 was fairly specific to long-term memory. The only other test that was affected by Covid-19 in our previous study focused on syntactic and semantic understanding, echoing the Picture Vocabulary Test which was used here and did not depict the same effect. However, unlike the task used in our previous study, which required both syntactic and semantic understanding, the current task only requires the latter. It is therefore possible, that the different outcome is due to the impaired performance being associated with syntactic understanding in particular.

Overall, these findings highlight the utility of efforts to replicate previously detected effects on other samples and using other tests of a given construct. This is especially true for fields that experience rapid growth in the number of publications and findings. In the cases where we have failed to replicate a given effect, this may be due to multiple reasons, such as moderating factors or difference in samples. In any case, these provide important information as to the generalisability of some of the previous findings.

We further examined the effect of vaccination status on cognitive performance following infection. We tested participants who had at least one dose of vaccine prior to infection against those who did not. This analysis did not yield any of the predicted results. Notably, the sample size for this analysis was substantially smaller than the overall sample size. In our previous study (Weinerova et al., 2025), a slightly larger sample have also yielded inconclusive results when examining vaccination effects (albeit with another design and statistical model). Together, these suggest that a larger sample size or more powerful study design might be required to obtain conclusive evidence as to whether vaccination can moderate the long-term effects of Covid-19 on cognition. It is also of note, that even if vaccination does not have an effect on alleviating the effects of Covid-19 on cognition, it still has a preventive effect on Long Covid through preventing the Covid-19 infection in the first place.

The direction of longitudinal development of cognitive performance following Covid-19 infection has been mixed in previous literature (Ballouz et al., 2023; Del Brutto et al., 2022; Hastie et al., 2022; Trender et al., 2024; Yeung et al., 2025). In an exploratory analysis, we examined whether the time that elapsed since infection has an effect on the cognitive scores in the Covid-19 group, but did not find any significant effects on any of the cognitive outcomes.

This suggests that cognitive effects may stagnate over time or that the effect size of these changes is smaller than what we can detect with this analysis. However, the question of whether cognitive performance changes over time would be more conclusively determined with data across multiple testing instances after the pandemic. A recent study by Yeung et al. (2025) examined the same participants across 3 time points, 10 -15 months after infection, and found that for most participants who reported some symptoms of ongoing illness since the infection, there was no improvement in cognitive function over time. Similarly, Trender et al. (2024) tracked cognitive function in a human inoculation study across 6 different testing sessions, and reported stable differences in performance for at least a year post-infection. Overall, while our current study does not offer a conclusive answer to this question, taken together with other evidence, it suggests that cognitive symptoms of Covid-19 may remain stable over time.

4.1 Limitations

A key limitation of our study is the non-representative ethnicity distribution in the sample outlined. Only about 3% of participants in the sample identified as other ethnic group than white. However, based on a 2021 census, 18% of people in England and Wales identify as non-white (Office for National Statistics, 2021). This might limit the generalization of the results to other ethnic groups, within the UK and beyond.

Second, participants with clinical diagnosis of Long Covid (Post-Covid Syndrome) were not specifically recruited for this sample (Gutzeit et al., 2025). The literature up to date differs on recruitment criteria, with some studies specifically recruiting people who have received clinical diagnosis (Díez-Cirarda et al., 2023), while others recruit participants from the general population (Petersen et al., 2023), or some combination thereof (Guo et al., 2022).

The terminology also varies across studies, with Long Covid sometimes being used to refer to any symptoms of Covid-19 which persist for more than 3 months and sometimes taken to refer only to clinical diagnosis. It is important to highlight that unless specified otherwise, the term Long Covid here is used to refer to the former (i.e.

symptoms which last long-term after Covid-19 infection) and not to clinical diagnosis. Consequently, given the sample specifications, the results presented here may not be generalisable to clinical populations.

Finally, there are some factors, which may play a role in influencing the extent of long-term cognitive symptoms.

Such factors include, for example, hospitalisation during/severity of acute phase of infection, although there are some studies suggesting no difference in long-term symptoms between people with mild or severe acute infection (Braga et al., 2022; Stavem et al., 2022). Covid-19 variant may also play a role, with some findings suggesting that earlier variants may have had larger effects on long-term symptoms (Latifi & Flegr, 2024). Finally, mental health may also be affected by both Covid-19 and the pandemic restrictions, and in turn may affect cognitive performance.

Consideration of these factors goes beyond the scope of the current study, but can be considered in future research to develop better understanding of the boundary conditions under which effects of Covid-19 on cognition might emerge.

4.2 Conclusions and future directions

In line with our previous study (Weinerova et al., 2025), we have found an effect of Covid-19 on long-term memory in the UK Biobank sample, but not in other domains, including executive function, problem solving and language.

Our findings open up potential new questions for investigation. First, estimating the moderating effect of other factors on the cognitive symptoms identified here, such as Covid-19 variant, may be of interest to identify who might be especially at risk of developing long-term symptoms. Second, as we did not have enough data to establish conclusive presence or absence of the effect of vaccination, further large-scale investigations would be key for informing public health policies and vaccination guidelines.

Overall, given the relative novelty of Long Covid and associated research, many studies use explorative approaches. In this study, we have based our predictions on previous findings, including our own, allowing us to robustly assess the effects of Covid-19 on cognition. We believe that our findings join others in providing important insights regarding the cognitive characteristics of this condition, and can aid future research by allowing researchers to develop and test more specific predictions within this area.
