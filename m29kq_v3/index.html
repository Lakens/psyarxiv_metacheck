Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. Abstract

Humans are highly social, and much of that sociality is regulatory. Individuals do not manage the demands placed upon their mind and body alone but through and with others. Attachment science has pursued how the brain accomplishes this regulation for the better part of a century, and its most recent contribution has been neurobiological. Within the Social Neuroscience of Human Attachment (SoNeAt), we previously specified a neuroanatomical architecture of two interacting systems and four domain general modules for organised attachment, extended it to attachment disruption and disorganisation, and moved it from single brains to dyads within Relational Neuroscience, which builds on second-person neuroscience and uses hyperscanning as its principal method. What that work established energetically, through social allostasis, it did not specify computationally. Here, we close that gap. Drawing on active inference, allostasis as interoceptive inference, and recent constructs of attachment as prediction, we propose the term social allostatic inference: the attachment system as one predictive-allostatic loop, the four modules as its functional components, and organised, disrupted, and disorganised attachment as characteristic settings of the precision parameters that govern it. We extend our account to the caregiving system, which we propose, as a testable extension, to share this architecture while inferring the state it regulates from exteroceptive evidence about another body rather than from interoceptive evidence about its own. At the level of the dyad, interpersonal neural and biobehavioural synchrony are read, as a hypothesis, as indices of where regulatory work is invested rather than of bond quality directly. We state the generative model that our account presupposes, specify observations that would count against it, and set out a measurement programme.

1. Introduction

Humans are highly social, and much of that sociality is regulatory. Individuals do not manage the demands placed upon their bodies alone but through and with others. How the brain accomplishes this regulation, and why individuals differ so markedly in their capacity and motivation to perform it, is the question that attachment science has pursued for the better part of a century. The most recent contribution to that integration has been the investigation of the neurobiology underlying attachment. It proceeded first through non-human animal models of pair bonding and parental care and then through the study of humans, where neurobiological measurement initially served to corroborate hypotheses of attachment theory that behavioural methods alone could not adjudicate (Gustison & Phelps, 2022).

The human-directed line of enquiry has since become an identifiable programme that we have termed the Social Neuroscience of Human Attachment, or SoNeAt (White et al., 2023).

Our own contribution to SoNeAt has been cumulative, and it is better characterised by what it collectively established than by the order in which its parts appeared. Across that work, we have made five overarching commitments, which the present manuscript takes as its foundation.

The first commitment is architectural. We began from the observation that the social neuroscience literature could be organised along attachment-theoretical lines, although large parts of it have been collected without attachment in mind (Vrticka & Vuilleumier, 2012).

Accordingly, we proposed that the neural substrates recruited in attachment-relevant situations are best described as two interacting systems, an affective evaluation system and a cognitive control system, decomposed into four modules: aversion and approach on the affective side, and emotion regulation and mental state representation on the cognitive side (Long et al., 2020).

None of the four modules is specific to attachment, and there is neither a dedicated attachment area nor a dedicated attachment system. Each module corresponds approximately to a domaingeneral large-scale network (see also Figure 1), and attachment-related variation is variation in the parameters of shared machinery rather than in its identity (White et al., 2023), as we will further develop below.

The second commitment is sequential. Onto this architecture, we mapped a prototypical initial attachment pathway, i.e., the most likely ordered sequence of processes elicited when the attachment system is activated (see also Figure 1). It runs from the appraisal of threat, through proximity seeking and co-regulation, to the alleviation of distress and the deactivation of the system, together with the derivatives of that sequence associated with organised attachment security, avoidance, and anxiety. (M. Long et al., 2020). Having specified this architecture and sequence for organised attachment, we subsequently extended the same account to the disruption and disorganisation of attachment (White et al., 2020). This extension characterised the divergent regulatory profiles that follow when the caregiving environment fails more severely than the organised insecure strategies can compensate for, distinguishing two routes by their caregiving origin. On the first, associated with maltreatment, the caregiver is present but is itself a source of threat, which we linked to a hyper-arousal neurophysiological phenotype. On the second, associated with neglect, the caregiver is instead absent, and it is that absence, and the distress it leaves unassuaged, that constitutes the threat, which we linked to a hypo-arousal phenotype.

The third commitment is to fine-grained neurobiological description. Where the architecture and the pathway operate at the level of modules and networks, we subsequently specified the cascade of neurobiological events that accompanies attachment behaviour at the level of circuits and signalling systems: from thalamic and amygdalar threat processing through noradrenergic and dopaminergic mobilisation, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) recruitment, and oxytocinergic and opioidergic modulation at reunion, to the consolidation of the episode in memory (Izaki et al., 2024).

The fourth commitment is extending. We moved the SoNeAt account from data assessed in one participant at a time and thus concerned with the isolated brain, to the simultaneous acquisition of brain activity in dyads assessed during direct interaction. This step followed second-person neuroscience, which pioneered the notion that social cognition differs fundamentally when individuals interact with rather than merely observe one another (Schilbach et al., 2013; Redcay & Schilbach, 2019), and which was later given Bayesian and active inference formulations (Bolis et al., 2017; Lehmann et al., 2024). In doing so, we incorporated biobehavioural synchrony (BBS) and interpersonal neural synchrony (INS) within what we have more recently called Relational Neuroscience, which builds on the second-person neuroscience tradition and uses hyperscanning as its principal method (De Felice et al., 2025; Long et al., 2020). And we emphasised co-regulation and mutual prediction as candidate neurobiological correlates of attachment.

The fifth commitment is to integration. We asked whether an overarching neurobiological account of human attachment begins to emerge when SoNeAt evidence is combined with adjacent social neuroscience theory. We argued that it does, by linking attachment to fundamental processes of energy conservation and co-regulation through social allostasis (White et al., 2023). When making this integrative suggestion, however, we did not supply a specification of the computations by which the brain accomplishes social allostasis in the context of attachment.

In the present manuscript, we advance our contribution to SoNeAt by closing that gap.

Our aim is to specify the computations that underpin social allostasis as part of human attachment interactions. To do so, we first draw on the recent literature pertaining to the neuroscience of homeostasis, allostasis and active inference, in accordance with the first formulation of infant attachment as a minimisation of free energy (Cittern et al., 2018) and a recent conceptualisation of attachment as prediction (Tottenham & Vannucci, 2025). That extension requires a further step not previously formalised within SoNeAt: the explicit differentiation of the attachment system from the caregiving system as two related but asymmetric loops, the first activated by threat or distress to the self and terminating in the restoration of one's own felt security, the second activated by another's signalled distress and terminating in its alleviation. 2.1 Homeostasis and allostasis, from individual to social

Every organism must keep its body and mind within survivable limits, which requires continual regulation against fluctuating demand. For a summary of Section 2 vocabulary, please refer to Box 1.

Homeostasis, in the classical sense, is reactive regulation. Its feedback loops correct a deviation after it has arisen to restore a regulated variable towards its reference value (or range of values) (Sterling, 2012). Homeostasis' social counterpart applies the same logic to social connection. On the account of social homeostasis, the brain treats connection as a basic need, and when it falls short, conserved neural systems register the deficit and drive the organism towards restoration (Allsop et al., 2025; Cressy et al., 2026; Matthews & Tye, 2019).

Allostasis extends homeostasis into the predictive domain. Rather than waiting for deviations to occur, the brain anticipates the demand that will fall on the regulated variable. By preparing for regulation in advance, future deviations can be reduced in magnitude and frequency. In doing so, allostasis governs the homeostatic loops, overrides them and, where demand persists, resets the reference values they defend (Sterling, 2012). Allostasis' social counterpart, social allostasis, distributes this predictive work across bodies. The predicted demands, and the resources mobilised to meet them, are shared between more than one organism. This is co-regulation, the process by which one individual's physiological states are regulated in part through the behaviour and physiology of others (Atzil et al., 2018; Saxbe et al., 2020).

Importantly, sustaining both reactive and predictive regulation through homeostasis and allostasis is costly. Each act of mobilisation and anticipation draws on the body's resources.

When demand is high, mobilisation is chronic and/or the anticipations are repeatedly in error, that cost accumulates rather than dissipating. Accordingly, at the individual level, allostatic load denotes this cumulative burden, which, as it builds, can come to exceed the organism's capacity to absorb it (McEwen & Stellar, 1993; Theriault et al., 2025). Social allostatic load extends the same logic of wear and tear to the dyad and the group (Saxbe et al., 2020).

Two positions supply the rationale for treating attachment in the terms of social allostasis. First, Social Baseline Theory holds that the human brain assumes proximity to others as its default ecology. It expresses this assumption in the economics of risk and effort, such that relational partners are incorporated into neural representations of the self, and their presence lowers the metabolic bid required to meet a given goal (Beckes & Coan, 2011; Coan & Sbarra, 2015). Second, an Evolutionary Account of Social Regulation proposes that social animals are those that cannot survive alone and depend on conspecifics to regulate their ongoing physiology. On this account, the human infant is born in a state of allostatic dependency whose external management is the condition under which their brain develops (Atzil et al., 2018). Both positions converge on the operative principle of our SoNeAt account (White et al., 2023), namely that personal and social resources are neurobiologically treated as nearly interchangeable, so that the more an individual can draw upon others' resources through coregulation, the less personal bioenergetic resource is expended. This proposition has recently been examined experimentally at the level of homeostatic efficiency. In preregistered experiments, participants underwent standardised physiological challenges once alone and once in the presence of a close partner. The time-integrated deviation from baseline, i.e., the incremental area under the curve (for glucose, above a fixed threshold), was found to be lower in the partner condition for glucose regulation after a carbohydrate load, for skin temperature during a cold challenge, and for electrodermal responses to a stressor in adults and in infants (Masalha et al., 2026). Effects were small within participants and small to moderate between participants, and the adult electrodermal effect reached significance only between participants. However, the effect on glucose regulation remained after controlling for electrodermal activity and touch, the pooled adult effect remained after controlling for relationship satisfaction, and the authors propose socially informed allostasis as one candidate route. Attachment and caregiving were not assessed.

2.2 Active inference

Social allostasis specifies what attachment relationships yield energetically, but not the computations by which the brain accomplishes them. Active inference supplies this specification. Because the brain has no direct access to the causes of its sensory and interoceptive signals, it must infer them. It does so by running an internal model that constantly predicts what it expects to sense, and by comparing those predictions against what actually arrives (Friston, 2010).

Three concepts are important within this context. First, priors are the expectations the generative model holds before new evidence arrives. They encode what the brain estimates as most probable based on past experience. Second, prediction error is the residual mismatch between those expectations and the actually received signals. And third, precision is the confidence, or inverse variance, assigned to a signal versus an expectation. This balance governs the weight a prediction error is granted in updating the model, such that a highly precise prediction error compels revision whereas an imprecise one is largely discounted. Yet, precision is not a single quantity, and we use four terms to refer to it (see Box 1).

First, sensory or interoceptive precision is the weight granted to ascending prediction error, whether interoceptive, arising from the body, or exteroceptive, arising from the world. Second, prior precision is the confidence with which an expectation is held. Third, volatility is the expected rate at which the environment changes, which sets how readily a prior is revised (Mathys et al., 2011). And finally, policy precision is the confidence with which one course of action is selected over its alternatives (Parr & Friston, 2018; Schwartenbeck et al., 2015).

Importantly, the content of a prior, e.g., whether an attachment figure is expected to be available, is distinct from all four. Given these quantities, the system can reduce prediction error along one of two routes. It may change its model to fit the world, which is perceptual inference.

Or it may change the world to fit its model, acting so as to bring about the states it predicts, which is active inference (Friston, 2010; Parr & Friston, 2018) Proximity seeking towards an attachment figure is, in these terms, a paradigm case of active inference in the following sequence. The model predicts safety or felt security as the expected, regulated state. When a departure from the predicted state is registered as part of threat detection, meaning that the world suddenly is registered as unsafe, a prediction error is generated. This prediction error initiates proximity seeking as the active inference response to that error, i.e., the individual acts on the world by recruiting the attachment figure to bring incoming sensory and interoceptive conditions back into line with the model's prediction of safety.

Two further constructs from this literature are required later and are introduced here.

The first is that the value of a course of action decomposes into two components. Its pragmatic, or extrinsic, value is the expected utility of its outcomes under the agent's prior preferences. Its epistemic value is the expected reduction in the agent's uncertainty about hidden states of the world, i.e., the information the action is expected to provide about them. The two are weighed jointly, so an agent may select an action of low expected reward where it is predicted to yield sufficient information about hidden states (Cittern et al., 2018). The second construct concerns the dynamics of large-scale neuronal networks, for which two terms are often conflated (Hancock et al., 2025; Kelso, 2012). A multistable system possesses several stable attractors, i.e., several states towards which its activity tends to settle. A metastable system moves among these attractors without being captured by any one of them for long, so that its networks integrate and segregate and several interpretations of the world remain concurrently available.

Metastability supplies a candidate neural quantity for the flexibility of inference, and its loss, in which activity is captured by a single attractor, a candidate signature of rigidity (Kotler et al., 2026).

2.3 Allostasis as interoceptive inference

Interoception, the modelling of the body's internal state, is inference: visceral signals are read against prior expectations about the body's condition and its likely trajectory (Barrett, 2017; Barrett et al., 2025). This top-down direction is not merely interpretive but part of the regulation itself, in two respects. First, the priors set the range of reference values against which bodily states are evaluated. Therefore, the values the allostatic budget is directed towards are itself predicted rather than a fixed physiological given (Sterling, 2012). Second, interoceptive priors condition the appraisal of exteroceptive signals, so that what counts as threatening in the world depends in part on the state the body predicts itself to be in (Barrett, 2017; Barrett et al., 2025).

Allostasis is this same inference carried through to action, which is why allostasis can be regarded as the brain's primary task (Theriault et al., 2025).

Affect and emotion are among what this inference produces. The state of the body budget is registered in consciousness as affect, and emotions are constructed when the brain categorises these same interoceptive signals, together with the exteroceptive signals that accompany them, in the light of prior experience. The regulation of emotion and the regulation of the body's energy budget are therefore not separable functions, and that regulation is allostasis (Barrett, 2017; Barrett et al., 2025).

Importantly, that the object of prediction is the body does not confine the solution to it.

Because active inference resolves error by acting on the world as readily as by revising the model, an interoceptive prediction error need not be met from internal resources alone. The interchangeability of personal and social resources that Section 2.1 imported as a premise is, on this reading, a consequence of the mechanism rather than a stipulation about it. An internal policy and the recruitment of another are alternative routes to discharging the same interoceptive prediction error, distinguished principally by their metabolic cost. Illness that prompts proximity seeking is the paradigm case: the error originates in the body, and the policy that discharges it is enacted in the world, through an attachment figure (Friston, 2010; Parr and Friston, 2018; Atzil et al., 2018).

2.4 Attachment as social allostatic inference

Much of homeostatic, and particularly allostatic regulation is delegated to others. Attachment is the system that manages that delegation. Proximity seeking under distress, as the primary (secure) attachment strategy, is expedient because closeness to significant others permits coregulation. The secondary organised strategies of attachment-system deactivation (i.e., avoidance) and hyperactivation (i.e., anxiety), and even the "tertiary disorganised strategies" of hyper-arousal and hypo-arousal, on this reading, are fallbacks when proximity seeking fails or is expected to fail. They represent compensation strategies invoked when accessible social resources are (perceived as) unavailable or unpredictable, and are initially adaptive in that they typically hold allostatic load in check (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2008; White et al., 2023).

Importantly, what the preceding paragraph describes, is the demand side of attachment only. Proximity seeking is activated by threat to the self and terminates in the restoration of one's own felt security. Its complement is the caregiving system, which Bowlby treated as an evolved behavioural system reciprocal to attachment. It is activated whenever a significant other is perceived to be in need, and deactivated once that need is met or the recipient is judged to be protected. Canterberry and Gillath (2012) specify the dynamics of that system in a form that is directly useful here. Individual differences arise at three separable points, namely in the perception of distress cues, in their interpretation, and in acting upon them. Once distress has been registered, the sequence is organised around two decisions. Where immediate help is available and the recipient's distress is alleviated, the system deactivates. Where it is not, a second decision concerns whether the provision of help remains viable, and it is the answer to this question that determines which secondary strategy is adopted. Persisting attempts to help produce caregiving hyperactivation, expressed as effortful, poorly timed and often intrusive care, whereas a judgement that further help is not feasible produces caregiving deactivation, expressed as distancing from the situation and the inhibition of empathic concern. These strategies are measurable and are not simply the attachment strategies under a different label. Examining the Caregiving System Scale, Colledani and colleagues (2022) found that caregiving deactivation is unidimensional whereas caregiving hyperactivation is better represented by two related but distinct constructs, namely caregiving-related worries and doubts, and intrusive or coercive caregiving. Attachment avoidance contributed positively to deactivation and attachment anxiety to the worry and doubt component of hyperactivation only, while relationship confidence was not significantly related to either non-optimal strategy. The correspondence between the two systems is therefore substantial but partial : an individual's caregiving regime cannot be directly and fully inferred from their attachment regime, and the two must be assessed separately.

The distinction between attachment and caregiving is consequential for a social allostatic account rather than merely taxonomic. If co-regulation is characterised simply as bidirectional resource sharing, the interface between the attachment and caregiving systems is lost from view, and it is at that interface that the metabolic accounting is settled. What is distributed is regulatory work, but the two partners are not performing the same work, and the costs and benefits accrue asymmetrically, ordinarily offset across repeated exchanges within an enduring relationship. Importantly, redistributing regulatory work between two bodies does not by itself lower its total cost. Whether co-regulation yields a net saving for the dyad, and not only for the recipient, is therefore a hypothesis of our account, and Sections 4.4 and 6 specify how it could be tested. The cases of greatest interest here are accordingly those in which the two systems are mismatched, as when a fully activated attachment system is met by an unresponsive or depleted caregiving system. Or when, as in the account of disruption and disorganisation, the same figure is at once the target of the attachment system and the source of the threat that activates it.

Two lines of adjacent work supply the terms in which social allostasis from an attachment perspective can be given a computational reading, and both treat the internal working models (IWMs) of attachment as priors. The first line proposed by Cittern et al. (2018) casts IWMs as the generative models of the dyad in explicitly Bayesian terms. They derived the organised patterns of infant attachment from the minimisation of free energy over interoceptive states, showing that an agent beginning with uninformative beliefs about caregiver responsiveness acquires organised secure, avoidant, or ambivalent (i.e., anxious) behaviour as a function of that responsiveness alone. The same model extends to disorganisation. Where caregiver cues are misleading, proximity seeking retains its epistemic value even when proximity is expected to increase stress, so the agent is left with an unresolved conflict between informational and regulatory demands.

The second line is the developmental construal of attachment as prediction. Tottenham and Vannucci (2025) propose that organised attachment schemas (i.e., IWMs) are abstracted from repeated pairings of the child's internal affective state, a need, with the affective outcome of the caregiver's response, so that regularities across "needs-met" pairings are internalised as secure schemas and regularities across "needs-not-met" pairings as insecure ones. On their account, these pairings are initially acquired through amygdala-mediated stimulus-outcome learning and are subsequently semanticised into generalised representations carried by connections between subcortical structures and cortical midline hubs. A complementary learning-theoretic account holds that individual differences in security are acquired through associative learning across repeated episodes of distress and caregiver response and generalised into expectations that guide later behaviour (Bosmans et al., 2020 (Bosmans et al., , 2022)). We do not claim that it subsumes learning theory, whose account of inhibitory learning, on which new experiences of reliable support establish competing associations rather than overwriting older insecure ones, raises a question we take up below. Both computational accounts treat the IWMs of attachment as predictions, which is the move we build upon, and both locate individual differences in the parameters of predictive models, and in particular in precision weighting. Both are also pitched at a level, and towards an end, that differs from ours. Cittern et al. (2018) provide a computational demonstration that is agnostic about implementing circuitry. Tottenham and Vannucci (2025) provide a developmental and neuroanatomical account organised around a single cortico-subcortical axis and around the construction of attachment schemas rather than the metabolic economy those schemas serve, and they do not explicitly treat disruption, disorganisation, or trauma.

Our account applies to attachment across the lifespan rather than in development alone, and differs in three further respects. First, it is pitched at the level of the four NAMA modules and the two balances between them rather than at a single anatomical axis. Second, that multinetwork architecture makes competing models expressible as a multistable system, in which an older insecure model can persist as an attractor alongside a newer secure one rather than being erased by it. Where flexibility is preserved, activity can leave the older attractor, and the newer, context-appropriate model can govern inference. Under stress or novelty, the basin of the older and more precisely held insecure model deepens, transitions away from it become less likely, activity is captured by it, the measured metastability of the networks should fall, and the earlier pattern re-emerges. This is the resurfacing of insecure expectations described by inhibitory learning, given a dynamical reading, and it is what neither adjacent account is positioned to express. Third, it makes the metabolic economy, rather than the schema, the object the computations serve.

On this basis, we propose the term social allostatic inference to denote the claim that the attachment system is one predictive-allostatic loop, that the four NAMA modules are its functional components, presented here in a heuristic order, and that organised, disrupted, and disorganised attachment are characteristic settings of the precision parameters that govern it, in association with a complementary caregiving system.

3.1 The unifying principle

The prototypical attachment pathway as part of NAMA is, in its most general form, a cycle of deviation from and return to a regulated state (see Figure 1) (Long et al., 2020). The reinterpretation we propose reads that cycle as active inference under a social allostatic imperative (see Figure 2).

Aversion registers precision-weighted interoceptive prediction error, i.e., the surprise generated by a departure from the predicted state of felt security, whether arising from internal signals or from the bodily consequences of externally appraised threat. Mental state representation supplies the relational priors, in the form of IWMs, that generate expectations about the availability and responsiveness of others and about one's own efficacy in recruiting them. Approach selects the policy, most often proximity seeking, predicted to reduce that error at the lowest metabolic cost. And emotion regulation executes the resulting allostatic correction, initially through co-regulation with an attachment figure and, with repetition, increasingly through self-regulation.

On this reading, the NAMA modules are components of one loop, and the loop's business is the predictive management of the body's energetic economy, in keeping with the proposal that allostasis is the primary charge of the brain (Theriault et al., 2025). Felt security is not a signal emitted once threat has passed, but a constructed interoceptive inference, i.e., the correlate of a generative model that predicts, with high precision, that anticipated demand will be met and that the resources to meet it, whether one's own or those supplied through coregulation, are reliably available (Barrett, 2017; Barrett et al., 2025). Therefore, felt insecurity that sustains or dismisses proximity seeking, is the correlate of a model that predicts a shortfall between demand and available regulatory resources. Crucially, because that model is built from and continually revised against sensory and interoceptive evidence, felt security ordinarily tracks the actual state of the world. Where the threat stems from the attachment figure, the model predicts precisely such a shortfall, which is why felt security cannot be restored while that threat persists. External threat differs, since the presence of a reliable figure is what allows the predicted shortfall to be met, and co-regulation under external danger is the paradigm case of the attachment system functioning as intended.

We stress that this is a specification at the level of computational function and not a revision of the anatomical or neurochemical claims of our earlier work. Simulation supports treating attachment patterns as outcomes of one inference process under different caregiving statistics. Organised secure, avoidant, and ambivalent (i.e., anxious) behaviour emerged in a Bayesian infant as the optimal response to caregivers differing only in the probability with which they attend to a bid for proximity (Cittern et al., 2018). That simulation varied caregiver responsiveness. Conversely, it did not vary, and therefore could not test, where precision is attenuated within the agent, which is how we define the regimes in Section 3.3. What we add, is the mapping of those settings onto the NAMA modules, the networks, and the neurochemistry of the loop as part of one overarching social allostatic inference mechanism. The elements of the generative model that this mapping presupposes, namely hidden states, observations, policies, preferences and precision terms, are stated for both the attachment and the caregiving loop in Box 2, together with the direction in which each parameter moves in each regime, so that our account can be implemented and simulated.

This mapping should be read as a functional decomposition of NAMA and not as a literal process model. Prediction, precision weighting, policy selection and regulation are recurrent and distributed, and each is expressed to some degree in every network. What we propose, is that each computation is most heavily loaded on one NAMA module, and we present the modules in the order in which a single episode of threat and relief would engage them. That order is heuristic. For instance, the priors are consulted at every pass of the loop and not only after an error has been registered (Section 3.2). The network-level part of this proposal is not new in itself. An active inference account of second-person neuroscience has already related mutual prediction, real-time bodily adaptation and policy selection to components of the default mode, salience and frontoparietal networks and the basal ganglia (Lehmann et al., 2024). What is specific to our account, is the assignment of these computations to an ordered attachment loop, the precision regimes defined over that loop, and its caregiving complement. This decomposition also makes claims that could be shown to be inaccurate. It predicts that trialwise estimates of sensory precision should load on the salience network, of prior precision on the default mode network, of policy value and policy precision on cortico-basal ganglia circuitry, and of the maintenance of a selected correction on the frontoparietal network. A pattern in which these estimates load equally on all four networks, or in which policy value and policy precision are carried by the default mode network rather than by dopaminergic midbrain and striatal circuitry (Schwartenbeck et al., 2015), would count against the decomposition and in favour of a fully distributed alternative. A second class of observation would discriminate active inference from reward-based alternatives as such. If proximity seeking carries epistemic as well as pragmatic value, it should be sustained by uncertainty about a figure's availability and should increase as that uncertainty rises, even where its expected regulatory return is low (Cittern et al., 2018), a prediction for which the active inference literature already supplies paradigms (Lageman et al., 2026). 3.2

The four modules as components of the loop

The architecture that the four NAMA modules describe, is assembled from domain-general large-scale networks rather than from any circuit dedicated to attachment (White et al., 2023).

The regions through which visceromotor prediction and interoceptive inference are jointly accomplished, have been delineated as a distributed allostatic-interoceptive system, mapped in cortical and subcortical detail, many of the areas among the most densely connected hubs in the brain (J. Zhang et al., 2025). The agranular limbic cortices possess the least differentiated laminar structure and are arranged to issue, rather than receive, cortical predictions, relaying descending allostatic predictions to the hypothalamic and brainstem nuclei that regulate the autonomic, endocrine, and immune systems. Ascending interoceptive signals, meanwhile, arrive at the granular interoceptive cortex to be compared against those predictions and yield prediction errors (Barrett, 2017; Parr & Friston, 2018).

Because this system is built from default mode and salience network regions bound by densely connected hubs, it is routinely engaged across psychological domains rather than reserved for the internal milieu, and the circuitry associated with social processing overlaps with it rather than forming a separable social system (Atzil et al., 2018; J. Zhang et al., 2025). This is the anatomical counterpart of the claim that attachment recruits shared machinery, and it is at this system that the four NAMA modules are situated. We take each in turn, specifying computational role, network correspondence, and the segment of the neurobiological sequence it implements, and let the synthesis table (Table 1) carry the detail.

The aversion module corresponds to the salience network (Long et al., 2020; Seeley, 2019). Its computational role is the registration of precision-weighted prediction error, and in particular of interoceptive surprise (J. Zhang et al., 2025). It does not detect threat as such but computes the mismatch that anticipated threat generates, and this mismatch sets the remainder of the loop in motion. The rapid, locus coeruleus-mediated noradrenergic response that mobilises the energy and attention required to act (Izaki et al., 2024) is a candidate substrate for the precision weighting of interoceptive prediction error, since the latter requires a mechanism that adjusts the gain on prediction errors.

The mental state representation module corresponds to the default mode network (Alves et al., 2019; Long et al., 2020). Its computational role is to furnish the generative model, and in particular the relational priors against which the error registered by aversion is evaluated. The IWMs of attachment are precisely such priors, namely expectations concerning the availability and responsiveness of attachment figures and one's own efficacy in recruiting them (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2008) so that this module is where the prior precision of the loop is set. These priors are not necessarily explicit or mentalised. The secure base script literature holds that they are organised as temporally ordered event sequences, rather than as propositional beliefs (Waters et al., 1998; Waters & Waters, 2006). The association of the default mode network with internally generated, prospective, and self-and other-referential cognition (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014) is consistent with this role, since to predict the conduct of an attachment figure is to run a generative model of another agent's hidden states (Lehmann et al., 2024). Because these priors are consulted at every pass of the loop rather than at a single moment, this module is less tightly bound to one segment of the neurobiological sequence than the others. Its clearest neurochemical footprint is the proposed catecholaminergic and oxytocinergic retrieval of the attachment figure's identity from episodic memory, a mechanism that remains to be established empirically (Izaki et al., 2024).

The priors are not fixed settings but the accumulated resolution of prediction error across many caregiving episodes, which is what the learning-theoretic account of attachment describes in associative terms and the script literature describes as the abstraction of a recurring sequence (Bosmans et al., 2020 (Bosmans et al., , 2022;; Waters & Waters, 2006).

The approach module corresponds to the cortico-basal ganglia reward circuit (Haber & Knutson, 2010; Long et al., 2020). Its computational role is policy selection, and it is the locus at which active inference is selected and initiated. Confronted with an error registered by aversion and evaluated against the priors supplied by mental state representation, the system may reduce that error by acting to change the world, and the paradigmatic policy it selects, is proximity seeking towards an attachment figure, i.e., the action predicted to return the body to its regulated state at the lowest anticipated cost. Policy value here is not exhausted by that pragmatic component: proximity seeking also carries epistemic value, since approaching the figure resolves uncertainty about their availability and responsiveness. Their divergence is what we take up in the case of disruption and disorganisation, where misleading cues can preserve the epistemic value of approach even as its pragmatic value turns negative (Cittern et al., 2018).

The dopaminergic signalling that supplies the decision and the motivation to move, redirected from undirected flight to "social flight" towards a specific figure, is in incentive terms the "wanting" that drives policy selection and its maintenance through sustained D1-mediated signalling in ventral striatal and nigral circuits, which subsides only when reward is obtained at reunion (Izaki et al., 2024).

Finally, the emotion regulation module corresponds to the frontoparietal, or central executive, network (Long et al., 2020; Seeley et al., 2007). Its computational role is the execution of the allostatic correction, the high-level control operation through which the predicted return to the regulated state is brought about and stabilised and through which the precision of competing signals is adjusted so that the selected policy can be carried through.

This is plausibly the most metabolically expensive operation in the loop, since sustained topdown control is particularly costly (Theriault et al., 2025). The distinction between coregulation and self-regulation is a distinction in where the correction is performed, not in its computational character. In early development, and in the prototypical case throughout life, much of the correction is supplied by the attachment figure, whose regulatory action furnishes the conditions the individual's model predicts will restore the regulated state, at what Social Baseline Theory proposes, is a lower cost than solitary regulation would exact (Beckes and Coan, 2011; Coan and Sbarra, 2015; Saxbe et al., 2020). The developmental transition from coregulation to self-regulation is, in these terms, a gradual transfer of regulatory labour, as the priors and control operations initially exercised by the caregiver are internalised and the individual becomes able to perform alone the correction formerly performed jointly.

3.3 Between-module dynamics and organised styles as precision regimes

NAMA does not treat the modules as independent but organises them around two reciprocal balances: between the affective evaluation and the cognitive control systems, and, within affective evaluation, between the aversion and approach modules (Long et al., 2020). Redescribed as one loop, these balances become a message passing within a predictive hierarchy, in which lower levels convey ascending prediction errors and higher levels return descending predictions, with the exchange weighted at each stage by precision (Friston, 2010; Parr and Friston, 2018). The balance between affective evaluation and cognitive control is then the balance between the weight granted to the ascending error registered by aversion and the weight granted to the descending priors supplied by mental state representation and the control exercised by emotion regulation. And the balance between aversion and approach is the balance within the error-driven system between registering a prediction error and committing to a policy that would discharge it, that is, between perceptual and active inference (see Figure 2).

Neither balance is a fixed anatomical property. Each is set dynamically by the precision assigned to the signals passing between modules. A prior is a distribution rather than a point estimate, so a securely calibrated prior encodes availability as highly probable rather than certain, and an isolated non-response generates an error small enough to be absorbed without revision. That revision only follows when accumulated prediction error exceeds what the precision of the prior can absorb, which depends jointly on the magnitude of the departures and the confidence with which the prior is held, and on the volatility the system expects, since an agent that expects its environment to change, grants recent evidence more weight and revises its priors more readily (Mathys et al., 2011). This is what allows attachment representations to be stable under ordinary variation yet revisable under sustained change.

Secure attachment corresponds to calibrated precision. The priors supplied by mental state representation encode the attachment figure as reliably available and are held with a precision that matches that reliability. In doing so, they neither resist correction inappropriately nor collapse under transient evidence, while interoceptive prediction errors are weighted such that genuine departures recruit the loop, but momentary fluctuations do not. Because the priors are accurate, the initiating error is resolved efficiently, frequently through co-regulation, and the loop terminates promptly once the high-precision prediction of met demand that constitutes felt security is reinstated. On our account, this is the regime that minimises the long-run metabolic cost of regulation, a hypothesis whose test we specify in Section 6. The premise of this hypothesis, though not the hypothesis itself, has recent support: the presence of a close partner lowered the physiological cost of standard homeostatic challenges on average, while a subset of participants regulated less efficiently in the partner's presence (Masalha et al., 2026).

However, this work did not assess attachment, and our account predicts that such individual differences vary with the attachment and caregiving regimes of the two partners.

Anxious, or hyperactivating, attachment corresponds to a regime in which aversion prediction error is chronically over-weighted while the prior concerning availability is held with low precision. Intermittent caregiving installs not a confident expectation of unavailability but a diffuse one: the figure is expected to respond with a probability that is neither high nor reliably estimable. Because intermittent caregiving is also changeable caregiving, the same history installs a high estimate of volatility. Policy precision is correspondingly low, so approach and protest alternate rather than resolve, which is the computational form of ambivalence. A diffuse prior under high volatility updates readily, which means that reassurance can shift it. What it does not do, is consolidate, since an agent that expects its environment to change, discounts earlier evidence as soon as the next ambiguous cue arrives, and the prior returns towards its diffuse state. The loop is therefore terminated by reassurance only briefly, and not durably, as in the secure regime. Proximity seeking is thus sustained beyond the point at which it has already procured co-regulation. The affiliative appearance of hyperactivation should not, however, be mistaken for the low-cost sociality of the secure regime, since its cost is borne as chronic over-recruitment of the loop and, over time, as the accumulation of social allostatic load (Saxbe et al., 2020). Box 2 states the parameter settings of this and the other regimes, and the observations that would distinguish them. The volatility prediction can fail. In a dynamic trust game, individuals with elevated anxiety learned less from negative social outcomes and did not raise their learning rate as uncertainty rose (Lamba et al., 2020), which is the opposite of the pattern we assign to the anxious regime. Anxiety in that study was not attachment anxiety, and whether attachment anxiety behaves likewise is therefore the first falsifier in Box 2 that should be tested.

Avoidant, or deactivating, attachment corresponds to the converse regime, in which social prediction error is down-weighed, and regulation is displaced onto costly individual correction. The priors encode attachment figures as unavailable, or the recruitment of others as ineffective, so that proximity seeking is assigned a low expected value and the precision granted to social prediction error is attenuated at source. Unlike the anxious regime, this prior is held with high precision: consistently unavailable caregiving supports a confident negative expectation, whereas inconsistently available caregiving supports only a diffuse one. The two insecure strategies therefore attenuate precision at different points in the hierarchy, avoidance at the level of ascending error and anxiety at the level of the descending prior. The composure that the avoidant regime presents, is therefore a relocation of regulatory work and not its absence. The relocation is visible physiologically from infancy. In the Strange Situation, infants classified as avoidant showed greater vagal withdrawal and higher salivary alpha-amylase than secure infants, despite their behavioural composure (Hill-Soderlund et al., 2008). It nonetheless forgoes the metabolic economy of the social baseline and is, on that reasoning, more expensive to sustain over time even where it presents as composed (Coan and Sbarra, 2015). The nearest existing test is longitudinal. Infants classified as insecure in the Strange Situation Procedure, whether anxious-resistant or anxious-avoidant, were more likely than those classified as secure to report an inflammation-related illness three decades later (Puig et al., 2013), and adult attachment orientations have been associated with inflammatory and cellular immune markers (Ehrlich, 2019). Meta-analytically, early insecurity has been associated with heightened RSA and cortisol reactivity upon reunion (Groh & Narayan, 2019). These findings are consistent with a greater long-run cost of insecurity but do not isolate avoidance, since the longitudinal association held for both insecure classifications. The prediction specific to the avoidant regime, that self-reliant regulation is costlier than delegated regulation at a matched regulatory outcome, therefore remains untested. The second prediction in Box 2, a lower learning rate for social than for nonsocial outcomes, is also not yet supported. In a differential fear-conditioning paradigm, the safety value of the attachment figure was reduced in more avoidant individuals, whereas the rate of new safety learning was not (Camerman et al., 2023), which locates the avoidant difference in the content of the prior rather than in the rate at which new evidence is learned.

3.4 Disruption and disorganisation

The organised styles are regulatory shifts within an otherwise functioning assignment of precision. Attachment disruption and disorganisation is better described as a departure from that assignment (White et al., 2020), and the NAMDA distinction between the consequences of maltreatment versus those of neglect corresponds to two different failures of the loop. The two routes are dissociable at their origin but not mutually exclusive in their course, and they do not constitute a diagnostic mapping, particularly because the disorganised classification has low specificity and low sensitivity with respect to maltreatment (Granqvist et al., 2017). We therefore describe the two routes as mechanisms of loop failure rather than as inferences from behaviour to cause. Each step in what follows, from a caregiving history to a computational regime, from that regime to hyperarousal or hypoarousal, and from these to dissociation, is a hypothesis, and the low specificity of disorganisation for maltreatment applies to every one of them. Nonetheless, what the two routes share, is that the IWMs issue their predictions largely in advance of, and irrespective of, the current interaction, whereas in the organised styles, they remain priors that incoming evidence can in principle correct.

Where the attachment figure is at once the source of threat and the figure to whom proximity would ordinarily be sought, which is the configuration that maltreatment installs, the model that accumulates is neither unimodal nor diffuse but multimodal. The same figure is represented under several mutually incompatible predictions, i.e., as safe haven, as threat, as unresponsive, as itself frightened, each held with high precision because each has been repeatedly confirmed by a recurring configuration of care. The corresponding policies are therefore each well specified and jointly unexecutable. The behavioural signature follows from the number of modes as well as their precision. Where two policies of comparable precision compete, the output is contradiction or its limiting case, arrest, i.e., approach initiated and reversed, contact sought while orientation is averted, action interrupted mid-sequence, or freezing where neither policy prevails. Where more than two compete, arbitration does not merely reverse but fails to stabilise, and fragments of several policies surface in succession, which is what presents as disorientation, contextually anomalous action, and stereotypy (Duschinsky & Solomon, 2017; Granqvist et al., 2017). The number of modes is not a free parameter but reflects the number of distinct caregiving configurations consolidated with sufficient frequency to acquire precision in their own right, which yields the prediction that more heterogeneous caregiving should shift the profile from contradiction toward disorientation.

It is worth marking the level at which these claims are pitched. Disorganisation is described at three levels that are not always distinguished, namely the Strange Situation Procedure classification, the system-level failure to sustain a coherent strategy under alarm, and the observable behaviours that may occur below classification threshold (Duschinsky & Solomon, 2017; Frau, 2026). What we have described, concerns the second and the third, and bears on the first only inasmuch as the classification is assigned based on the behaviours.

A brainstem-based account derives the same behavioural signature from competition among incompatible sensorimotor action programmes, none of which achieves stable dominance, so that contradictory movement, interrupted sequences and freezing follow from a failure of arbitration rather than from a deficit of representation or of top-down regulation (Frau, 2026). That account locates the motor expression of the competition in midbrain circuitry, principally the superior colliculus in interaction with the periaqueductal grey. We take the two accounts to be complementary, ours specifying that arbitration fails and theirs where the resulting conflict is realised in action.

A complementary formalisation locates the failure in the epistemic rather than the pragmatic term, in that disrupted affective communication sustains a high epistemic value for a proximity seeking whose ambiguity approach continues to promise to resolve (Cittern et al., 2018), which is also what prevents the competing modes from being extinguished, since each retains the informational value that would come from resolving it. What consolidates across repetition is narrower: of the competing modes, only the prediction of threat is never disconfirmed, and it is that mode which comes to dominate. Moreover, because the error is never discharged, the sensory data of these episodes are assigned a high precision that subsequent evidence is not granted the weight to revise, and the IWM that accumulates comes to predict threat as imminent by default. This is the state we hypothesise to underlie hyperarousal in NAMDA, arising specifically from the maltreatment route (White et al., 2020).

Flexibility is, accordingly, compromised twice on this route, within episodes because activity switches among competing attractors without settling on any, and across development because it is captured by a single one.

Finally, our use of hyperarousal is attachment specific. In the computational trauma literature, hyperarousal is formalised as an excessive readiness of defensive policy following from prior aversions held with unduly high precision, and the prior is shielded because defensive action removes the organism from the situation that could disconfirm it (Kotler et al., 2026; Linson et al., 2020). In the attachment-specific case, the competing policies prevent any action from being completed, so that no disconfirming evidence is generated at all.

The neglect route differs in kind. Where care is reliably absent rather than itself threatening, no approach-avoidance conflict arises, and the initiating prediction error instead fails to be discharged because proximity seeking does not procure the co-regulation it predicts.

The IWMs that accumulate encode not danger in the figure but the futility of action, the expectation that no available policy will resolve the deviation. Where that expectation generalises across the policy space, the loop ceases to invest in regulation altogether. In the terms of Section 2.2, the expectation of futility is itself held with high prior precision, since reliably absent care confirms it without exception, and what falls is policy precision, across social and solitary policies alike, so that no course of action is selected with confidence, and the system disengages. Crucially, this should not be mistaken for the avoidant regime.

Avoidance also discounts the value of turning to others, but it takes the regulatory work back onto itself and continues to perform it, at considerable cost. Here, that work is not relocated but relinquished. Avoidant caregiving still offers a partner from whom the operations of regulation can be internalised, and avoidance learns that others will not help, which leaves solitary action intact. Neglect learns something about action itself, namely that it does not alter outcomes, and this has no reason to stop at the boundary between social and solitary policies. The system therefore withdraws from self-regulation as well, which is the difference between an expensive strategy and no strategy at all. As such, the resulting presentation of neglect, i.e., low in energy, blunted, and often dissociative, is better described as a metabolic conservation in the face of a body budget already in deficit than as a dedicated immobilisation mechanism, which is consistent with the finding that the passivity produced by uncontrollable aversive events is a default response that the detection of control would otherwise inhibit (Maier & Seligman, 2016). This is the state we hypothesise to underlie hypoarousal in NAMDA (White et al., 2020).

Finally, the two routes of maltreatment and neglect are dissociable at their origin but are not mutually exclusive in their course. Hypoarousal can follow hyperarousal where activation is sustained beyond what the energetic budget can support, in which case the collapse of policy precision is the terminal phase of a regime that began as its opposite. We hypothesise that the transition has neuroendocrine content, since chronic threat exposure is associated with suppression and habituation of HPA-axis output (Miller et al., 2007) and with downregulated dopaminergic function, which is not a return to regulation but a costly failure state, and the source of the anergia and blunted motivation the presentation displays. The same sequence may apply to the neglect route itself. Blunted diurnal cortisol has been reported in preschool children with a history of child protective services involvement (Bernard, Zwerling, et al., 2015). At 4 months, by contrast, maternal childhood neglect, but not abuse, has been associated with higher infant cortisol output during the Still-Face Paradigm (Khoury et al., 2026). Maternal neglect history combined with disoriented caregiving predicted higher infant cortisol output, and maternal abuse history combined with negative-intrusive caregiving predicted lower output (Chasson et al., 2025). These designs index the mother's history and current caregiving rather than the infant's own maltreatment. Even so, at the level of infant cortisol, they run opposite to the arousal profiles we have assigned to the two routes, and they accord with the proposal that, in infancy, the absence of the caregiver is itself the primary threat (Lyons-Ruth et al., 2024).

The mapping of the two routes onto hyper-and hypoarousal should therefore be treated as agedependent, with hypoarousal on the neglect route a later phase rather than its starting point. We tentatively place dissociation here as well. Its association with severity of childhood abuse, mediated in part by disorganised attachment (Byun et al., 2016), fits the terminal phase of the maltreatment route rather than neglect proper, and we therefore predict dissociative presentations to concentrate where hypoarousal follows a sustained hyperaroused regime rather than where care was simply absent.

3.5 The caregiving system as a second instance of the loop: a testable extension of NAMA Sections 3.1 to 3.4 describe the attachment system, and the account would be incomplete if the complementary system named in Section 2.4 were carried into the dyadic argument without a corresponding specification. Caregiving is a single-brain system in the same sense that attachment is, and we therefore treat it here (see Figure 2 and Table 2).

We propose, as a testable extension of NAMA, that caregiving is a second instance of the same loop rather than a separate architecture. The anatomical evidence is consistent with this proposal. Reviewing the neural mechanisms of paternal care across species, Feldman and colleagues (2019) describe the subcortical structures that support maternal caregiving as having been elaborated into a global network of parental care, and characterise paternal behaviour as highly malleable, accompanied by substantial neural plasticity, and shaped by prior and current caregiving experience as well as by infant and ecological input. Human parental brain findings partition that network in a manner that maps onto our modules. Comparing primary caregiving mothers, secondary caregiving fathers and primary caregiving fathers, Abraham and colleagues (2014) reported that parenting integrated the functioning of a set of subcortical and paralimbic structures implicated in emotional processing, and cortical structures implicated in social understanding, with amygdala and superior temporal sulcus activation weighted differently across the groups and their functional connectivity scaling with time spent in direct childcare. 1 and Box 2 carry the detail. Read through the loop, the four NAMA modules take the following caregiving side roles, and we state them as a proposal to be tested rather than as an established mapping.

Aversion registers the other's signalled distress as a precision-weighted prediction error, i.e., as a mismatch between the recipient's inferred state and the state the caregiver's model predicts for them. Mental state representation supplies the caregiving side priors, comprising expectations about the recipient's needs, about the efficacy of one's own care, and about one's standing as a caregiver. Approach selects the caregiving policy, and emotion regulation executes the correction, which on this side includes the regulation of the caregiver's own distress at the recipient's distress, since the correction cannot be delivered by a caregiver whose own loop has been recruited by it. Reviews of the human parental brain independently distinguish reward and motivation systems, salience and threat systems, regulatory systems, and systems for empathy and mentalizing (Swain et al., 2014), which is the same fourfold division arrived at from a different starting point. What our account adds, is the organisation of that partition as one loop, and the claim that the settings governing the transitions between its stages are what individual differences consist of.

One asymmetry between the two instances is computational rather than anatomical and, in our view, carries the most weight. Both loops proceed by inference. On the account given in Section 2.3, the bodily state the attachment loop regulates, is itself inferred rather than read off.

The difference lies in the evidence each has to work with. The attachment loop is served by interoceptive afferents arising in the same body whose state it regulates, whereas the caregiving loop must infer a state in another body from exteroceptive evidence, e.g., cry, face, posture, voice, and later speech. The caregiver accumulates a model of this particular recipient across repeated episodes, and the accuracy of that model is itself learned, and therefore varies across caregivers and improves with experience to a degree that the accuracy of inference about one's own body need not. The caregiving loop's precision on its initiating error is thus a function of dyad-specific learning, which is why mental state representation occupies a more load-bearing position on the caregiving side than on the attachment side, and why the same signal may be read correctly by one caregiver and not by another.

The two instances are not sealed off from one another within a single brain. For example, maternal attachment representations have been shown to predict caregiving side neural responses to one's own infant, with securely attached mothers showing stronger activation of the ventral striatum and the oxytocin-associated hypothalamic and pituitary region to their own infant's faces than mothers classified as insecure-dismissing on the Adult Attachment Interview (Strathearn et al., 2009). This pattern is consistent with our account, since the priors an individual holds about the availability and efficacy of others are not obviously partitioned by which loop consults them. We note it here as a point requiring development, because it implies that the parameters of the two instances are linked in ways the present formulation does not yet specify.

The two non-optimal caregiving strategies can then be read as precision regimes in the same terms as the organised attachment styles of Section 3.3. Caregiving deactivation attenuates, at source, the precision granted to the recipient's signals, so that a demand does not register as warranting investment, and the withheld investment is intelligible as a policy predicted to fail. Caregiving hyperactivation over-weights those signals or holds a prior that assigns high probability to unmet need, so that investment is made out of proportion to demand.

Neural evidence bears on the latter directly. Comparing synchronous and intrusive mothers, Atzil and colleagues (2011) found that synchronous caregiving was characterised by nucleus accumbens activation functionally connected with social-cognitive and empathy networks and by temporally organised activity, whereas intrusive caregiving was characterised by amygdala activation functionally connected with action-oriented regions and by less organised temporal patterns. Read through the loop, intrusive care is not simply care in excess, but aversion over-weighted and approach engaged without integration with mental state representation, i.e., a failure of the balance between affective evaluation and cognitive control rather than a setting on any one module.

The two-component structure that Colledani et al. (2022) recover, is informative here, since caregiving-related worry and doubt is naturally read as low precision on the prior concerning one's own efficacy, whereas intrusive and coercive care is naturally read as a policy sustained past the evidence that it is required. On this reading, the two components are not two indicators of one construct, but two distinct parameter settings, which would explain why only the former was associated with attachment anxiety. That these settings are modifiable, is indicated by randomised intervention trials, in which attachment-based programmes targeting caregiver behaviour were followed by more normative cortisol regulation in the child than a control intervention (Berlin et al., 2019; Bernard, Dozier, et al., 2015). In one trial, the difference in cortisol regulation in middle childhood was statistically mediated by observed parental sensitivity (Garnett et al., 2020). Reading the change in sensitivity as a recalibration of caregiving-side precision is our interpretation and was not measured. What the trials show, is that a change in caregiver behaviour is followed by an allostatic change in the other body, which is consistent with the two loops being linked through regulation and not only through behaviour.

Two limits should be stated. First, the human parental brain literature is predominantly maternal and the paternal literature smaller and more recent (Abraham & Feldman, 2022), and the caregiving strategy measures show partial rather than full invariance across gender (Colledani et al., 2022; Feldman et al., 2019). The mapping proposed here should therefore not be assumed to hold uniformly across caregivers. Second, no study we are aware of has assessed the four NAMA modules jointly on the caregiving side, so the partition rests on convergence between separate literatures rather than on a single design. The convergence is notable, although the evidence remains predominantly correlational, and the organisation we propose has not been tested directly. We therefore treat the caregiving loop throughout as a testable extension of NAMA and not as an established architecture.

4. Attachment as social allostatic inference: The dyadic account

Every step of the argument to this point has been pitched at the level of one brain, and the loop has been closed inside one head, even where the regulatory work it describes is supplied through caregiving by somebody else. Because attachment is for co-regulation, and the unit in which co-regulation occurs is the dyad, an attachment account confined to one brain describes only half of the process (Long et al., 2020; Redcay & Schilbach, 2019; Schilbach et al., 2013).

That limitation takes a second and more specific form. The loop specified in Section 3 describes the attachment system alone. The partner in an interacting dyad is ordinarily not running a second copy of that loop, but the complementary caregiving system, which is activated by another's signalled or anticipated distress or vulnerability and terminates in the alleviation of that distress (Sections 2.4 and 3.5). Second-person neuroscience suggests that social cognition differs fundamentally when individuals interact with rather than merely observe one another, and that its mechanisms must be studied in real-time reciprocal interaction (Schilbach et al., 2013; Redcay & Schilbach, 2019; Schilbach & Redcay, 2025). Bolis et al. (2017) gave this position a Bayesian formulation. They discussed that predictive processing accounts confined to single individuals are insufficient for understanding social interaction and psychiatric conditions, and proposed the dialectical misattunement hypothesis, on which psychopathology is expressed as a dynamic mismatch between interacting individuals as well as a disturbance within either of them. Bolis, Dumas and Schilbach (2023) have since developed this into an account of interpersonal attunement across behavioural and physiological scales. A parallel line located the social origins of the body's own model at the start of life, proposing that interoceptive inference is itself shaped by embodied interaction with caregivers (Fotopoulou & Tsakiris, 2017), and tracing that process to co-homeostasis between two bodies before birth (Ciaunica et al., 2021). Formally, two agents engaged in reciprocal active inference converge on a shared generative model, with neural synchronisation as its signature (Friston & Frith, 2015). The active inference treatment of second-person neuroscience that we draw upon below (Lehmann et al., 2024) develops the second-person line. It has already related mutual prediction, bodily adaptation and policy selection to the default mode, salience and frontoparietal networks and the basal ganglia, that is, to the same four networks to which NAMA assigns its modules. Coupled active inference has also been proposed as a framework for tracking rupture, co-regulation and re-attunement in hyperscanning data (Hinrichs et al., 2026). Relational Neuroscience (De Felice et al., 2025) builds on this tradition. Accordingly, the mismatches between the attachment and caregiving systems identified in Section 2.4, and projected onto the dyad in Section 4.5, are a specific case of misattunement, one in which the two partners run different systems with different activating and terminating conditions. We keep two things apart throughout. Hyperscanning is a method for recording two or more individuals' brain activity simultaneously, and second-person interaction is the behavioural phenomenon of interest. Simultaneous recording does not by itself establish that an interaction was reciprocal, and synchrony between two recordings does not by itself establish that the partners were interacting at all (Section 4.2).

Two existing accounts formalise the relations at issue, and we adopt an element of each.

The first is an active inference treatment of dyadic interaction (Lehmann et al., 2024). Where two brains interact in real time, each infers the causes of the sensations the other generates, and the partners align their generative models through the reciprocal exchange of predictions and prediction errors. Attunement of the interoceptive and proprioceptive systems is driven by the demands of the exchange rather than accompanying it incidentally, which places bodily regulation inside the alignment rather than alongside it.

Prediction error within the dyad falls by two routes, which correspond to the perceptual and active branches of inference distinguished in Section 2.2. A partner may revise their model to fit the other, which is the acquisition of partner-specific accuracy across repeated encounters, or may instead act such that their own conduct conforms more closely to what the other predicts.

Both features bear on our argument, since the encoding and resolution of prediction error is metabolically costly (Theriault et al., 2025), and alignment of this kind is therefore bioenergetic and not only informational.

The second is a developmental account of the attachment prior (Tottenham and Vannucci, 2025). On this account, the prior is dyadic in construction and not merely acquired in a dyadic setting. One partner generates the "need" term and the other supplies the "need metor-not-met term", so that neither is recoverable from either brain alone, and the unit of learning is the interaction itself. We adopt this formulation for one specific implication: the quantity the two loops align around exists only across them, and is not two private quantities that happen to correlate.

Three further accounts have addressed the same dyad with related formalisms, and we distinguish our proposal from each. Diaz-Rojas and colleagues (Diaz-Rojas et al., 2025) model parent-infant allostatic co-regulation in control-theoretic terms, treating the caregiver as a controller whose sensitivity and reaction speed determine how rapidly the infant returns towards a preferred state. Sensitivity in their terms corresponds to a precision assignment in ours, and reaction speed to a property of policy selection. Santaguida and colleagues (Santaguida et al., 2026) treat the infant-mother pair as a co-evolving dyadic system under active inference and derive the attachment styles from it, which is the closest formal precedent for the present proposal, although this system is pitched at the developmental origin of those styles rather than at the architecture that implements them. And Tsubamoto & Horii (2026) model parent-infant co-regulation by combining a partially observable formulation of active interoceptive inference with a decentralised Bayesian account of communicative alignment. Their asymmetry concerns access to the infant's visceral state, whereas ours concerns two systems with different activating conditions and terminations.

Furthermore, none of the above accounts is pitched at our level of organisation, which is the four NAMA modules and the two balances between them, re-described as components of one loop and the message passing that aligns them. Nor does any of them extend beyond development, or treat the disrupted and disorganised cases specified in Section 3.4. In the prototypical caregiving dyad, the child's loop runs the attachment system and the caregiver's loop the caregiving system, and the shared quantity is the estimate of whether the demand, signalled or anticipated, will be met. In adult pair bonds, each partner runs both systems, and the roles alternate across episodes, which is one reason why paradigms that fix the roles for the duration of a measurement are more readily interpretable. What is distributed across the dyad is regulatory work, but the partners do not perform the same work. Costs and benefits therefore accrue asymmetrically and are ordinarily offset across repeated exchanges within an enduring relationship rather than within any single episode. Whose cost is at issue must therefore be stated in each case, whether the recipient's, the caregiver's or the dyad's total, since a saving to the recipient may be offset by a cost to the caregiver.

First, synchrony is generated asymmetrically, so its magnitude does not disclose which partner's loop contributed the greater share (Section 4.2). Second, the attachment and caregiving sides can, in principle, be characterised separately within the same dyad, and need not necessarily converge on a single dyadic value (Section 4.3). The choice among estimators is itself an inferential commitment, since sampling rate, window and whether coupling is modelled as contemporaneous, lagged or dynamical determine which forms of coordination can be detected at all (Danyluck et al., 2026). Dependence can arise from common sensory input, shared task structure, arousal, movement, speech and turntaking as well as from reciprocity, so any reading of a measured value as reflecting an interpersonal mechanism is an inference beyond the measure. In the following, we use synchrony for the measured dependence and avoid terms that imply a specific mechanism.

Synchrony has been interpreted in several ways that are not mutually exclusive: as entrainment to shared perceptual and communicative rhythms, as a signature of mutual prediction between interacting agents, as an index of emotion co-regulation, and as a correlate of interaction and relationship quality, (see e.g., De Felice et al., 2025; Feldman, 2017; Hasson et al., 2012; Hoehl et al., 2021; Kingsbury et al., 2019).

We propose an additional interpretation, which we state as a hypothesis. On our account, INS and BBS may reflect, in part, where predictive and regulatory work is being invested within a dyad. Under most conditions, this agrees with the interaction quality interpretation. That said, predictive and regulatory work diverge under two conditions: where investment is high because regulation is failing, and where it is low because the available budget is depleted. Both are considered in Section 4.4. We stress at the outset that investment does not equal benefit. Even where synchrony does reflect such work, it records that work is being expended, not the state it is expended towards, and the remainder of this section should be read with that distinction in view.

Two of these interpretations, mutual prediction and emotion co-regulation, correspond to components of the loop. Mutual prediction is located primarily in the mental state representation module, since predicting an attachment figure's conduct requires a generative model of another agent's hidden states (Lehmann et al., 2024). Across a dyad, two such models run reciprocally and are brought into register, a relation that has begun to be formalised at the interbrain level (Mayo & Shamay-Tsoory, 2024). Emotion co-regulation is located predominantly in the emotion regulation module. Across a dyad, it is the allostatic correction performed jointly: one partner supplies, through their own action, the conditions the other's model predicts will restore the regulated state. The two are not independent, since a correction cannot be delivered by a partner who cannot predict when it is needed. Mutual prediction is therefore a precondition for co-regulation rather than a parallel mechanism. This priority accords with the definition of parental sensitivity, in which the perception and interpretation of the infant's signals precede a prompt and appropriate response (Ainsworth et al., 1978), and with the meta-analytic finding that parental mentalisation predicts attachment security in part through sensitivity (Zeegers et al., 2017).

In humans, the asymmetry has been established behaviourally. Beebe and colleagues (2016) found mother-infant face-to-face communication to be bidirectional but asymmetric, maternal contingent coordination with the infant exceeding the infant's coordination with the mother. And Somers and colleagues (2022) reported second-by-second bidirectional affective influence in which the two directions were separately estimable and differently moderated.

Each partner's own moment-to-moment stability accounted for more variance than interactive contingency did (Beebe et al., 2016), which is consistent with co-regulation operating on the parameters of self-regulation rather than displacing it.

The clearest demonstration that synchrony is generated asymmetrically at the neural level comes from freely interacting mice, in which the partner's behaviour was more robustly encoded in the subordinate animal, so that the animal investing the greater predictive effort contributed disproportionately to the measured synchrony (Kingsbury et al., 2019). We cite this as an analogy and not as evidence concerning attachment (Kingsbury & Hong, 2020). The caregiving dyad is asymmetric by construction, and an alignment procured largely by one partner accommodating the other need not divide any metabolic saving evenly.

Emotion co-regulation has an outcome signature instead, namely an association between the magnitude or timing of synchrony and a measured regulatory result. Goldstein and colleagues (Goldstein et al., 2018) recorded INS in romantic partners while one received arises from common input and task structure alone, and they are a precondition for any interpersonal reading (Hakim et al., 2023; Mayo et al., 2025; Moulder et al., 2018). Eventlocked analyses, which estimate synchrony relative to behavioural events rather than averaged across a block, are a further safeguard, since fine-grained behavioural interdependence can leave artefacts in the neural signal that resemble inter-brain coordination (Marriott Haresign et al., 2024).

Synchrony is also not confined to cooperative exchange. In romantic couples, conflictual discussion was associated with greater INS than supportive discussion, and the effect tracked arousal rather than emotional valence (Long et al., 2022); competitive tasks have been linked to INS distinct from, and at times exceeding, that linked to cooperation (Liu et al., 2021; H. Zhang et al., 2025). Such synchrony need not be benign either, in the currency our account requires. In married couples discussing a standing disagreement, stronger in-phase high-frequency heart-rate-variability synchrony predicted higher levels of three inflammatory markers across the day (Wilson et al., 2018). Synchrony of this kind thus appears to index an inability to disengage from a costly exchange rather than the delegation of regulatory load and is therefore an accrual of social allostatic load rather than its distribution (Saxbe et al., 2020).

Where the attachment figure is at once the source of threat and the figure to whom proximity would be sought, the attachment system may remain strongly engaged while physiological regulation is impaired (Graf et al., 2022). And the dyad may become tightly coordinated around the wrong target, namely the monitoring of threat, the management of the caregiver's state, and/or the maintenance of proximity despite fear. It is instructive that mutual prediction can be fully achieved under these conditions: a child may come to anticipate a caregiver's anger with considerable accuracy while remaining oriented to danger rather than regulated towards safety. Predictability is therefore not sufficient for co-regulation. Where care is instead reliably absent, no such coordination is established, and the expected signature is the failure of synchronisation rather than its misdirection. We would accordingly expect the two routes to be dissociable in the form of the synchrony and not in its magnitude alone.

In a still-face procedure, greater maternally reported maltreatment severity was associated with weakened concordance in respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) co-regulation in 47 low-income mother-infant dyads (Brown et al., 2021), a meta-analysis reported lower mother-child RSA synchrony in samples characterised by maltreatment, clinical difficulty or socioeconomic disadvantage (Miller et al., 2023), and maternal psychological aggression has been associated with weaker concordance, particularly during unstructured interaction (Lunkenheimer et al., 2017). A mean computed across an interaction is not a description of its episodes, and the informative unit is the profile across perturbation, escalation, repair and recovery, rather than the aggregate coefficient. Within this context, the pattern reported by Brown and colleagues (2021) is of particular interest because the weakened concordance took a divergent form, relatively higher maternal RSA together with lower infant RSA, which the authors interpret as greater infant distress alongside reduced maternal support, and which is the signature we anticipate in Section 4.5 where an activated demand side meets a supply side that is not investing.

Subtype may matter more here than severity. In preschool-aged samples, physically abusive dyads showed positive RSA concordance overall, with abuse severity associated with lower mean child RSA and a mother-driven stress response. Conversely, neglectful dyads showed no association between mother and child RSA over time, with neglect severity associated instead with higher mean child RSA and a child-driven response (Lunkenheimer et al., 2018). This is the dissociation in form rather than in magnitude that our two routes lead us to expect, and we regard it as the closest available evidence bearing on that expectation.

The modalities of BBS are not interchangeable. Neural and autonomic synchrony have been found to be distinct rather than equivalent indices of the same dyadic state in mother-child dyads (Reindl et al., 2022). Systematic reviews across autonomic, endocrine, neural and behavioural measures report the same dissociation more generally, with concordance in one system being a poor guide to concordance in another (Davis et al., 2018; DePasquale, 2020).

4.3 Interpersonal closeness, interaction quality, and attachment

Interpersonal closeness indexes the type of relationship. It is reliably registered in synchrony, which is generally greater in dyads bound by an intimate and enduring connection than in dyads of friends or strangers (Feldman, 2017; De Felice et al., 2025). Interaction quality concerns the reciprocity, contingency and success of the exchange in progress. And attachment (and its caregiving complement) index the specific attachment-related quality of a relationship of a given type. The three are nested rather than competing. A design comparing romantic partners with strangers does not assess attachment quality per se, but it is not thereby uninformative about attachment, since child-parent relationships and romantic partnerships are attachment bonds in the technical sense. Such designs leave the specific quality of a bond unassessed rather than its presence. Coverage is uneven across the three levels and thinnest at the third.

At the level of closeness, Djalovski et al. (2021) compared long-term couples, best friends and strangers across a joint-motor and an empathy-giving task. Couples showed the highest neural and behavioural synchrony and the fastest motor performance, but in the empathy task they showed the highest behavioural and the lowest neural synchrony, with strangers showing the reverse. The same dyads therefore produced opposite neural signatures across two tasks while remaining the more efficient dyads in both. A comparable reversal has been reported in conversation, where friends and strangers differed less in the amount of synchrony than in its character, the closer dyads diverging where the unacquainted converged (Speer et al., 2024).

One reading, which we advance as an interpretation to be tested, is that the joint-motor task is symmetric whereas the empathy-giving task is complementary, one partner occupying the insecure attachment representations showed higher INS in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (Nguyen et al., 2024). The last two diverge in direction (Section 4.4).

Almost all the above associations are caregiver side. Maternal attachment anxiety and insecure maternal attachment representations were the predictors in two of the three studies, and only the association with avoidant child attachment concerned the demand side, where it remained preliminary. The available INS evidence therefore characterises the caregiving system more than the attachment system, and it does so from an attachment perspective, which is worth stating because the two need not behave alike. Where contingent coordination is itself asymmetric (Beebe et al., 2016), the caregiver's parameters would be expected to bear more heavily upon a symmetric estimate of synchrony, and the remedy is directed estimation.

Two of our own studies illustrate this point, since in both the predictor was a caregiving side construct. Fathers' beliefs about the importance and sensitivity of their own parental role were positively associated with father-child INS during cooperative problem solving (Nguyen, Schleihauf, Kungl, et al., 2021) and, in the structural counterpart of that study, with hypothalamic volume, whereas adult attachment style was not (Long et al., 2021). The null attachment result, obtained in the same sample as the caregiving association, is a concrete instance of the separability argued for in Section 2.4, although separable does not mean independent (Section 3.5).

Evidence at the wider level of BBS is clearer with the roles distinguished. In the empathic stress paradigm, the stressed partner occupies the demand side and the observer the caregiving side for the duration of the measurement. Inducing psychosocial stress in one member of romantic couples while the other observed, we found higher cortisol resonance in dyads with securely rather than insecurely attached observers, assessed by narrative interview (Gallistl, Handke, et al., 2025). In a companion study using self-reported attachment, the presence of an insecure-avoidantly attached partner operated as a stressor rather than a support, with lower psycho-endocrine covariance (Gallistl, Hamann, et al., 2025). Yet, these are again on the observer side and therefore targeting the caregiving system.

4.4 Convergence, tension, and what remains unknown

Three convergences are worth stating. The first is demand sensitivity. Synchrony rises when a dyad is required to perform regulatory or coordinative work. During cooperation (relative to independent) performance, during handholding under noxious stimulation, and in the presence of an acutely stressed partner (Goldstein et al., 2018; Miller et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2020; Gallistl, Handke, et al., 2025). This is what a loop recruiting external regulation in proportion to registered prediction error would produce. Synchrony also rises during conflict and competition (Section 4.2), and coercive exchanges are demanding in exactly the sense at issue, so that a dyad coordinated around the anticipation of aggression satisfies the criterion as fully as one coordinated around its relief. The discriminating quantity is not the rise but the release, since a loop that terminates upon restoration of the regulated state predicts synchrony that is relinquished once the correction has been delivered, whereas synchrony around an unresolved threat has no termination condition. The first convergence is in this sense a precondition for the third.

The second convergence is the developmental transfer of regulatory labour described in Section 3.2, which appears to be visible dyadically and not only in isolated participants. It also is as much a change on the caregiving side as on the attachment side, since it consists in the caregiver relinquishing a mode of regulation that is no longer warranted (Motsan et al., 2021).

And the third is that synchrony has been tied most directly to a benefit where a co-regulatory outcome was measured rather than interaction alone (Goldstein et al., 2018; Reindl et al., 2018; Reddan et al., 2020), which is the form our account requires if the currency is metabolic.

The discrepancy concerns the direction of the attachment-related associations. Greater maternal attachment anxiety accompanied reduced INS during co-viewing (Azhari et al., 2023), whereas insecure maternal representations accompanied elevated INS during cooperative problem solving (Nguyen et al., 2024). A context-specific interpretation is plausible. Coviewing supplies a shared stimulus without a clear regulatory requirement, so a preoccupied mother may have her predictive resources drawn away from the stimulus whose shared processing the measure indexes. Instead, cooperative problem solving supplies a live interactive demand, so the same preoccupation may be directed at the partner and raise the measured value.

This interpretation is untested, since it requires the same dyads to be measured under both kinds of demand.

Two further explanations are possible. The first concerns the construct rather than the context, the two studies having assessed dimensional self-reported anxiety in the one case and interview-assessed representations in the other. The second concerns timescale. Attachment has a state as well as a trait expression, and everyday experiences of effective or ineffective support have been reported to shift state attachment in middle childhood against a background of positive carry-over (Verhees et al., 2023). Conversely, synchrony itself has proved markedly unstable across occasions. In couples measured twice some weeks apart, heart rat variability synchrony during disagreement showed near-zero correspondence between visits and behaved as a state that any couple might enter rather than as a stable dyadic characteristic (Wilson et al., 2018). If a trait-level attachment measure is related to a quantity that varies substantially from occasion to occasion, divergence between single-session studies is to be expected on those grounds alone.

The relation between synchrony and interaction quality is similarly conditional. Our account adds a reason to expect the relation not to hold generally. Physiological synchrony has been found to be stronger in dyads with more anxious mothers who responded even to small fluctuations in their infant's arousal (Smith et al., 2022). And a systematic review reported greater synchrony with better emotion regulation, and that parenting stress and depression were associated with less synchrony (Sfeir et al., 2026). Both are caregiving side observations, and our account treats them as distinct states rather than as two points on one scale. Parenting stress and depression deplete the supply side, so investment and hence synchrony should fall, whereas maternal anxiety over-assigns precision to the child's signals, so synchrony should rise. No single monotonic expectation accommodates both. The non-monotonicity has also been demonstrated experimentally rather than only observed. Inducing stress in mothers raised both infant physiological reactivity and mother-infant physiological covariation (Waters et al., 2014), so that a manipulation which worsened the infant's state increased the measure. It shows that covariation can index the transmission of dysregulation rather than its correction.

On our account, the informative quantity is not the central tendency of synchrony but its distribution over time, comprising the degree of departure from alignment, the latency of return, and the variability of both across contexts (Gordon et al., 2025; Müller et al., 2022; Tsubamoto & Horii, 2026). Where the mean is nonetheless the available quantity, we expect its relation to regulatory outcome to have an interior optimum rather than a monotonic form, so that estimators assuming monotonicity are misspecified for it, and a literature testing linear associations would be expected to produce the heterogeneity it has produced. We therefore predict that curvilinear and moderated specifications, declared in advance rather than fitted following a null linear result, will recover relations on these data that linear models cannot, and that the location of the optimum will shift with demand rather than being fixed. Two conditions attach to this prediction. Detecting curvilinearity reliably requires samples appreciably larger than are usual in this literature. And given the instability of synchrony across occasions noted above, the optimum may be recoverable only within dyads across repeated measurement rather than between dyads measured once, which is a different design from any we have drawn upon and which we specify in Section 6.

This is precisely the question we raised in NAMA without being able to answer it.

Because the methods used to derive INS detect any temporal contingency between two time courses, it remained open whether more synchrony is always better, and we proposed that the degree of desynchronisation and the latency of resynchronisation would prove more informative than the level of synchrony as such (Long et al., 2020; White et al., 2023). Dyadic coordination has since been characterised as a metastable process moving between alignment and segregation (Mayo & Gordon, 2020). This was later extended into a theory of flexible multimodal synchrony, in which the informative quantity is flexibility across contexts (Gordon et al., 2025). The developmental evidence is consistent: only a minority of caregiver-child interaction is synchronous, miscoordination and its repair appear beneficial for the child's developing sense of efficacy, and excessive synchrony may indicate intrusive and/or overstimulatory parenting (Markova et al., 2019; Hoehl et al., 2021).

Two further lines of support have since accumulated. Empirically, the quantity we proposed, has now been measured in an attachment-assessed sample. In a longitudinal study, a longer latency to interactive reparation during the reunion episode of the Face-to-Face Still-Face procedure at 3 to 8 months predicted insecure attachment at 12 to 24 months (N = 58), and, in a separate analysis of an overlapping subsample (N = 39), children classified as insecure showed higher cortisol reactivity at 5 to 6 years (Müller et al., 2022). A direct association between reparation latency and cortisol was not tested. The two analyses are consistent with a pathway from the dynamics of early interaction, through attachment, to endocrine regulation, but they do not establish it. What matters for our account, is that the predictive quantity was the latency of return following a mismatch rather than the level of coordination, which is the form our proposal specified.

Constructively, the same asymmetry emerges from a model that was not built to produce it. In the simulation described in Section 4.1, alignment between parent and infant was not a state the dyad attained and held, but a low-divergence regime punctuated by transient separations and followed by rapid return (Tsubamoto and Horii, 2026). Two points follow. The informative quantities in that model are time-integrated divergence and the latency of realignment rather than mean similarity, which is the proposal we advanced. And desynchronisation is not a failure of the dyad but an unavoidable consequence of the partners' asymmetric access to the state being regulated, arising in a system that is functioning as intended.

Our framework therefore predicts an "optimum in the midrange" rather than a monotonic benefit (Beebe & Steele, 2013), since two models that remain aligned irrespective of demand fail to release regulatory load, while two that never align cannot share it. Adaptive coordination should accordingly include counter-regulation and disengagement alongside matching (Wass et al., 2024). The caregiving policy is to correct a deviation, not to reproduce the recipient's state, and there are conditions under which correction requires opposing rather than joining it. The precedent for an interior optimum is behavioural. Midrange coordination of vocal rhythms at 4 months was optimal for attachment security at 12 months (Jaffe et al., 2001), and both higher and lower self-and interactive contingency at 4 months predicted insecure and disorganised classifications at 12 months, depending on partner and communication modality (Beebe et al., 2010). The largest test to date supports the form of the prediction but narrows its scope. In 625 high-risk mother-infant dyads, a model with linear and quadratic terms showed that both very high and very low maternal contingent responsiveness at 4 months predicted disorganised attachment at 18 months, whereas responsiveness was unrelated to security (Mitsven et al., 2022). In a community sample, rated maternal sensitivity rose with contingent responsiveness only up to a moderate level and fell beyond it (Bornstein & Manian, 2013). We are not aware of a physiological or neural study that has tested a quadratic term against an attachment or regulatory outcome. The two closest studies report moderation rather than curvature. Greater mother-infant EEG synchrony predicted less infant positive emotion when maternal anxiety was high but was unrelated to it when anxiety was low (Mallin et al., 2026), and higher maternal anxiety predicted lower infant RSA only in dyads with high physiological synchrony (Aitken et al., 2026). Three competing models therefore remain open: a monotonic relation, as the cooperative hyperscanning literature implies (Section 4.3); a threshold or plateau, on which synchrony is beneficial up to a level and uninformative beyond it; and a relation whose sign depends on context, whether on the kind of demand, positive under cooperative and negative under conflictual demand (Section 4.2), or on the state of the caregiver, as the two studies just described suggest. The interior optimum would count as disconfirmed if, in adequately powered designs with repeated measurement within dyads, a quadratic term added nothing to a linear or threshold model of the relation between synchrony and an independently measured regulatory outcome, across more than one kind of demand.

Finally, two things remain unknown. First, the metabolic quantity in which our account trades, i.e., the cost at which a given regulatory outcome is achieved, has been measured only for the individual and only as a function of a close partner's presence. Masalha et al. (2026) held physiological challenges constant and found the time-integrated deviation from baseline lower in a partner's presence than alone (Section 2.1). That work did not assess attachment or caregiving, measured the cost to each participant and not to the dyad, and in the infant experiment compared the mother's presence with a separation, which is itself a demand. The cost as a function of an attachment and caregiving regime, and the cost to the caregiver and to the dyad, have not been measured. Inflammatory outcomes have been related to synchrony (Wilson et al., 2018), and endocrine outcomes to attachment classification (Müller et al., 2022), which is nearer to the currency we require than an interaction-quality index, but a marker of accumulated cost is not a measure of the cost of a particular correction. Second, neural flexibility measures have not been applied to attachment-assessed samples, so the claim that flexibility is the more informative quantity in INS remains an expectation. The remaining gaps, concerning the correlational character of the designs, anatomical coverage and the stability of synchrony across occasions, are set out with the limitations in Section 6 and Table 4.

4.5 Precision regimes projected onto the dyad

The precision regimes of Section 3.3 can now be projected onto the interacting pair. We state these as expectations rather than as established findings, and separately for the attachment and caregiving sides, since the same regime may produce different signatures depending on which system it characterises (see Figure 3 and Table 3). Following Section 4.4, we frame them wherever possible in terms of the temporal profile of synchrony, i.e., its responsiveness to demand, its release, and the latency of return following a departure, rather than its mean level, since the regimes are distinguished by the conditions under which synchrony is entered and relinquished rather than by how much of it obtains on average.

Calibrated precision, which we characterised as secure attachment, should produce synchrony that rises when demand requires it and releases once the correction has been delivered. Its signature should therefore be flexibility across contexts rather than a uniformly high mean. On the caregiving side, the corresponding regime grants the partner's signals precision proportional to their informativeness, so that the caregiver detects demand promptly, invests in proportion to it, and withdraws that investment once the correction is made. Securely attached observers resonated proportionally with a partner's acute stress rather than matching it indiscriminately or ignoring it (Gallistl, Handke, et al., 2025). We are not aware of a corresponding attachment side observation. Because the loop is defined by departure from and return to a regulated state, the regime should be characterised by short latency of return following a rupture, which is the quantity described in Section 4.4.

Deactivation, associated with attachment avoidance, relinquishes the social policy in favour of individual regulation and should produce synchrony that is both lower and less responsive to demand. This is the direction of the absent cortisol resonance in couples with insecure observers (Gallistl, Handke, et al., 2025) and of the dissociation between subjective and endocrine responding in insecure-avoidant participants (Gallistl, Hamann, et al., 2025). An attachment-side observation points the same way and refines the prediction. In couples discussing a disagreement, momentary increases in state attachment avoidance were accompanied by heart rate becoming misaligned from the partner's physiology, termed contrarian coregulation, and dispositional avoidance amplified this response (Schreiber et al., 2021). The estimate was directed, and the sample was oversampled for personality pathology.

If it generalises, deactivation appears as active misalignment rather than as the mere absence of synchrony, a distinction that only signed or directed estimators can make. The caregiving side counterpart is a system that does not register the partner's signal as warranting investment. The two are not distinguishable from synchrony magnitude alone, since a demand that is not made and a demand that is not met both yield a low value. Directed measures (Section 4.2) and decomposition would separate them. Where each partner's intrinsic return towards their own baseline is estimated separately from partner-driven synchrony, the two quantities can diverge: in mother-infant interaction, synchronisation from mother to infant was obtained principally among mothers lower in emotion dysregulation, while higher maternal dysregulation was associated with weaker return strength in the infant at reunion (Gao et al., 2023).

This supplies a supply-side signature that magnitude alone would not distinguish.

Hyperactivation, associated with attachment anxiety, should produce synchrony maintained beyond the point at which co-regulation has been obtained and directed towards signals that do not warrant it. This accords with the elevated mother-child INS observed with insecure maternal representations during interactive problem solving (Nguyen et al., 2024) and with the elevated autonomic synchrony of more anxious mothers (Smith et al., 2022), both caregiving-side observations. On the attachment side, momentary increases in state attachment anxiety were accompanied by heart rate becoming aligned towards the partner's physiology, termed dependent coregulation, most strongly in dispositionally anxious individuals (Schreiber et al., 2021). If the prior concerning availability is held with low precision under a high volatility estimate, the regime should be marked by instability as much as by level, namely synchrony entered readily, relinquished incompletely, and re-entered on evidence that a calibrated system would absorb without response. The prediction is therefore of a high mean accompanied by high within-dyad variability and prolonged return following rupture, and it is separable from the caregiving side over-weighting described above, which predicts elevation without the same instability.

Disruption and disorganisation, in which no assignment of precision allows action to discharge error (White et al., 2020), should produce neither sustained synchrony nor its orderly release. The only relevant observation we are aware of is at the attentional level and was not statistically robust: infant attachment disorganisation moderated the correspondence between mother and infant monitoring of a separation-reunion animation (Biro et al., 2017). At the individual level, the defensive hand-blink reflex scaled with the proximity of a threat in adults with organised attachment representations but not in those classified as disorganised, and activating the attachment system moved the two groups in opposite directions (Fossataro et al., 2023). We read this as graded versus ungraded assignment of precision in proportion to predicted demand. The finding is individual rather than dyadic, and the sample was small and enriched for disorganised classification.

The account given in Section 3.4 nonetheless yields a more specific expectation than the absence of consolidation. Where several mutually incompatible predictions concerning the same figure are each held with high precision, the failure is one of arbitration rather than of specification, and its dyadic expression should be synchrony that is entered and abandoned within an episode, with the leading partner reversing, as first one and then another mode governs the exchange, rather than synchrony that is merely weak. Detecting this requires estimators that recover direction, such as lagged or information-theoretic measures, and, where a signed quantity is available, as in lagged cross-correlation of autonomic series, alternation between positive and negative dependence (Table 3). Most INS estimators are non-negative, so sign alternation is not defined for them. A baseline rate of desynchronisation must be established before any excess can be identified as maladaptive (Section 4.4).

Our account bears most directly on the mismatches identified in Section 2.4, since the metabolic accounting is settled at the junction of the two systems, and these are the cases on which the dyadic literature is close to silent. Where a fully activated attachment system meets an unresponsive or depleted caregiving system, we expect a dissociation rather than a uniform reduction: sustained or escalating signalling from the demand side against a supply side that is not investing. This should present as synchrony that fails to consolidate, rather than as synchrony that is simply low, and should be more readily detectable in its direction than in its magnitude. The divergent parasympathetic pattern under maltreatment severity (Brown et al., 2021) and the dependence of the driving partner on maltreatment subtype (Lunkenheimer et al., 2018), both described in Section 4.2, approximate it. Both observations are autonomic and were made in samples not assessed for attachment. Where the same figure is both the target of the attachment system and the source of the threat activating it, both systems are engaged and neither can complete, and we expect synchrony that neither stabilises nor releases.

Trauma may offer the most informative evidence on flexibility, since the levels of BBS can dissociate within the same dyads. In a cohort of war-exposed children and their mothers, dyads meeting criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder showed the tightest autonomic and the lowest behavioural synchrony, whereas resilient dyads showed the highest behavioural and the lowest autonomic synchrony. Only among the resilient dyads did moments of behavioural synchrony coincide with increases in the child's parasympathetic tone (Motsan et al., 2021). We interpret this as the developmental transfer of regulatory labour described in Section 3.2 being visible between brains and bodies. It is the one design we have drawn upon in which the coregulatory outcome is estimated at the level of the moment rather than the session, which is the form in which the regime predictions above would need to be tested.

On the individual-level account of trauma, over-precise prior aversions confine the system to a narrow region of the free-energy landscape (Kotler et al., 2026; Linson et al., 2020).

The same over-precision should then be visible between brains as synchrony that cannot be released, making flexibility rather than magnitude the candidate marker of resilient functioning.

Consistent with this, the association between subsequent traumatic exposure and posttraumatic, depressive and general psychiatric symptoms was weaker in individuals who had earlier shown greater INS with an unfamiliar partner, a moderation that survived control for participants' general perceived interpersonal closeness to significant others (Mayo et al., 2025).

The propensity to align with a new partner is not attachment security, and the design cannot establish causation, but the direction is what a social allostatic account would anticipate: an individual whose model readily aligns with a new partner has a wider social niche across which regulatory load can be distributed (Coan and Sbarra, 2015; Saxbe et al., 2020).

The dyadic evidence therefore neither confirms the individual account developed in Section 3 nor counts against it. It leaves a substantial area unexamined, comprising the mismatch between the two systems at the neural level, the metabolic quantity itself, and the group level. Because synchrony has proved unstable across occasions (Section 4.4), none of these expectations can be tested adequately at a single measurement. Section 6 sets out what the requisite designs would need to do.

5. Clinical and practical implications

>Clinical and practical implications

5.1 Attachment assessment and change

Our account has consequences for how attachment is assessed and for how attachment-related change is understood. We state five. Following Sections 2.4 and 4.1, we state them separately for the attachment and the caregiving system wherever the two diverge, since the same dyadic observation carries different implications depending on which system it characterises.

Safety is prior to co-regulation.

If synchrony indexes, as we hypothesise, where predictive and regulatory work is being invested, rather than the state a dyad is coordinating around (Section 4.2), a clinically important consequence follows immediately: a dyad may be tightly coordinated around the anticipation of aggression, and coordination of that kind is an accrual of load rather than its distribution. An intervention that increases proximity, contingency or synchrony is therefore not therapeutic in itself, and where the relationship is a source of threat it may entrench the coordination it is intended to improve. Felt security is a constructed inference that ordinarily tracks the actual state of the world, and it cannot be reinstated while a genuine threat from within the relationship persists, because the model correctly predicts a shortfall between demand and available resources. Where adversity is current rather than historical, the first requirement is a change in the conditions themselves, and an intervention directed at the felt state alone is misdirected. Assessment should, accordingly, establish whether a relationship is safe enough to serve as a regulatory resource before it asks how well the pair co-regulate, and dyadic work is contraindicated where it would increase danger or extend a coercive partner's control.

Change. Because the precision regimes and the internal working models (IWMs) that weight them are learned, they remain open to revision whenever incoming evidence is granted sufficient precision to update them (Section 3.3). Earned security (Roisman et al., 2002) and therapeutic improvement (Taylor et al., 2015) are, on this reading, instances of model updating rather than of a fixed disposition overcome. The therapeutic relationship is a source of coregulation that supplies the correction the person's own model cannot yet generate, returning the loop to a regime in which evidence is again granted the precision to revise the prior (Coan and Sbarra, 2015; Saxbe et al., 2020). This places part of the target of treatment in the dynamics of the interaction itself. Second-person neuropsychiatry suggests that psychiatric disorders may be construed as disorders of social interaction, and that a focus on the dynamics of interaction may support the transdiagnostic investigation of their neurobiology (Schilbach, 2016). The dialectical misattunement hypothesis likewise locates part of psychopathology in the mismatch between interacting partners (Bolis et al., 2017). The therapeutic relationship is a setting in which such a mismatch can be observed and, within limits, revised.

The clearest evidence that such revision can be induced rather than merely observed, comes from randomised trials on the caregiving side. Children at risk of neglect whose caregivers received an intervention targeting nurturance, following the child's lead and the reduction of frightening behaviour showed more normative diurnal cortisol rhythms than children in an active control condition (Bernard, Dozier, et al., 2015). The difference was still present in the preschool years (Bernard, Hostinar, et al., 2015), and in middle childhood it was statistically mediated by observed parental sensitivity (Garnett et al., 2020). In maltreated infants, preventive interventions were followed by the absence of the progressive decline in morning cortisol observed under community-standard care (Cicchetti et al., 2011). We take these as the strongest available evidence bearing on the mechanism our account proposes, since what is manipulated is caregiver behaviour and what changes is an allostatic parameter in the other body. The mechanism itself, including our reading of the change in sensitivity as a change in caregiving-side precision, remains open.

Four qualifications attach to this. The first is that revision does not require erasure. On the reading set out in Section 2.4, a multistable system holds a newer and context-appropriate model as an attractor alongside the older insecure one, rather than replacing it, and under stress, the basin of the older and more precisely held model deepens and activity is captured by it. The re-emergence of an earlier pattern under load is therefore what our account predicts, and not evidence that change did not occur. Treatment can be asked to secure the preservation of flexibility under demand rather than the removal of the older model (Bosmans et al., 2020 (Bosmans et al., , 2022)).

The second is that the supply side is itself a variable. The therapist, and the caregiver in a parenting intervention, occupies the caregiving side, and the caregiving system has its own precision regime and its own budget. Parenting stress and depression have been associated with reduced synchrony and maternal anxiety with elevated synchrony (Smith et al., 2022; Sfeir et al., 2026), and our account treats these as opposite states rather than as two points on one scale (Section 4.4). They accordingly call for opposite first moves, replenishment of a depleted supply side in the one case and recalibration of an over-assigned precision in the other.

The third is that a prior held with a precision that resists correction may discount evidence of availability rather than incorporate it, so that the rate at which a relationship can be granted evidential weight is part of what treatment must address. Early adversity has been reported to impair the capacity to use familiar others as buffers later in development (Gunnar, 2017; Hostinar & Gunnar, 2013), so that the regulatory resource a treatment offers may not initially be usable by the person to whom it is offered, and its usability is itself among the things treatment must build.

The fourth is that the target is calibration and not maximisation. The calibrated regime of Section 3.3 grants a partner's signals precision proportional to their informativeness and relinquishes investment once the correction has been delivered, and the optimum we expect lies in the midrange rather than at the maximum (Section 4.4). What treatment should aim at is flexible movement between regulation performed with another and regulation performed alone, these being on the reading of Section 3.2 the same computation differing in where it is carried out.

The two disruption routes. If the distinction drawn in Section 3.4 holds, it identifies distinct treatment targets rather than degrees of a single severity. Where the caregiver is present but is itself the source of threat, the maltreatment route installs a standing, high-precision prediction of threat that no policy discharges. Its clinical problem is a danger prior insulated from correction, and the corresponding requirement is the re-establishment of conditions under which disconfirming evidence can revise the model rather than be assimilated to it (White et al., 2020; Kotler et al., 2026). Where the caregiver is absent, the neglect route installs a a confidently held expectation that no action will resolve the deviation, together with a collapse of policy precision. Its clinical problem is disengagement rather than defence, and the corresponding requirement is the restoration of an expectation of efficacy (Section 3.4).

Because disorganised attachment is neither a sensitive nor a specific marker of maltreatment (Granqvist et al., 2017), neither route can be inferred from a presentation, and the caregiving history is required for either judgement.

A caution is attached to both routes. Where the attachment system has been organised around a figure that was itself threatening, proximity may reactivate the prediction rather than revise it. Predictable boundaries, titrated contact and the person's own control over proximity are therefore conditions under which the evidence a relationship offers can be granted precision at all. Our framework we have set out is a theoretical proposal, and social allostasis is not a diagnostic category. The synchrony measures discussed above are research instruments whose relation to the latent quantities our account posits has not been established (Section 4.4), their values depend upon task, timing, age, analytic method and modality, and no threshold exists by which an individual dyad may be classified. The experimental evidence is confined to a small number of caregiving intervention trials, and normalisation of a physiological index is not itself evidence of improved safety, functioning or relational quality. We accordingly offer the foregoing as a framework for formulation and as a specification of what would be worth measuring, rather than as a basis for present clinical decision-making about individuals.

Three persistent misconceptions. Three claims remain prominent in attachment-and trauma-informed practice that our framework does not support, although each rests on a clinical observation worth retaining. The first is that attachment and therapeutic change are organised in the right hemisphere (Schore, 2022). Some perceptual operations are right-lateralised (Palomero-Gallagher & Amunts, 2022), but lateralisation is a local, connection-specific property of brain networks without a whole-brain left-or right-brained phenotype (Nielsen et al., 2013), and the networks on which our account relies are distributed and largely bilateral (J. Zhang et al., 2025). The second is the triune brain schema, on which attachment and trauma reside in an ancient reptilian layer beneath a newer cortex, a hierarchy that does not correspond to how nervous systems evolved (Cesario et al., 2020). The third is the literal reading of the claim that the body keeps the score (van der Kolk, 2014). The body is central to the traumatic process, but the storage metaphor mislocates the mechanism. On a predictive account, what endures is a set of danger priors held with a precision that resists correction, expressed in the body through altered autonomic and endocrine regulation (Section 3.4), plausibly stabilised in part by epigenetic regulation of stress-responsive genes such as the glucocorticoid receptor (Turecki & Meaney, 2016; Weaver et al., 2004), and open to revision (Kotler et al., 2026). The clinical intuitions behind all three claims are retained by the account we have set out.

6. Open questions and future directions

The core of our account, namely the reading of the four NAMA modules as components of one predictive allostatic loop and of the organised styles as precision regimes over that loop, is a specification at the level of computational function, stated in Box 2 so that it can be implemented. It retains our earlier work's anatomical and neurochemical claims intact and adds a unifying principle from which their arrangement can be derived (Long et al., 2020; White et al., 2020; Izaki et al., 2024). Its value is accordingly that of an extended framework that generates predictions rather than that of an established mechanism.

The evidential status is not uniform across the account. The single mapping is consistent with the anatomy of the allostatic interoceptive system and with the simulation of the organised styles as parameter settings of a common loop (Cittern et al., 2018; J. Zhang et al., 2025). The projection of the precision regimes onto the dyad, and the mapping of particular regimes onto particular synchrony signatures, are stated as expectations to be tested and not as findings the literature currently supports (Sections 4.4 and 4.5). The attachment-specific evidence is preliminary, the direction of the reported associations is not fixed, and no design has yet separated the three explanations of the divergence offered in Section 4.4.

Ten limitations are illustrated in Table 4. Summarised very briefly here, these limitations concern (1) the measurement of attachment separately from interpersonal closeness (Section 4.3); (2) the separation of the attachment and caregiving systems, which most designs do not achieve, so that the available attachment-related synchrony evidence is predominantly caregiving side (Section 4.3); (3) the symmetry of the estimators in general use (Section 4.2); (4) the absence, in much of the literature, of controls for common input and task structure (Section 4.2); (5) the metabolic quantity, which has been measured only for the individual and not as a function of attachment or caregiving regime (Sections 2.1 and 4.4); (6) the concentration of hyperscanning on dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the temporo-parietal junction (Section 4.4); (7) the status of metastability as a proposed rather than an established marker (Kotler et al., 2026); (8) the correlational character of most designs (Moreau & Dumas, 2021; Novembre & Iannetti, 2021); (9) the instability of synchrony across occasions, which almost every study has measured once (Wilson et al., 2018); and (10) the modelling of the relation between synchrony and regulatory outcome as monotonic (Section 4.4).

These limitations define a measurement programme, for which we set out the following priorities (see Table 4). The first is to move the outcome variable from magnitude to dynamics, quantifying the depth of desynchronisation and the latency of resynchronisation, and characterising rupture and repair sequences with the flexibility and metastability metrics developed for dyadic coordination (Mayo and Gordon, 2020; Gordon et al., 2025). The quantity has already proved informative where it has been measured (Müller et al., 2022; Section 4.4).

But a rate of departure from alignment cannot be interpreted without a baseline, since brief desynchronisations arise in well-functioning complementary dyads as a consequence of the partners' asymmetric access to the state being regulated and not as a failure of the pair (Tsubamoto and Horii, 2026; Section 4.4).

The second follows from the instability noted above and is a precondition for most of what follows. Designs should sample repeatedly within dyads, across occasions and across levels and kinds of demand, rather than estimating synchrony once. Models should also be specified to allow non-monotonic relations, declared in advance rather than fitted after a null linear result, and should separate within-dyad from between-dyad variation. And because the reliable detection of curvilinearity requires samples appreciably larger than are usual in this literature, the design that is most likely to succeed is intensive repeated measurement of fewer dyads rather than single assessment of more (Moffat & Cross, 2026).

The third is to pair hyperscanning and BBS assessment with validated interview, observational or questionnaire measures of attachment rather than with proxies for closeness, so that the attachment dimension and the closeness gradient can be estimated independently, as the more recent studies have begun to do.

The fourth follows from the two-system extension and is, in our view, the most consequential. Designs should fix the two attachment and caregiving roles for the duration of a measurement, assess the attachment and caregiving system of both partners rather than one of them, and apply directed estimators so that the asymmetry of investment becomes an observable rather than an assumption. Every design should include surrogate or pseudo-dyad controls and should record gaze, speech and/or movement, so that dependence arising from common input and shared task structure can be estimated and separated from dependence arising from the interaction itself (Section 4.2). Designs should also assess the caregiving system with caregiving strategy measures rather than through the caregiver's attachment orientation (Colledani et al., 2022). For high-risk or clinical dyads, a further requirement is interpretive as well as ethical. Because synchrony may reflect coordination around threat as readily as coordination towards its relief (Section 4.2), safety, coercion and power asymmetry within the relationship must be assessed directly rather than inferred from the synchrony itself, and a measured value obtained without them is not interpretable in the terms our account proposes.

The fifth is to impose graded regulatory demand, through stress induction, acute pain, or cooperative problem solving, and to manipulate the availability of co-regulation directly, so that synchrony can be tied to a regulatory outcome and the prediction that co-regulation lowers the cost of achieving it can be placed at risk. Demand should be varied in kind as well as in degree, since synchrony rises under conflictual and competitive demand as it does under cooperative demand (Section 4.2), and a design that varies intensity alone cannot distinguish the two.

The sixth is to index the metabolic quantity itself. We define it operationally as the timeintegrated deviation of an autonomic, endocrine or metabolic signal from its baseline until a criterion return, i.e., the incremental area under the curve, under a challenge held constant across conditions. This quantity is measurable and independent of any synchrony estimate, and it has been used to show that a close partner's presence lowers the cost of standard homeostatic challenges (Masalha et al., 2026). Our account adds requirements to the design around it. Both partners should be measured with their roles fixed, so that the recipient's cost, the caregiver's cost and their sum can be computed separately. Attachment and caregiving regimes should be assessed in both partners. And the comparison condition should remove the partner's regulatory contribution without imposing a separation, since separation in infancy is itself a demand.

Because the modalities are not interchangeable (Reindl et al., 2022), they should be modelled jointly rather than treated as substitutable indices of one dyadic state.

The seventh is to adopt longitudinal designs, in which earned security and therapeutic change are operationalised as measurable shifts in prior precision, in which the persistence of an older model alongside a newer one is tested by re-assessment under load rather than at rest, and in which the two disruption routes are followed as dissociable trajectories rather than inferred from a single presentation. Randomised caregiving interventions already manipulate the caregiving side and measure allostatic outcomes (Bernard, Dozier, et al., 2015; Bernard, Hostinar, et al., 2015; Cicchetti et al., 2011; Garnett et al., 2020; Section 5.1), and the addition to them that our framework calls for is the measurement of mechanism alongside outcome, so that a change in the recipient's allostatic parameters can be referred to an estimated change in the caregiver's precision regime rather than to the intervention as a whole.

And the eighth and last concerns the unit of analysis. We have moved the account from one brain to two, and the dyad is where measurement is presently feasible. But an individual's attachment consolidates across a network of ties rather than a single relationship. Moving to the dyad required two extensions. We propose that the partner in an interacting pair is ordinarily not running a second copy of the attachment loop, but the complementary and asymmetric caregiving system, which is activated by another's signalled or anticipated distress and terminates in the alleviation of that distress. Therefore, what is distributed across a dyad is regulatory work, although the two partners do not perform the same work, and the metabolic accounting is settled at the junction of the two systems.

Our proposal belongs to the line of work that moved second-person neuroscience towards computational models of reciprocal interaction (Bolis et al., 2017; Lehmann et al., 2024), and joins a small but rapidly growing set of accounts that treat the caregiving dyad in predictive and regulatory terms (Cittern et al., 2018; Diaz-Rojas et al., 2025; Santaguida et al., 2026; Tottenham and Vannucci, 2025; Tsubamoto and Horii, 2026). It differs from the closest of these, the developmental account of attachment as prediction (Tottenham and Vannucci, 2025), in the respects set out in Section 2.4, and it extends to disruption and disorganisation, which none of the adjacent accounts treats. The rationale for adopting our framework is integrative. It does not displace the anatomy, the neurochemistry, or the dyadic measurement of our earlier work, but supplies a single regulatory principle from which their arrangement follows, and against which organised, disrupted, and disorganised attachment can be expressed as settings of a common loop rather than as separate descriptions.
