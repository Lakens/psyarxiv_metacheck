Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Non-motor symptoms (NMS) affect nearly all patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) and related movement disorders, with a mean of roughly 8 to 10 discrete symptoms per individual. Their effect on quality of life often exceeds that of motor features, yet NMS remain under-recognized and undertreated, partly because patients and caregivers lack a working model of why these symptoms arise in a "movement" disease. This chapter applies neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE) to that gap and positions it relative to conventional psychoeducation, health communication, and behavioral science.

We propose the Map-Normalize-Differentiate-Navigate (MNDN) workflow as a movement-disordersspecific implementation of the NIPE framework described in Chapter 1 of this volume. MNDN is a clinical proposal, not a validated protocol: its components are specified so that it can be tested, but its effects on patient outcomes have not been evaluated. Drawing on the tripartite cortico-basal gangliathalamocortical loop model and multi-transmitter degeneration, we provide modules for seven high-yield NMS: apathy, anxiety, executive dysfunction, REM sleep behavior disorder, neurogenic orthostatic hypotension, psychosis, and fatigue. Each module summarizes the neural systems implicated, at the level of certainty the evidence supports, together with common misattributions, reversible contributors that must be excluded, management options and their limits, and patient and caregiver handout content. We describe delivery formats from 3-5 minute "micro-NIPE" sessions in routine visits to group and telehealth programs, specify the minimum components, provider competencies, dose, fidelity criteria, and proximal outcomes needed to test MNDN, and examine the ethical risks of neurobiological framing (determinism, oversimplification, over-attribution, and false reassurance), with particular attention to prognostic counseling in REM sleep behavior disorder. NIPE in movement disorders is designed to reduce self-blame, support accurate symptom attribution, and improve collaborative management.

Whether it does so is an empirical question that this chapter aims to make answerable.

1. CLINICAL RATIONALE AND LEARNING OBJECTIVES

>CLINICAL RATIONALE AND LEARNING OBJECTIVES

1.1 Why Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation in Movement Disorders?

Movement disorders, Parkinson's disease (PD) foremost among them, are conventionally taught as motor syndromes. Yet nearly all patients report at least one non-motor symptom (NMS; 98.6% in the PRIAMO study), with a mean of roughly 8 to 10 discrete NMS per individual. 1, 2 Depression, anxiety, apathy, cognitive impairment, sleep disturbance, autonomic dysfunction, fatigue, and psychosis often affect quality of life more than the motor features that dominate the clinical encounter. NMS nonetheless remain under-recognized and undertreated. 2 We suggest two reasons: patients and caregivers have no working model of why these symptoms appear in a "movement" disease, and clinicians lack a structured way to explain them in the time a visit allows.

Neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE) offers one response to this gap. As defined in Chapter 1 of this volume, NIPE differs from conventional psychoeducation on two dimensions: content that centers on brain-based explanations of symptoms, treatment response, and recovery, and a delivery structure designed around how the brain learns. 3, 4 This chapter adapts NIPE to movement disorders and proposes the Map-Normalize-Differentiate-Navigate (MNDN) workflow as a movement-disorders-specific implementation of that framework rather than a separate model. MNDN is presented as a clinical proposal whose components are specified well enough to be tested (Section 6.2); its effects on patient outcomes have not been evaluated. The workflow is intended for neurologists, advanced practice providers, psychologists, nurses, and social workers, in formats from brief bedside sessions to group telehealth programs.

1.2 Theoretical Foundations: Psychoeducation, Health Communication, and What Neuroscience Adds

Psychoeducation has a substantial evidence base outside neurology. In schizophrenia, structured programs combining illness information, skills training, problem-solving, and family involvement reduce relapse and rehospitalization and improve adherence, and psychoeducation is classed as an evidence-based practice in its own right. 5, 6 Its active ingredients are usually described as information transfer, emotional processing, skill acquisition, and support, delivered within a collaborative relationship rather than as a lecture. 5 Psychoeducation programs for PD patients and carers follow the same pattern and have been implemented and evaluated in primary care; in the completed controlled evaluation, psychoeducation and education arms both improved, with no between-group difference. 7, 8 Behavioral science explains why information changes behavior only under particular conditions, and each condition maps onto a step of MNDN. The Common-Sense Model of self-regulation holds that patients build their own representation of an illness (identity, cause, timeline, consequences, controllability) and act on that representation rather than on the clinician's; education that does not engage the patient's existing model is unlikely to displace it. 9 Self-efficacy theory predicts that beliefs about one's capacity to act, not knowledge alone, sustain self-management. 10 Cognitive load theory and the principles derived from it (one message per encounter, chunking, visual support, spaced repetition, teach-back) limit how much can be delivered per visit. [11] [12] [13] Motivational interviewing supplies the elicit-provide-elicit sequence (ask-offer-ask in the fourth edition), in which information is offered with permission, in the patient's frame, and followed by eliciting their reaction, 14 and shared decision-making models describe how options and preferences are integrated once the problem is understood. 15 What does neuroscience add? Chapter 1 answers at three levels. 3 Content: NIPE explains symptoms at neurochemical, circuit, and cognitive-functional levels, links each treatment to a mechanism, and includes brain strategies and self-monitoring alongside facts. Structure: content is selected and delivered to be salient, trustworthy, dosed to avoid overload, repeated, motivating, multimodal, and interactive.

Mechanism: brain-based framing is proposed to work by improving mechanism-informed self-awareness, reappraising the emotional meaning of a symptom (from personal failing to circuit dysfunction), reducing internalized stigma, and supporting agency through explicit links between understanding and action.

Chapter 1 also sets the boundary condition that governs this chapter: NIPE operates within the biopsychosocial model and is not a reductionist alternative to it. 3, 16 The most tested precedent in a neurological population is pain neuroscience education, in which reconceptualizing chronic pain as a nervous-system phenomenon rather than tissue damage produces modest but consistent reductions in pain, disability, and catastrophizing when combined with active treatment. 17, 18 That literature also shows the limits: education alone rarely changes outcomes, effects depend on delivery quality, and brain-based framing carries risks of its own. Biogenetic explanations of psychiatric symptoms reliably reduce blame but can increase prognostic pessimism and the belief that symptoms are fixed, and explanations that mention the brain are judged more credible than they deserve even when they add nothing. [19] [20] [21] [22] These findings shape the design choices in this chapter: explanations are stated as associations, paired with what can be modified, and revised as the differential diagnosis proceeds (Section 7.2).

2. NON-MOTOR SYMPTOM BURDEN AND MEASUREMENT-BASED CARE

>NON-MOTOR SYMPTOM BURDEN AND MEASUREMENT-BASED CARE

2.1 Epidemiology of Non-Motor Symptoms

The range of NMS in PD is wide. A large clinic-based cohort study using the MDS-UPDRS Part I identifies cognitive impairment (74.3%), nighttime sleep problems (71.6%), urinary dysfunction (71.6%), fatigue (68.7%), pain (64.2%), daytime somnolence (61.9%), and depressed mood (60.8%) as the most frequently reported NMS. 23 Hallucinations (13.8%) and impulse control disorders (7.7%) are less common early in the disease but carry disproportionate clinical weight.

NMS burden increases with disease duration, cognitive impairment, levodopa dose, and the postural instability/gait disorder (PIGD) motor subtype. 23 Gastrointestinal dysfunction, neuropsychiatric disturbances, and sleep disorders may predate motor onset by years, which is one reason PD is now regarded as a multisystem synucleinopathy from its inception. 24 As a group, NMS affect health-related quality of life more than motor severity does. 25

3. TRANSLATIONAL CIRCUIT ATLAS FOR NON-MOTOR SYMPTOMS

>TRANSLATIONAL CIRCUIT ATLAS FOR NON-MOTOR SYMPTOMS

3.1 The Tripartite Cortico-Basal Ganglia-Thalamocortical Model

The classical Alexander/DeLong model describes three functionally segregated, parallel cortico-basal ganglia-thalamocortical (CBGTC) loops: 30, 31 1. Motor loop: Supplementary motor area (SMA) ↔ putamen ↔ globus pallidus internus/substantia nigra pars reticulata (GPi/SNr) ↔ ventrolateral (VL) thalamus → SMA. This loop governs voluntary movement and is the primary target of levodopa therapy and deep brain stimulation.

2. Associative (cognitive) loop: Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) ↔ dorsal caudate ↔

GPi/SNr ↔ ventral anterior/mediodorsal (VA/MD) thalamus → DLPFC. This loop supports working memory, cognitive flexibility, planning, and set-shifting. 31 3. Limbic loop: Orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) ↔ ventral striatum (nucleus accumbens) ↔ ventral pallidum/medial SNr ↔ MD thalamus → OFC/ACC. This loop processes reward valuation, motivation, emotional regulation, and social cognition. 30 Although traditionally drawn as closed and parallel, later work describes cross-talk between loops, including a ventral striatum to medial substantia nigra to motor thalamus route through which limbic activity can influence motor output, demonstrated by tract-tracing and circuit manipulation in animals. 32, 33 This open pathway offers a plausible, though not established in humans, substrate for clinical observations such as worse motor performance during severe apathy or tremor amplification under anxiety.

3.2 Dopamine Depletion Gradient and Sequential Circuit Dysfunction

In PD, dopamine loss follows a characteristic dorsal-to-ventral gradient within the striatum: the putamen is depleted earliest and most severely, followed by the dorsal caudate, and finally the ventral striatum.

One proposed staging model (PDFCD) maps this gradient onto a sequence of cognitive-behavioral change: executive dysfunction and mental fatigue first (Stage I; DLPFC-dorsal caudate), then depression and anxiety (Stage IIa; OFC-ventral caudate) and apathy (Stage IIb; ACC-nucleus accumbens), with dementia (Stage III) when cortical dopamine projections themselves degenerate. 34 The sequence describes group tendencies. Individual patients depart from it, and cholinergic, serotonergic, and noradrenergic loss, cortical Lewy pathology, medication, and comorbidity alter the picture at every stage.

3.3 Multisystem Neuromodulators Beyond Dopamine

Although dopamine loss dominates the PD narrative, NMS have been linked to degeneration across several neurotransmitter systems: • Serotonin (raphe nuclei): associated with depression, fatigue, and PD psychosis (5-HT₂A receptor upregulation is one proposed mechanism) [35] [36] [37] • Norepinephrine (locus coeruleus): associated with anxiety, autonomic failure, and attentional dysfunction 38, 39 • Acetylcholine (nucleus basalis of Meynert, pedunculopontine nucleus): associated with cognitive decline, hallucinations, and REM sleep behavior disorder 40 • GABA/glycine (brainstem reticular formation): implicated in REM sleep behavior disorder through failure of REM atonia circuits 41 Explaining this multi-transmitter reality to patients is foundational to NIPE: it normalizes NMS ("these symptoms come from the same disease process") and explains why motor medications alone may not resolve every complaint.

Two caveats govern how the atlas is used in this chapter. First, its associations are population-level: an imaging difference between apathetic and non-apathetic groups does not establish the mechanism in a given patient. Second, most NMS are multiply determined, by disease stage, medication state, sleep, mood, comorbid illness, psychological adjustment, and social circumstance, in proportions that differ between patients. The atlas is therefore used to generate candidate explanations that the Differentiate step tests, not to assign each symptom to a circuit.

4.2 The MNDN Workflow

The result is a practical clinical workflow (Figure 1). Each step below states its purpose, a sample script, and the error it is designed to prevent. Step 2 -NORMALIZE Purpose: remove blame. Normalization has two targets, the experience (common, recognized, not a character flaw) and the attribution (a symptom of the illness rather than of the person): "Roughly four in ten people with Parkinson's have this. It is not laziness and it is not giving up." Normalizing the experience does not require asserting that this patient's symptom is disease-intrinsic; that question stays open until Step 3.

Reduced self-blame and caregiver frustration are the proximal targets. 43, 44 The error prevented is moral attribution by the patient or family.

Step 3 -DIFFERENTIATE

Purpose: test the population-level association against the individual. The clinician names the alternatives aloud, because doing so is itself psychoeducation: it shows the patient that "Parkinson's" is a hypothesis rather than a verdict. Alternatives fall into five groups: medication effects (impulse control disorder from dopamine agonists, sedation from anticholinergics or benzodiazepines, wearing-off), comorbid medical conditions (hypothyroidism, anemia, sleep apnea, B12 deficiency), psychiatric comorbidity (a preexisting anxiety or depressive disorder), psychological and social contributors (grief over the diagnosis, role loss, caregiver strain, financial pressure), and reversible precipitants (infection, dehydration, sleep loss). The step ends with a stated working formulation and, where indicated, tests. The error prevented is over-attribution, in which every new symptom is assigned to PD.

Step 4 -NAVIGATE

Purpose: convert the formulation into a plan the patient has chosen. Using elicit-provide-elicit, the clinician asks what the patient already knows or wants, provides options with their mechanism and limits, and elicits a response before agreeing on a first step: "Given what we have discussed, what feels like the most important first step to you?" 14, 15 The plan may include medication adjustment, behavioral strategies, referral, and a defined follow-up at which the explanation is revisited and revised if the response is not as expected.

An explanation that cannot be revised is the deterministic framing this chapter tries to avoid.

The error prevented is a plan the patient did not choose and will not follow.

A Note on Metaphor

Metaphors appear only in the patient-facing handouts, at most one per module, introduced as a simplification ("one way to picture this"). The clinician-facing Circuit Explanation in each module states the scientific account separately so that teaching device and evidence are not conflated. Metaphors are memorable, but they also shape inference: an amygdala "alarm" implies a single locus for anxiety, which the evidence does not support, and a "CEO" prefrontal cortex implies a hierarchy the anatomy does not show. 45, 46 The handouts retain two labeled metaphors (an alarm system that is easier to set off, for anxiety; a brake on movement during dreams that no longer holds, for RBD) and drop the rest.

MODULE I: APATHY, MOTIVATION, AND REWARD CIRCUITRY

Circuit Explanation

Apathy in PD has been associated most consistently with dysfunction of the mesocorticolimbic dopamine system (ventral tegmental area to nucleus accumbens, anterior cingulate, and orbitofrontal cortex), with additional serotonergic involvement. In this model the ACC-accumbens link integrates effort cost with expected reward; when its dopaminergic modulation falls, patients find it harder to mobilize effort toward goals they still value. Imaging studies report ventral striatal and ACC gray-matter loss and blunted reward sensitivity (for example, reduced pupillary dilation to incentive cues) in apathetic compared with nonapathetic patients, and dopaminergic medication partly restores reward sensitivity in the ON state without abolishing the deficit. [47] [48] [49] [50] These are group-level differences. Apathy is heterogeneous: it overlaps with depression and cognitive impairment, varies with dopaminergic state, can appear after abrupt dopaminergic reduction (for example after subthalamic DBS), and is shaped by mood, cognition, and the social environment. MODULE IV: SLEEP DISTURBANCE AND REM SLEEP BEHAVIOR DISORDER

Circuit Explanation

REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) arises from loss of the brainstem circuit that produces REM atonia.

Glutamatergic neurons of the sublaterodorsal tegmental nucleus (SLD, subcoeruleus) excite GABA/glycinergic neurons in the ventral medulla and spinal cord that inhibit motor neurons during REM sleep; alpha-synuclein pathology and loss of specific neuronal populations (including Crhbp-expressing neurons) in the SLD have been documented in PD patients with RBD, and lesions of these nuclei reproduce the phenotype in animals. 41, 61 This is among the better-established circuit accounts in this chapter. MODULE V: NEUROGENIC ORTHOSTATIC HYPOTENSION

Circuit Explanation

Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (nOH) in PD results from alpha-synuclein-mediated degeneration of peripheral sympathetic (postganglionic noradrenergic) neurons and, to a variable and less wellcharacterized extent, central autonomic pathways. Cardiac sympathetic denervation is a hallmark of PD and distinguishes it from MSA, where preganglionic loss predominates. 68 The result is failure to release norepinephrine on standing, inadequate vasoconstriction, and a fall of ≥20 mmHg in systolic or ≥10 mmHg in diastolic blood pressure within 3 minutes of standing. 69, 70 The peripheral mechanism is well established; in practice, medication contributions are so common that nOH is best regarded as intrinsic denervation plus whatever the drug list adds.

• Medication dizziness (anti-PD medications can worsen orthostatic hypotension, but nOH is often intrinsic) • Dehydration • Anxiety-related lightheadedness • Vestibular disorder • Medication-induced hypotension (antihypertensives, dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors) • Dehydration • Cardiac arrhythmia • Adrenal insufficiency • Postprandial hypotension

Non-pharmacologic measures first: • Liberalize salt and fluid intake • Elevate the head of the bed 10-15° • Rise slowly from supine positions • Avoid large carbohydrate-heavy meals Pharmacologic options: • Droxidopa (synthetic norepinephrine precursor; FDA-approved for nOH; 100-600 mg three times daily) improves symptoms, standing systolic pressure, and daily activities 70 • Fludrocortisone (0.05-0.2 mg/day; volume expansion via renal sodium retention; avoid in heart failure) 70 • Midodrine (alpha-1 agonist) • All pressor agents carry a risk of supine hypertension, which must be monitored 70

What is neurogenic orthostatic hypotension?

When you stand up, your body normally tightens blood vessels to keep blood flowing to your brain. In Parkinson's, the nerves that control this reflex can be damaged, causing a blood pressure drop when standing. This leads to lightheadedness, dizziness, or fainting. Some medicines add to the problem, so your doctor will review your full list.

Practical strategies:

MODULE VI: PARKINSON'S DISEASE PSYCHOSIS

Circuit Explanation

PD psychosis (PDP), which encompasses visual hallucinations, illusions, sense of presence, paranoid ideation, and delusions, has several partly overlapping explanatory models rather than one. The most influential pharmacological model treats it as a serotonin-dopamine imbalance: cortical Lewy pathology is proposed to drive compensatory upregulation of 5-HT₂A receptors in temporal and ventral visual cortex, and excessive 5-HT₂A signaling is hypothesized to generate visual hallucinations and, through enhanced ventral striatal dopamine activity, delusions. The model draws support from the 5-HT₂A agonism of serotonergic psychedelics and from the efficacy of the 5-HT₂A inverse agonist pimavanserin. 35, 37, 71 Other models emphasize cholinergic deficit (hallucinations improve with cholinesterase inhibitors and worsen with anticholinergics), impaired bottom-up visual processing with over-reliance on top-down expectation, attentional network dysfunction, and intrusion of REM phenomena into wakefulness. 40, 72 Dopaminergic medication lowers the threshold, but psychosis also occurs at low medication exposure, and cognitive impairment, visual loss, sleep disturbance, and age are independent risk factors. The accurate summary for patients is that several brain systems are involved, medications interact with them, and treatable precipitants are common.

Common Misattributions

• "Going crazy"

• Dementia (PDP can occur with preserved cognition) • Medication intolerance alone (medications commonly unmask or worsen PDP, but disease-related changes usually contribute) • Medication-induced hallucinations (anticholinergics, amantadine, dopamine agonists; simplify first) • Delirium (from infection, metabolic derangement) • Charles Bonnet syndrome (visual loss-related hallucinations with preserved insight) • DLB (if dementia is prominent) • Psychiatric comorbidity (late-onset schizophrenia, bipolar disorder)

MODULE VII: FATIGUE

Circuit Explanation

Fatigue is among the most common NMS (68.7% in the MDS-UPDRS cohort above; pooled prevalence of about 50% across studies) and among the least understood. 75, 76 It is multifactorial. In a small PET study, fatigued patients showed markedly lower serotonin transporter binding in putamen, caudate, ventral striatum, thalamus, cingulate, and amygdala than non-fatigued patients, while striatal ¹⁸F-dopa uptake did not differ, which argues against striatal dopamine deficiency as the sole explanation. 36 Other reported associations include elevated CSF inflammatory markers, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysregulation, cardiac sympathetic denervation, and, in network-mapping work, involvement of somatomotor and frontoparietal networks with transcriptomic enrichment in synaptic and cytoskeletal processes. 77, 78 None of these findings has been replicated at a scale that would support a single mechanism, and fatigue in an individual patient often reflects depression, a sleep disorder, deconditioning, medication, or medical illness as much as disease-intrinsic change.

What is Parkinson's fatigue?

Fatigue in Parkinson's is a persistent, overwhelming tiredness that is out of proportion to physical effort and is not relieved by rest. It is a real symptom of the disease, linked to changes in several brain chemical systems, and it is not a sign of being out of shape or lazy. 6.1 Micro-NIPE in Clinical Practice

The most scalable delivery model is the 3-5 minute "micro-NIPE" embedded in a routine clinic encounter. After reviewing NMS screening scores, the clinician selects one symptom module, by the patient's priority rather than the clinician's, and walks the patient through the MNDN workflow using a laminated reference card or tablet-based visual aid. A printed handout is provided at checkout. Over successive visits, modules accumulate and build the patient's and caregiver's understanding of NMS as manifestations of the disease and of the other factors that shape them.

Practical tips for micro-NIPE: • Prepare materials in advance (laminated circuit diagrams, pre-printed handouts) • Select one NMS per visit to avoid overwhelming the patient • Use the MNDN workflow as a structured guide, including at least two named alternatives in the Differentiate step • Close with teach-back: ask the patient to say in their own words what the symptom is linked to and what the first step is • Document the psychoeducation provided in the clinical note • Schedule follow-up to assess understanding and outcomes

6.2 Minimum Specification of MNDN for Implementation and Testing

MNDN is described here at the level of detail needed for replication and evaluation, following the TIDieR checklist for intervention description and the Medical Research Council framework for complex interventions. 79, 80 Table 4 lists the minimum components, provider competencies, dose, fidelity criteria, and outcomes. Two design choices deserve comment. Proximal outcomes are prioritized: an educational intervention should first be shown to change what it directly targets (attribution, self-blame, self-efficacy, caregiver understanding, engagement, shared decision-making) before it is expected to change symptom severity or quality of life, which depend on many other inputs. Fidelity is defined behaviorally so that it can be rated from a recording or a structured note, consistent with NIH Behavior Change Consortium recommendations. 81

6.3 Group and Dyadic Sessions

Where resources permit, group sessions (6-10 patients with or without caregivers; 60-90 minutes; 4-8 weekly sessions) allow deeper engagement. Each session covers one NIPE module with didactic content, facilitated discussion, and brief self-assessment exercises. Group formats provide peer support, normalize experiences, and enable shared problem-solving.

Dyadic sessions (patient plus primary caregiver) are particularly valuable for: • Caregiver scaffolding training (Module III) • Psychosis management education (Module VI) • Safety planning for RBD (Module IV)

Group psychoeducation programs such as ReNACE have been delivered to PD patients and carers in primary care and evaluated in a controlled design. 8,84

7.2 Ethical Considerations

Benefits and Risks of Neurobiological Framing

The case for NIPE rests on the reappraisal it makes possible: a symptom understood as circuit dysfunction is harder to experience as a moral failing, and stigma and self-blame are proposed to fall accordingly. 3, 43 The same literature identifies risks. First, biological determinism: biogenetic explanations reduce blame but can increase prognostic pessimism, the belief that a symptom is fixed, and the sense that nothing the patient does matters; a message of malleability delivered alongside the biology attenuates this effect. [19] [20] [21] Second, oversimplification and unearned credibility: explanations that mention brain regions are rated as more satisfying even when they add nothing, so a fluent circuit story can close inquiry rather than open it. 22, 46 Third, false reassurance and false hope, in opposite directions: "it is the disease, not you" can be heard as "nothing can be done," while "we know what is happening in your brain" can be heard as "so it can be fixed." Fourth, over-attribution: once a patient carries a PD label, new symptoms are readily assigned to it (diagnostic overshadowing), delaying recognition of depression, thyroid disease, sleep apnea, medication effects, or the grief and social strain of chronic illness. 89 Neurobiological framing can also change the clinician: in experimental settings, biological explanations reduce clinicians' rated empathy for the patient, presumably by making the person's experience seem mechanical. 90 The design responses used in this chapter follow from these risks. Explanations are phrased as associations, with the uncertainty stated. Every Map statement is paired with something modifiable.

Differentiate names alternatives aloud and treats the biological account as one hypothesis among several.

Psychological and social contributors are asked about explicitly and enter the formulation, because the biopsychosocial model is the frame within which NIPE operates rather than a competitor to it. 3, 16 The patient's own model of the symptom is elicited before the clinician's is offered, since an unaddressed prior belief tends to persist. 9 Explanations are revisited at follow-up and revised when the course does not fit. Individual variability is stated as a fact, not offered as an apology.

Preserving Patient Agency

NIPE should inform choice, not replace it. All management decisions are collaborative. Deterministic language ("this will get worse") is replaced by probabilistic language ("in some people this symptom changes over time; we will watch for it together"), and the patient's priorities set the first step.

Normalization and Its Limits

Normalization addresses the person's experience and the legitimacy of the symptom; it does not settle the cause. The message that best protects against over-attribution is that understanding the neuroscience helps clinician and patient generate and test explanations rather than assign each symptom to a circuit. In practice, a symptom is not attributed to PD, in the conversation or in the note, until the module's differentials have been considered, and the attribution is recorded as a working formulation that later visits can overturn.

RBD Disclosure and Prognostic Counseling

RBD is the module in which NIPE conveys future risk as well as mechanism, and the risk is large. Four principles apply. Ask first: assess what the patient wants to know, and how much, before disclosing.

Preferences vary, some patients want full numerical risk and others want monitoring without prognosis, and both positions are legitimate and can change over time. 66, 67 Distinguish what is known from what is not: cohort risk (about 6% per year, roughly three quarters over 12 years in sleep-center cohorts) is known; the individual's timing, phenotype, and whether conversion will occur in their lifetime are not, and community-detected RBD may carry lower risk than the cohorts suggest. 62 Say what can be done now: the symptom can be treated, the bedroom made safe, and motor, cognitive, autonomic, and olfactory function monitored so that treatment starts early if needed; observational and interventional studies are available for those who want to take part. Be accurate about lifestyle: regular aerobic exercise and good sleep are recommended for general and brain health, and exercise is being tested for disease modification in early PD, but no lifestyle measure has been shown to prevent or delay phenoconversion, and none should be presented as if it had. 91 Disclosure is staged over visits, includes the bed partner when the patient wishes, is documented, and respects a stated wish not to know.

Health Literacy

All handouts should be written at a 6th-8th grade reading level with attention to cultural and linguistic diversity. Visual aids should not rely solely on text. Translation should be planned for non-Englishspeaking populations, and teach-back used to confirm understanding. 13

Equity

Telehealth NIPE improves access but presupposes internet connectivity and device availability. Programs must plan for technology-limited populations with telephone-based or in-person alternatives.

Data Privacy

Symptom logs and self-assessment data collected through patient portals or apps must comply with institutional privacy standards and local regulations (for example, HIPAA in the United States and GDPR in Europe).

7.3 Research Agenda

The evidence base for NIPE in movement disorders is at an early stage. Priorities are ordered from proximal to distal: 1.

Proximal outcome trials: attention-matched randomized comparisons of MNDN micro-NIPE with standard education on attribution accuracy, self-blame, self-efficacy, caregiver understanding, treatment engagement, and shared decision-making, using the specification in Table 4.

2. Dose and format: micro-NIPE alone versus micro-NIPE plus group or telehealth modules, and the number of modules needed for durable change.

3. Distal outcomes: whether proximal changes translate into lower NMS burden, better quality of life, lower caregiver burden, or changed healthcare utilization, and through which mediators.

4. Harms: prognostic pessimism, anxiety after RBD disclosure, over-attribution rates, and clinician empathy, measured prospectively rather than assumed absent. 5. Mechanism: whether circuit-based education changes metacognitive accuracy (for example, apathy self-awareness, which may itself relate to ACC-accumbens connectivity) or interoceptive awareness, and whether these changes track response.

6. Extension to other movement disorders: dystonia (sensorimotor integration), Huntington's disease (caudate-predominant degeneration with prominent psychiatric NMS), functional movement disorder (where predictive-processing explanations are already used therapeutically), and tic disorders (habit-loop circuitry). 7. Digital and cultural adaptation: app-based NIPE modules analogous to those developed for addiction, with gamified exercises, circuit animations, and self-monitoring, evaluated across languages, literacy levels, and cultural models of neurological illness. 92, 93

7.4 Limitations and Honest Uncertainties

The MNDN workflow and module content are a proposed framework. No trial has tested them, and the evidence for NIPE in movement disorders is limited to implementation studies of general psychoeducation in PD and to studies of NIPE in other conditions. 7, 84, 93 The circuit explanations are simplified for teaching and describe group-level associations that vary between patients and change with disease stage. Several NMS lack approved treatments, and off-label strategies carry uncertain benefit. NIPE complements pharmacologic and rehabilitative care and does not replace it. Clinicians should say as much: "This symptom is linked to brain changes that we understand in part. Several things help many people. We will find out what helps you, and we will change the explanation if the course tells us to."

KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR THE CLINICIAN

• Non-motor symptoms affect nearly all patients with PD and often cause more disability than motor features. Systematic screening with validated instruments is essential.

• NIPE extends established psychoeducation with brain-based content and a delivery structure built around how people learn; MNDN is its movement-disorders implementation, not a separate model.

• The tripartite CBGTC loop model and the multi-transmitter framework provide a translational scaffold for explaining NMS. Its associations are population-level and are stated with the certainty the evidence supports.

• The MNDN workflow delivers one symptom per visit in 3-5 minutes. The Differentiate step is what keeps normalization from becoming over-attribution.

• Patient and caregiver handouts reinforce clinic teaching; they use at most one labeled metaphor per module, and the scientific account is stated separately.

• Implementation can begin with micro-NIPE and scale to group, dyadic, and telehealth formats. Table 4 specifies the components, dose, fidelity criteria, and outcomes needed to test it.

• Caregivers are co-therapists in most modules. Including them is expected to benefit both patients and caregivers, and this expectation should be tested.

• Neurobiological framing has known risks (determinism, oversimplification, over-attribution, false reassurance, and reduced clinician empathy); the design features of MNDN are chosen to address them.

• • Proximal outcome trials should precede claims about effects on symptom severity or quality of life.
