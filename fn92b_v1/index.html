How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Since Roman antiquity, artisans have sawn marble blocks into mirror-image pairs and mounted them side by side (book-matching, opus sectile symmetricum), a costly wager that irregular symmetry makes veined stone more beautiful. The wager has never been tested. What does this imperfect symmetry do to a beholder? In two eye-tracking studies (𝑁 = 128), viewers inspected and rated images of real book-matched, artificially mirrored, and non-mirrored marble slabs. Book-matched marble sustains ambient and symmetry-probing gaze longer than its perfect mirror, yet this oculomotor engagement is not reflected in aesthetic judgement. Liking followed the perceived order more than the figures. Beholders largely agreed on how much order and pareidolia they saw in this ancient ornament but were divided in preference. Perception thus appears to have changed less over time than taste, and this labour-intensive tradition may owe more to habituation within a marble-clad culture than to a universal appeal of symmetry.

1 Introduction

Around AD 100, Romans began sawing blocks of polychrome marble into successive slices and mounting them side by side as mirrored images, a practice called book-matching, for which the art-historical neologism opus sectile symmetricum has been proposed (Rosenberg, 2025). The technique turned the natural veining of stone into near-symmetric ornament, spread as a cladding of polychrome stone slabs from the Pantheon in Rome (Fig. 1) through Byzantium and early Islam to Venice and Baroque Rome (Barry, 2011 (Barry, , 2017;; Rosenberg et al., 2026), and has become ubiquitous in lobbies, bathing rooms, and luxury retail since the twentieth century. Two thousand years of deliberate, costly effort to mirror stone embody an implicit empirical claim that book-matched marble is aesthetically superior to the plain slab it is cut from and to any perfect, manufactured symmetry, as normally used for ornamental patterns. That claim has not been tested against controls that hold the material constant. Kiilerich (2012) offers the one published argument, which runs from the laboratory finding that people prefer symmetric abstract patterns to the suggestion that Byzantine builders book-matched because symmetry pleases, thereby presupposing the claim. In this paper, we test the claim directly. Close-up of the frieze in (a), five book-matched panels, alternating light (quartered, four slabs each) and dark (twinned, two slabs each). The Pantheon was rebuilt from foundation to dome after the fire of AD 110. The designs for its wall incrustation date to Trajan's reign (before 117) and were completed under Hadrian. Book-matching itself was invented in Rome after AD 79 and before AD 119, perhaps by artisans working at the Pantheon, more likely slightly earlier in buildings of the capital now lost; the Pantheon is the most complex ensemble among the earliest surviving examples (Rosenberg, 2025). Photograph: Bibliotheca Hertziana, Max Planck Institute for Art History, Rome. Reproduced with permission.

Two theoretical traditions offer competing accounts of why mirrored stone should please. On the mimetic account, veined marble is a field of potential images (faces, bodies, landscapes seen in chance patterns, Janson, 1973; Gamboni, 2021), and bilateral symmetry multiplies that potential, because faces and bodies are themselves bilaterally symmetric. Rorschach's mirror-symmetric plates rest on the same principle, since they were used to suggest no single determinate reading, leaving the figure for the beholder to find (Rosenberg, 2007). The marble tradition itself cultivated the tendency to see figures in the stone (what psychology terms pareidolia). Onians (1980) shows sixth-century viewers reading slabs as meadows, islands, and seas, and reports figures in Hagia Sophia formed at the junctions of symmetric slabs, some with eyes bored in to secure the reading. On the amimetic account, by contrast, the pleasure is in ordered irregularity itself, in repetition, rhythm, and correspondence of forms without any figure being recognised (Rosenberg, 2015 (Rosenberg, , 2021)). In view of this, because the two slab faces are similar but never identical, book-matching offers a symmetry that the viewer has to discover and verify over time, a "delayed form recognition" placed at the centre of its appeal (Rosenberg, 2016 (Rosenberg, , 2021)). In predictive-processing accounts of aesthetic reward, pleasure tracks the resolution of prediction error (Van de Cruys and Wagemans, 2011; Van de Cruys et al., 2023). Those accounts have not been applied to marble, but the delayed recognition of the natural mirror is, in their terms, a delayed resolution that they would locate near their optimum. To date, neither tradition has been tested with eye movements and preference judgements on photographs of the material itself.

The empirical aesthetics of symmetry provides a firm baseline and a caution. Since Fechner (1876) founded the discipline, symmetry has anchored its search for lawful beauty, and Birkhoff (1933) made it an element of order in the first formalisation, his aesthetic measure. The modern verdict is that most laypeople prefer symmetric over asymmetric abstract patterns in explicit beauty judgements (Jacobsen and Höfel, 2002; Weichselbaum et al., 2018), and symmetry carries positive implicit associations (Makin et al., 2012; Bertamini et al., 2013). This explicit preference replicates even in a culture that valorises asymmetry (Leder et al., 2024). The preference is often read as an evolved sensitivity to bodily symmetry as a fitness signal (Møller and Thornhill, 1998). The caution is that it weakens or reverses under expertise (Leder et al., 2019; Gartus et al., 2020) or in particular stimulus domains, where symmetry preference grades from strong for human faces to absent for abstract artworks (Little, 2014), and landscapes made artificially symmetric lose appeal (Bertamini et al., 2019), as do artificially symmetrised abstract paintings, monotonically with each added axis of symmetry (Locher and Nodine, 1987; Krupinski and Locher, 1988).

However, the base of this literature is almost entirely abstract, achromatic, perfectly regular patterns (Jacobsen and Höfel, 2001; Bertamini and Rampone, 2020). The oculomotor symmetry responses known so far come largely from the same tradition, where the axis of symmetry serves as a landmark that organises and restricts exploration (Locher and Nodine, 1973, 1989), local symmetry draws early fixations even in natural scenes (Kootstra et al., 2011), fixational saccades are directionally biased parallel to the axis within 500 ms of onset and stay so across 3-second trials (Meso et al., 2016), and axis scanning is the same whether symmetry is being classified or aesthetically evaluated (Makin et al., 2020). In the nearest approach to imperfection, dot patterns were bred from viewers' gaze, and preference settled slightly short of the perfect mirror, but the tolerance was measured on average, and where on the pattern viewers looked was not analysed (Makin et al., 2016). Photographed real material with imperfect, discovered symmetry has therefore not been measured. Moreover, the two literatures that share this object, empirical aesthetics and the art history of marble, cite almost none of the same sources (compare, for example, the reference lists of McManus, 2005; Bertamini and Rampone, 2020; Van Geert and Wagemans, 2020 with those of Kiilerich, 2012; Barry, 2017; Gamboni, 2021 Gamboni, , 2026)), so translating between them is part of this paper's work.

We report one pre-registered experiment and one archival validation. The main study (𝑁 = 67) shows fifteen marble objects, each in three windows (rectangular sections cut from one photographed block sequence): plain (non-mirrored, with no constructed axis), book-matched (the natural mirror pair), and artificially mirrored (one face beside its exact digital mirror, abbreviated artificial in figures and tables). All three conditions thus show the same marble. The study combines aesthetic liking, engagement, figureawareness (mimetic) and pattern-awareness (amimetic) ratings with 30-second free viewing and crossed mixed-effects inference. Given its 2000 years of use, book-matched marble was predicted to be preferred and more engaging (H1) and to elicit an intermediate mimetic perception between plain and artificially mirrored surfaces (H2). Both self-reported perceptual modes (mimetic vs amimetic) were expected to predict evaluation (H3). Book-matched marble was expected to hold ambient viewing longer than perfectly mirrored and plain marble, and symmetry-probing gaze longer than its perfect mirror (H4). Finally, perceptual mode was expected to be recoverable from eye-tracking signals (H5). The axis study (𝑁 = 61) focuses on aesthetic viewing of plain, vertically, horizontally, and quarter-book-matched marble, both natural and artificially mirrored. Its descriptive findings appeared in an art-history book (Rosenberg et al., 2026). We reanalyse its eye-tracking data with the main study's full pipeline. The dataset supplies the replication and axis-generalisation tests that a single-axis design cannot provide.

The two studies turn the practice's implicit claim into a measurable variable, testing whether bookmatched marble is, in fact, preferred and tracing its appeal through gaze, self-reported perception (the two percept items), and aesthetic judgement. Five findings emerge. First, the preference prediction reverses, in that book-matched marble is the least liked of the three conditions. Second, the deficit against the perfect mirror is carried by the two percepts, primarily the amimetic one, that is, by awareness of the structure itself more than by the perception of figures. Third, the condition manipulation drives two implicit gaze channels, ambient viewing and symmetry probing, and an autonomic one, the pupil response.

Fourth, the gaze effects from the main study replicate in the axis study, and the seam-following signature generalises across symmetry axes. Fifth, every indirect route from gaze to evaluation nonetheless tests null, and a sequence model that recognises the condition from the raw eye movements reliably above chance recovers no rating. In sum, book-matched marble holds symmetry probing and ambient viewing longer than its perfect mirror, yet viewers do not prefer it, and they differ on that preference far more than on what they see. Section 4 discusses how a practice sustained for two millennia can find no majority on first viewing. There we read the result against the history of taste and argue that the tradition may rest on habituation more than on a universal preference for imperfect symmetry.

2.1.6 Eye-tracking metrics

Ambient and focal viewing. Coefficient K (Krejtz et al., 2016) indexes the balance between two viewing modes (Unema et al., 2005; Velichkovsky et al., 2005): ambient viewing (short fixations paired with long saccades, typical of orienting across a scene) and focal viewing (long fixations paired with short saccades, typical of close inspection). Fixation durations and the amplitudes of the saccades that follow them are 𝑧-scored within each participant, and K is their mean difference. Positive values indicate focal viewing, and negative values indicate ambient viewing. The ambient-to-focal transition time is the moment a participant's K trajectory first crosses zero. Each trajectory is estimated per participant and condition using Gaussian kernel smoothing (𝜎 = 1.5 s) over that condition's fixation-level K values, then pooled across its trials onto a common 0-30 s axis. A trajectory already focal at its first estimate is scored at 0 s, and one that has not crossed by the end of the trial is scored at 30 s. Conditions are compared by paired 𝑡 tests on the crossing times. Fixation and saccade fields. Fold symmetry (the similarity of a saccade field to its own mirror image across the axis), inter-observer agreement, and vein alignment all use the HeatMatch saccade-field framework (Long et al., 2026), which rasterises each trial's saccades into a smoothed spatial field so that viewing patterns can be mirrored, compared, and correlated across viewers and images. Vein orientation fields are computed by a structure tensor (Bigün and Granlund, 1987). All analysed fields use a Gaussian width of 1 • of visual angle. Inter-observer agreement is reported for two fields: the saccade field and a fixation field constructed in the same way from fixation positions. Saccade-pattern similarity scores the full saccade field, where the saccades run (𝑆 loc ) and how the flow is oriented there. Fixation similarity is the correlation between two Gaussian fixation densities, rescaled as (𝑟 + 1)/2.

Pupillometry. We interpret only the task-evoked pupil change within a trial, mean pupil size over the remainder of the trial minus a baseline over the trial's first second, 𝑧-scored within participant and recorded eye. Stimulus luminance enters as a covariate.

2.1.7 Statistical analysis

Mixed models. Confirmatory models are linear mixed models (LMMs) with crossed random intercepts for participant, object, and image. Fixed effects are tested with Satterthwaite-corrected (Satterthwaite, 1946) 𝑡 and 𝐹 statistics (denominator degrees of freedom approximately 25 on this design).

Multiple comparisons.

Pairwise contrasts between conditions are corrected for multiple comparisons with the Holm procedure (Holm, 1979), a deviation from the pre-registration, which named Bonferroni. Both control the family-wise error rate, Holm uniformly at least as powerfully, and no reported decision differs between the two corrections. The three pairwise contrasts of each confirmatory outcome form one Holm family per outcome. Other contrast sets are Holm-corrected within analysis, the trait moderations and the further tests by the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). All tests are two-sided. Contrasts are written first condition vs second and signed in that order.

Temporal claims.

Condition × time claims use participant-clustered bootstrap inference (2,000 resamples, 10,000 for the MSS peak difference). Time-course figures are smoothed for display only (Supplementary Note 1.5), and no statistic is computed on the displayed curves.

Bayesian estimation and resampling. Bayesian components (the binomial GLMM and the mediation system) were estimated with PyMC and Bambi. Posteriors were sampled with the No-U-Turn sampler (four chains of 1,000 to 2,000 draws after 1,500 to 2,000 tuning steps). We report posterior means with 95% highest-density intervals (HDIs) and the posterior probability of direction (pd). All bootstrap and permutation 𝑝-values use the add-one convention.

Software. Analyses used Python 3.12 with NumPy 2.4, SciPy 1.18, pandas 3.0, statsmodels 0.14.6, scikitlearn 1.9, PyTorch 2.13 and Matplotlib 3.11, and for the Bayesian models PyMC 6.2 (Abril-Pla et al., 2023), Bambi 0.19 (Capretto et al., 2022) and ArviZ 1.2 (Kumar et al., 2019).

2.2 The axis study

The axis study reanalyses an archival dataset that Rosenberg et al. (2026) described without inferential statistics in an Italian art-historical volume on marble. Sixty-one sessions were recorded in June and July 2022 and analysed. Demographic data were not recorded.

Participants viewed 8 out of 14 marble photographs (stone-dealer slabs from the same source as the main study's modern objects) for 30 s each, 7 categories × 2 stimuli spanning the plain, vertically bookmatched, horizontally book-matched, and quartered (two-axis) groups, each seamed group in a natural and an artificially mirrored rendering (Fig. 3b). Each participant saw both plain stimuli plus one natural and one mirrored stimulus per seamed group. Participants were assigned to one of two complementary stimulus sets (𝑛 = 30/31). Within each set, a group's natural and artificially mirrored stimuli were built from different physical halves of the same cut sequence, and the two sets swapped the assignment, so no participant ever saw both the book-matched and the mirrored rendering of the same physical half. Trial order was randomised per participant. The two sets form a single counterbalance and are pooled across all analyses. After each image, participants rated its pleasingness. These ratings were intended to elicit an aesthetic attitude during viewing and are not analysed here.

Eye movements were recorded with the main study's apparatus and display geometry. The reanalysis applies the main study's full pipeline with the same event filters and metrics. Cross-study contrasts use only the vertical-seam group and the book-matched vs artificially mirrored contrast, because each group is a different marble block, so that contrasts against plain are block-confounded.

4 Discussion

Across the two studies and 128 participants, book-matched marble evoked longer ambient viewing and symmetry probing than its perfect digital mirror, yet it was the least liked and least engaging condition, the reverse of the prediction (H1). Perfect mirroring raised mimetic perception, while book-matching did not raise it above plain (H2). Both percepts predict evaluation, with the amimetic path more important for liking book-matched marble (H3). The manipulation creates two implicit gaze channels and an independent autonomic signal. In the gaze channels, perfectly mirrored marble shifts into focal viewing more quickly than naturally book-matched marble, which sustains symmetry probing for longer (H4).

The archival dataset replicates these differences on the vertical seam and shows the seam-following signature to be axis-general. However, no rating can be recovered from gaze beyond the manipulation (H5), and every tested indirect route from gaze to evaluation yields null results.

4.1 Theories of symmetry preference

None of the available accounts predicted that book-matched marble would be the least liked. It has been shown that Viennese students spanning low to high levels of art expertise exhibit an explicit symmetry preference for abstract patterns, with its implicit component untouched by expertise (Weichselbaum et al., 2018), and this explicit preference persists even in a culture that valorises asymmetry (Leder et al., 2024).

The trait-moderation nulls give no sign of a Jacobsen-style subgroup of symmetry-dislikers carrying the effect (Jacobsen and Höfel, 2002). The reversal is therefore a property of the stimulus class, photographs of real material with discovered and imperfect symmetry. The same conclusion can be drawn from stochastic polygon compositions, in which symmetric and non-symmetric arrangements assembled from the same matrices are liked equally, or the irregular ones more (Perillo and Mastandrea, 2023, 2026). In the standard model (Leder et al., 2004), aesthetic appreciation involves several stages of processing, with symmetry classified as part of automatic perceptual analysis, whilst judgement occurs at a later, conscious stage.

These two levels are reflected in our eye movements and our judgements, respectively. Concerning this reversal, four explanatory approaches can be distinguished: (i) Processing fluency (Reber et al., 2004) characterises the ease of processing as a pleasure, and perfect bilateral symmetry is the fluency manipulation par excellence.

Therefore, perfectly mirrored marble should be liked most and unstructured plain stone least. In the data, however, plain is liked most and perfectly mirrored marble gains nothing over it (their raw parity decomposes into opposing direct and percept-mediated components), so the level form of fluency does not hold.

(ii) The transition and optimal-challenge approach predicts the opposite ordering. In this, predictionerror accounts locate pleasure in the fulfilment of expectation (Van de Cruys and Wagemans, 2011), fluency dynamics in its improvement (Topolinski and Reber, 2010), Berlyne's (1970) Wundt curve and medium ambiguity (Jakesch and Leder, 2009) in intermediate challenge, and the orderedirregularity optimum, articulated for visual composition in general (McManus, 2005), is tested directly in this material. Book-matched marble, whose symmetry must be discovered and verified, is exactly the intermediate case, and came last. Prediction-error models can account for this by positing that the real mirror's error never resolves, and their most recent version already allows for this case. There, viewing is considered as an epistemic arc, from curiosity through active probing to closure. The faster-than-expected arrival of closure brings pleasure. If an arc remains open beyond what the viewer is prepared to invest in, it can lead to disengagement (Van de Cruys et al., 2023).

We measure the two phases of this arc, expectation and resolution, separately, and they diverge.

Perfectly mirrored marble completes its arc inside the trial, as symmetry probing reverses after about 20 s, the image-specific agreement between viewers decreases (Fig. 7b, Fig. 8f ), and liking is higher than for book-matched marble. For book-matched marble, the arc remains open, as symmetry probing shows no significant turnover within the trial and liking is lowest. The eye-movement record thus gives the never-resolving error independent content, while the ratings show no hedonic benefit from sustained examination. The data are consistent with the account's mechanism and contradict its predicted optimum, because here liking is higher where the pattern is resolved within the trial, and book-matched marble does not reach that point in 30 s. An exploration sustained for such a long time comes close to what Shklovsky (1917) demanded of art a century ago, a device that makes form difficult and prolongs perception because the perceiving is itself the aesthetic goal.

(iii) Redundancy and monotony accounts interpret the mirror as a cost, on which symmetry is redundant information (Barlow and Reeves, 1979), a regularity grasped at once (van der Helm and Leeuwenberg, 1996), a sterile rigidity that turns to monotony (McManus, 2005). On these accounts, preference should decline monotonically as redundancy increases (plain > book-matched > perfectly mirrored). The data do not show this decline, as perfectly mirrored marble ranks higher than bookmatched marble in liking.

(iv) Accounts of naturalness and authenticity, based on landscapes and abstract paintings which lose appeal when artificially symmetrised (Bertamini et al., 2019; Bertamini and Rampone, 2020; Locher and Nodine, 1987; Krupinski and Locher, 1988), predict that perfectly mirrored marble is rated the lowest. Here, the artificially symmetrised image is liked more than the naturally symmetric one, so that the aversion to manipulation does not transfer to a material whose natural state already contains the mirror.

The data do not fit any of the four approaches, as the pattern is a non-monotonic V with its minimum at the real mirror, below untouched stone and below its own perfect copy. Why, then, might the real mirror fall lowest? Mediation offers a possible answer, in that the deficit against the copy stems from what viewers report perceiving, primarily through their awareness of the structure itself. One reading is an uncanny valley of symmetry, in which perfect mirroring is perceived as clean design and plain stone as untouched nature, while the real book-match is noticeably ordered yet visibly imperfect. A second result, anticipated by no account, is plain stone's credible positive direct path on liking, that is, viewers like the untouched slab for reasons neither percept item captures.

4.2 Art-historical implications

The reversal extends the empirical art-history programme from which it emerged (Rosenberg, 2007), as a non-monotonic relation between order and pleasure had already been envisaged there, in the proposal that viewers "like to discover structures -mimetic and/or amimetic -but dislike them when they are too prominent" (Rosenberg, 2025), although this had not yet been tested. The present design provides a direct test of the non-monotonic form as well as the mechanism of the deficit against the perfect mirror, namely, mediation through perception, particularly through the amimetic ratings.

Of the two theoretical approaches to explaining the appeal of marble, the main-study ratings support the amimetic one (Rosenberg, 2021), with one qualification for each. Perfectly mirrored marble elicited the strongest perception of figures (H2), confirming that perfect bilateral symmetry raises mimetic perception (Gartus et al., 2020; Bertamini et al., 2018 , Rosenberg, 2007) and qualifying Gamboni's (2016) position that it matters little whether a pattern is natural or made once image-making is located in the beholder. Yet liking takes a different path, since, within conditions, it mainly follows the amimetic percept (structure, repetition, rhythm), especially for the book-matched stimuli. At the same time, the mimetic route becomes significant only for plain stone, where there is no symmetry to see. Both mirrored conditions link liking to amimetic perception, but only for the natural book-match does that pathway dominate over the mimetic one after correction. This pattern corresponds to the condition-bound coupling described for indeterminate art (Ishai et al., 2007; Fairhall and Ishai, 2008; Pepperell, 2011). It is consistent with a historical record, since, on Barry's reading, the earliest Byzantine descriptions of book-matched walls praise disciplined disorder and harmony, and do not mention hidden figures. Figural readings became the default only centuries later (Barry, 2011 (Barry, , 2017)), and our lay viewers reflect the emphasis of the sixth-century descriptions.

The percept items serve as indicators of perception in their own right, since the most-liked condition scored lowest on pattern awareness and the least-liked condition scored high. No evaluative halo can produce this inversion. Gamboni's (2021) division of viewers into iconophiles and iconophobes has no counterpart here. The two percepts co-occur within trials (Fig. 5c). A corollary of the amimetic theory, that structural pleasure is felt "all the more when not associated with representation" (Rosenberg, 2021), is also borne out in the engagement item, where the two percepts substitute for each other (Supplementary Note 2.2).

The experimental conditions account for only part of the reversal. Viewers were far more in agreement on the two percepts than on liking (Section 3.1.4), and the direction of the condition effect was almost uniform for the percepts and split for liking. The reversal is therefore the most common ordering, as more viewers ranked book-matched marble last than first, although every possible ordering occurred.

One candidate source of this variation, a preference for complex images among the viewers who like the plain window best (Van Geert and Wagemans, 2020), is not borne out by the image statistics (Supplementary Note 2.3). The split itself has implications for the history of taste, because a preference that varies so greatly within a sample of this kind leaves room for learning. The history of this tradition is geographi-cally limited, moving from Rome through Byzantium to Venice and their imitators (Barry, 2011 (Barry, , 2017;; Rosenberg et al., 2026). Habituation within a marble-clad culture is therefore a more plausible account of the tradition than a universal preference for imperfect symmetry. Familiarisation is known to spare, even strengthen, the preference for symmetric abstract patterns (Tinio and Leder, 2009), and the minority who liked book-matched marble most on first viewing shows that this reading is available to lay viewers.

The patrons who commissioned the slabs differed from our participants in terms of exposure and setting, since they chose the stone, lived with it, and saw it in buildings designed to display it. The question that arises is whether the reversal narrows or inverts with such exposure (Section 4.4).

Our 30-second trials cannot assess the tradition's own time scale, since the "aesthetic boredom" predicted for flawless seriality (Rosenberg, 2021 (Rosenberg, , 2016) ) concerns lived exposure to walls one passes by every day, whereas the perfect mirror, on first glance, shows no sign of this, and is preferred over the natural match.

Art-historical sources also document what the laboratory context omits, namely that book-matching as a decorative technique served to conceal the artifice ( joins hidden in veins, compositions disguised as nature, Gamboni, 2026) and was praised within a collective performative and institutional framework of perception (Gamboni, 2021; Barry, 2017). In the laboratory, with neither this setting nor the habituated familiarity of a marble-clad culture (Rosenberg, 2021; Zajonc, 1968), the oculomotor effects appear without a corresponding majority preference.

The only published inference that we test directly is the syllogism from symmetric-pattern aesthetics to the builders' motive (Kiilerich, 2012). The inference was plausible, since its premise, the preference for symmetric patterns over asymmetric ones, is among the most replicated findings in empirical aesthetics (Jacobsen et al., 2006; Jacobsen and Höfel, 2001). However, this preference was measured on perfectly regular, achromatic, constructed patterns, and does not apply to photographs of real stone. The Roman workshops nonetheless strove systematically toward the most symmetric arrangement the block allowed, which can be seen in the reconstructed sawing and laying sequence itself (Passchier et al., 2021), and modern, lay viewers, when shown the same logic without its context, direct their gaze to the seam and mirror correspondences the builders created, yet like book-matched marble least. Whether the imperfect symmetry of this tradition reflects design or merely the limits of stone-working can be tested where these limits vanish, namely in painted imitation. When Giotto, for instance, painted book-matched incrustations in the Arena Chapel, and had the freedom to mirror perfectly, he introduced the minor irregularities of real slabs (Rosenberg, 2021). Painters who emulated the genre regarded imperfection as constitutive of its appearance, and they worked within a tradition that, as Barry (2017) traces, had since Pliny hailed veined and book-matched marble as painting in stone. That converges with our finding, in that perfect mirroring changes what viewers perceive, raising figure perception above the level the natural match produces. 4.3 The gaze-judgement dissociation

The dissociation is near-total. The manipulation affects all three implicit channels. Still, neither oculomotor channel is associated with evaluation on any of the three tests: mediation indirect paths, the engagement item inverted against measured looking, and a bounded sequence-model null. The evoked pupillary change does not predict any of the four ratings (Supplementary Note 2.5). This decoupling is not specific to liking, since no gaze channel mediates the engagement rating either, and within what the decoder can detect, no rating of any kind can be reconstructed from gaze once the manipulation is accounted for, so the dissociation covers aesthetic judgement in the broad sense, preference included.

Elements of this pattern were to be expected, since symmetry is processed spontaneously and its electrophysiological signature is task-independent, while aesthetic evaluation only takes place under evaluative conditions (Jacobsen and Höfel, 2003; Höfel and Jacobsen, 2007; Jacobsen et al., 2006). The dissociation also resolves the apparent tension with the perceptual and neural literature. Wagemans (1995 Wagemans ( , 1997) ) and Bertamini et al. (2018) describe a parametric, automatic symmetry response, and it can coexist with a nonmonotonic liking because the two fall within separate groups of measures (Fig. 9). Even the method that measures preference based on gaze carries its authors' caution that attracting gaze and liking are distinct constructs (Makin et al., 2016).

The eye-mind assumption, which has been the prevailing view since Yarbus's (1967) task-dependence demonstrations, has gained its name in the reading literature (Just and Carpenter, 1980). It was most clearly demonstrated in the gaze cascade, where looking and liking reinforce each other in a positive feedback loop (Shimojo et al., 2003). This is precisely the inference eye-movement methodologists have warned is unsafe (Orquin and Holmqvist, 2018; Velichkovsky et al., 2005). The decoupling has precedents.

Looking and liking have decoupled from one another since the earliest exposure experiments (Zajonc, 1968; Bornstein, 1989). A precedent from developmental psychology can be found in the fact that fouryear-olds, before aesthetic norms have developed, attend more to symmetric patterns yet express no preference for them (Huang et al., 2018). In choice paradigms, looking time predicts the choice but not the aesthetic value behind it when the two differ (Isham and Geng, 2013), and mere exposure time is sufficient to build the preference that was previously attributed to active looking, because the effect occurs even without any eye movement (Bird et al., 2012). Appraisal research likewise finds interest and pleasantness essentially independent across the same paintings (Turner and Silvia, 2006), which sets a precedent for an engagement item whose ratings run counter to the looking it refers to. The present design adds the most direct within-episode test of the assumption so far: the dissociation demonstrated in adults, within single viewing episodes, without any choice task, a mediation model showing where the coupling fails, and a positive control that sets the detection limit.

4.4 Limitations and future directions

The design examines one stimulus domain, namely photographic reproductions of veined marble on a screen, in 30-second exposures, so the claims are bounded to the first viewing of images and do not extend Imperfect symmetry in book-matched marble 4.4 Limitations and future directions to lived architecture (Rosenberg, 2025), where historical panels were often pieced composites (Gamboni, 2026). The sample is one lab's German-speaking convenience sample, so whether the reversal is culturally local remains open. Only the vertical axis was tested, the maximally attended case (Wenderoth, 1994), but the archival dataset supplies the axis generalisation.

The percept ratings are self-reports collected retrospectively in a second block (Section 2.1), so the common method variance and the observational status of the mediators mean that the mediation architecture represents an explanation of covariation that does not establish a causal chain. Liking was measured with a single global item, chosen because the first historical question concerns whether the stone was liked.

The grounds of a rating (whether, for example, a slab pleased because its structure was easy to take in or because it took time to decipher) are not observed, and the two percept items capture only part of them.

The percept items record how clearly figures and patterns were seen. Which figures, and at what moment of the 30 s they appeared, remain unrecorded, because a report during viewing would have made the viewing a detection task (Supplementary Note 1.3). The two percept items measured each percept separately and did not cover the balance between them. Therefore, they show that mimetic and amimetic perception co-occur (their trial residuals correlate at +.25) but cannot test an account on which a viewer sees a slab in one mode or the other. A single bipolar item, or a forced choice, would be the direct test. A measure of implicit affect (e.g., an IAT on marble) was not administered, so it remains open whether the explicit deficit coexists with intact implicit symmetry positivity (Weichselbaum et al., 2018; Makin et al., 2012). The trait-moderation nulls have limited power, since only |𝑟 | of about .34 or larger is detectable at this N. The saccade-field analysis also shows no qualitative viewer types, as each participant maintains a stable personal saccade-field signature across all three conditions (Supplementary Note 2.4). Finally, one reading of the reversal that this design cannot exclude is that the ornament's appeal depends on the architectural and institutional context the laboratory setting strips away. We regard habituation as the likelier account, for the reasons given above.

Two predictions follow directly. First, if the reversal reflects a culturally specific reading of conspicuous order rather than a universal response to the stimulus class, the same 45 stimuli shown to viewers enculturated in a different ornamental tradition should alter the minimum. Second, the habituation interpretation predicts that the reversal weakens with increasing exposure and expertise, which reflects an established moderator family in symmetry aesthetics (Leder et al., 2019; Gartus et al., 2020; Vogt and Magnussen, 2007). The cohort in the main study was unselected for art-historical training, and art interest showed no moderation surviving correction. Viewers professionally habituated to opus sectile (conservators, Byzantinists, architectural historians), or lay viewers familiarised over repeated sessions, should therefore show an attenuated, displaced, or reversed preference ordering. Such a test would separate properties of the stimulus class from properties of the audience. Exposure-scale designs, real-material presentation, and fidelity dose-response manipulations are the obvious tools for an empirical cognitive aesthetics of bookmatching. Free or prompted reports of what a viewer saw, and of when it appeared, would supplement the content-related and temporal aspects that remain open in the clarity items.

4.5 Conclusion

In summary, the symmetric seams of book-matched marble organise viewing through an axis-general, replicated implicit mechanism. Liking is dependent on what viewers report seeing in the stone, above all awareness of abstract structure, and no measured route connects gaze and liking. For eye-tracking research in aesthetics, the implication is methodological, in that longer ambient viewing or symmetry probing does not indicate preference, and inferring liking from gaze would have ranked book-matched marble above its perfect mirror, even though viewers in fact liked it least.

Fixations and saccades were parsed by the manufacturer's velocity-based algorithm (highsensitivity "psychophysical" preset, velocity threshold 22 • /s, acceleration threshold 3800 • /s 2 , "extra" heuristic filtering), and all analyses use the event reports in a stimulus-centred coordinate frame. Blinkcontaminated saccade pairs and trial-final truncated fixations were excluded from event-level analyses, while completed trial-final saccades are kept. All analyses use a filtered event set (on-stimulus, starting within the 30 s, blink-free, saccade amplitude ≥ 0.1 • , fixation duration 50 to 3,000 ms).

Symmetry probing. The mirror-symmetry score (MSS) measures H4's mirror-symmetric probing directly, quantifying per saccade whether and how much gaze jumps across the axis between mirrorcorrespondent locations. The axis-conformity score (ACS) quantifies the complementary behaviour of travelling along the axis. Both behaviours have so far been described only verbally, by Rosenberg et al. (2026) in a chapter on the history and perception of book-matching. Both scores range from 0 to 1, use Gaussian tolerances of 1 • of visual angle, and score saccades under 2 • of amplitude as zero. The formal definitions are given in Supplementary Note 1.4. The cross-axis share is a coarser companion measure, the proportion of a trial's saccades whose start and end points fall on opposite sides of the axis, and is fitted as a binomial generalised linear mixed model (GLMM).
