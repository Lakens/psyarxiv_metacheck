How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

How does a moment become a memory trace? The transition from perceived duration to the when of memory is likely shaped by attention and working memory (WM). In two experiments, we investigated how memory decay, interference between stored durations, and internal versus external attention affect the precision of recall from temporal WM. For this, we used a delayed reproduction task in which participants listened to a sequence of time intervals and reproduced them after a retention period (Herbst et al., 2025). In Experiment 1, we manipulated the ratio between the target interval and the retention interval, showing that both the duration of the retention interval (decay) and its ratio to the target (temporal distinctiveness yielding interference) affected WM performance. In Experiment 2, we examined interference between intervals within a sequence by manipulating attention via a cue indicating which interval to reproduce. The cue appeared either before (pro-cue) or after (retrocue) the retention period, directing external attention to sensory encoding or internal attention to WM representations, respectively, either for a single item or the full sequence. External attention selectively benefited accuracy and precision for the longest item when cued alone in the final position. Retro-cueing, by contrast, more generally reduced precision for single-item compared to the whole-sequence recall.

Finally, a mixture modeling approach revealed swap errors consistent with those observed in visual WM. Together, these findings compare the contributions of decay and temporal distinctiveness to temporal WM performance with a dynamic resource allocation framework.

Introduction

Adaptive human behavior depends critically on representing time and on integrating ongoing sensory inputs with past experiences to guide future perception and action (Buonomano et al., 2023; Gibbon et al., 1984; Shi et al., 2013; J. Staddon, 2005). Yet, theories offer inconsistent answers regarding the specific role of memory for interval timing. For example, some view memory as a central component of dedicated timing mechanisms (Gibbon, 1977; Gibbon et al., 1984), while others argue that duration is estimated by reading off memory traces (French et al., 2014; J. Staddon, 2005; J. E. R. Staddon et al., 1999). Although timing theories do not clearly explain how memory contributes to time perception, perhaps even sharing some basic computational structure (de Jong et al., 2025) and neural mechanisms (Buonomano & Maass, 2009; Buonomano & Merzenich, 1995; Karmarkar & Buonomano, 2007; Lewis and Miall) may be shared. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying their coupling remain poorly understood (Buonomano et al, 2023; Kwok et al., 2025), in part because they are rarely studied together. Bringing them together highlights the central role of attention, as both time and memory fidelity are susceptible to the dynamic allocation of attentional resources: greater attention to time lengthens perceived duration (Fortin & Schweickert, 2016; Polti et al., 2018) whereas greater attention supports more stable representations in memory (Ricker et al., 2018). Despite the evidence supporting the notion that attention and memory may rely on shared neural mechanisms (Ku, 2018; Panichello & Buschman, 2021), most research has examined their effects on time perception separately (Block & Gruber, 2014; Matthews, 20160519; Nicolaï et al., 2024; Polti et al., 2018; Pouthas & Perbal, 2004). In the current study, we explore the interplay between time, attention and memory processes. In this context, working memory (WM) provides the natural approach for investigating the interaction between time, memory and attention. WM is a dynamic process that relies on the encoding, maintenance, and retrieval of information (Baddeley, 2012; Cowan et al., 2002; Miller, 1956). It is also subject to well-characterized capacity limits so that representations maintained in WM degrade through two distinct mechanisms: gradual decay over time (J. Brown, 1958; Ricker et al., 2016) which can be countered by attentional refreshing (Barrouillet et al., 2004 (Barrouillet et al., , 2011)), or interference between items maintained in WM (Oberauer & Lin, 2024; Waugh & Norman, 1965). Applied to time perception, memory decay models (J. Staddon, 2005; J. E. R. Staddon et al., 1999) propose that duration is estimated by reading off the strength of the memory trace (French et al., 2014), such that the trace's activation level directly determines the estimated duration. This makes decay rate a consequence of absolute elapsed time. In contrast to decay, models that posit shared mechanisms for time and memory (de Jong et al., 2025; Karmarkar & Buonomano, 2007; Lewis & Miall, 2006) instead imply that temporal information in WM will itself constitute a source of interference. Specifically, behavioral performance governed by the relative (ratio-based) relationships among intervals, rather than their absolute durations, would determine performance. These two accounts -the Decay hypothesis and Interference hypothesis -yield distinct, testable predictions about behavioral performance in temporal WM, which we illustrate in Figure 1a.

Temporal WM can be defined as the temporary maintenance and manipulation of taskrelevant duration information, which is presumably subject to capacity limitations analogous to those reported in visual WM (Baddeley, 2012; Bays & Husain, 2008; Cowan et al., 2002; Miller, 1956). Recent work using continuous temporal reproduction measures goes in this direction: temporal precision decreases with the number of time intervals to be remembered (Herbst et al., 2025 (Herbst et al., , 2026;; Teki & Griffiths, 2014). However, duration is a magnitude that differs fundamentally from the circular dimensions of visual information like color. The former displays scale invariance so that temporal noise scales proportionally with duration (constant coefficient of variation (CV); Gibbon et al., 1984), whereas visual features such as color do not. Because the noise of a color representation does not depend on its value, the representation of one colored item does not depend on the values of the others, and traditional visual WM models can therefore assume independence between colored items (Bays et al., 2009; Fougnie & Alvarez, 2011; Zhang & Luck, 2008) and explore the possibility of distance-dependent relationship between color items (Bae et al., 2014). However, in time perception, durations maintain scale invariance so that temporal noise scales proportionally with duration (constant coefficient of variation (CV); Gibbon et al., 1984). This strongly suggest that durations are ratio-dependent, thus that durations cannot be treated out of the temporal context or singledout like color items would. The distinction between visual color feature space and duration (not a sensory feature) has important implications for classic serial position effects in WM (Henson, 1998; Hurlstone et al., 2014; Miller, 1956). For instance, the evidence for primacy and recency effects in temporal WM is inconsistent across studies One study (Manohar & Husain, 2016) reported a recency effect of temporal precision (and a recency and a primacy effect on reaction time) when participants reproduced a single probed time interval in a temporal reproduction task. Using a similar task (with empty interval), another study (Teki & Griffiths, 2014) reported a recency effect for supra-second durations, and both recency and primacy effects for subsecond durations. Another study using a n-item delayed temporal reproduction task, in which participants reproduced the entire sequence of time intervals, the authors reported inconsistent primacy effects across different number of items (Herbst et al., 2025). This heterogeneity may also affect another aspect of serial recall: transposition errors in which adjacent items are confused during recall (Baddeley, 2012; Hurlstone et al., 2014; Oberauer, 2019), which have not yet been explored in the context of temporal WM. Thus, to which effects typically described in visual WM can generalize to temporal WM is unclear. Here, we tested the Decay and Interference working hypotheses in temporal WM (Experiment 1; Figure 1b) and subsequently examined how these mechanisms are shaped by dynamic resource allocation (Experiment 2; Figure 1c). Experiment 1 aimed to examine whether poorer temporal WM performance is driven by absolute decay or relative interference from the retention interval using a single time interval (Figure 1a). Experiment 1 used a 1-item delayed reproduction task (Herbst et al., 2025) in which participants were presented with a single empty time interval followed by a retention interval (1.04 s or 2.08 s), allowing us to assess the ratio between the two (ratio = 0.5 or ratio = 1) (Figure 1a). In Experiment 1, the impact of decay was manipulated by the length of the time interval itself, while interference between temporal information (Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2008) was captured by temporal distinctiveness (G. D. A. Brown et al., 2007). Temporal distinctiveness reflects how easily a memory item can be differentiated from others in WM along the temporal dimension, and is operationalized as the inverse of its summed confusability with all other items (G. D. A. Brown et al., 2007). Confusability between two intervals is determined by the ratio of temporal distances at retrieval, which is consistent with the scale-invariant nature of temporal representations (Gallistel & Gelman, 2000; Gallistel & Gibbon, 2001; Gibbon, 1977) such that higher confusability predicts worse WM performance. Temporal distinctiveness, unlike decay rate, assumes that temporal representations are governed by ratio-based, rather than absolute timing. Following the general hypothesis (Figure 1a), the decay and interference views offer distinct predictions about temporal WM performance with the decay hypothesis predicting that temporal WM performance should decline with a longer retention interval (Figure 2a, left panel, "Decay WH"). Concurrently, if the retention interval interferes with temporal WM, a lower temporal distinctiveness between the retention and the target time interval was expected to disrupt temporal WM performance (Figure 2a, right panel, "Interference WH"). To test this, we operationalized the temporal distinctiveness as ratio between the target interval and the retention. Our results showed that WM performance not only decayed with the retention interval, but also interfered with its duration. Experiment 2 extended the exploration to the possible interference between time intervals within a sequence of two intervals (Figure 1c). For a sequence, decay and interference are forgetting mechanisms that predict only the recency effect based on one assumption: the worst performance of earlier items is attributed to longer decay time (J. Brown, 1958; Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2011; Ricker et al., 2016) or to retroactive interference from later items (Oberauer et al., 2012; Waugh & Norman, 1965). In contrast, temporal distinctiveness (G. D. A. Brown et al., 2007) can not only explain the recency but also the primacy effect because items at the beginning and end of the sequence can both have distinctive temporal contexts compared with items in the middle of a sequence. The primacy effect was incorporated into subsequent iterations of the decay and interference models for sequences with two possible sources arising from different stages of WM processing: the primacy gradient formed during encoding (Page & Norris, 1998) and output interference accumulated during retrieval (Cowan et al., 2002; Kobayashi et al., 2026). The former is often explained as resulting from a decline in attention along the sequence (G. D. A. Brown et al., 2000); the latter is explained by interference, whereby unretrieved items are affected by the representations of retrieved items (Oberauer, 2003; Peters et al., 2018). To capture these effects, we manipulated selective attention by cuing participants to reproduce either the first interval, the second interval, or the entire sequence. The cue could be presented before (pro-cueing; Figure 1c) or after (retrocueing; Figure 1c) the temporal sequence. Pro-cueing is known to modulate the external attention, which filters and prioritizes sensory content during the encoding stage to compensate the primacy gradient, whereas retro-cueing modulates internal attention through the selection and prioritization of mental representations against the output interference before retrieval (Chun et al., 2011; Van Ede et al., 2019). In accordance with the general working hypothesis (Figure 1a), decay and interference were predicted to affect temporal WM differently, and to be reflected as an interaction between item position and item duration. When the whole sequence is pro-cued or retro-cued, attention is not directed to any particular item, so both items receive the same amount of attention. Under these conditions, differences between positions should reflect decay and interference alone (Figure 1). Applied to Experiment 2, the Decay WH (Figure 1a) predicts that the first item should show lower precision relative to the second item and this effect should be stronger for the short compared to the long item. In addition, if external attention decreases across the sequence, early items should be remembered better, producing a primacy effect. Applying the Interference WH (Figure 1) to Experiment 2, we expect that the second item would be less precise relative to the first item and this effect should be stronger for long than short items (Brown et al., 2007; see the supplement for details). When pro-cueing a single item, we focused on how external attention modulates the primacy effect: the successful filtering of uncued items can leave only a single item to be remembered. This predicts a recency effect under the Decay WH but a primacy effect under the Interference WH. For internal attention, we focused on how internal attention attenuates output interference (Oberauer, 2003; Oberauer & Lin, 2024). Accordingly, retro-cueing a single item should yield higher precision compared with cueing the full sequence, particularly for the second long item. In sum, we predicted item duration × item position × attention interactions. Design and procedure

The testing was conducted in a soundproof both to minimize external noise and distraction. Before the main experiment, the researcher provided the instructions to the participant, who subsequently performed a familiarization block composed of 5 trials. Familiarization trials were similar to the main experimental trials (Figure 1b, see below). Participants were given the option of repeating the familiarization block to ensure they fully understood the task before taking part in the main experiment. Data from the familiarization block were excluded from the analysis. The main experiment used the N-Item Delayed Reproduction Task (Herbst et al., 2025). The task consists of four successive phases (encoding, retention, reproduction, and feedback, REF to Figure). Each trial began with a white fixation cross displayed at the center of the screen for 500 ms after which a red cross signaled the encoding phase. Following the presentation of the target interval, the red cross turned yellow to indicate the beginning of the retention period. Following the retention period, a green cross appeared on the screen and incited participants to reproduce the target interval. Participants were instructed to reproduce the durations as precisely as possible and start their reproduction as quickly as possible. The duration reproduction was performed using the left arrow key, with two key presses for a single duration (one initiating the interval, the second terminating it). After completing the reproduction, participants pressed the right arrow key to proceed to the feedback period. Three types of feedback were provided: if participants produced the incorrect number of keypresses (i.e., one/three keypresses for a single duration), 'Clicks incorrect' (meaning 'wrong clicks' in French) was displayed on the screen. These trials were labelled as missed, and repeated at the end of the block. When the correct number of keypresses was made, feedback was presented through one black vertical bar. The direction of the bar indicated whether the reproduced duration was underestimated (down) or overestimated (up), and the bar's height represented the magnitude of the relative temporal reproduction error (Figure 1b). If the relative error of each reproduction was less than 0.1, the message 'BRAVO!!!' appeared on the screen. Experiment comprised 2 blocks of 40 trials each. Each block lasted around 7 minutes. Between blocks, participants took a break of a self-determined length. Overall, data collection lasted around 15 minutes.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted in Python, R (R Core Team, 2022) within the RStudio environment, employing the R packages MASS (Venables & Ripley, 2002) lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), and emmeans (Lenth & Russell V, 2020). We examined the distribution of all behavioral performance indices from the final datasets. For the coefficient of variation (CV), relRP, and RT, statistical analyses indicated that the data followed a positive-skewed gamma distribution. This supported the use of a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a gamma distribution and log-link functions. We applied these models in all relevant analyses (see Supplemental Table for model details). For all GLMMs in Experiment 1, item duration, retention interval, and their ratio were dummy-coded with 0.52 s, 1.04 s and 1 as their respective reference categories. To account for inter-individual variability, participants were included as a random intercept in the model (Kenneth Knoblauch & Laurence T, 2012). The significance of each fixed effect and their interaction was assessed using likelihood ratio tests (LRT), in which a model containing the term of interest was compared against a reduced model without it, evaluated against a Chi-square (χ²) distribution. AIC was additionally used to compare model fit, penalizing for model complexity, such that the model with the lowest AIC and significant LRT was retained as the best-fitting model for each analysis. Fixed effects were tested using T-ratios with Satterthwaite-approximated degrees of freedom. Follow-up simple contrasts were estimated using the emmeans package and evaluated using z tests. The resulting p values were Bonferroni-adjusted across the combined family of contrasts. A full description of each model selection is provided in Supplementary Tables 1 2 3 4.

Central tendency and scalar properties of 1-item delayed reproduction

We examined the temporal reproduction for each time interval across all experimental conditions (Figure 2b) to ensure that participants complied with task instructions. We observed that participants' reproduced durations monotonically increased with the item durations while showing the expected under-reproduction of longer durations (Petzschner & Glasauer, 2011; Vierordt, 1868). To assess the central tendency, we examined the effect of item duration (0.52 s, 1.04 s and 2.08 s) on relRP and CV using GLMMs. Our results showed a main effect of item duration on reIRP (b = 0.177, SE = 0.042, t = 4.236, p < .001, 95% CI [0.095, 0.285]; Table S1, Figure 2b) indicating that relRP decreased significantly with item duration, in agreement with the central tendency effect. Our results also showed a main effect of item duration on CV (b = -0.06, SE = 0.013, t = -4.683, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.085, -0.035]; Table S2, Figure 2b) indicating that CV decreased with item duration. Because these values are averaged across RI and Ratio, we examine this deviation from the scalar property separately for each condition in the next section.

Precision decreases with smaller ratios

To investigate how the length of retention interval (RI) and the ratio (TI/RT) affected the precision of temporal reproduction for a single time interval, we analyzed CVs using GLMMs. First, we found a main effect of RI (b = 0.135, SE = 0.047, t = 2.848, p < .005, 95% CI [0.042, 0.228]; Table S2; Figure 2c, left panel) indicating that CV was larger for the long retention interval compared to the short retention interval. Second, we found a main effect of Ratio on the CVs of reproduced intervals (b = -0.137, SE = 0.047, t = -2.883, p < .005, 95% CI [-0.230, -0.044]; Table S2; Figure 2c, left panel) indicating that CV was lower for a ratio of 0.5 compared to a Ratio of 1. However, we found no interactions between the length of the retention intervals and the ratios. This confirms that the precision of the reproduced time interval decreases (CVs increase) with longer absolute retention intervals and smaller ratios (i.e., shorter relative retention intervals). These observations thus support both our Decay and Interference working hypotheses.

Both the length of the retention interval and the Ratio affect retrieval speed (RTs)

To investigate how the length of the retention interval and the Ratio affected retrieval speed from WM, we analyzed participants' RT using GLMMs. First, we found a main effect of the RI (b = -0.084, SE = 0.023, t = -3.603, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.130, -0.038]; Table S3; Figure 2c, right panel): participants responded faster after the long retention interval compared to the short retention interval. Second, we found a main effect of Ratio on RT (b = -0.245, SE = 0.023, t = -10.522, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.291, -0.199]; Table S3; Figure 2c, right panel), indicating that RT was slower for Ratio = 0.5 compared to Ratio = 1. We also found a RI × Ratio interaction (b = 0.135, SE = 0.033, t = 4.094, p < .001, 95% CI [0.070, 0.199]; Table S3; Figure 2c, right panel). The interaction was decomposed using follow-up simple contrasts. P values were Bonferroni-adjusted across the contrasts. These analyses revealed that the effect of Ratio was reversed across retention intervals: when the retention interval was short (b = -0.084, SE = 0.023, t = -3.603, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.107, -0.061]), RT was faster for Ratio = 0.5 compared to Ratio = 1; to the contrary, when the retention interval was long (b = 0.051, SE = 0.023, t = 2.186, p < .001, 95% CI [ 0.028, 0.074]), RT was slower for Ratio = 0.5 compared with for Ratio = 1. This pattern of RTs results differed from our initial working hypothesis.

Discussion

Experiment 1 examined how the duration of the retention interval affects the maintenance of a single time interval in WM. We contrasted two possible effects: the decay model, manipulated through the length of the retention interval, and the interference model, manipulated through the ratio. Temporal WM performance was assessed by CV and RT.

Our single-item reproduction task showed that participants performed the timing tasks in a manner consistent with Vierordt's law (Petzschner & Glasauer, 2011; Vierordt, 1868). Additionally, we observed that the precision of temporal reproduction decreased with longer retention intervals: this is consistent with the Decay WH according to which the precision of an item held in WM decreases with the passage time. We also found that the precision of temporal reproduction was lower for Ratio = 1 compared with Ratio = 0.5: this observation aligns well with the Interference WH according to which the interference between the target interval and the retention interval affects performance. Thus, temporal WM displays characteristics of both decay and interference. The effects on RT were not in full accordance with our initial working hypothesis. We observed that RTs fastened as retention intervals lengthened, which contradicts the predictions of the Decay WH. Rather, we interpret these effects as evidence for a temporal expectancy effect (A. Nobre et al., 2007): given the task demands, participants could continuously predict the arrival time of the 'Go' signal (i.e., the green cue prompting the start of the reproduction phase). Consequently, longer retention periods increased the occurrence probability of 'Go' signal, allowing for faster reproduction. The effect of Ratio on RT exhibited a more complex pattern: in partial accordance with the Interference WH, RT for short retention interval (1.04s) was slower for Ratio = 1 as compared to Ratio = 0.5, whereas RT for the long retention interval (2.08s) was faster for Ratio = 1 as compared to Ratio = 0.5. We propose that this crossover interaction reflects two concurrent mechanisms: the first one is a ceiling effect when retention intervals are sufficiently long leaving little space for retrieval speed to improve; the second is an increase in the memory resources required by longer target intervals. Although WM capacity limits did not affect the CV, the target interval was longer under the long retention interval, which may have required more memory resources. Whether such WM capacity constraints might influence the RT remains unclear.

Overall, we found in Experiment 1 that the duration of the retention interval, and the ratio between the time interval and the retention interval jointly influence temporal WM. These observations provide evidence that both the Decay WH and the Interference WH operate concurrently (Barrouillet & Camos, 2021; Lemaire & Portrat, 2018). Crucially, we observe that the interference effect was manifested by temporal distinctiveness with bigger interferences when ratio was equal to 1 as compared to when ratio was equal to 0.5. Together, these findings extend the role of WM in the retention of temporal intervals by showing that the temporal properties of ongoing processing can influence memory for duration. The observed ratio-based interference indicates that temporal WM cannot be understood solely as the passive maintenance of an encoded duration; rather, temporal information arising throughout the WM process must also be considered. This interpretation is consistent with temporalcontext models, in which temporal discriminability depends on the separation between events on a logarithmically compressed timeline (Howard & Kahana, 2002; Howard & Shankar, 2018).

Stimuli

The stimuli and apparatus were identical to those of Experiment 1 with the exception of the number of time intervals, and the addition of attentional cues. In Experiment 2, we presented temporal sequences composed of two empty time intervals delineated by three pure tones (1 kHz, 50 ms duration, including 10 ms onset and offset ramping). Each temporal sequence was composed of a short (1.04 s) and a long (2.08 s) time interval. The time intervals could be presented in two possible orders: the short interval followed by the long one or the long interval followed by the short one (SL or LS, respectively; Figure 1c).

Design and Procedure

We used a Cued 2-Item Delayed Reproduction Task (Figure 1c). The procedure was adapted from Experiment 1, with the same four phases (encoding, retention, reproduction, and feedback). To this design, we added a cueing manipulation, which allows to manipulate internal and external attention (Panichello & Buschman, 2021). Specifically, a cue indicated which of the two time intervals in the sequence participants had to reproduce. The possible cues were: '1', '2', or 'tout' (the 1 st item in the sequence, the 2 nd item in the sequence, or the whole sequence, respectively). The cue lasted 500 ms and was either shown before the encoding (pro-cueing) or after the retention period (retro-cueing). The cueing direction (pro/retro) was manipulated per block. Experiment 2 comprised one pro-cueing block and one retro-cueing block. Each block consisted of 120 trials each, and lasted approximately 30 minutes. Between blocks, participants took a break of self-determined length. Overall, data collection lasted around 60 minutes. The order of the pro-cueing and retro-cueing blocks was randomized across participants.

Dependent measures

The dependent measures CV, relRP and RT were identical to those of Experiment 1. CV and relRP for a sequence were calculated separately for each item and subsequently averaged to obtain a single sequence-level measure. However, this average procedure was performed only as sanity check; in all other analyses, the two items in the sequence were treated as separate measurements.

Participant exclusion and outlier trials

The participant removal procedure followed the same steps as Experiment 1. For each participant, the Sn scale estimator was calculated separately for wrong click rates and RTs. Participants were classified as outliers if their wrong click rates and relative errors exceeded four times the corresponding Sn scale estimator (Sn > 4). Outlier values included wrong click rates of 15.3%, 15.5%, 8.8%, and a relative error of -24.8%. After excluding one participant with poor performance due to exceeding the outlier criteria on wrong click rates and relative errors, we examined the distribution of reproduced items. The density plots (see Figure S1a) show bimodal distributions, revealing that on some trials, participants reproduced the duration of the un-cued item, rather than the cued one (e.g., the short item when the long item was cued). These trials might contaminate the measurement of working memory performance for the cued item. Thus, we marked and removed trials as mis-recalled if their reIRP exceeded three times the Sn (Sn > 3) for that item. On average, this resulted in an exclusion of approximately 3.19% of trials per participant.

Previous studies showed that responses in the delayed visual working memory task are influenced by three components (Bays & Husain, 2008): the target responses, the non-target responses, and random guesses. We observed non-target error when the analysis included outlier trials and combined data from all participants (Figure S1a). To estimate the contribution of each response source in our task, we fitted a first threecomponent mixture model (Model 1, following Bays et al., 2009) with the probability of a given temporal reproduction (𝑝(𝑥)) arising from a weighted mixture of three components: recall of the target item, recall of a non-target item, and random guessing:

The target reproduction was modeled with a Gaussian distribution centered on the target value ( 𝑡𝑑 ), with weight ( 1 -𝑝 𝑛𝑡 -𝑝 𝑔 ). Non-target reproduction was modeled with a Gaussian centered on the non-target value (𝑛𝑜𝑛_𝑡𝑑), with weight (𝑝 𝑛𝑡 ). Random guesses were modeled with a uniform distribution between the maximum and the minimum of reproduction with weight (𝑝 𝑔 ). The mixture weights sum to one. The model has three free parameters: a standard deviation (𝜎) shared by both gaussian components, 𝑝 𝑛𝑡 as the proportion of trials in which participants reproduced the duration of the non-target item (that is, the probability of 'swapping'), and the guess rate (𝑝 𝑔 ). To capture the scalar property and central tendency of temporal reproduction, we defined a second model (Model 2), as the two-component mixture model for time intervals:

As in model 1, the target reproduction was modeled with a Gaussian distribution centered on the target value (𝑡𝑑), but this time with weight (1 -𝑝 𝑛𝑡 ). Non-target reproduction was modeled with a Gaussian centered on the non-target value (𝑛𝑜𝑛_𝑡𝑑), with weight (𝑝 𝑛𝑡 ), and no random guess. As participants' reproduction errors increased with item duration, we included an offset bias (𝑏) to accommodate the overall bias rate for each duration. Thus, the mean reproduction of the target duration is 𝑡𝑑(1 + 𝑏) and of the non-target duration is 𝑛𝑡𝑑(1 + 𝑏). Furthermore, in accordance with the scalar property, the standard deviation of the reproduced duration increases with its mean, so we included a Weber fraction (𝑤𝑓) to accommodate this feature.

Thus, 𝒩( 𝑥 | 𝑡𝑑(1 + 𝑏), 𝑤𝑓 ⋅ 𝑡𝑑(1 + 𝑏) ) is a normal distribution with standard deviation (𝑤𝑓 𝑡𝑑(1 + 𝑏)) for target duration. Here, three free parameters were: a shared Weber fraction (𝑤𝑓) across both duration reproductions, 𝑝 𝑛𝑡 as in model 1, and the bias for reproduction errors. All model parameters were estimated by minimizing the negative log-likelihood using the basin-hopping global optimization algorithm (Wales & Doye, 1997) as implemented in the SciPy package in python (Virtanen et al., 2020). Local optimization at each step used the Sequential Least Squares Programming method (SLSQP) with parameter bounds and mixture weight constraints. Initial parameter values and bounds were specified as described above. Model evaluation metrics were calculated using scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011). Models were compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC; Akaike, 1974) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC; Schwarz, 1978), with lower values indicating better fit. A difference of ΔAIC > 10 was taken as strong evidence for the better-fitting model (Burnham & Anderson, 2004). Goodness of fit was assessed using 3-fold across validation. Parameter uncertainty for the selected model was estimated by nonparametric bootstrap resampling (Wichmann & Hill, 2001). Trials were resampled with replacement and the models were refit to the resampled data over 1000 iterations. We obtained the bootstrapped distributions of the maximum likelihood estimates of the free parameters for each experimental condition, enabling crosscondition comparison. We hypothesized that temporal sequences would produce transposition errors. These errors were quantified by the estimated probability of reporting an item from an incorrect serial position (𝑝 𝑛𝑡 ). We further expected that the probability of such errors would wary with respect to the attentional conditions. The mixture model characterizes each item individually, without distinguishing item attributes (duration, position) and attention manipulations (cueing direction, number of cued items). However, swap errors reflect confusion between item duration and positions, and their likelihood may vary with item properties. It remains unclear how item property affects swap errors and how attention influences these errors. Hence, by comparing 𝑝 𝑛𝑡 under different item property and attention conditions, we exploratively examine the interaction between item duration and item position on swap errors, and further investigate the attention modulation at each item level. Data were analyzed in two steps. First, trials were collapsed across cueing direction and item duration, hereafter, termed merged conditions (pro-cueing vs retro-cueing; short item vs long item; Figure S1b) to create a 2*2 design, reducing the 16 conditions to four aggregate blocks. This was done to compare candidate models and select the besting fitting model with sufficient trials counts per cell. Second, the winning model was fitted separately to all 16 conditions to examine how the free parameters varied as a function of each experimental manipulation (procueing vs retro-cueing; single item vs whole sequence; short item vs long item; 1 st position vs 2 nd position see Figure S1b). Bootstrap contrasts were conducted on the swap error rate (𝑝 𝑛𝑡 ) in a 2*2 design cross the cueing direction (pro-cueing vs retro-cueing) and number of cued items (one vs two items). All p-values were FDR-corrected across six contrasts (Benjamini-Hochberg, FDR = 0.05). All results were in a significantly wider 95% confidence interval.

Discussion

Experiment 2 explored how attention dynamically modulates temporal sequences in WM, by manipulating cueing direction (pro / retro) and the number of to-be retrieved items. We found that pro-cueing yielded overall higher CVs and faster RTs compared with retro-cueing, and higher CVs for single-item compared with for the whole-sequence. Our central result in Experiment 2 is that attention modulated the item position × item duration interaction: higher CV for the second long durations, and this was only observed in retrocueing, irrespective of single item or whole sequence. The reIRP showed a primacy effect of long duration only for retro-cueing a single item. However, reIRP also showed a recency effect for short durations for both pro-and retro-cueing, irrespective of the number of cued items. The computational modeling results were broadly consisted with these findings, with shortlong sequence mostly affected by cueing direction and number of items. For RTs, however, a different pattern was observed: a primacy effect was found irrespective of item duration, with a larger effect for retro-cueing than for pro-cueing. The following discussion will first address the attention modulation of the item position × item duration interaction observed on precision (CV), accuracy (reIRP) and in the behavioral model, and then turn to retrieval speed (RT).

Attention improves temporal WM through interactions with item position and item duration Attention attenuates the duration x position interference on precision

At the sequence level, participants performed the timing tasks in a manner consistent with Vierordt's law (Petzschner & Glasauer, 2011; Vierordt, 1868). Additionally, the cueing manipulation successfully and selectively modulated recall precision: CV was larger (lower precision) for single items compared with the whole sequence, and for retro-cueing compared with pro-cueing.

As predicted, we found that attention modulated the duration x position interaction. Subsequent analysis showed that the interaction was only significant under retro-cueing not pro-cueing. Consistent with the dynamic resource theory (Ma et al., 2014), this may reflect the effect of external attention on WM encoding. Based on retrieval probability and WM load, the experimental conditions with respect to external attention can be ordered from the lowest to the highest memory encoding priority. Starting from the lowest priority, retro-cueing, and irrespective of the number of cued items, participants likely encoded the full sequence but with limited and unevenly distributed resources, leaving temporal representations more vulnerable to interference. Next, under pro-cueing for the whole sequence, participants could allocate their full external attention to whole sequence during encoding. In turn, this could have contributed to time representations being robust enough to resist interference. Finally, in the pro-cueing single-item condition, no interaction was found possibly due to a ceiling effect where precision was already near-optimal. Nonetheless, retro-cueing showed a lower precision compared with pro-cueing (specifically for the second long duration), suggesting that external attention in pro-cueing can help stabilize WM representation. This retro-cueing specific interaction reflected a primacy effect for long intervals, showing the interplay between temporal distinctiveness and primacy effects. Shorts intervals may be sufficiently distinctive and robust to resist interference (Y et al., 2017), yielding no significant effect of position. The present results offer one potential explanation for such mixed findings for temporal WM. Our results converge with a previous study (Herbst et al., 2025), which reported inconsistent primacy effects across different number of items in the temporal sequence, that the authors attributed to either dynamic resource or temporal contingency.

Here, by manipulating item duration and attention, our results showed that the two may not be mutually exclusive and rather work together. Interestingly, retro-cueing single items showed less precision compared with cueing the whole sequence, regardless of item position or duration. This finding contrasts with our prediction that internal attention would resist output interference. It also contrasts with previous findings (Herbst et al., 2025) where precision decreased with number of items when WM load was manipulated. Here, WM load was held constant but differences did arise from attentional item selection. Importantly, the result also runs counter to the visual domain, where single-item report is more precise compared with whole-sequence report (McAteer et al., 2023). We interpret this as a temporal contiguity effect (Healey et al., 2019; Kahana, 1996): intervals in a temporal sequence may support each other in memory, such that isolating a single item from its temporal context comes with a cost in precision. This suggests that temporal WM is shaped by the local temporal structure of the sequence as would be predicted by temporal context models (Burgess & Hitch, 2005; Howard & Kahana, 2002).

Taken together, the duration × position interaction varied as a function of both cueing direction and number of cued items, pointing to a conjunctive relationship between interference and attention. This pattern is discussed further in the General Discussion.

External attention lengthens perceived duration following primacy gradient

As predicted by central tendency, we found an overestimation of short durations and an underestimation of long durations. Short durations were reproduced longer when cued as single items than when the whole sequence was cued. They were also reproduced longer in the first than in the second position. This is in line with our expectation that greater external attention would lead to longer perceived durations (Fortin & Schweickert, 2016; Polti et al., 2018). We interpret this position effect as reflecting the primacy gradient in sequence memory (Page & Norris, 1998): the first position received more attentional resources inducing a longer perceived duration compared with the second position (Rose & Summers, 1995). However, a more complex interaction was found for long durations; longer reproduced duration in the first position compared with the second position was significant only for retro-cued single items. Although this pattern resembles the trend observed for short durations, we interpret it as a combined effect of the lowest temporal distinctiveness (long-2 nd ) and the least external attention (retro-cueing single item), resulting in the shortest perceived duration in the second position, rather than reflecting higher external attention at the first position. A detailed discussion is provided in the General Discussion.

Attention modulates the duration x position interaction on swap errors

To examine transposition effects, we fit an augmented version of the memory mixture model (Bays et al., 2009), including scalar property and central tendency (Petzschner & Glasauer, 2011; Vierordt, 1868) to our data. This model fit better than the original, suggesting that swap errors could be identified in WM performance for sequences of time intervals like visual items (Bays et al., 2009), but require taking into account timing-specific parameters. Model parameters were primarily compared using 𝑝 𝑛𝑡 which provides an index of the probability of swap errors (higher 𝑝 𝑛𝑡 , higher swap errors). The bimodal fitting for the long-1 st interval, when recalling the whole sequence irrespective of cueing direction, indicated that participants' reproduction did not exhibit swap errors in these conditions. Additionally, when looking into all the conditions, the effects of attention varied across temporal distinctiveness (temporal distinctiveness: long-1 st > short-1 st > short-2 nd > long-2 nd ). No swap errors were observed at the highest temporal distinctiveness (long-1 st ), whereas no attention modulation (short-2 nd ) or main effect of cueing direction (short-1 st : less error for pro-cueing compared with retro-cueing) were observed at the intermediate temporal distinctiveness. Importantly, an interaction between the two attention factors (long-2 nd : cueing direction and number of cued items) was observed at the lowest temporal distinctiveness with less error for pro-cueing compared with retro-cueing, whereas higher error was observed for the whole sequence compared to a single item when pro-cueing, and the reversed pattern when retro-cueing. This pattern suggests that attentional influence on swap errors becomes more complex as temporal distinctiveness decreases. Rather than exerting independent effects, the two attention factors may interact more when temporal distinctiveness is low. In line with dynamic resource model (Ma et al., 2014), durations, like visual items, thus show mis-binding yielding interference on swap error (Gorgoraptis et al., 2011) modulated by attention. More specifically, the second long durations were encoded with more swap errors, which is also under the modulation of attention. Taken together, these finding suggest the attention affected the transposition errors in WM.

Compressed replay at retrieval and slower reproduction

We found that participants took significantly longer time to initiate responses for the second item compared to both the first item and the entire sequence, regardless of cueing direction and order. This finding contradicts the well-established size effect, where response time typically increases with greater WM load (Sternberg, 1966). Moreover, the slower recall of the second item compared to the first item and the whole sequence suggests an order-related cost. Notably, some participants explicitly reported that they needed to recall the first item in order to retrieve the second item, indicating a possible sequential retrieval strategy. This effect was not affected by the duration of first item (short vs. long). Hence, it may indicate the compressed nature of temporal representations before reproduction. This is in line with recent evidence of temporal compression during internal speech planning suggests that sequential information may be retrieved more efficiently than previously assumed (Kurth-Nelson et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019; Wimmer et al., 2020). This suggests that the retrieval or preparation of sequential information in WM can occur faster than the overt production through compression.

General discussion

Our study systematically tested the Decay and the Interference WH for a single time interval (Experiment 1) and a sequence of two-time intervals (Experiment 2) in temporal WM. Our results robustly support the Interference WH across multiple measures. In Experiment 1, precision improved with a higher ratio between the retention interval and the target duration.

In Experiment 2, a primacy effect emerged for long but not for short time intervals on both precision and accuracy. The mixture model applied in Experiment 2 further revealed complex transposition error patterns. Together, these findings are consistent with interference operating through temporal distinctiveness from temporal context and confirm the possibility of shared mechanisms between time and memory. As shown in Figure 6, the retention interval affects temporal distinctiveness as implicit timing (A. Nobre et al., 2007). The size of the interference effect was predicted by the ratio (rather than the absolute duration), consistent with temporal information continuously represented in WM and on a log scale, as proposed by the temporal context models (Howard & Kahana, 2002; Howard & Shankar, 2018). The size of the interference effect was predicted by the ratio (rather than the absolute duration), as expected under the interference hypothesis. As hypothesized, this pattern is compatible with the ratio model proposed by Brown et al. (2007), which attributes ratio-dependent interference to a logarithmically compressed temporal representation. It is also compatible with temporal-context accounts that posit a logarithmically compressed temporal representation (Howard & Kahana, 2002; Howard & Shankar, 2018), although the present result does not provide direct evidence for such a representational scale. Importantly, attention consistently modulated the inference effect in Experiment 2 through dynamic resource allocation, with effects observed across CV, reIRP, and 𝑝 𝑛𝑡 captured by the mixture model. Based on temporal distinctiveness, the item position × item duration interaction can be ordered from the lowest to the highest memory interference (long-1 st > short-1 st > short-2 nd > long-2 nd ). Pro-cueing improved precision, lengthened relative reproduction and diminished the swap errors than retro-cueing for the single-cued second long interval which has the lowest temporal distinctiveness, suggesting that external attention serves multiple functions, with differential susceptibility across distinct aspect of WM performance. This can be accounted for by the latest perspective on visual WM representations (Bays et al., 2024), stating that these WM representations encode not only their features but also uncertainty, relying on a limited resource that is flexibly allocated according to behavioral goals. Under this framework, precision, bias, and swap errors can be understood as dissociable manifestations of the same underlying noisy representation. Thus, the converging behavioral evidence from these behavioral indices is consistent with the limited-resource model where attentional allocation controls the cognitive resource under different temporal distinctiveness conditions (Bays & Taylor, 2018; Ma et al., 2014).

Internal attention, however, showed an unexpected pattern: retro-cueing a single item produced bigger swap errors and lower precision compared to cueing the whole sequence. This contrasts with the typical retro-cueing benefit reported in visual WM (Griffin & Nobre, 2003; Landman et al., 2003; Souza & Oberauer, 2016), where the cue is presented during retention with enough time before retrieval. Nobre and Gresch (2025) proposed that internal attention actively selects, protects and integrates presentations. However, in our experiment, participants need to initiate their reproduction as soon as they see the retro-cue. Hence, the poorer performance for retro-cueing a single item may reflect insufficient time for the abovementioned protective processes, as well as a cost of separating an item from its temporal context after selection. It is worth noting that the interplay between temporal context and attention modulation was also supported by the evidence from reaction time. For example, while pro-cueing improved the retrieval speed overall, the second item's reproduction needed longer reaction time irrespective of the length of the first item, suggesting a compressed temporal representation which is consistent with the previous results.

Limitations

One limitation of the present study is that the explored factors of interest included only two levels. In Experiment 1, we investigated two ratios, limiting our ability to explore trends in effect variations. Future research may increase the number of ratio levels to gain a more comprehensive understanding. In Experiment 2, the use of only two items might have limited the generalizability of our findings regarding serial effects. Sequences with more items would be needed to fully examine the primacy gradient and its interaction with attention. Besides, the retro-cueing may have been insufficient for exploring internal attention, resulting in a complex pattern of precision and reaction time. Future work should therefore examine a wider range of levels of durations and number of items to better characterize the function of temporal WM, and could further investigate internal attention by manipulating timing and content of retro-cueing.

Conclusions

To summarize, we found that external and internal attention-modulated interference in temporal WM. Attentional resources help mitigate interference, but show differential effectiveness for distinct aspects of WM performance with varying susceptibility, which interacts with temporal context (item duration and item position). This observation extends the limited resource model of temporal WM by demonstrating that continuous WM representations are dynamically modulated by temporal distinctiveness through attentional allocation, as reflected in measures of temporal precision, relative reproduction, and swap errors. During Encoding (red), participants listened to one target interval (TI) demarcated by two pure tones. Retention (yellow) could last 1.04 s or 2.08 s and was tested within blocks. Reproduction (green) instructed participants to reproduce the TI as precisely and as quickly as possible. After the reproduction, a bar plot informed participants about their relative temporal reproduction error for each item in the sequence: an upward bar indicated overestimation; a downward bar indicated underestimation. Along the timeline of a trial, each panel illustrates what participants saw at each stage. The four vertical solid lines represent the four main phases of the trial, and the dashed line indicates a cueing manipulation that varied in its direction. Encoding (red): participants listened to the sequence of time intervals demarcated by pure tones. The sequence could be LS (top) or SL (bottom). Retention (yellow): The retention period following encoding was constant across trials and set to 3.12 seconds. The cueing could occur either before the encoding (pro-cueing) or after the retention period (retro-cueing). Reproduction (green): participants were instructed to reproduce the cued item (1 or 2) or the whole sequence (tout) as precisely and as quickly as possible. After the production (green), a bar plot informed participants about their relative temporal reproduction error for each item in the sequence. At the aggregate sequence level (collapsed across item position), we expected attentional manipulation (number of items and cueing direction) to facilitate a stable WM representation with more precise and faster reproduction. We also expected a longer relative reproduction for procueing compared with retro-cueing because of higher external attention expected to induce longer perceived duration.
