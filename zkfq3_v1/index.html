What we found

We found 15 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. This module retrieves information from repositories.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Many adults with dyslexia understand texts well despite persistent word reading difficulties, and sentence context has been proposed as a compensatory resource. We tested whether contextual facilitation during word reading reflects a benefit for reading comprehension, or reliance on context driven by slow word identification or by limited semantic knowledge.

Fifty-one French-speaking young adults (22 with dyslexia, 29 typical readers) read a target word aloud after four words forming a related or a control context, in semantic and syntactic conditions. Reading comprehension (without a time limit) and reading efficiency were assessed with standardized tests.

Dyslexia status was strongly associated with reading efficiency (r = -.70) and only weakly with reading comprehension (r = -.22). Context facilitated naming in both groups. In proportion to naming latency, an equivalence test excluded a group difference in facilitation larger than about 12 ms; in raw milliseconds, facilitation was larger in adults with dyslexia, who were slower overall. Semantic facilitation was larger in readers with lower reading comprehension (27 vs. 14 ms at 1 SD below and above the mean), mainly among those with the lowest scores. This relation was specific to semantic context and held when reading efficiency and dyslexia status were taken into account. Reading efficiency did not modulate proportional facilitation, but the comprehension and efficiency slopes did not differ significantly. We suggest that semantic facilitation indexes reliance on context, not a benefit for comprehension, and that compensation in adult dyslexia should be tested in individuals, against reading comprehension.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1. Reading comprehension in adults with dyslexia

Developmental dyslexia is characterized by persistent difficulties with decoding and recognizing words, which are often attributed to deficits in phonological processing that result in inefficient orthographic processing (Snowling & Melby-Lervåg, 2016; Ziegler et al., 2009). These difficulties often persist into adulthood, and adults with dyslexia still show weaknesses in reading fluency, phonological processing, and spelling after entering higher education (Cavalli, Brèthes, et al., 2024). Nevertheless, persistent word-level reading difficulties can coexist with relatively intact reading comprehension, as observed in university students with dyslexia when reading comprehension is assessed without time constraints (Brèthes et al., 2022). In a person-centered analysis of 199 adolescents and young adults assessed with the same test, reading comprehension profiles did not follow diagnostic status (Lefèvre et al., 2026): a profile with weak text reading efficiency and spelling but preserved reading comprehension included 38 of the 104 participants with dyslexia, whereas the reverse profile included 48 of the 95 typical readers. These findings raise the question of which resources support reading comprehension despite ongoing difficulties in word recognition.

According to the Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer, 1986), reading comprehension depends on both word recognition and linguistic comprehension. In university students with and without dyslexia, word reading, decoding, and spelling contributed to text reading fluency, whereas oral language comprehension, vocabulary, and general knowledge contributed to reading comprehension (Brèthes et al., 2022). These linguistic resources could support reading comprehension when word recognition remains slow or effortful.

Here we asked whether the use of sentence context during word reading is such a resource, that is, whether the readers who rely most on context understand texts best.

1.2. Compensation in adults with dyslexia

In university students with dyslexia, the contribution of these resources is usually examined within the framework of compensation, defined as the processes that contribute to an improved behavioral presentation despite persisting core deficits at the cognitive or neurobiological level (Livingston & Happé, 2017; see Colé et al., 2026, for a review). For example, university students with dyslexia did not differ significantly from typical readers in vocabulary breadth and performed better in vocabulary depth (Cavalli et al., 2016), and their morphological abilities were relatively preserved (Cavalli, Duncan, et al., 2017). In adolescents with and without dyslexia, listening comprehension was associated with reading comprehension that exceeds what decoding difficulties would predict, and vocabulary and socioeconomic status (SES) were indirectly associated with it (Lefèvre et al., 2025).

However, the availability of these resources does not establish how they are used during reading or whether their use contributes to reading comprehension. We therefore distinguish three criteria: availability (a resource is preserved, e.g., vocabulary), reliance (readers use it during reading), and benefit (this reliance improves the outcome that the resource is assumed to protect). Reliance and benefit need not go together. A reader may rely on a resource because word identification is slow or because other knowledge is weak, and in that case reliance signals a need without showing that the need is met. Under the benefit criterion, among readers with comparable word reading difficulties, those who rely more on the resource should understand texts better. Reliance may also have a cost, in the form of extra time or effort to reach the same outcome (Hebert et al., 2018). To our knowledge, few studies have related an online measure of reliance on context to reading comprehension in the same readers.

1.3. Reliance on context during word reading

One mechanism through which readers may use context is linguistic prediction. The preceding context generates graded and probabilistic expectations about upcoming information, which need not concern the exact identity of the next word (Ferreira & Lowder, 2016; Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016). For example, a semantic context can constrain the meaning or semantic category of an upcoming word, whereas a syntactic context can constrain its grammatical category or position within a sentence. The two kinds of facilitation differ in their sensitivity to presentation time and are not reliably correlated across individuals (Gavard & Ziegler, 2022 , 2024), so they should be examined separately.

Whether linguistic prediction provides a compensatory resource in dyslexia remains an open question. In spoken language, adults with dyslexia used grammatical gender to anticipate a forthcoming referent, but later than typical readers (Huettig & Brouwer, 2015), and they were facilitated by more probable sentence endings in strongly but not in weakly constraining contexts (Engelhardt et al., 2021). In reading, Corkett and Parrila (2008) found that university students with a history of reading difficulties, unlike their peers, showed significant inhibition when a target word was preceded by an incongruent sentence, whereas facilitation by a congruent sentence did not differ significantly between groups.

Predictive processing in dyslexia thus seems neither absent nor fully preserved, but these studies compared groups and did not relate the use of context to reading comprehension.

To investigate the contribution of semantic and syntactic context to word reading, Gavard and Ziegler (2022) developed a predictive reading task in which participants silently read a sequence of words before naming the final target word aloud. Gavard et al. (2025) used this paradigm in an fMRI study of university students with and without dyslexia, expecting greater reliance on semantic and syntactic predictions in students with dyslexia.

Behaviorally, facilitation was significant in typical readers in both conditions but not in students with dyslexia, although the two groups did not differ significantly in the size of facilitation. In students with dyslexia, syntactic prediction correlated positively with reading ability, measured with the Alouette test (r = 0.49). The better readers showed the larger facilitation, a direction opposite to the one expected if context served to offset slow word identification. The authors interpreted these results as suggesting that readers with dyslexia use linguistic context to understand sentences rather than to identify words: "Compensation is suggested to operate at the sentence level rather than the word level" (Gavard et al., 2025, p. 27).

We refer to this view as the compensation account, under which reliance on context supports reading comprehension. Our task measures the use of context at the word level, and it tests this account under the assumption that readers who use context to understand sentences also use it when they name a word that follows a related context. Under this assumption, the account predicts that facilitation should be larger in readers with dyslexia and in those who understand texts best. Two questions remained open. Reading comprehension, by which the authors defined compensated readers, was not measured, and a non-significant group difference does not indicate whether, and by how much, the groups differ in facilitation.

Larger contextual facilitation, however, does not necessarily indicate more efficient reading, and readers differ in how much they rely on context (Wong et al., 2025). According to the interactive-compensatory model, readers whose word identification is slow or inaccurate rely more on context, whereas skilled readers identify words rapidly with or without context (Stanovich, 1980). This prediction is consistent with the lexical quality hypothesis for the orthographic and phonological constituents of word knowledge (Perfetti, 2007). We refer to this view as the word-identification account. Because slow word identification is the persistent difficulty of adults with dyslexia, this account predicts larger facilitation in these readers and, more generally, in readers with lower reading efficiency.

There is less agreement about the relation between contextual facilitation and reading comprehension. In adult readers, predictability effects have been reported to be larger in highly skilled readers, as indexed by literacy and verbal ability, but also in slower readers (Wong et al., 2025). In children, Nation and Snowling (1998) found that readers with dyslexia showed greater contextual facilitation than typical readers, whereas poor comprehenders (i.e., children with poor comprehension despite adequate decoding) showed less facilitation, which suggests a positive relation between facilitation and comprehension.

However, lexical quality also has a semantic constituent (Perfetti, 2007). When the meaning of a word is less precisely represented, its identification should depend more on information outside the word, such as a semantically related context. Consistent with this view, among skilled adult readers, those with less vocabulary knowledge show larger semantic priming for words that are harder to identify (Yap et al., 2009). Because reading comprehension depends in part on vocabulary and general knowledge, readers with lower reading comprehension may have less precise lexical-semantic representations, and their word identification may be facilitated more by a related context. We refer to this view, which predicts a negative relation between facilitation and reading comprehension, as the semantic-knowledge account. It attributes reliance on context to the quality of semantic knowledge, not to orthographic or phonological processing, and it concerns individual differences in reading comprehension rather than dyslexia status. The compensation and semantic-knowledge accounts make opposite predictions for this relation, which makes it the critical test.

1.4. The present study

The present study had three aims. The first aim was to establish whether semantic and syntactic contexts facilitate word naming in adults with and without dyslexia, and whether the size of this facilitation differs between the two groups. The second aim was to test, across the full sample, whether facilitation is modulated by reading comprehension and by reading efficiency, each adjusted for the other. The third aim was to determine whether these modulations are specific to semantic context. Before addressing these aims, we describe how reading efficiency and reading comprehension vary within and between the two groups.

The three accounts make different predictions for four terms of our models. If reliance on context is compensatory (compensation account), then facilitation should be larger in adults with dyslexia (Context × Group) and should increase with reading comprehension (positive Context × Comprehension slope). If reliance on context is driven by slow word identification (word-identification account), then facilitation should also be larger in adults with dyslexia, and it should decrease as reading efficiency increases (negative Context × Reading efficiency slope) in both conditions. If reliance on semantic context is driven by limited semantic knowledge (semantic-knowledge account), then semantic facilitation should decrease as reading comprehension increases (negative Context × Comprehension slope), adjusted for reading efficiency. This relation should be absent for syntactic facilitation (Condition × Context × Comprehension), because a syntactic context constrains the grammatical class of the upcoming word but not its meaning. Because reading comprehension is relatively preserved in university students with dyslexia (Brèthes et al., 2022), this account predicts at most a small group difference in facilitation.

Two features of the design serve these aims. Because a preserved ability or the absence of a group difference is usually inferred from a non-significant result, which cannot distinguish a small effect from a lack of statistical power, we used equivalence tests with a bound taken from an independent sample tested with the same task (Gavard et al., 2025). In addition, because reading comprehension varies widely among readers with and without dyslexia (Lefèvre et al., 2026), we treated reading comprehension and reading efficiency as continuous moderators in the full sample.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Participants

Fifty-four young adults were recruited in Tours, Angers, and Lyon (France) through speech and language therapists, universities, and student health and disability support services.

Participants were students or had graduated within the previous two years, were native speakers of French, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing, and reported no neurological or psychiatric disorder. One participant with dyslexia was excluded during the study because she met an exclusion criterion, and two typical readers were excluded because of missing data, mainly on the reading comprehension test. The final sample comprised 29 typical readers (hereafter, TR; 19 women, 10 men; aged 19-27 years) and 22 adults with dyslexia (hereafter, DR; 16 women, 6 men; aged 18-34 years). Three DR reported attention or hyperactivity difficulties in childhood (self-report, without a verified diagnosis); this information was not used in any analysis. All participants gave oral and written informed consent (see Ethical considerations).

Nineteen DR had received a formal diagnosis of dyslexia between the ages of 7 and 18 years, and three reported persistent reading difficulties without a formal diagnosis. On the French version (Cavalli, Brèthes, et al., 2024) of the Adult Reading History Questionnaire (ARHQ; Lefly & Pennington, 2000), a 23-item self-report questionnaire scored as a proportion of the maximum score (higher scores indicate more reading difficulties), 19 DR scored above the cut-off of .47 established in that study. We kept the groups as initially defined; analyses without the three DR without a formal diagnosis, and the overlap between groups on the published cut-offs, are reported in Online Resource 1.

2.2. Materials

Predictive reading task. The predictive reading task (Gavard & Ziegler, 2022 , 2024) was run online with LabVanced (Finger et al., 2017; Fig. 1). On each trial, a fixation cross (500 ms) was followed by four context words, which participants read silently, and by the target word, which they read aloud. Each word was displayed for 500 ms with an interstimulus interval of 250 ms, naming responses were recorded for 1,500 ms from target onset, and the intertrial interval was 500 ms. Unlike in Gavard et al. (2025), every target was read aloud. In the semantic condition, the context consisted of words from the same semantic field as the target, which made its general category, but not its identity, predictable. In the syntactic condition, the context formed a short, grammatically correct sentence, which constrained the grammatical class and position of the target but not its meaning. The control contexts were unrelated words and the words of the syntactic context in an ungrammatical order (Fig. 1a).

Because the same 80 targets were used in both conditions and both contexts, each participant read each target four times (320 trials per participant; 16,320 trials in total).

Online Resource 1 describes the semantic and syntactic norms of the items. Condition order was counterbalanced across participants, and 49.2% of the targets were first encountered in a related context. Naming onsets were coded with CheckVocal (Protopapas, 2007). Reading and reading-related measures. All participants completed a battery developed and standardized for French-speaking adults with and without dyslexia (Cavalli, Brèthes, et al., 2024). Reading efficiency was assessed with the Alouette test (Lefavrais, 1967), in which participants read aloud a meaningless 265-word text as fast and as accurately as possible within 3 min. The efficiency score was computed as CTL = (A/RT) × 180, where A is the number of words correctly read and RT is the reading time in seconds (Cavalli et al., 2018).

We used this score as the index of reading efficiency because it contributes to the identification of dyslexia in this population (Cavalli, Brèthes, et al., 2024; Cavalli et al., 2018) and was the reading measure of Gavard et al. (2025). In addition, the battery included oneminute word reading, two-minute pseudoword reading, connected-text reading fluency, phoneme segmentation, and spelling to dictation. Verbal reasoning was assessed with the Similarities subtest of the WAIS-IV (Wechsler, 2011), and non-verbal reasoning, online, with the two 9-item short forms of Raven's Standard Progressive Matrices (18 items; Bilker et al., 2012). Socioeconomic status (SES) was estimated with the Hollingshead (1975) index, computed as in Lefèvre et al. (2025): highest parental occupation (nine levels) × 7 + mean parental education (seven levels) × 4, for a possible range of 11 to 91.

Reading comprehension.

We used the reading comprehension test of Brèthes et al. (2022), designed for adults. Participants silently read three printed excerpts from articles in Le Monde about the Great Barrier Reef (about 30 lines each), without a time limit, and the time taken to read the three texts was recorded (text reading time). After each text, they answered eight questions without rereading it: four literal questions and four inferential questions (two text-connecting inferences, which require integrating information within the text, and two knowledge-based inferences, which require background knowledge), half multiple-choice and half open. The experimenter read each printed question aloud and wrote down the answer. Each answer was scored 2 (complete), 1 (incomplete), or 0 (incorrect) (Delem et al., 2025). When a question called for several elements (multiplechoice questions with more than one correct option, open questions asking for a list), 1 point was subtracted for each incorrect element, with a minimum of 0. The questions score (maximum = 48) was our main measure of reading comprehension. The original version of the task also included a graphic organizer, which was not reported in Brèthes et al. (2022): after the three texts, participants stated the issue common to the texts and its causes, consequences, and solutions (graphic organizer score, maximum = 20).

The internal consistency of the questions score was α = .78 in the validation study (Brèthes et al., 2022) and α = .37 in the present sample. This lower value partly reflects the narrower spread of scores (SD = 4.1 and 4.6 in the two groups, compared with 5.5 and 5.8 in the validation study) and the heterogeneity of the items, which assess literal and inferential comprehension of three texts (Online Resource 1). We therefore examined whether the results held with the graphic organizer score, an independent measure from the same test, and with a score computed without three items close to floor (mean scores of 0.10, 0.18, and 0.25 out of 2).

2.3. Procedure

Participants first completed an online session on their own computer (about 45 to 70 min), in which the two parts of the predictive reading task (semantic and syntactic, in counterbalanced order) were separated by questionnaires, including the ARHQ and the SES questionnaire, and by Raven's matrices. They then completed a single session with an experimenter, in which the tasks were administered in a fixed order, ending with the reading comprehension test. The reading comprehension test was scored by a single experimenter for all participants.

2.4. Data preprocessing

Of the 16,320 trials, 522 (3.20%) were excluded: trials without a valid naming onset (no response, naming error, or onset that could not be coded) and latencies shorter than 150 ms or longer than 1,500 ms. Exclusions were more frequent in DR (5.82%) than in TR (1.21%), but no term involving context was significant in a logistic mixed-effects model of exclusions that included group, reading comprehension, and reading efficiency (all ps > .26;

Online Resource 1). The remaining latencies were winsorized at ±2.5 SD around each participant's mean, which affected 404 trials (2.56%), and log 10 -transformed (skewness = 0.56, excess kurtosis = 0.33).

2.5. Statistical analyses

We analyzed naming latencies at the trial level with linear mixed-effects models in R (version 4.3.3; R Core Team, 2024), using lme4 (version 1.1-35.1; Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest (version 3.1-3; Kuznetsova et al., 2017) with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Condition (semantic vs. syntactic), context (related vs. control), and group (TR vs. DR) were coded with centered contrasts (±0.5). Continuous predictors were standardized. All models included by-participant random slopes for condition, context, and their interaction, and bytarget random intercepts. With the maximal by-target structure (Barr et al., 2013), all estimates were unchanged, except that the Condition × Context interaction was p = .045 (p FDR = .067; Online Resource 1).

Model M1 included condition, context, group, and their interactions. Model M2 included condition, context, and their interactions with reading comprehension (questions score) and reading efficiency, entered simultaneously. Group was entered as a main effect. In a sensitivity analysis, we added the interactions of group with condition and context (Fig. S1).

We estimated Context × moderator slopes for each condition as linear combinations of the fixed effects. Given the size of the DR group, we did not model three-way interactions between group, context, and the moderators. We analyzed log 10 -transformed latencies, in which facilitation is expressed in proportion to each participant's naming latency. Because DR were slower overall, raw latencies would confound the size of facilitation with overall speed (Faust et al., 1999); analyses of raw and within-participant standardized latencies are reported in Online Resource 1 and summarized in the Results.

The study was not preregistered. We designated nine terms as of primary interest because they correspond to the predictions of the three accounts: in M1, the effect of context and its interactions with condition and with group; in M2, the Context × Comprehension and Context × Reading efficiency slopes in each condition, the difference between these two slopes in the semantic condition, and the Condition × Context × Comprehension interaction. These terms were tested at α = .05, and their p values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure (p FDR ; Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) within four families: the terms of M1, the terms of M2, and the equivalence tests of each model. All other analyses are exploratory.

We inferred the absence of an effect only from equivalence tests (two one-sided tests, TOST; Lakens, 2017), and never from a non-significant p value. For group differences, the equivalence bounds were ±0.008 log 10 units (≈ ±12.3 ms at the mean latency of related trials, 659.6 ms), the smallest facilitation reported for typical readers with the same task by Gavard et al. (2025; b = -0.008 and -0.009). This bound comes from an independent sample, and it corresponds to a group difference as large as the facilitation itself. For Context × moderator slopes, the bound was ±0.0058 log 10 units per SD (≈ ±8.8 ms per SD), i.e., the slope that would produce a difference of 0.008 log 10 units across the 1.39-SD gap in reading efficiency between groups. This bound is larger than the comprehension slope reported below: it can exclude a large modulation, but it cannot establish that a slope is zero. We report 90% confidence intervals for equivalence tests (Tables S1 and S2), and comparisons between slopes rest on direct contrasts. Effects are reported in log 10 units and, for readability, in milliseconds at the mean latency of related trials.

We examined the robustness of the critical slopes with alternative specifications (Fig. S1) and by refitting the models after removing each participant in turn (Fig. S2). The following analyses were exploratory. As an index of the cost of reliance on context, we regressed the log-transformed text reading time on semantic facilitation, adjusted for reading comprehension, reading efficiency, and group. We also added verbal reasoning (Similarities), spelling, and SES, in turn, as moderators in M2, and we replaced the questions score with the graphic organizer score. Individual facilitation scores (difference between the mean log 10 latencies of control and related trials) are shown in Fig. 3, and their correlations with the reading measures in Table S3; their split-half reliability was estimated with 1,000 random splits and the Spearman-Brown correction. Based on the standard errors of the models, the smallest group difference and the smallest moderator slope detectable with a power of .80 (α = .05, two-tailed) were about 10.8 ms and 6.6 ms per SD.

To determine whether the DR with weak reading efficiency also had weak reading comprehension, we compared each DR with the 29 TR on reading efficiency and on the questions score, with single-case methods (Table S4 and Fig. S3; method in Online Resource 1). These comparisons are descriptive.

Claude (Anthropic) was used to assist in developing and testing the statistical analysis code and for English-language editing; the authors reviewed and verified all outputs and take full responsibility for the content of the manuscript.

4. Discussion

We asked whether contextual facilitation during word reading reflects a benefit for reading comprehension in adults with dyslexia (compensation account), reliance on context driven by slow word identification (word-identification account), or reliance driven by limited semantic knowledge (semantic-knowledge account). Our results favor the third reading.

Reliance on semantic context varied with reading comprehension, in the direction opposite to a benefit, and this relation held when dyslexia status was taken into account.

Three results support this answer. First, both contexts facilitated naming in both groups, and a group difference in mean facilitation larger than about 12 ms was excluded in proportion to naming latency, although facilitation was larger in adults with dyslexia in raw milliseconds. Second, semantic facilitation was larger in readers with lower reading comprehension, mainly among those with the lowest scores, and this modulation was specific to semantic context. Third, a large modulation of proportional facilitation by reading efficiency was excluded, although the two modulations did not differ significantly.

The three accounts are not equally supported. The compensation account, in its word-level form, predicted that the readers who rely most on semantic context would understand texts best, and we found the opposite relation. The word-identification account cannot yet be decided. The modulation by reading efficiency that it implies was excluded in proportion to naming latency, but in raw milliseconds semantic facilitation was larger in readers with lower reading efficiency, as both this account and general slowing predict (Faust et al., 1999). The semantic-knowledge account is supported on two of its three predictions, the relation with reading comprehension and its specificity to semantic context. Its third prediction, the absence of a large group difference, was supported for facilitation averaged over conditions but could not be evaluated in the semantic condition alone. This support remains indirect, because semantic knowledge was not measured.

4.1. Reliance on context in adults with dyslexia: comparison with previous work

According to the interactive-compensatory model (Stanovich, 1980), readers with dyslexia should rely more on context than typical readers because their word identification is slower, and Gavard et al. (2025) extended this view to linguistic prediction. Our data qualify this prediction. Facilitation was larger in adults with dyslexia in raw milliseconds, as the model predicts, but its size in proportion to naming latency did not distinguish the two groups. A larger context effect in slower readers can thus arise from overall slowness alone (Faust et al., 1999), and it does not by itself show greater reliance on context. Previous findings in adults converge on this point. Facilitation of word naming by a congruent sentence did not differ significantly between university students with and without a history of reading difficulties (28 vs. 42 ms; Corkett & Parrila, 2008). Semantic priming did not differ significantly between students with dyslexia and typical readers (p = .08; Cavalli, Colé, et al., 2017), nor between adolescents with and without dyslexia (Lefèvre, Law, et al., 2023).

The semantic condition taken alone is a possible exception. Facilitation was larger in adults with dyslexia than in typical readers (26 vs. 16 ms), in the direction predicted by the compensation and word-identification accounts. This contrast was exploratory, however, and the group difference was 7.1 ms (95% CI [-2.0, 16.3]) when reading comprehension was added to the model. Our facilitation score also does not separate facilitation by the related context from inhibition by the control context, which distinguished the groups in Corkett and Parrila (2008).

The most direct comparison is with Gavard et al. (2025), who used the same task during fMRI. Facilitation was significant in typical readers but not in students with dyslexia in their study, and in both groups here, but in neither study did the overall effect of context differ significantly between groups. The authors proposed two explanations. One was that the predictive network of students with dyslexia, which did not differ significantly from that of typical readers in the whole-brain analysis, supports implicit comprehension of the context without speeding the naming of single words. The other was that only compensated readers benefit from context, as suggested by the positive correlation between syntactic facilitation and reading level in the dyslexia group. Our data bear on this second explanation. There is no measure of reading comprehension in that study, and when comprehension is measured, the readers with the largest semantic facilitation are those who understand texts least.

What these studies share is that reliance varies among readers with dyslexia more than between groups, and that its relation to reading skill depends on the resource. In the MEG study of Cavalli, Colé, et al. (2017), morphological priming was larger in students with dyslexia (p = .03), and within this group the students with the lowest reading level showed the largest morphological effects in left inferior frontal and fusiform regions (correlations of about .50 in absolute value). In adolescents, morphological and orthographic priming were likewise larger in less fluent readers (Lefèvre, Law, et al., 2023). The opposite direction has been reported for other resources. Syntactic facilitation was larger in the better readers with dyslexia (Gavard et al., 2025). In a representational similarity analysis of fMRI data, more fluent readers showed greater sensitivity to semantic information in the left fusiform gyrus, whereas less fluent readers showed greater sensitivity to orthographic information in its right-hemisphere homologue (Cavalli, Chanoine, et al., 2024). These correlations come from small samples and should be read as exploratory. Together with our results, they nevertheless suggest that greater reliance on a resource is often found in the readers with the weakest skills, and that it may mark a need at least as much as a benefit.

4.2. A functional account: reliance on semantic context as an index of need

We propose that semantic facilitation indexes how much a reader needs the context to identify a word, and that this need depends on the semantic constituent of lexical quality (Perfetti, 2007). Our semantic contexts, series of words from the same semantic field, may facilitate naming by preactivating a semantic category, as in semantic priming, without the prediction of a specific word. Readers whose word meanings are less precisely represented may identify a word less readily from its form alone, and their identification may be facilitated more by a related context. Reading comprehension scores partly reflect vocabulary and general knowledge (Brèthes et al., 2022), although not in all reader profiles (Lefèvre et al., 2026), whereas our measure of reading efficiency, based on a meaningless text, does not tap this knowledge. On this view, the reading comprehension score acted as a proxy for semantic knowledge.

Three observations are consistent with this account. The relation was specific to semantic context. A syntactic context constrains the grammatical class of the upcoming word but not its meaning, it should not draw on the knowledge indexed by reading comprehension, and the two kinds of prediction engage partly distinct brain networks (Gavard et al., 2025). The relation was also observed with the graphic organizer, a production task that requires integrating information across the three texts. Finally, the relation is unlikely to reflect general verbal reasoning, general naming speed, the scale of facilitation, years of schooling, or a ceiling on facilitation in the best comprehenders, since Similarities did not modulate facilitation and none of these factors accounted for the comprehension slope (Fig. S1;

Online Resource 1). Spelling, which indexes the precision of orthographic representations and has been linked to reliance on sentence context (Hersch & Andrews, 2012), did not significantly modulate facilitation either. It is possible that the constituent of lexical quality that governs reliance on context depends on the kind of context.

There are two findings that seem to contradict the account. University students with dyslexia, in whom vocabulary has been found to be preserved (Cavalli et al., 2016), rely more than typical readers on the semantic properties of morphemes (Cavalli, Colé, et al., 2017), and more fluent readers are more sensitive to semantic information in the left fusiform gyrus (Cavalli, Chanoine, et al., 2024). We suggest that semantic knowledge plays two roles. It is a resource that makes context usable and, when it is weak, a source of reliance on context. These two roles predict relations of opposite sign between semantic knowledge and facilitation, and which one dominates may depend on what the context requires. A morpheme or a sentence presumably has to be analyzed before it can help, whereas a series of related words may preactivate a category with little analysis. The account proposed here concerns the second role and individual differences in reading comprehension, not the diagnosis.

There are two further results that limit the account. In adolescents with and without dyslexia, semantic priming with single-word primes was not significantly correlated with vocabulary (r = .07; Lefèvre, Law, et al., 2023), whereas the account predicts a negative relation. Reading comprehension was measured in the same cohort (Lefèvre et al., 2025), but its relation with semantic priming was not reported, so the test is not direct. In children, poor comprehenders showed less contextual facilitation than typical readers (Nation & Snowling, 1998), when the account would predict more. Their contexts were spoken sentences, from which facilitation presumably requires building a representation of the message, whereas our semantic contexts required no such integration. In our data, moreover, the relation was carried mainly by the eight readers with the lowest comprehension scores, who were also weak on the graphic organizer. Reliance on semantic context may thus increase mainly when semantic knowledge is weak, and its relation to reading skill may depend both on the constituent of word knowledge that is weakest in the reader and on what the context requires (Wong et al., 2025).

4.3. From reliance to compensation and resilience

Reliance on a resource and compensation by that resource are distinct claims. In the framework of Livingston and Happé (2017), a compensated individual performs better than expected from their cognitive profile, and reliance on the semantic context of words is among the strategies they cite for compensated adults with dyslexia. Research on resilience in learning, which searches for protective factors and compensatory mechanisms (van Viersen et al., 2026), draws a similar distinction. A promotive factor is associated with better outcomes in all learners, whereas a protective factor reduces the effect of a risk, which requires testing its interaction with that risk (Slomowitz et al., 2025). Both frameworks lead to the benefit criterion defined in the Introduction. If reliance on semantic context is compensatory, then among readers with comparable reading efficiency, those who rely most on it should understand texts best, and this relation should be stronger in readers with dyslexia.

The present data do not meet this criterion. Semantic facilitation was related to lower, not higher, reading comprehension, and we could not test whether the relation differed between groups. In the terms of this literature, semantic facilitation behaved neither as a promotive nor as a protective factor for reading comprehension. Reading comprehension resiliency in adolescents with dyslexia has instead been related to vocabulary and listening comprehension (Lefèvre et al., 2025). Our conclusion fits the view that prediction can facilitate language processing without being necessary for comprehension (Huettig & Mani, 2016). It also bears on the reading of neural data. The greater activation of the left precentral gyrus and right anterior insula reported in students with dyslexia during predictive reading (Gavard et al., 2025) can be called compensation only if it is related to a behavioral benefit, which remains to be tested.

Three points restrict the scope of this conclusion. The design is cross-sectional, so lower reading comprehension might lead readers to rely on context, reliance might limit comprehension, or a third factor such as vocabulary depth might affect both. A relation between readers also does not exclude a benefit within readers. Those who rely most on context might understand even less without it, which only a within-participant manipulation of context could test. Finally, our task measured the effect of context on the naming of a single word, and whether compensation operates at the sentence level, as proposed by Gavard et al. (2025), remains open. Reliance may also have a cost in time or cognitive resources (Livingston & Happé, 2017). Semantic facilitation was not significantly associated with text reading time in our exploratory analysis, but reading time without a time limit does not measure effort. For instance, eye tracking during text reading would allow reliance (shorter fixations on predictable words) and cost (total reading time and regressions) to be measured on the same words.

4.4. Socioeconomic status and the conditions of compensation

Environmental variables are rarely studied in dyslexia (Lefèvre et al., 2025), and to our knowledge SES is seldom available for adults who reach higher education. It offers an indirect view of the role of semantic knowledge. In adolescents with and without dyslexia, lower SES was associated with weaker vocabulary (r = .43) and, through vocabulary and listening comprehension, indirectly with lower reading comprehension resiliency, an influence that was more marked in adolescents with dyslexia (Lefèvre et al., 2025). In the same adolescents, however, semantic priming was not related to vocabulary (Lefèvre, Law, et al., 2023). Because SES, vocabulary, reading comprehension, and semantic priming were all measured in this cohort, its openly available data would allow a direct test of whether SES relates to reliance on semantic context through vocabulary.

If SES partly indexes semantic knowledge, the semantic-knowledge account predicts larger semantic facilitation in readers from lower SES backgrounds. Our data go in this direction without establishing it. The SES slope in the semantic condition had the same sign as the comprehension slope, but its confidence interval included zero and it did not differ significantly from the syntactic slope. SES was not significantly correlated with reading comprehension, and it did not account for the comprehension slope. There is probably a restricted range of SES among university students, which may have limited these relations, and SES may also reflect other factors, such as print exposure.

SES may matter for a second reason, which concerns the capacity to compensate and not only the knowledge available. Tooley et al. (2021) proposed that the childhood environment affects the pace of brain development. In their hypothesis, chronic stress accelerates maturation, whereas positive and novel experiences slow it down, and a faster pace would close periods of high plasticity earlier. This proposal rests mainly on cross-sectional data and does not concern dyslexia. We suggest, however, that it leads to a testable prediction about compensation. If resources such as vocabulary and morphological knowledge are built through prolonged experience with language and print, readers with dyslexia from lower SES backgrounds might have had less opportunity to build them. They might then show reliance on context without benefit more often than their peers. Low SES would be an additional risk factor in dyslexia, as Lefèvre et al. (2025) suggested for reading comprehension. Our sample does not allow this prediction to be tested.

4.5. Limitations

The sample limits these conclusions in several ways. Adults with dyslexia who reached postsecondary education are the population in which compensation is usually assumed (Livingston & Happé, 2017), and the relation might not hold in adults with lower educational attainment, nor in other languages and orthographies. The size of the dyslexia group did not allow us to test whether the moderators acted differently in the two groups. The relation with reading comprehension was carried by a small number of readers, and the absence of a large group difference holds for proportional facilitation, so both results should be tested in a larger, preregistered sample. Three participants in the dyslexia group had no formal diagnosis and the groups differed in years of schooling, but the modulation by reading comprehension remained significant without these participants and with years of schooling in the model (Fig. S1). Attentional difficulties were not assessed with a standardized measure.

The measures also have limits of reliability and overlap. Dyslexia status and reading efficiency were strongly correlated (r = -.70), so the model cannot separate their contributions, although the comprehension slope was almost unchanged with the interactions of group with condition and context in the model (-6.1 ms per SD; Fig. S1). The reliability of individual semantic facilitation scores was low (.23), as is common for differences between two highly correlated conditions (Hedge et al., 2018), and the comprehension slope was close to the smallest slope that the study could detect, so its size might be overestimated. Internal consistency was also low for the questions score (α = .37).

Under classical assumptions, measurement error in reading comprehension should attenuate its slope and bias the contrast with the reading efficiency slope toward zero. If the Alouette test did not measure reading efficiency reliably, however, part of the comprehension slope might reflect variance in reading efficiency that the model did not capture (Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016).

What the measures cover is a further limit. Reading comprehension was assessed with a single test without a time limit, the condition most favorable to compensation (Corkett & Parrila, 2008), and because the questions were answered without rereading the texts, the score also depends on memory for the text. Vocabulary and listening comprehension were not measured, so the semantic-knowledge account was not tested directly. Four context words presented for 500 ms each may also encourage strategic expectations that differ from the predictions made during natural reading. Finally, the task was run online, which may have added noise to naming latencies. This is unlikely to bias facilitation, a within-participant difference on which a constant delay of the equipment has little influence, and its size in typical readers was of the same order as that reported by Gavard et al. (2025).

4.6. Implications for research, education, and remediation

For research, our results imply that a group difference in the use of a resource shows reliance on that resource, and that its benefit has to be tested separately (Livingston & Happé, 2017). Because reading comprehension varied widely within both groups, the benefit criterion would be best tested with continuous measures and at the level of the individual, as in person-centered analyses of literacy profiles (Lefèvre et al., 2026), and with designs that can separate promotive from protective effects (Slomowitz et al., 2025).

For education, reading comprehension would be worth assessing individually in adults with dyslexia. In the present sample, four of the 22 adults with dyslexia had a reading comprehension deficit, whereas seven scored above the mean of typical readers. A diagnosis of dyslexia thus says little about how well a student understands texts, and support based on the diagnosis alone may not reach the students whose difficulty lies in comprehension. Materials that provide meaning from outside the word are one option to evaluate. For example, university students with dyslexia relied more than skilled readers on the pictures of comics and gained more reading time from this format, with comparable comprehension (Rasamimanana et al., 2026).

For remediation, two targets should be distinguished. The word-level deficit is persistent.

Phonological reading deficits were still present at age 17 in a longitudinal study (Lefèvre, Cavalli, et al., 2023), and intensive training based on repeated reading has improved reading efficiency in children with dyslexia (Leloup et al., 2021). Our results suggest that encouraging readers to use context is unlikely to be sufficient, since the readers who relied most on semantic context were those who understood texts least. If this reliance reflects weak semantic knowledge, then vocabulary and oral language are more plausible targets, as proposed for reading comprehension resiliency in adolescents (Lefèvre et al., 2025). This proposal is causal, whereas our data are correlational. It should be evaluated in training studies that measure both reliance on context and reading comprehension.

5. Conclusion

In adults with and without dyslexia, semantic facilitation of word reading was larger in readers with lower reading comprehension, and this relation held when reading efficiency and dyslexia status were taken into account. The readers who relied most on semantic context were not those who understood texts best, contrary to the word-level form of the compensation account. We propose to describe semantic facilitation as an index of need, which may reflect weaker semantic knowledge and, more distally, the conditions in which this knowledge was acquired. Whether this reliance helps each reader remains to be tested by manipulating the availability of context within participants and by measuring vocabulary directly. We conclude that compensation and resilience in adult dyslexia should be tested at the level of the individual and against reading comprehension, because the use of a resource does not establish its benefit. Until such tests are available, larger semantic facilitation in adults with dyslexia is better described as reliance on context than as compensation.
