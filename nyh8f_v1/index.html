How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) is associated with an increased risk of multiple physical (e.g., cardiovascular and metabolic) and mental (e.g., depression and substance use) health conditions (Asnaani et al., 2014; Pietrzak et al., 2012; Qassem et al., 2021), including an increased risk of non-suicidal self-injury, suicide attempts, and death by suicide (Alharbi et al., 2020; Forehand et al., 2022). Despite ~70% of the population experiencing at least one potentially traumatic event in their lifetime (Benjet et al., 2016; Kessler et al., 2017), PTSD has a lifetime prevalence of ~7% (National Institute of Health, n.d.). The risk of developing PTSD is heightened in those with exposure to interpersonal violence (IPV), especially victims of sexual assault who are at a 3-4 times risk (Kessler et al., 2017). Similarly, women have an increased risk of developing PTSD due to several factors, including but not limited to IPV (Kessler et al., 2017; Kimerling et al., 2018).

Exposure-based cognitive behavioral therapies (CBT), such as prolonged exposure (PE), are gold-standard psychosocial treatments for PTSD (Foa et al., 2007; Rauch et al., 2012). Exposurebased treatments help patients safely process their traumatic experience(s), learn how to tolerate distressing emotions and trauma reminders, and overcome maladaptive internal and external avoidance behaviors (Foa et al., 2007). Unfortunately, approximately a quarter of patients prematurely drop out of PE (22-28%; Varker et al., 2021), response rates are modest (53-60%; Schnurr et al., 2007), and significant relapse of symptoms is common (57%; Brooks & Greenberg, 2024). It is crucial to develop and implement strategies that improve exposure-based treatments for PTSD.

Fear extinction processes are important mechanisms of exposure therapies, as the formation and consolidation of new safety learning inhibits conditioned fear responses and associated reactivity to trauma cues when safety learning memories are effectively recalled (Craske et al., 2008; Fullana et al., 2020). Through repeated imaginal and in vivo exposures, individuals learn to more effectively distinguish threat and safety cues across contexts (e.g., environments and interoceptive states) and to more appropriately recall learned inhibitory safety memories (Maren et al., 2013).

Changes in experiential and emotional avoidance are putative safety learning mechanisms, such that an increased willingness to engage with emotions during exposures may reduce subsequent maladaptive avoidance of negative emotions, posttraumatic intrusions, and reminders of trauma (Wang et al., 2024). Changes in cognitive processes (e.g., reappraisal) about posttraumatic beliefs are another putative mechanism of safety learning, as reductions of negative cognitions about oneself (e.g., "It was my fault") predict improvements in PTSD symptoms in cross-lagged analyses of data collected during PE (Knowles & Tolin, 2022; Kooistra et al., 2023).

An increased capacity to tolerate distressing emotions is likely another mechanism of safety learning during PE, as individuals break the associations of these emotions as trauma-related cues (Boffa et al., 2018). Others have suggested that measuring negative affectivity, itself, is also useful in characterizing treatment outcomes (e.g. Hoffman et al., 2022).

Methods for improving the consolidation of safety learning may be a promising augmentation strategy for exposure-based treatments to reduce unwanted return of fear. Aerobic exercise is associated with several mental and physical health benefits and has well-established neurocognitive, neuroplastic, and neuromodulatory effects that improve safety learning memory consolidation (Azar et al., 2024; Crombie et al., 2023). Animal studies using acute exercise during or after fear extinction report large improvements in fear extinction consolidation, as evidenced by reduced fear expression (i.e., freezing) during return of fear testing up to a week later (Tanner et al., 2018). Animal studies collectively indicate that exercise following safety learning could serve as an augmentation strategy for exposure therapy to enhance the strength and retention of safetylearning consolidation. Attempts to translate preclinical findings of exercise and fear extinction or exposure therapy into human models have yielded mixed results, primarily due to methodological inconsistencies and limitations.

Most human studies that have attempted to use exercise prior to or separate from exposure/fear extinction have reported null or temporary improvements in clinical, fear extinction, or return of fear outcomes (Bischoff et al., 2018; Jacquart et al., 2017; Voorendonk et al., 2021; Weisman & Rodebaugh, 2018), which is unsurprising given the animal literature showing null effects of exercise before extinction (Tanner et al., 2018). Conversely, human studies that have examined exercise after exposure therapy or fear extinction, which is aligned with preclinical data, have yielded more promising results. In a sample of healthy women, fear extinction training followed by aerobic exercise at 70-75% MHR reduced threat expectancy ratings for the unconditioned stimulus (shock) 24 hours later compared with the active control condition (40-50% MHR; Crombie et al., 2023). Similarly, women with PTSD who underwent extinction training followed by 30 mins of exercise at 70-75% MHR, relative to the 40-50% MHR control condition, reported reduced threat expectancy following reinstatement (Crombie et al., 2021).

A large-scale randomized controlled trial (RCT) examined changes in PTSD severity following nine weekly exposure-based CBT sessions, followed by 20 minutes of active stretching (control) or 10 minutes of exercise at 65-85% MHR on a high stepper (plus 5 minutes of warm-up and cool-down). This study reported a moderate effect size (d = 0.60) for between-group differences in PTSD symptom severity at five-month follow-up, and suggests that exercise following CBT enhanced long-term retention of safety memories and/or attenuated the return of fear over time, consistent with laboratory research (Bryant et al., 2023). Unfortunately, this study failed to detect statistically significant differences in PTSD symptom severity between the aerobic exercise and control conditions one week post-treatment. These findings may have been due to ceiling effects from a large dose of psychotherapy, the choice of an eclectic therapy approach that targets several treatment mechanisms in addition to safety learning (e.g., cognitive reappraisal), or an insufficient dose of exercise (e.g., duration or intensity; Bryant et al., 2023).

The current study attempted to augment safety learning through isolating and targeting exposure therapy mechanisms in a twice-weekly treatment using adequately dosed exercise (70-75% MHR for 25 minutes) within the consolidation window (i.e., after exposures). The BET protocol attempted to overcome the largest barrier to PTSD treatment (i.e., participant dropout) by using shorter exposures, twice-weekly sessions, and no homework, while also trying to improve treatment response and retention of treatment gains by targeting exposure therapy mechanisms.

Although neuroplasticity was not directly assessed, the combined protocol was designed with exercise timing and dosage hypothesized to promote neuroplasticity during the post-exposure consolidation window (Azar et al., 2024). This study is an essential first step to determine whether participants tolerate the combined treatment, whether the protocol can be delivered as intended, and whether individual-level changes justify future research using exercise to augment exposure therapy in a large-scale randomized controlled trial (Tanious & Onghena, 2019).

Single case experimental designs (SCEDs) are parsimonious research designs that are ideal for preliminary investigations of treatment mechanisms and the efficacy of interventions, as they use each subject as their own control, resulting in high internal validity and the ability to examine effect sizes within-and between-subjects (Barlow et al., 2008; Ledford & Gast, 2018). SCED clinical trial methodology is increasingly popular for initially evaluating the efficacy and mechanistic engagement of PTSD interventions (Au et al., 2017; Kooistra et al., 2025). The current study used a staggered baseline SCED in an open-label clinical trial to pilot the feasibility and acceptability of twice-weekly brief exposure therapy (BET) followed by exercise for adults with PTSD. We hypothesized that the BET and exercise protocol would be feasibly administered, with all sessions having less than a 20% deviation from the protocol checklist, and that individuals would report BET and exercise as an acceptable means of PTSD treatment. We also hypothesized that individuals who received the BET and exercise protocol would exhibit improvements in PTSD symptom severity, experiential avoidance, posttraumatic cognitions, and negative affect during treatment and follow-up relative to the baseline phase.

Methods

Participants

Participants were included in the study if they could ethically provide informed consent, were aged 18-64, could safely engage in 25 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, and met DSM-5 criteria for PTSD within the past month. Participants were excluded if they were currently undergoing PTSD psychotherapy or had recent changes in psychiatric medication or psychotherapy (i.e., < 4 weeks). Psychiatric exclusionary criteria included recent (within the past 3 months) severe substance use disorder(s), high risk for suicide (i.e., plan and intent to die by suicide or recent suicide attempt), mania, or psychotic symptoms. Exercise-related exclusion criteria consisted of reported health conditions (e.g., heart disease, leg injury, asthma) that were not medically stable or deemed potentially unsafe for exercise. Study completers consisted of six White women with a mean age of 33.0 years (range = 22-45 years). Although all individuals were diagnosed with PTSD as their principal diagnosis, four individuals (67%) also met diagnostic criteria for other co-occurring psychiatric disorders.

Recruitment.

All participants previously completed a lab-based study (IRB# 90369) examining emotional learning and exercise in PTSD and agreed to be contacted about additional research. Participants were invited to complete a prescreening form via REDCap. Participants who screened positive for probable PTSD and a likely safe ability to exercise were able to schedule a virtual telehealth intake via Zoom with the master's level clinician (TH) to assess study eligibility.

Fifteen volunteers were prescreened; twelve were invited to participate. Ten consented to participate in the present study and completed an intake. Three participants were deemed ineligible as they did not meet the DSM-5 criteria for PTSD during the psychodiagnostic interview. One eligible participant withdrew from the study after enrollment but before starting baseline assessments due to logistical issues. The final sample consisted of six participants (n = 6). See Table 1 for demographic and treatment variables for study participants.

Procedures.

Clinical Trial Design.

This study was approved by the University of Kentucky Non-Medical Institutional Review Board (IRB# 101765) and preregistered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07080606). The current pilot open-label, randomized baseline study aimed to characterize the acceptability, feasibility, and efficacy of the BET and exercise protocol. We combined CONsolidated Standards Of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) and SCED guidelines for the development and reporting of the current SCED clinical trial (Butcher et al., 2022; Kratochwill et al., 2026).

After providing IRB-approved informed consent, participants completed a battery of questionnaires, and a clinician administered psychodiagnostic interview to evaluate inclusion/exclusion criteria. Eligible participants were randomized (1:1:1) to a three-, four-, or five-week baseline period. After the baseline assessment phase, participants completed eight sessions of BET, including six sessions of imaginal exposure to traumatic memories followed by moderate-intensity aerobic exercise. During the last treatment session, participants were asked to provide satisfaction ratings and qualitative feedback during a semi-structured interview.

Participants completed four weeks of follow-up questionnaires after treatment, resulting in a total of 11-13 weeks of repeated self-report assessments across the baseline, treatment, and follow-up phases. Self-report assessments were administered weekly via REDCap links sent the day before the first session of each week; However, many participants did not complete questionnaires prior to the first session of each week, but 100% of questionnaires were completed before the subsequent week of treatment. Consequently, symptom change is reported by study week rather than by session number to ensure consistency across timepoints.

Demographics and Baseline Characteristics. Age, sex, gender, race, ethnicity, household income, marital status, medication usage, and occupational status were assessed with single-item self-report questions.

Treatment Acceptability and Feasibility.

Acceptability of the treatment protocol was assessed using a pretreatment questionnaire, a post-treatment participant-rated treatment feedback survey, and a clinician-administered interview. The Credibility and Expectations Questionnaire (CEQ; Devilly & Borkovec, 2000) is a 6-item questionnaire used to assess participants' initial thoughts about the credibility and expectations of a treatment before it begins. Items were rated on a 9-point Likert scale ranging from1 = 'Not at All' to9 = 'Very.' The percent improvement items were converted to a 1-9 linear scale aligned with the other 4 items on the CEQ. The credibility and expectation subscale scores range from 3-36, with higher scores indicating greater perceived credibility and expectations of treatment.

A modified treatment satisfaction questionnaire was used to assess participants' perceptions of the BET and exercise treatment. The treatment satisfaction questionnaire included 6 Likert scale items, comprising five items from the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire-8 (CSQ-8; Attkisson & Zwick, 2016) and an additional item to assess perceptions of symptom reduction. Items on the CSQ are scored on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (low) to 4 (high), and the additional symptom reduction item was a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (low) to 5 (high). Lastly, a clinician-administered treatment feedback interview was conducted to gather qualitative information on participants' perceptions to the treatment (Supplemental file 1).

Treatment drop-out, late cancellation, and no-show rates were used to characterize the feasibility of participant engagement. Lastly, therapist adherence to the treatment protocol was assessed by the primary author (TH) using a checklist for each session; the protocol's feasibility was defined as the percentage deviation per session across all participants.

PTSD Symptoms.

The Trauma History Questionnaire (THQ; Hooper et al., 2011) was administered during the study eligibility prescreen to assess for a criterion A index trauma. The THQ is a 24-item questionnaire that participants could endorse = 'Yes' or deny = 'No' exposure to 24 potentially traumatic experiences. The THQ was modified for the current study to evaluate for the presence of a criterion A traumatic experience (i.e., index trauma). The PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015) was used to assess past-month and past-week PTSD symptom severity during the intake and clinical trial, respectively. The PCL-5 is a 20-item self-report measure with each item corresponding to one of the PTSD symptoms from the DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Items on the PCL-5 were anchored to participants' reported index trauma on the THQ and asked how much they are bothered by each symptom on a five-point Likert-type scale from0 = 'Not at all' to4 = 'Extremely.' Items are summed to produce an overall score ranging from 0 to 80. PCL-5 scores ≥ 31 were classified as a positive screen for probable PTSD during the prescreen (Blevins et al., 2015). The Diagnostic Interview for Anxiety, Mood, Obsessive-Compulsive, and Related Neuropsychiatric Disorders (DIAMOND; Tolin et al., 2018) semi-structured psychodiagnostic interview was used during the study intake to establish diagnoses of past-month PTSD and co-occurring psychiatric disorders for eligibility and exclusion criteria. Items on the PANAS-NA were summed to create a total score ranging from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating a greater intensity of various negative emotions.

Intervention

Brief Exposure Therapy. Participants underwent eight sessions of twice-weekly BET, which primarily consisted of imaginal exposures and emotional processing similar to the PE protocol (Foa et al., 2007). Twice-weekly shorter-duration (45-min) exposure therapy sessions were used to improve acceptability for participants (Booysen & Kagee, 2021; Levinson et al., 2022; Sripada et al., 2022). Fewer and shorter sessions were also chosen, in part, to prevent ceiling effects that may have otherwise occurred with a full dose of PE. That said, shorter duration exposure treatments are shown to be efficacious in treating PTSD (Booysen & Kagee, 2021; Cigrang et al., 2017), and shorter imaginal exposure sessions (60-min) have been shown to be noninferior to full-length (90-min) sessions (Nacasch et al., 2015; Minnen & Foa, 2006). BET was administered by a master's level clinical psychology doctoral student (TH) with the consultation and supervision of licensed clinical psychologists (TA and SSZ) who have extensive experience in treating PTSD and conducting treatment outcome research.

The first BET session included psychoeducation about PTSD symptoms, rationale for imaginal exposures, treatment planning and goal setting, and the development of each participant's negative expectations related to undergoing the exposures (e.g., the exposure would cause them to break down and lose control). For sessions two through seven, participants underwent imaginal exposures where they recounted their traumatic experience aloud in the present tense for 20 minutes. Participants were asked to report pre-, post-, and peak subjective units of distress (SUDs) across all exposure sessions, a commonly used measure in exposure therapy (Wolpe & Lazarus, 1966). Before and after exposures, participants were asked to evaluate the likelihood of their negative expectations about the exposure to facilitate expectancy violations (Craske et al., 2008; Kooistra et al., 2025). Following each exposure, participants underwent 10-15 minutes of emotional processing, consisting of semi-structured questions targeting exposure mechanisms and unstructured, open-ended questions and reflections. Instead of using out-of-session homework between exposures, participants were instructed to practice allowing themselves to experience thoughts, images, or feelings that arise between sessions, similarly to the instructions used in written exposure therapy (Sloan & Marx, 2019). The last BET session covered treatment progress, next steps, referrals, if requested, as well as questionnaires assessing acceptability and providing feedback on treatment.

Aerobic Exercise.

Following each imaginal exposure and emotional processing session, participants engaged in 20 minutes of moderate-intensity (70-75% MHR) exercise, with a 3-minute warm-up and a 2-minute cool-down, using a stationary bicycle and a heart rate monitor. During the warm-up, participants had 3 minutes to reach the target heart rate. Participants were instructed to adjust their heart rate to stay within their 70-75% MHR range. During the exercise sessions, participants biked in silence and watched a video of a bike path.

Data Analytic Approach

Aligned with CONSORT and SCED recommendations, a combination of analytical approaches was used to analyze the acceptability and feasibility, treatment efficacy, and mechanism engagement of the BET and exercise protocol (Butcher et al., 2022; Kratochwill et al., 2026). Data cleaning and statistical analyses were completed with R (v4.3.1; R Core Team, 2026).

Participants' perspectives on the BET and exercise protocol, participant engagement with treatment (i.e., cancellations, no-shows, dropouts), and therapists' adherence to the treatment protocol were evaluated to characterize acceptability and feasibility.

Visual analysis was used to explore changes in weekly self-reported PTSD symptom severity (i.e., PCL-5) and exposure therapy mechanisms (BEAQ, PTCI-9, and PANAS-NA) between baseline, treatment, and follow-up phases for each participant. Visual analysis is regarded as a conservative method for SCED data since it relies on observing differences between phases, which often demand larger effects than statistical tests, and allows comparisons within and between subjects. (Kazdin, 2010; Lane & Gast, 2014). Weekly scores of outcome variables for each participant are graphically plotted to compare levels (i.e., mean scores) between phases, trends (i.e., slopes) of scores within each phase, variability of scores within each phase, immediacy of the treatment's effect, consistency of effects between subjects, and overlap of data between phases (Lane & Gast, 2014; Ledford et al., 2018).

It is also recommended to use SCED-appropriate statistics to accompany visual analyses and to comprehensively quantify the magnitude of change in outcome variables attributable to a treatment effect (Kratochwill et al., 2026). First, non-overlap statistics between treatment phases were calculated with Tau-U effect sizes using the SCAN R package (Wilbert & Lueke, 2025). The Tau-UA vs B is a non-parametric statistic that evaluates the percentage of non-overlap and is appropriate for small SCED data (Parker, Vannest, & Davis, 2011). As no participants exhibited significant baseline trends, the baseline-corrected Tau-U-Trend A statistic was not used in the current study, as it can overly penalize non-overlapping data without a monotonic trend (Fingerhut et al., 2021). Tau-U effect sizes were interpreted using established cut-offs: < .2, small; .2 to .6, moderate; .6 to .8, large; and > .8, large to very large (Vannest & Ninci, 2015).

To characterize between-case (i.e., group-level) effects, we first calculated a weighted Tau-U-A vs. B statistic by dividing the sum of the raw S statistic (i.e., the net count of concordant and discordant pairs) by the total data pairs across all participants (Parker et al., 2011). Next, the between-case Hedges, Pustejovsky, and Shadish (HPS) standardized mean difference effect size was calculated using a hierarchical linear model approach. The HPS compares all data points within a phase and accounts for within-and between-participant variance, making it relatively comparable to Hedges' g in group-level designs and appropriate for staggered baseline designs (Hedges et al., 2013). The hlmscd R package was used to calculate the HPS effect size (Pustejovsky et al., 2026). To interpret the HPS effect sizes, Hedges' g established guidelines were used: 0.20 to 0.49, small effect; from 0.50 to 0.79, moderate effect; and ≤ 0.80, large effect (Cohen, 2013).

Results

Treatment Acceptability And Feasibility

Following their intake, participants completed the CEQ to assess the credibility and initial expectations of the BET and exercise protocol. Most participants viewed the BET and exercise treatment as credible (M = 23 out of 36, range = 18-27) and expected the treatment to improve their symptoms at least somewhat (M = 18.3 out of 36, range = 14-26). On the post-treatment satisfaction questionnaire, 50% of participants reported that the BET and exercise treatment was very difficult, while the other 50% reported that the treatment was moderately difficult. Despite its difficulty, all participants (100%) reported that they would recommend BET and exercise to a friend, that it has helped their problems a great deal, and that the treatment has improved their problems much more than they originally thought it would. Participants reported that the BET and exercise treatment met most of their needs (50%) or almost all of their needs (50%) for which they sought help. Across all measures and time points, no significant adverse reactions or lasting symptom exacerbations occurred because of the BET and exercise protocol.

Participants provided qualitative feedback about the acceptability and feasibility of the BET and exercise treatment during the treatment feedback interview. Themes from the interview included finding twice-weekly sessions helpful (P1, P5, P6; 50%), a positive experience of combining therapy and exercise (P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P6; 100%), that exercise after exposures helped regulate emotions and reduce dissociation (P1, P2, P6; 50%), and that exercising in silence helped facilitate independent reflection of the session (P1, P2, P4, P6; 66%). Regarding negative feedback, participants reported that they wished the treatment were one to two weeks longer (P3, P5, P6; 50%) and could include more than one traumatic experience (P2 & P5; 33%).

The current study had a 0% attrition rate, 0% no-show rate, and 2% latecancellation/reschedule rate (i.e., one session). All six participants who started treatment completed all of the twice weekly treatment sessions. The current study maintained good fidelity to the treatment protocol, as 100.0% of individual study sessions demonstrated less than a 20% deviation from the protocol and 75.0% had perfect adherence (0% deviation). Protocol adherence, averaged across all participants and sessions, was 97.9% (SD = 3.9%, range = 85.7%-100%).

Visual Analysis

All participants (6 of 6) displayed improvements in PTSD symptom severity level and/or slope between baseline and treatment phases (Figure 1). Improvements in PTSD symptom severity level and/or slope between treatment and follow up continued for three participants (P3, P4, P6), stabilized at minimal values for two participants (P1 & P2), and slightly trended upwards for one participant (P5). Across 5 of 6 participants, sharp changes in PTSD symptom severity were observed during the third week of treatment; one participant displayed a notable decrease in scores during the fourth week of treatment. The observed reductions in PTSD symptom severity were staggered across baseline lengths and occurred relative to the start of treatment, providing visual evidence of experimental control.

Participants displayed baseline stability in experiential avoidance, posttraumatic cognitions, and negative affect. Participants displayed improvements in level and/or slope for experiential avoidance (4 of 6), posttraumatic cognitions (6 of 6), and negative affect (5 of 6) scores between baseline and treatment phases. Participants' improvements in experiential avoidance (4 of 6), posttraumatic cognitions (6 of 6), and negative affect (5 of 6) continued or were maintained in the follow-up phase relative to the treatment phase. The observed reductions in symptoms were staggered across baseline periods and occurred after treatment initiation, providing visual evidence of experimental control.

Quantitative Effect Sizes.

The Tau-U statistic was used to evaluate the percentage of overlap in scores across phases for each participant; a greater negative non-overlap indicated a larger within-case treatment effect.

Four participants (P1, P3, P4, and P6) exhibited large non-overlap (Tau-U Range = -.80 to -.100) for PTSD symptom severity scores between baseline and treatment phases. Between treatment and follow-up phases, all participants displayed medium to large non-overlap (Tau-U Range = -.63 to -.100), indicating a continued reduction of PTSD symptoms following treatment for all participants. All six participants showed 100% non-overlap (Tau-U = -1.00) in PTSD symptom severity scores between baseline and follow-up. Medium to large treatment effects were also observed for experiential avoidance, posttraumatic cognitions, and negative affect for all participants (Table 2).

Between-case effect sizes were calculated using a weighted Tau-U and the between-case SMD (HSP). Between-case effect sizes indicate a large reduction in PTSD symptom severity scores from the baseline to treatment (Weighted Tau-U = -.79; HSP SMD = -0.91), treatment to follow-up (Weighted Tau-U = -.83; HSP SMD = -1.22), and from baseline to follow-up phases (weighted Tau-U = -1.00; HSP SMD = -1.23). Discussion

This pilot SCED study (n=6) used an open-label, staggered-baseline design to evaluate the acceptability, feasibility, efficacy, and treatment-mechanism engagement of a twice-weekly PTSD treatment protocol that combines a mechanism-focused exposure therapy with moderate-intensity aerobic exercise. Findings add to a growing body of literature evaluating the benefits of combining exposure-based treatments with aerobic exercise. With implementation in mind, the current treatment protocol aimed to fit psychotherapy and exercise within a standard treatment session (≤60 minutes). Results from the current study suggest that a treatment comprised only of 20-minute imaginal exposures and 10-15 minutes of processing combined with 25 minutes of exercise was sufficient to reduce PTSD symptoms and engage exposure therapy mechanisms in six consecutive patients.

The BET treatment approach adds to an emerging trend of abbreviated treatments for PTSD (e.g., brief imaginal exposure and PE-PC), suggesting that shorter duration treatments may be an appropriate approach to PTSD treatment (Sripada et al., 2022; Zoellner et al., 2023) and treatment augmentation research (Benito et al., 2025; McLean & Foa, 2024). That said, two participants (33%) reported wanting treatment to be longer as they felt they benefited from the BET treatment and wanted to reduce their symptoms further. A flexible-duration exposure treatment may be appropriate for higher-severity cases and those with multiple index traumas, as recommended in the PE protocol (Foa et al., 2007).

Following treatment, participants reported that the BET and exercise treatment was difficult, a commonly reported sentiment of imaginal exposures during PTSD treatment (Alpert et al., 2024). Despite this, all participants reported positive overall experiences with the treatment and indicated greater symptom reduction than they expected relative to past therapy experiences.

Almost all participants reported an increased desire to continue exercising after completing the BET and exercise treatment, which, if maintained, could have lasting physical and mental health benefits beyond PTSD symptoms (Villella & Villella, 2014; White et al., 2024). Preliminary feasibility results indicate that the BET and exercise was able to be administered with good therapist fidelity and participant engagement, as adherence rates per session and rates of patient drop-out, no-show, and late cancellations/reschedules did not exceed what has been reported in PE and exposure-based clinical trials for PTSD (Lewis et al., 2020; Varker et al., 2021).

Participants in the current study reported that exercising after exposures was helpful. Prior research demonstrates that aerobic exercise can reduce sensitivity to physiological symptoms of anxiety (LeBouthillier et al., 2016), exert anxiolytic effects (Su et al., 2026), and improve emotion regulation (Wang et al., 2024). Taken together, post-exposure exercise could strengthen the consolidation of safety learning while attenuating the emotional burden of imaginal exposures through anxiolytic and mood-regulating effects. These latter interpretations are consistent with participants' subjective reports; several participants noted that exercise helped them regulate their emotions and process their exposure before leaving the sessions. Future studies should examine how exercise could be used to regulate participants' emotions following exposures and characterize the degree to which self-directed reflection may influence safety learning.

Quantitative results of the current study should be interpreted with caution due to its small sample size and a relatively short baseline phase for some participants. Nonetheless, preliminary results suggest the BET and exercise protocol produced large reductions in PTSD symptom severity. Unlike prior exposure and exercise clinical trials, the current BET and exercise protocol included fewer treatment components to better target safety learning and memory processes (i.e., only imaginal exposures), was shorter in duration, and used a more restrictive and intensive exercise threshold (i.e., 70-75% MHR).

Alongside reductions in PTSD symptom severity, the BET and exercise protocol engaged putative mechanisms of exposure therapy as suggested in extant research (Hoffman et al., 2022; Kooistra et al., 2023; Y. Wang et al., 2024). Individual differences in the timing and magnitude of these changes were observed. Visual inspection of exposure mechanisms indicates that one or more mechanism changed in tandem with their PTSD, suggesting the potential influence of more than one mechanism driving symptom change. Future studies should examine the temporal influence of exposure mechanism scores in conjunction and independently of each other to examine if putative mechanisms affect distinct or synergistic effects on PTSD symptoms (Benito et al., 2025). Future studies should also identify which mechanisms of exposure therapy influence the most change in PTSD symptom severity and elucidate whether individual profiles of exposure therapy mechanisms exist (i.e., behavioral phenotypes; Benito et al., 2025; Dalgleish et al., 2020).

Limitations.

SCEDs are an emerging clinical trial methodology for pseudo-phase 1 or 2 clinical trials in psychotherapy research but come with several limitations. The current pilot study did not include an active comparison group or a treatment-as-usual control. Instead, treatment effects were examined for each participant relative to their own baseline; a staggered-baseline design with three-, four-, and five-week baselines was used to improve feasibility and reduce study attrition. This approach does not, however, elucidate the extent to which exercise may improve safety learning and exposure therapy, how the BET and exercise protocol compares to other PTSD treatments, or how much change occurs between timepoints. Despite efforts to standardize assessment timing relative to therapy sessions (i.e., the day before the first session of the week), only 12% of weekly assessments were completed before the first session of each week; though 100% were completed before the first session of the subsequent week. As a result, analyses focused on changes relative to the week of the study and to study phases rather than on specific sessions during the treatment phase.

The current treatment protocol, study design, and adherence assessments were completed by the primary author (TH), which introduces bias into the study's results. All participants from the current study were recruited following completion of another study investigating neural mechanisms in emotional learning and exercise in PTSD among adults exposed to IPV. The shared pipeline was used to decrease the duration of study recruitment and improve feasibility. This may have had positively biased participant views of BET and exercise due to their involvement in the prior study (Rosencrans et al., 2023). Lastly, The current study's sample of six participants is acceptable for an SCED study but is nonetheless underpowered for standard inferential tests. The current sample was relatively homogenous and may not generalize to other populations as participants were all white females with IPV-related trauma. Future studies should assess the efficacy of BET and exercise using more rigorous clinical trial methodologies (e.g., active control comparison, blinding, independent raters, diverse samples etc.) and representative samples of individuals with PTSD.

Conclusion.

Results from the current study indicated that the treatment of PTSD symptoms with six sessions of brief imaginal exposure to traumatic memories following by moderate-intensity exercise was acceptable to participants, feasibly administered, and resulted in medium to large reductions of PTSD symptom severity all participants. The current study highlights the potential clinical utility of a combined brief exposure and exercise treatment for PTSD and demonstrates a clear need for additional research about this and related treatment approaches. Future studies should examine the additive effects of exercise on exposure therapy relative to active control, using larger, representative samples and rigorous clinical trial designs.
