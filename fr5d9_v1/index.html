To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 10 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: For higher education students, the Internet has become the most important source of information. Because of the vast amount of misleading or biased information on the Internet, it poses challenges students need specific skills for, which are described by Critical Online Reasoning (COR). Other studies on comparable literacy constructs employed closed information spaces and simulated online environments.

Objectives: We identify log data indicators for one of COR's facets, Online Information Acquisition (OIA), and accumulate them into a factor structure using an open online environment.

Methods: 566 first-year students of three German universities logged into a virtual machine and completed a questionnaire consisting of tasks requiring an online search. Through logging their search behaviour, we extracted log events and combined them into indicators for OIA based on literature as well as our own developments. We then employed factor analysis to find their underlying structure.

Results and Conclusions: We identified ten log data indicators for OIA, of which six could be accumulated into a factor structure. Despite our initial assumption of a single underlying factor, we found two factors for OIA, one concerning search term formulation and the other concerning behaviour on the search engine results page. This expands our knowledge about OIA and COR and shows the feasibility of using log data indicators for digital literacy research.

Introduction and theoretical background

The Internet is an indispensable learning resource that has only grown in importance in recent years (Grothaus et al., 2021; Castro & Tumibay, 2021; for a literature review, see Zlatkin-Troitschanskaia et al., 2021). The use of the Internet for learning can be seen as a continuous presentation of information problems (Brand-Gruwel et al., 2009) that learners need to solve. To do so, they need to use appropriate search strategies to locate information, critically evaluate the adequacy and trustworthiness of that information, and finally integrate information from different sources to arrive at appropriate conclusions. Learners differ in the skills that support them in meeting the challenges associated with information problems (Zlatkin-Troitschanskaia et al., 2021). Such skills are not only relevant for finding information in formal learning contexts (e.g. higher education), but also to solve information problems in everyday contexts. We refer to these skills as "Critical Online Reasoning" (COR; Molerov et al., 2020; Nagel et al., 2022). Based on the prototypical process of online information problem-solving (Brand-Gruwel et al., 2009), COR comprises three interconnected facets (Molerov et al., 2020 Constitutive for these skills are both knowledge of different types of online media and cues for their trustworthiness as well as strategies to evaluate sources, for example, by researching information about media or organizations.

Reasoning based on Evidence, Argumentation and Synthesis (REAS) refers to the skills needed to ultimately synthesize the selected information and use it to develop evidencebased arguments. Thus, these skills include uniting the initially appraised information, weighting (potentially contradictive) information and perspectives, accounting for uncertainty, and using the integrated information evidence to construct a convincing argument.

The present work investigates and evaluates how the OIA facet can be measured by observing students' behaviour during their work process, as opposed to inferring it by scoring students' work products. Unlike existing research on COR (e.g. Hartig et al., 2024), we employ log data to map students' approaches to use the Internet in their search for answers to given information problems.

Our focus on the OIA facet arises from its substantial potential benefit from log data measurement, especially in an authentic setting, and the lack of comprehensive OIA examination in prior research (Hartig et al., 2026; Molerov et al., 2020). Additionally, our approach is supposed to be transferrable to other process-based measurements. Thus, the OIA facet can serve as an example of how log data indicators can give insight into constructs that are otherwise difficult to measure. In line with Molerov et al. (2020), we further distinguish between COR skills required for solving online information problems in everyday life that do not require domain-specific expertise (generic COR skills; GEN-COR) and those required for solving online information problems in settings of specific study domains, for example, medicine or economics (domain-specific COR skills; DOM-COR). In the present study, we focus on GEN-COR problems, as they provide students with a general opportunity to demonstrate their COR skills (Molerov et al., 2020). Nevertheless, our approach, with its associated possibilities and limitations, should also be applicable to DOM-COR problems.

Although OIA overlaps with several related constructs concerned with information use in digital environments, it can be distinguished by its scope and its specific function within COR. Digital literacy (Bancroft, 2016; Coiro, 2021) is a general assessment of a wide range of digital skills. It encompasses finding and consuming digital content, describing behaviour of querying search engines and navigating hyperlinks. These skills overlap with OIA, but do not isolate the strategic processes in information acquisition as a function of a wider epistemic process.

Further, OIA can be distinguished from web navigation (Gao et al., 2022). While the latter describes how users move between pages and distribute their attention across the available information, OIA is conceptualized as an ability encompassing the strategic decisions that generate such behaviour, including search planning, query formulation and modification, and the selection of sources based on their expected relevance and trustworthiness.

ICT literacy also encompasses some of the skills described by COR, with a focus on civic skills needed to effectively participate in soviety (Lennon et al., 2003). As OIA within COR, the access component of ICT literacy describes locating and collecting information online. However, access focuses primarily on the technical and procedural mechanics of searching for information within structured environments. In contrast, OIA involves cognitive and metacognitive strategies for navigation in open web spaces (Molerov et al., 2020).

The most closely related constructs regarding online information use can be found within the online information problem-solving framework (IPS-I; Brand-Gruwel et al., 2009).

GEN-COR assessment approach: Task design & evidence

To assess students' GEN-COR skills, it is essential to employ tasks that effectively elicit evidence across all facets of the construct (OIA, CIE, REAS). Following the evidence centered design approach (Mislevy et al., 2003), developing such assessments involves constructing different models within a conceptual assessment framework. This ensures that all parts of the process are aligned within a structured approach to planning and design. This enables reliable conclusions about students' skills and abilities. The models provide a framework for specifying the skills to be measured (student model), the conditions under which students' behaviour will be observed (task model), and the nature of the evidence gathered to support the intended conclusions (evidence model).

The student model for GEN-COR tasks in this paper describes the OIA skills necessary for effective information search. The skills necessary include making decisions about which search engine and search terms to use, which search strategy to employ, and which websites to visit from the search engine results page (SERP). These skills emphasize universal cognitive skills that apply across domains and express themselves via the COR facets.

The task model for our GEN-COR assessment defines both the structure and content of the tasks and the ways in which they encourage specific student behaviours. Previous research on related digital literacy constructs has utilized various methods to assess individual differences, including self-report measures of self-efficacy and objective knowledge tests with multiple-choice or open-ended questions (Goldhammer et al., 2014; Kaya & Korucuk, 2022; López-Meneses et al., 2020; Siddiq et al., 2016). However, these methods would fall short of fully capturing GEN-COR, as they lack interactivity with open information environments.

Instead, recent studies (Molerov et al., 2020; Nagel et al., 2020 Nagel et al., , 2022 highlight the importance of using authentic, interactive task environments to better assess GEN-COR skills. For example, simulation-based tasks, such as those used in the PISA digital reading assessment, provide a standardized environment where test-takers navigate pre-selected sources (OECD, 2011). While beneficial, these simulations have limitations, as they cannot nearly replicate the vast, unstructured nature of the Internet -a critical component for demonstrating COR-related strategies, such as evaluating meta-information about sources. To address these limitations, an assessment is required that allows students to work in the open environment of the Internet.

Finally, the evidence model specifies how student performance is observed and assessed, providing detailed guidelines for updating our understanding of student model variables based on work products and student behaviour. To construct the evidence model, it is necessary to identify indicators of GEN-COR skills. From these indicators, the evidence model then specifies how scores can be interpreted to allow for inferences on the skills specified in the student model. In the present study, we assume that -in addition to scoring student work products -OIA skills can be assessed through logged behavioural data, such as time spent on sources, search terms used, and navigation patterns. This approach enables a comprehensive assessment of GEN-COR capabilities.

Assessment of GEN-COR skills through log data

Unlike other COR facets like REAS, which that can be measured using students' written responses to the GEN-COR information problems (Hartig et al., 2024), OIA is not sufficiently represented in these responses (task product). Instead, evidence of the OIA skills can be found in the work process employed by students when searching for information on the Internet. Event-based data, such as log data, can shed light on the work process because a log event informs about what happened (event type and event-specific attributes, such as copying text or opening a website) and when it happened (timestamp). Conceptually, both product and log data indicators have the function of representing evidence for measuring a construct, but they differ in terms of the observational raw data used to identify evidence. The product indicator typically represents the evaluation of the final work product (e.g. correctness of a task solution), whereas the log data indicator represents behaviour related to the evolving work product (e.g. application of a certain strategy for solving a task; see Mislevy, 2019).

Previous research has increasingly employed log data to derive indicators of latent skills and strategies that are not available from task product data alone (for a systematic review, see He & Cui, 2025). For example, time-based indicators have been used to investigate cognitive processing during reading and problem-solving activities. Goldhammer et al. (2014) found that the relationship between time on task and performance depended on task demands and difficulty. For complex problem-solving tasks requiring controlled processing, spending more time was positively associated with better performance under certain conditions, whereas different relationships emerged for more routine reading tasks.

Response time has also been used to represent test-taking engagement. Very short responses may indicate rapid guessing and disengagement (Goldhammer et al., 2017). Thus, similar time-based behavioural features may represent different underlying processes, depending on the assessment context and the theoretical interpretation assigned to them.

Similarly, action-based log data have been used to characterize different latent processes and strategies. In computer-based assessments of complex problem-solving, patterns and frequencies of interactions have been used to distinguish different exploration strategies (Greiff et al., 2018). Depending on the context and correlation to time-based indicators, for example, a greater number of actions can indicate a higher latent skill or a lower efficiency than a lower number of said actions. Han et al. (2023) identified latent profiles of test-takers in which longer response times and more actions were associated with poorer performance and inefficient trial-and-error approaches.

Other previous research identified such profiles also based on process indicators of navigation and information-selection behaviour. For example, Hahnel, Kroehne, et al., (2023) categorized the information processing approach of adults in an online environment into five types that were associated with successful task-performance.

Taken together, both time-based and action-based log data indicators can help in identifying and classifying a broad range of behaviours and latent constructs. However, the prior research also underlines that their interpretation depends on the task context and theoretical evidence model.

In the GEN-COR assessment, contextualized raw log events are used for measuring OIA skills. The underlying assumption is that OIA skills determine individual differences in event sequences when students do a web search, such as using a search engine or a database.

As it is up to respondents how they approach the task, for instance, running multiple web searches to solve the given information problem, their behaviour needs to be continuously assessed in terms of construct-related evidence (continuous or ongoing assessment, DiCerbo et al., 2016; Mislevy et al., 2012) and summarized at the task-level.

The final work product -that is, a students' answer to a given information problem -is indicative of other COR facets and has been used to measure them (Hartig et al., 2026).

However, it cannot provide insight into the behaviour-focused OIA facet. Conceptually, COR tasks are made up of multiple different subtasks, each of which requires effort relating primarily to one of the three facets. COR product performance is seen as indicative of the REAS facet, though it is influenced by both preceding OIA and CIE performance (Molerov et al., 2020). Log data can bridge this gap by providing insight into users' moment-to-moment behaviour and performance. Since OIA, and by extension COR, is conceptualized as a latent skill (Molerov et al., 2020), indicators for the facet can be seen as expressions of the specific abilities required to navigate the Internet and access information. In this study, log-data indicators are treated as candidate behavioural performance indicators of OIA skills. The use of multiple complimentary indicators enables a thorough, data-driven examination of possible OIA measures.

The identification of evidence starts from event-based raw data, which is transformed into meaningful low-level features (i.e., actions, states) that are aggregated into indicators for the target construct (Goldhammer et al., 2021; Kroehne & Goldhammer, 2018). In the present study, the aggregation step is rule-based, that is, based on theoretical considerations (see Hahnel et al., 2023).

As an example, it can be argued that OIA skills are demonstrated empirically through an indicator that Osborne et al. (2022) called "click restraint". They observed that skilled individuals (e.g. fact checkers) "... scan the results page and decode the snippets of information there before deciding which might be worth pursuing. Commonly, they do not pursue the top results but pause to consider which seem most likely to provide the desired information before moving on" (p. 15). The time spent initially on the SERP might serve as an observable variable because click restraint suggests spending more time on the SERP before navigating to a linked web page. In this way, through contextualizing and aggregating high-resolution log events, it is possible to define log data indicators for OIA skills, thus increasing the measurability when compared to just using product data.

Research questions

While numerous investigations into online information problem solving (Brand-Gruwel et al., 2009) have been conducted, they usually map problem solving skills using a closed information space by limiting the websites participants may visit (e.g. Hahnel et al., 2020; Tenison & Sparks, 2023), or they use retrospective methods instead of directly observing behaviour (e.g. Boetje et al., 2024). The GEN-COR assessment differs from these studies in that it allows students to freely use the Internet, allowing for a multitude of different approaches to solve the information problem as in real-world settings. The present study aims at exploiting these extensive log data to identify indicators for OIA skills. Because this method of measuring digital skill is more open than other approaches, and in turn less standardized, the indicators used in previous research on similar skills may not be applicable.

New indicators of OIA must be defined and aggregated on a scale in order to measure OIA skills. Thus, we aim to answer the following research questions: 1.

How can log data obtained from an unconstrained Internet search be used to identify evidence for OIA skills?

2. How does this evidence accumulate into one or more distinct factors?

To address these questions, we provide an overview of the tasks used to measure GEN-COR and describe in detail how OIA indicators were defined and extracted from the µdata (RQ1). We then present the definitions of identified indicators as well as their empirical characteristics before performing analyses on their dimensional structure (RQ2).

Method Sample

For our investigation, we employed data from the Critical Online Reasoning in Higher Education (CORE) project, a longitudinal research project funded by the German Research Foundation (DFG). The CORE project followed multiple cohorts of bachelor's degree students at three German universities to investigate the development and assessment of COR competencies. During the first data collection period of the project (winter term 2023/2024), N = 566 students worked on the GEN-COR tasks. Of these, 517 participated in the main data collection track, while the remaining 49 took part in a session including eye tracking-study.

We removed cases in which participants did not work on the tasks according to the instructions (i.e., opened neither a website nor a SERP).

The final sample consisted of N = 459 students (Mage = 20.14, SDage = 2.96, 55% female). They were enrolled in medicine (n = 128), economics (n = 161), physics (n = 59), or sociology (n = 66) programs; n = 45 reported a different field of study. After agreeing to take part in the study, participants were sent information on how to log in to a virtual machine (VM), which was used to collect log data. As such, they were able to participate using their own computers and could complete their work at any time during the data collection period.

Upon completing their participation, participants received 40€ as compensation.

Instruments and Test Design

GEN-COR skills were assessed using four tasks, each of which dealt with an everyday information problem. The topics of the tasks were chosen so that (a) they did not require any domain-specific prior knowledge, (b) they were unusual enough to require some form of research (i.e., most people were not familiar with the topic), (c) they were not politically charged or mentally distressing, and (d) online research would not provide overly straightforward and one-sided results. The topics selected referred to (1) the potential health risks of radio towers, (2) the effectiveness of heat sticks in treating mosquito bites, (3) the recyclability of Tetra Pak cartons, and (4) possible toxic components in green sauce, a regional German dish.

The tasks were presented in text form and embedded in everyday-life-scenarios. In the radio tower task, for example, the test-taker was asked to imagine that they had been looking for a suitable apartment to share with a friend and had finally found a property that met their criteria.

However, during the viewing, their friend notices a radio tower on the other side of the street and claims that they do not want to move in because such towers pose a health hazard. The test-taker was then asked to conduct online research on this claim and write a short statement based on at least two Internet sources they had found. In a second open text field, they were instructed to report at least two Internet sources and the respective text sections they had used for their statement. A third text field requires them to state the three most important criteria by which they selected the sources. Figure 1 shows the radio tower task as it was presented to participants.

We designed test booklets to ensure a balanced distribution of tasks and instruments among the test takers. Within each study programme (i.e., economics and medicine), booklets were randomly assigned to the students. For the purpose of this study, it is relevant to note that two-thirds of students received one out of the four GEN-COR tasks, while the remaining students received two out of the four GEN-COR tasks. A total of 212 students completed the radio tower task, 133 students the green sauce task, 108 participants the tetra pak task, and 136 students the heat stick task.

The overall test-taking time was approximately 2.5 hours. Students could choose when and for how long they wanted to work on the assessment. They could log on to the virtual machine at any time of the day. Students were encouraged to finish a task as soon as possible, but otherwise they could take breaks and continue the assessment on another day. All GEN-COR and DOM-COR tasks had timers and had to be completed within a specified time limit.

GEN-COR tasks should be completed in 20 minutes: Five minutes for the Internet search, ten for the statement, and another five for quoting and justifying the sources. These time limits were specified in the instructions and were also displayed as a timer counting down during task processing. The timer ran out after 25 minutes, and the students were informed of this in advance.

Operationalization and extraction of indicators

Log events generated and stored by the assessment system are not meaningful per se.

Therefore, in a first step, they were labelled or translated into low-level features (Goldhammer et al., 2021; Hao & Mislevy, 2018; Kroehne & Goldhammer, 2018; Mislevy, 2019). Low-level features represent interpretable actions of the test-taker when responding in a specific task situation or states in which the test-taker finds himself. A particular action or state, as identified by the log event(s), is also determined by the situational context. That is, the same log event(s) may represent different actions or states depending on the current context and the test-taker's past or future actions and states. In a second step, low-level feature(s) are aggregated by task to log indicators (e.g. presence, duration, count, sequence, pattern of actions and states, respectively). In the present study, the rules of how to aggregate low-level features are suggested by previous research, the COR construct definition, and by empirical insights.

We illustrate these steps for the SERP time which we proposed above as an indicator of "click restraint". In the COR assessment, the SERP time can be derived from the PageOpen Finally, the indicator 'SERP time' is obtained by aggregation, that is, computing the median SERP time by person and task.

Text entries such as students' search queries were recorded as part of the log data. To assess the quality of those search queries, we employed human ratings based on the specificity of the query to the information problem. Specifically, we followed the approach of Hartig et al. (2024), rating queries on a 3-point scale based on the amount of either useful or restricting keywords. Three student raters evaluated all initial search queries. In case of disagreements across the three raters, we computed the mode for a final score.

To identify log data indicators for OIA, we first considered different search and evaluation approaches and behaviours and how these might translate into behaviour that is indicative of proficiency in OIA. We considered behaviours that arise from students' understanding of the search task and their approach to formulating queries, as well as behaviours that relate to the purposeful and task-driven selection of search results and websites for closer inspection.

Task understanding and query formulation. Before they start searching, students need to determine what information they need to solve a task.

That is, they need to reflect on what they already know and what the task requirements are that will influence their decisions and actions (see Rouet et al., 2017). Students who are deeply engaged in this process of task understanding and reflection may (1, see Table 1) take more time to enter an initial query into a search engine and (2) ensure a higher degree of relatedness of their initial query to the underlying information problem. However, throughout the search process, students may adapt or modify their knowledge structures as a result of interacting with the information they encounter (Vakkari, 2016). Therefore, students may engage in narrower follow-up searches that increase (3) their number of searches in more specific directions and (4) adapt or tune their queries to better lead to desired results.

Task-driven selection of search results and websites.

Based on information foraging theory (Pirolli, 2005), students will attempt to predict the value of a website for their information needs and estimate which search result will provide the most value for completing their search task. Accordingly, we can expect OIA proficient students to closely examine SERP snippets before engaging with information on individual websites ("click restraint"). Such a focus on identifying the most valuable information may be reflected in students (5) spending more time on SERPs as well as (6) scrolling further down in order to evaluate multiple snippets before making an initial decision about which website to open.

Once they have selected a particular website, they may even be demonstrating the practice of ( 7) efficiently searching for specific information by using search functions via keyboard shortcuts or, if present, the outlines of articles.

Similar to observations of Kirschner and Van Merriënboer (2013), less OIA proficient students may be unsure as to which websites to visit, resulting in them (8) opening many websites without interacting with them. Regardless of the amount of websites opened, (9) students might show differing ratios of time spent on websites and SERPs. A large disparity between time spent on websites and time spent on SERPs may be indicative of either an unwillingness to consider other sources or disengagement from website inspection, suggesting that a balanced approach is likely to be indicative of OIA. The ratio was thus logittransformed. Extreme ratios in either direction result in a high absolute indictor value. In the case of disengagement from website inspection, the behaviour ( 10) may indicate students consuming information directly from snippets without any further reflection.

Data management

Since the assessments were conducted within a VM, log data was automatically recorded from students via a Firefox browser add-on while they worked on their tasks. Apart from being restricted to using this browser during the task, this process was made transparent to the students and did not interfere with their workflow. The plugin communicated with a server application ("dashboard") which handled the assessment for each student and received the stream of log data. This data was stored in a Microsoft SQL Server (MSSQL) relational database. In the database, the log data was stored in the form of normalised event tables, such as the aforementioned PageOpen event. This event data was then used to create indicators at a later stage. To this end, the server provided a REST-based API for log data access.

For the further analysis presented in this paper and the implementation of the indicators, the required events (i.e., student interactions within the four GEN-COR tasks) were cleaned. Cleaning steps included the removal of duplicate entries and traces of websites visited in a previous task. Following the operationalization planning and the indicator requirements, the indicators were implemented using the following log data events. Note that not all indicators were calculated automatically using log data (i.e., "initial query quality" and "query tuning" were rated by human annotators).

(1) The events "PageOpen" and "PageFocus" provide information on when test subjects open new web pages and switch focus between active pages during their search.

Further information about the page, such as the URL or title, is available in the related Page table. While working on the assessments, users produce text in a variety of ways. For example, they may write a statement in response to a task question or use a search engine to find information.

(2) The "InputChange" event captures changes in input fields on websites (i.e., changes to the text content in text fields and text areas or the state of checkboxes). Text or words that the students highlighted or selected are available in (3) "TextMarked" events.

By using the (4) "Keystroke" log event, more fine-grained analysis of what and how students wrote can be performed. We also collected (5) "Scroll" events, which can provide information on how students interacted with search engine results pages, for example. Using (6) "LinkClick" events, we tracked how participants navigated the web, including both internal links (e.g. table of contents navigation) and external hyperlinks to other websites. Finally, students' written answers were made available in the (7) "SurveyAnswers" data.

The preparation of event data and implementation of indicators were performed in the R programming language using the tidyverse package (Wickham et al., 2019). Each indicator was reviewed by multiple authors prior to implementation. To facilitate collaborative development, the source code and derived datasets are managed using version control in a self-hosted GitLab instance.

Data analysis

After defining and implementing indicators to describe OIA skills based on log data to address research question 1, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to examine our assumption about the factor structure underlying the OIA indicators to answer research question 2. We used all the indicators that showed suitable interindividual variance. In a first step, we conducted a CFA for each available indicator from the four GEN-COR tasks.

Specifically, we tested the hypothesis that indicator variables from all tasks would load onto a single (indicator-specific) factor. Indicators whose CFA models showed an inconsistent loading structure or failed to converge were subsequently excluded from further analysis. In a second step, we employed a one-dimensional CFA model with the remaining indicators, since we assumed all indicators to be reflective of OIA skills. If the model showed a severe misfit to the assumed one-dimensional structure, we proceeded to exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to investigate the factor structure (i.e., the number of factors, their correlations, and how the indicators loaded onto the factors). All factor analyses were conducted using MPlus version 8.8 (Muthén & Muthén, 2019). For both CFA and EFA, we used the Maximum Likelihood Robust (MLR) estimator to address the various measurement scales (i.e., count, ordinal, and continuous variables) as well as violations of normality (Muthén, & Muthén, 2017; Yuan & Bentler, 2000). We applied listwise deletion for missing values. Given the categorial and count variables, absolute fit indices are unavailable under maximum likelihood estimation in Mplus. we used the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to compare EFA models regarding the number of factors. For model selection we also considered the interpretability of factors given the loading structure.

Discussion

In this study, we developed several indicators to measure the GEN-COR facet of Online Information Acquisition (OIA) based on log data. First, we elaborated on the development of potential OIA indicators (RQ1), then we evaluated the internal structure they present (RQ2). Through a process-oriented approach, we modelled the process that students follow when presented with a task, from generating a search query to refining it and ultimately selecting one or more search results. We identified ten indicators based on log data that represent aspects of OIA. These can be divided into multiple components of the search process that students need to complete. First, they have to come up with a search query that results in a satisfactory SERP. This step is illustrated by the indicators Time to first query (1), Initial query quality (2), Amount of searches (3), and Query tuning (4). Our results show that, although these process components converge in OIA, they are distinct from one another and that students' behaviours differ meaningfully in each of them. In a second step, the SERP is inspected and described by the indicators Time on SERP (5) and Scroll on SERP (6) as well as Looking for specific information (7). While the former two are meaningful descriptors of students' differences in OIA skills, our results imply that at least for first-year students in a GEN-COR context, it is not common to use browser features to look for specific pieces of information on websites or SERPs. In the final step of the OIA process, students use the information found on the SERP, either indirectly by deciding which website to visit, as described by Fluttering (8) and their personal Ratio of time spent (9), or directly through Unreflected information consumption (10). While this is theoretically meaningful, our results showed that our derived indicators were unable to represent this aspect of OIA adequately, as we discuss below.

Of the ten original indicators, only five were considered for the final model. Two of these (looking for specific information and unreflected information consumption) were excluded due to minimal individual variation. This does not necessarily mean that these indicators are not meaningful. As this study only involved first-year students, it is possible that differences in these indicators have not yet appeared but may develop over the course of their studies. Another indicator (fluttering) was removed from the analysis due to inconsistent factor loadings across tasks. This may indicate different approaches based on the task, or an insufficient operationalization. The former option seems unlikely, as no other indicator showed similar issues. The latter option seems more probable. Kirschner and Van Merriënboer (2013, p. 171) described fluttering as "behaving as butterflies fluttering across the information on the screen, touching or not touching pieces of information", which is a general statement that leaves room for interpretation. Further studies are needed to establish whether this behaviour can be found systematically in students and other Internet users. Another indicator (the ratio of time spent) resulted in a Heywood case, which can indicate model misspecification (Farooq, 2022) and was therefore dropped as recommended by Bollen (1987).

The subsequent CFA results revealed a misalignment between our preconceived assumptions about the structure of the OIA indicators and the data. Literature on the COR construct assumes OIA to be composed of a single underlying factor (Molerov et al., 2020).

However, our exploratory data analysis supports a representation by multiple distinct factors that warrant closer inspection. The initial three-factor solution produced difficult-to-interpret results, with one indicator (time to first query) accounting for most of one factor. While this could indicate the confounding influence of a different typing speed when entering the initial query, it is also conceivable that there is a third, highly specific OIA factor. This factor may consist of indicators of problem decomposition or planning indicators (Eichmann et al., 2018, although more research is needed to gain insight into this possibility.

Interpretation becomes considerably simpler for the final two-factor solution. First, the factors can easily be differentiated in terms of their content. One factor seems to be concerned with query formulation and consists of indicators for initial query quality, the amount of searches, and the amount of query tunings. Thus, this factor considers both student behaviour during the initial task presentation and during their time on the SERP. Evidence of such a factor was also identified in the study by Hartig et al. (2024). However, the present study expands beyond this OIA structure by adding a second factor. The second factor consists of two indicators: time spent on the SERP and scrolling on the SERP. It comes into play as soon as a student decides to evaluate their search results. The factor's indicators can be directly linked to the concept of click restraint introduced by Osborne et al (2022): Both are concerned with behaviour on the SERP that leads to a more comprehensive understanding of its contents, rather than quickly clicking on the first suitable-looking result. Therefore, the present study presents a measure of this concept using log data for the first time.

An important question regarding log-data based measurements concerns the generalizability across tasks. To this end, our results suggested a more parsimonious model that constrained factor loadings to be equal across the four tasks. The information criteria provided mixed evidence, resulting in a higher AIC, but a substantially lower BIC when compared to the unrestricted model. While the findings suggest a common measurement structure across tasks, they should not be interpreted as fully supporting task invariance.

For the COR construct, our findings suggest that student behaviour during the acquisition of information can be broadly categorised in two areas. First, students design a search query. Depending on the result, some students may spend a significant amount of time on the SERP right away, while others may change their search approach or tune their query, refining the SERP until they see fitting results. Our findings imply that students' behaviour in this area is reliant on interindividual differences as well as on the task itself. Second, students scan the snippets presented on the SERP. They spend time reading or skimming some of them and scrolling down the SERP to view more results. The amount of effort invested in this step also differs from student to student. While the time spent on the SERP remains relatively consistent across tasks, the extent of scrolling varies considerably (e.g. radio tower: M = 5.57 vs. heat stick: M = 13.46). This could be explained by differences in the SERP structure, with those in the heat stick task often consisting of many more ads and obvious distractions than those in the radio tower task. Of note is that both factors did not correlate with each other significantly. This is surprising, given the seemingly narrow profile of the identified indicators within the overarching COR construct. It may suggest an even more complex structure of OIA and COR in general.

Limitations

An important limitation of this study is the relatively low sample size. Although more than 500 students participated in total, the study design resulted in overlaps in the low double digits between any two tasks. This is challenging for covariance-based methods, such as the factor analytical procedure employed in this study. Although both the loading pattern as well as absolute loading values in the final factor solution were satisfactory, it is possible that some correlations that would have occurred with larger case numbers have not been revealed in our model. Thus, it remains to be seen if the structure of our results remains robust when tested with other, larger samples.

As the data was collected during the winter term 2023/24, the participants had not yet encountered the AI-supported snippets that Google, the most widely used search engine by students in this study and globally (Jamali & Asadi, 2010), implemented in 2025. These snippets operate under a more advanced aggregation system (Chen et al., 2025) and show much more information than conventional snippets. They can also be expected to provide students with more opportunities to employ the strategy described by indicator 10 (unreflected information consumption), as they contain machine-composed statements that are substantially more complex compared to the relatively basic summary of conventional snippets. Nevertheless, our results remain relevant. Since the AI-supported snippets of 2025 often contain biased or insufficient information (Chen et al., 2025), skilled searchers do quality checks, also by consulting conventional snippets to find supporting or contradictory reliable sources. Thus, most of the indicators described in this paper still apply. Although our identified indicators can still be used in the current age of AI-supported search, the everchanging nature of Google and other search engines could mean that indicators for measuring OIA may need to adapt in the future. For example, if the way in which snippets can be interacted with changes, some indicators will need to be revised in terms of their operationalization. The same would be true if snippets were eliminated altogether. However, the underlying concept of the indicators is likely to remain largely the same. Furthermore, this paper focused exclusively on GEN-COR tasks. The presented factor structure should also be applicable for domain-specific OIA as well, although further research is needed to test this assumption. Additionally, both the factor structure as well as the identified indicators themselves require further validation, for example by employing other sources of data such as eye-tracking.

Conclusion

In this paper, we employed log data to construct indicators for one of COR's three facets and were able to show that OIA consists of at least two factors, one concerning query formlation and one concerning search result evaluation. Additionally, we identified hints towards a third indicator, concerning problem decomposition. The present study is among the first to use log data in an open information space to assess a digital literacy construct. We showed the feasibility of both adapting theoretically known indicators as well as designing new ones specifically for COR log data. Most of these indicators should be resilient or adaptable to new AI-supported search engines as the skills depicted in the indicators still hold value.

Further research should be concerned with the development of OIA, both in GEN-and DOM-COR, over the course of students' studies. Additionally, our results show systematic differences in indicator scores between GEN-COR tasks. While the individual differences between tasks were consistent, it is possible that some tasks are better suited to measuring specific indicators or factors rather than OIA as a whole, which should also be tested in further research.
