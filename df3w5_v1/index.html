Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Understanding how the human brain encodes visual information remains a fundamental challenge in cognitive neuroscience. Sparse dictionary learning (SDL) has recently been applied to fMRI to discover visual features represented in the visual cortex. Existing approaches, however, depend on a vision-language model to identify the feature at discovery, and report limited discovery of the low-level visual features processed in early retinotopic visual regions that play an essential role in human visual cognition. We propose an automatic framework that systematically discovers visual features encoded in the human visual cortex, encompassing both high-level and low-level visual features. The framework decomposes fMRI responses across the full visual hierarchy with Matryoshka sparse autoencoders applied per region of interest, interprets the resulting fMRI activity patterns with a vision-language model, and introduces a set of feature channels to recover the low-level visual features that language-based interpretation fails to capture. On the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) and on the Brain Encoder Generated Dataset (BEGD), a separate set of synthesized responses from a transformer brain encoder, the low-level stream recovers thousands of interpretable low-level visual features from early retinotopic visual regions, where prior language-based pipelines report limited discovery. The high-level stream recovers interpretable semantic visual features on both datasets and generalizes to BOLD5000. Our analysis further reveals a systematic confound in language-based interpretation: fMRI activity patterns whose true representation is a low-level visual feature can be misidentified as a semantic visual feature when that feature co-varies with a salient semantic visual feature.

1 Introduction

Understanding how the human brain encodes visual information is a central question in cognitive neuroscience. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) offers a non-invasive view of cortical activity during perception, and large-scale datasets now let us study natural-image representations across the visual hierarchy (Allen et al., 2022; Gifford et al., 2023). Despite this progress, most of what we know about this hierarchy-orientation and spatial-frequency tuning in early areas, category selectivity for faces, places, and bodies in higher cortex (Kanwisher et al., 1997)-is the product of decades of hypothesis-driven probing, one feature at a time (Kay et al., 2008; Hubel & Wiesel, 1962). A complementary, data-driven approach that automatically discovers what features cortical activity encodes, across the entire hierarchy, has remained elusive.

Sparse dictionary learning (SDL) has recently emerged as a promising route for interpretability. By disentangling polysemantic representations into a sparse, overcomplete set of features, sparse autoencoders (SAEs) recover monosemantic, human-nameable patterns from networks that were previously opaque (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024), and a fast-moving line of architectural and theoretical work has improved their stability and feature quality (Bussmann et al., 2024; 2025; Tang et al., 2025b; 2026b). The same toolkit has proven portable across scientific domains, from radiology to protein language models (Abdulaal et al., 2024; Simon & Zou, 2025). Recent work has begun to apply it to the brain. BrainExplore (Wasserman et al., 2025) discovers candidate interpretable patterns in fMRI activity through unsupervised decomposition, including SAEs, and explains each pattern from the images that most strongly elicit it. SAE-BrainMap (Mao et al., 2025) trains layer-wise SAEs on vision-model activations and correlates those unit activations with voxel-level fMRI to the same images.

These advances, however, leave two gaps. First, in coverage: in the literature, existing methods recover far fewer patterns in early retinotopic visual regions than in higher cortex, explaining far fewer candidate concepts in V1, V2, and V3 compared to regions such as the extrastriate body area (EBA) (Wasserman et al., 2025). The concepts assigned to early retinotopic visual regions are also implausible: V1 and V2 patterns are labeled clock tower, clear sky, and clock, and a V3 pattern top left, a retinotopic coordinate rather than a visual concept. Second, in methodology: the prevailing recipe prompts a vision-language model (VLM) openendedly on a latent's top-activating images and treats the resulting explanation as its meaning, conflating feature recovery (identifying what a latent responds to) with explanation (naming it). Recovery thus becomes contingent on interpretability-a feature must be nameable by an agent to be found at all (Table 1). This is fragile exactly where naming is hardest, since agents reading images, whether human or VLM, attend to salient objects and overlook orientation, spatial frequency, and color (Tavakoli et al., 2017; Torralba et al., 2006; Rahmanzadehgervi et al., 2025). Faced with a low-level latent, an open-ended readout may declare it uninterpretable, and hence absent, or name a salient semantic feature that merely co-varies with it (Jacovi & Goldberg, 2020). We therefore hypothesize that low-level visual features are recovered less often than semantic features and are more often mischaracterized by open-ended language-based interpretation.

In this work, we propose a pipeline that systematically and automatically decodes both high-level and lowlevel visual features represented in visual cortex. The pipeline decomposes fMRI responses with sparse autoencoders (SAEs), interprets the resulting patterns with a vision-language model, and introduces a set of feature channels to detect the low-level visual patterns that purely language-based interpretation tends to miss. We instantiate this framework on the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) (Allen et al., 2022), augmented with synthesized responses from a transformer brain encoder (Adeli et al., 2026) applied to the Microsoft Common Objects in Context (COCO) dataset, decomposing each region of interest with a Matryoshka SAE (Bussmann et al., 2025). The resulting dictionary spans 8 subjects and 20 regions across the full visual hierarchy. Using these feature channels, we recover thousands of interpretable low-level visual patterns in early retinotopic visual regions that prior pipelines fail to recover, with interpretability comparable to semantic features. Features are recovered and validated through statistical tests in both streams to reduce the dependency on agents. We additionally replicate the high-level stream on BOLD5000 (Chang et al., 2019) in 3 subjects and 4 regions (LOC, OPA, PPA, RSC), yielding 2,933 semantic visual features across 1,202 units. Our analysis further reveals a systematic confound in language-based interpretation and shows that interpretability and feature recovery are distinct objectives.

We summarize our contributions: • A unified fMRI pattern decoding framework.

We present an SDL-based interpretability framework that recovers high-level and low-level visual patterns in a unified pipeline, achieving substantial gains on early retinotopic visual regions where prior methods fail.

• Large-scale interpretable pattern dictionary. We construct a large dictionary of identified visual patterns, with natural-language interpretations and quantitative quality metrics, to support follow-up neuroscience and interpretability research.

• A warning for mechanistic interpretability. We show that many patterns, especially those encoding low-level visual structure, can be systematically misinterpreted by VLM-based pipelines, revealing a failure mode that mechanistic interpretability must guard against.

• A dissociation between recovery and explanation. We show empirically that how selectively a unit encodes a feature carries little information about how well that feature can be explained, and that the dissociation is bi-directional.

2 Related Work

2.1 Sparse Dictionary Learning in Mechanistic Interpretability

Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse-engineer neural network representations into humanunderstandable components (Sharkey et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026b). Sparse autoencoders and related dictionary learning methods recover monosemantic, human-nameable features from activations that linear probing cannot (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2026a), with recent variants improving training stability, multi-granularity recovery, and language grounding of the discovered features (Bussmann et al., 2024; 2025; Tang et al., 2025a; Zhao et al., 2025). The same toolkit has proven portable across scientific and medical domains, including protein language models (Simon & Zou, 2025), chest radiography (Abdulaal et al., 2024), automatic failure-mode analysis (Tang et al., 2025c), and behavioral model control (Saini et al., 2026a; b). In the vision domain, SAEs have been applied to image encoders such as CLIP to surface visual concepts inside the network (Gandelsman et al., 2024; Fel et al., 2023), and recent work has begun to extend SDL to fMRI activations (Mao et al., 2025; Wasserman et al., 2025; Saini et al., 2026a). QA-Emb (Benara et al., 2024) is adjacent: yes/no questions answered by a large language model as an embedding for language-evoked fMRI, not a visual-feature recovery method. Gao et al. (2025) use large language models to score discovered units.

2.2 Brain Decoding via fMRI

Computational neuroscience has long modeled the mapping between visual stimuli and cortical responses, from Gabor-wavelet encoding of early visual cortex (Kay et al., 2008) and motion-energy reconstruction of natural movies (Nishimoto et al., 2011) to semantic maps of higher cortex (Huth et al., 2012; 2016), with broader reviews of the encoding-decoding paradigm (Naselaris et al., 2011). Large-scale stimulus-fMRI datasets such as NSD (Allen et al., 2022; Gifford et al., 2023), BOLD5000 (Chang et al., 2019), and THINGS (Hebart et al., 2023) have enabled a new generation of transformer-based brain encoders that predict cortical responses to natural images at high accuracy (Adeli et al., 2026). A complementary line of work applies sparse decomposition to fMRI representations themselves to surface interpretable cortical patterns, including SAE-BrainMap (Mao et al., 2025), sparse concept atoms for brain encoding (Zeng & Gallant, 2025), and BrainExplore (Wasserman et al., 2025).

3.1 Data Augmentation

We augment NSD with synthesized responses, giving dictionary learning more image-response pairs and broader coverage of visual content. To check the quality of the synthesized responses, we freeze one dataset's dictionary and reconstruct the other (Appendix G).

3.3 Low-level Visual Feature Recovery and Interpretation

The low-level visual feature (LVF) stream evaluates units learned from early retinotopic visual regions (V1v, V1d, V2v, V2d, V3v, V3d, hV4) in NSD and BEGD, which carry comparatively sparse semantic representations (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008) and are classically modeled through channels tuned to natural image statistics (Olshausen & Field, 1997). First, we define an LVF as an elementary visual property that the fMRI patterns in those areas may represent, such as orientation, spatial frequency, local contour, color, and relative depth (Hubel & Wiesel, 1962; Kay et al., 2008; Olshausen & Field, 1997), and identify seven candidate low-level feature channels from findings in relevant literature as plausible feature modalities under which the LVFs can be represented in those areas. For each feature channel, we then choose an academically validated image-decomposition tool. It maps each image to a tensor that approximates the signal processed in the corresponding visual areas. As these feature channels are also functionally localized across these ROIs, we test units only under the biologically plausible combinations of feature channels and ROIs of Table 7.

Table 6 summarizes the feature channels, supporting evidence, corresponding tools, and the shapes of the tensor maps. Second, to discover whether an LVF stably evokes each unit, for each candidate channel we extract a candidate feature by a response-weighted average (RWA) of the channel tensor maps against an equally weighted prior, score each image by an expression score-the cosine similarity between the priorcentered image tensor and the candidate feature-and retain a feature only if it passes three conjunctive gates (salient, biologically predictable, enriched) and, among co-varying features, unique predictability, as detailed in Algorithm 1. Third, we interpret each retained unit within its top-N top sample by visualizing the feature's spatial expression as a signed heatmap and running the shared two-stage explainer-evaluator procedure of Section 3.5, constraining the explainer to the feature's channel and away from semantic content to reduce VLM bias (Tavakoli et al., 2017; Dahou et al., 2025) (prompts in Appendix B.1 and B.2). BOLD5000 groups the early retinotopic visual areas into a single region of interest labeled EarlyVis, so the learned patterns cannot be localized to individual ROIs. This potentially mixes localized, meaningful response patterns with irrelevant activity in other areas; as a result, MVPA excludes over 90% of otherwise salient unit-channel pairs, and the LVF stream cannot be precisely replicated here. We nonetheless recovered 53 low-level features across 49 units carried by fMRI patterns shared across early visual areas, and show them qualitatively in Appendix D (Figure 5).

3.4 High-level Visual Feature Recovery and Interpretation

The high-level visual feature (HVF) stream recovers semantic visual features from units learned from all ROIs in NSD and BEGD, and is extended to LOC, OPA, PPA, and RSC in BOLD5000. Features recovered on BOLD5000 are reported separately and are not added to the NSD and BEGD totals. In NSD and BEGD, this includes the early retinotopic areas (V1-V3, hV4), as we intend to contrast the recovery outcomes of these units under an alternative semantic assumption. First, to provide a detailed representation of the semantic concepts every image carries, we prompt a VLM (Gemini-3.1-flash-lite-preview) to convert each image into a structured caption covering its primary focus, salient objects, setting, colors, activity, and any visible text (Appendix B.3). These captions are then atomized into standalone semantic visual features, and deduplicated into a canonical dictionary of the semantic visual features in the stimuli. For each image, we shortlist features from that dictionary by lexical match and high CLIP cosine similarity between each dictionary feature and the image's caption. Finally, we use Gemini-3.1-flash-lite to verify whether each shortlisted visual feature is supported by the image's caption and structured fields (Appendix B.4). Failed or blocked requests are completed using GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.5 on NSD and BEGD, and GPT-5.4-mini on BOLD5000. The resulting labels yield a sparse binary vector v(i) ∈ {0, 1} K over the canonical dictionary features. Second, to recover the semantic features of each unit, we separately retrieve all semantic visual features that are systematically enriched or depleted in its top-activating images, beyond or below their natural prevalence, as detailed in Algorithm 2. To test whether selectivity extends past the images used to recover the feature, we additionally repeat the enrichment check on activation ranks 81-100. Third, we interpret each unit with at least one recovered feature under a two-stage procedure described in Section 3.5, by passing all its recovered features to the explainer as text, together with the montage of its top-20 activating images. We use a different schema for units with only enriched features versus those carrying at least one depleted feature (prompts in Appendix B.5 and B.6).

For 3,529 enriched features the positive-activation list is shorter than this, so the window is skipped and the top-20 result is kept; 72.2% of recovered enriched features hold at q ≤ 0.01 and 45.0% at q ≤ 0.001.

A further 9,603 depleted features across 3,058 units capture concepts conspicuously absent from a unit's top activations. For 119 depleted features, the ranks 21-40 window contains fewer than 10 images with positive activation, so validation on that window is skipped and the top-20 result is kept. Depleted features are recovered with comparable selectivity (median depletion 0.45) and higher interpretability (median AIS 0.85 vs. 0.75 for enriched), and 271 units are recovered by depletion alone-features that one-tailed, overrepresentation-only criteria cannot reach. At the unit level, 63.1% of units are selective at 3× base rate and 93.6% reach an AIS of 0.5. HVF selectivity is barely explained by dataset, ROI, or subject (model R² = 0.072), whereas recovery counts are dominated by dataset (partial deviance = 0.659; Poisson pseudo-R² = 0.798): semantic features are far easier to find in BEGD. The asymmetry is extreme in early retinotopic visual regions, where the stream recovers 10.4% of BEGD units but only 0.15% and 0.14% of the corresponding NSD units. We suggest that prior reports of rich semantic representation in early retinotopic visual regions (Wasserman et al., 2025) partly reflect pooling generated data over natural fMRI responses. Unit selectivity also generalizes beyond the head sample: 56.7% of features in BEGD and 48.6% in NSD remain selective at ranks 81-100. On BOLD5000 (CSI1-CSI3; LOC, OPA, PPA, RSC) the same stream recovered 1,202 units and 2,933 semantic features (4.89% of that dictionary; median AIS 0.650 on the 359 interpreted units).

4.3 Feature Sparsity Drives Recovery

The selectivity of recovered LVF features is largely explained by their natural sparsity. We measured selectivity by AUROC (top-20 activation membership as target, expression score as continuous score), which-unlike enrichment-does not share the prevalence factor with sparsity. Recovered features are generally selective, ranging from relative depth (median AUROC 0.704) to skeleton (0.929), and channel identity again dominates (R² = 0.380, partial η² = 0.255). Measuring sparsity as the proportion of images in which a feature is absent, we find it strongly correlated with selectivity across channels (channel-level Spearman ρ = 0.77): the rarer a feature type is, the more selectively its recovering unit fires. Table 5 reports the mean sparsity and median AUROC of each channel. The two orders agree except that symmetry and oriented energy swap, and saliency and relative depth swap. Figure 12 illustrates the mechanism, contrasting one selective skeleton feature with two non-selective features from the depth and saliency channels. The skeleton feature is present in only a sparse set of fewer than 100 images, and its unit fires selectively on exactly that set (AUROC = 1.000). The depth and saliency features are present across nearly the entire image set: the natural scenes here are cluttered everyday images (Lin et al., 2014), so a foreground-background depth contrast and a figure-ground saliency structure appear in almost every image. Each unit still fires on only a sparse subset, but that subset is merely a fraction of all images containing the feature, so the unit cannot rank images by feature strength and its selectivity collapses to chance (AUROC = 0.498 and 0.540). Yet both units still recover a genuine, highly interpretable feature (AIS ≥ 0.9; Figures 15 and 16). We therefore suggest that a feature is selectively recovered when the architectural sparsity of the SAE aligns with the natural sparsity of the feature it encodes; when a feature is far denser than the sparsity constraint, the unit capturing it tends to subset the images containing it under impaired selectivity. We tested this by removing the TopK constraint from the Matryoshka encoder, leaving the linear map followed by JumpReLU, and retraining on NSD alone. Where a feature is as rare as that constraint, or rarer, mean AUROC is already high, and removing TopK lowers it: skeleton moves from 0.917 to 0.865. Where a feature is present in more images than the constraint keeps active, mean AUROC rises: relative depth moves from 0.600 to 0.707. The with-TopK means pool both NSD replications and the without-TopK means are rep1 only. The original mean AUROC predicts that gain across the seven channels (Pearson r = -0.954, 95% interval [-0.993, -0.714], p = 8.5 × 10 -4 , R² = 0.91, n = 7; Figure 4).

Five channels differ at Benjamini-Hochberg q ≤ 0.001. Symmetry (2 features with TopK, 3 without) and opponent color (2 features without TopK) do not (q = 0.060), because the smaller group in each pair has only two or three features.

5 Discussion

5.1 Selectivity and Interpretability Are Distinct

Our experiments show that selectivity and interpretability are largely independent across recovered primary features on NSD and BEGD (Spearman ρ = -0.084 between AUROC and AIS). Many features are selectively encoded but difficult to explain, while others are readily described despite weak selectivity. This contrast is especially clear across channels: skeleton features are the most selective yet least interpretable, whereas relative depth shows the opposite trend. A similar lack of correlation appears in the HVF stream between a unit's strongest enrichment and its AIS, although the two streams evaluate different objects and need not share the same underlying mechanism. We believe this distinction reveals some fundamental limitations for prevalent feature discovery pipelines used in mechanistic interpretability. If a feature is retained only when an agent can describe it convincingly, selective but poorly verbalized features will be systematically missed. The two ns pairs are symmetry and opponent color; each has only two or three features on the smaller side.

5.2 Language-Based Interpretation Can Misattribute Features

Our results also demonstrate that language-based explanations can identify the wrong feature. Since early retinotopic visual regions are primarily associated with visual properties such as orientation, spatial frequency, and position (Hubel & Wiesel, 1962; Kay et al., 2008) rather than semantic visual features (Kanwisher et al., 1997), semantic visual representations are less expected based on our prior knowledge. When a low-level representation is interpreted through a semantic proxy, however, the explainer may still identify a semantic exemplar that co-occurs with the underlying feature rather than the feature itself, merely due to its agentic biases-a failure mode we term masquerade. We quantitatively demonstrate this by the most frequent semantic concept we have discovered from these areas-sky. Sky was recovered in 6,744 of the 12,308 units (54.8%), against 2,386 units for the next most frequent feature, outdoor natural scene (19.4%).

Sky was recovered at a median enrichment of 7.24 times over base rate and reached a median AIS of 0.65. Nonetheless, the explanatory power of sky was systematically weaker when compared with the low-level visual features recovered from the same units. We first identified 388 units from which sky was semantically recovered, and at least one low-level visual feature was also recovered under the LVF stream (411 low-level features in total). We then compared their presence in the images that evoke the unit across these 411 pairs. In the median case, two-thirds of the sky-absent images among a unit's top-20 activating images still express its low-level visual feature (0.667), and the low-level visual feature is present in more of the top-activating sample than sky is (0.800 against 0.650). The semantic feature therefore accounts for less of a unit's topactivating sample than the low-level visual feature recovered from the same unit. Figures 18 and 19 show that comparison within one unit, between its recovered semantic feature (sky) and its low-level visual feature (structured edges). We suggest, through this case, that a plausible explanation does not guarantee that the correct feature has been identified (Jacovi & Goldberg, 2020).

5.3 Baseline Comparability

No prior study is a direct numerical baseline for CortexLens. An eligible study would decompose visually evoked fMRI without supervision, discover within-ROI activity patterns, and automatically identify and evaluate the visual features those patterns represent. Among the methods in Table 1, only Brain-Explore (Wasserman et al., 2025) meets the task criteria, and its numbers are not comparable: it uses pattern-hypothesis alignment for discovery and for interpretability, whereas CortexLens separates statistically tested recovery from explanation quality. The public release did not include the pipeline, concept dictionary, and ranking data a common-protocol reanalysis would need. A direct comparison will require the same dataset, feature ontology, activation sample, and evaluation protocol.

6 Conclusion

CortexLens learns fMRI activity patterns across the human visual hierarchy through sparse decomposition, and recovers and interprets the semantic visual features and low-level visual features in the images that evoke those patterns through an automatic pipeline. On NSD and BEGD this yields thousands of interpretable low-level visual features in early retinotopic visual regions, where existing approaches depend on a vision-language model to identify the feature at discovery and report limited discovery there. Across NSD, BEGD, and BOLD5000, CortexLens also discovered a rich collection of interpretable semantic visual features represented by the fMRI activity patterns across the visual cortex. Together, we believe CortexLens provides a comprehensive framework for studying visual representations in the visual cortex.
