Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Contemplative science is entering a third wave of research. This third wave furthers the study of experienced meditators, advanced meditative states, stable trait changes, and meditative development, and references contemplative traditions and spiritual/transpersonal approaches. The interdisciplinary intersection of these fields creates opportunities and tensions. We show how these trends in contemplative science would benefit from incorporating theories of adult cognitive development, especially in the shift from studying states to studying traits under the general theme of wisdom. We also note several conceptual dissonances within and between the fields, in addition to offering a framework for resolving them. We suggest that integrating contemplative science with developmental theory (in a way that is compatible with spiritual/transpersonal studies) requires giving equal importance to movements of structural "release" (apophatic, "descending") and vertical (hierarchical, "ascending") growth. We elaborate on the nature of descending release, its interactions with ascending growth processes, show how it relates to existing scholarship in the field, and describe research directions implicated by the framework. This conceptual frame is intended to support a generative interdisciplinary integration of contemplative science, developmental psychology, and spiritual/religious perennial philosophy.

Introduction

In this introduction, we describe emerging trends and key questions in contemplative science and argue that this research would benefit from incorporating adult developmental theories and, further, that doing so helps coordinate contemplative science with the domain of spiritual/wisdom studies. This sets the stage for an interdisciplinary argument that all three fields would benefit from a clearer distinction between structural growth processes ("ascending" complexity-building) and structural release processes ("descending" or unlearning)-a claim elaborated in the body of the article.

Historical context

The scientific study of contemplative practices has matured over the last 35 years. Sacchet & Lieberman (2026) frame this progress in terms of three overlapping waves: a first wave focused on "mindfulness" and its health and therapeutic effects; a second wave focused on more rigorous clinical trials, performance enhancement, and biological and psychological mechanisms; and a new third wave focused on advanced meditation, including states and stages of practice that unfold with time and mastery. Though there were notable early studies focused on third wave themes, such themes are finding increasing prominence and will continue to mature. 1 The third wave broadens inquiry beyond novice practitioners and short-term interventions to include states, stages, and meditative endpoints (including pinnacle states of absorption, cessation, and nondual awareness) across meditative mastery and development (Sacchet et al., 2024; van Lutterveld et al., 2024; Chowdhury et al., 2023; Ehmann et al., 2024; Sezer & Sacchet, 2024; Chowdhury et al., 2024; Treves et al., 2024; Potash et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024a,b; Schoenberg et al., 2018); persistent traits and developmental stages (Singleton et al., 2021; Galante et al., 2023; Britton, 2019); mixed methods research combining physiological measurement with phenomenological reports (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022 , 2024; Louchakova-Schwartz, 2013; Petitmengin & Bitbol, 2009), and more expansive references to wisdom traditions, virtue, psychedelic and flow states, as well as adverse or unintended effects (Wright et al., 2023 (Wright et al., , 2024 (Wright et al., , 2025;; Vieten et al., 2018; Loizzo, 2017; Goldberg et al., 2018; Palitsky et al., 2023).

Many of these trends point to the need to add the study of traits, skills, or developmental capacities to the predominant focus on meditative states. Key scholars in adult development include William Perry, Lawrence Kohlberg, James Fowler, Clair Graves, Jane Loevinger, Michael Commons, Kurt Fischer, Robert Kegan, Bill Torbert, Suzanne Cook-Greuter, Terri O'Fallon, and Ken Wilber (see References). A (non-comprehensive) query-based search of the contemplative science literature suggests that there are extremely few papers in recent decades that mention adult development or reference these 12 authors.

In recent papers mentioning the third wave of contemplative science (Galante et al., 2023; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025; Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026; Sacchet et al., 2024), scholars evaluate research trends and argue for more interdisciplinary and stakeholder-participatory approaches, more standardization and rigor in tracking factors such as dosage, practice details, and mindset, larger sample sizes, and more longitudinal studies-including developmental studies of meditative outcomes (which we call "meditative development"). 2 In this article, we take the next step by focusing on advances in theoretical and conceptual foundations rather than research methodologies.

Developmental Theories, Ego Development and Wisdom

Adult developmental psychology studies the progression of the following overlapping constructs through the lifespan: meaning-making, perspective-taking, self-understanding, identity, wisdom, world-view, cognitive complexity, moral capacity, and depth of insight (e.g. Piaget, 1971; Kohlberg, 1973; Loevinger, 1976; Kegan, 1994; Fischer, 1980; and Torbert, 2014; Cook-Greuter, 2000; Commons & Richards, 1984; Fowler, 1981). These constructs have been tested and refined over 50 years, with assessments demonstrating strong psychometric properties (see Murray, 2017, for an overview).

Comparing and contrasting various developmental theories is beyond the scope of the current discussion, but persistent overlapping themes appear across these works (as noted in Kegan, 1994; Wilber, 2000; Demetriou et al., 2013). Wisdom is a general umbrella term we use for the set of "overlapping constructs" mentioned above. It is less precise than many other developmental constructs, especially those accompanied by validated assessments, but it has good coverage over the range of themes alluded to above, including reflective abstraction, perspective-taking, meaning-making sophistication, self-understanding, and moral reasoning. A commonly accepted definition of wisdom is the ability to make good decisions or have deep insights in complex, multiperspectival, and uncertain situations, aiming to benefit the most stakeholders over the longest time period (for other definitions of wisdom see Walsh, 2015; Sternberg & Glück, 2022; Stanovich & West, 2008).

The historical roots of contemplative practice point beyond the quotidian and practical toward the ultimate, foundational, aspirational, moral, soteriological, and/or esoteric (Hadot, 1995; Davis & Vago, 2013; Palitsky et al., 2023). Applying Developmental Theory to Contemplative Science

The developmental lens can enhance contemplative science in at least five ways.

1. Such theories of meaning-making maturity can help explain or predict how meditation practitioners interpret the purpose of contemplative practice and related experiences. Earlier stages of development may be oriented toward following traditions or teachers, or to concrete conventional goals of ethical behavior, and practitioners may interpret unusual states in mythical or magical terms. Middle stages may be oriented to self-improvement goals and scientific evidence; and later stages may be intentionally working on deconstructing or integrating aspects of the self or the unconscious, while becoming more comfortable with uncertainty and paradox.

2. Developmental assessments can be used to measure before and after interventions or ongoing longitudinal changes from meditation practices or related interventions. We should note that developmental advancement is not necessarily good or desired. Developmental theory can be used to better understand and appreciate an individual wherever they might be in development and also to frame interventions that might work on or fortify weaknesses in developmentally earlier strata of the psyche.

3. Developmental assessments can help explain or predict participants' motivations, expectations, and meaning-making around meditation studies. Assessed developmental levels could help customize practice instructions or participation invitations according to likely motivations, values, depth of self-reflection, or cognitive complexity, and could also be used as a covariate in explaining outcomes in a given study. 4. Developmental theory is the study of prerequisites: models can map hierarchical sequences of sub-skills that often remain implicit. Contemplative practices and related skills, for example, focused attention, metacognition, and ego-awareness, may be arranged according to prerequisite order; formal developmental analysis could clarify instructional sequencing toward positive outcomes. 5. Developmental theory can frame the complexity and evolution of theoretical models and research designs overall. This includes moves from anecdotal to empirical methods, from strictly quantitative to mixed methods, from objectivist or reductionist frames to participatory or constructivist approaches, from linear causation to coupled dynamics, and from disciplinary to inter/trans/multi-disciplinary approaches.

It seems clear that collaborating with developmental science would be fruitful for contemplative science (and vice versa, though we do not map out that space here). Yet there are some conceptual and philosophical quandaries within both fields that inhibit such an integration. In a companion paper "Harmonizing Interdisciplinary Conundrums in Advanced Contemplative Science: Maturity and Release in Wisdom Development, Meditation, Spirituality, and Psychological Healing" ([authors redacted]) we list six conceptual "conundrums" facing research within and conjoining the domains of contemplative science, developmental sciences, and spiritual/wisdom traditions, especially as ("third wave") meditation research focuses on more advanced states and stages: (1) Presumptions that complexity capacity and spiritual clarity track together into later stages.

(2) The "growth to goodness fallacy" that leads to unwarranted assumptions about state achievements and contemplative/spiritual adepts and teachers. (3) Conflation of state and stage, or the assumption that state experiences automatically confer stable wisdom or status. (4) Paucity of research or understanding of what causes adverse outcomes in meditation (and nonordinary state induction). (5) Controversies concerning the relative priority or ordering of achievements or practices among various maps of the territory. (6) Legitimizing the indigenous and traditional knowledge that motivates or informs contemplative science.

Below we propose a developmental meta-framework (the Wisdom Skills framework) that ameliorates these conundrums. We describe the framework below. In the companion paper, we give more detail about how each of these conundrums is resolved.

On Growth vs. Release Phenomena

On Growth vs. Release Phenomena

On structural growth and standard developmental theory

The "neo-Piagetian hierarchical complexity" theories of Michael Commons and Kurt Fischer cast developmental growth as simply the building of new cognitive structures (schemas or levels) upon prior ones. 8 New stages of capacity emerge through the integration of a set of skills from the prior level. Though most developmental theories map out specific sequences of skills and capacities, at the core, developmental theory concerns the rigorous mapping out of prerequisites-i.e. it is a science of discovering necessary orderings among skill and sub-skill acquisition. For example, the integrated skill of riding a bicycle builds upon its compositional subskills, including steering, balancing, and pedaling. Commons' axiomatic definition of development defines the "complexity order" of a capacity in terms of how many hierarchical levels of one thing building upon another that a task or skill implies (Commons & Richards, 1984; see more in the Supplementary Appendix A).

Our model emphasizes the unlearning of structural release because, though it is an important element of wisdom development and is implicated in the development of many other capacities (e.g. ego-awareness, servant leadership, perspective-taking, spiritual intelligence), it is not formally differentiated or incorporated into developmental studies-and needs to be for developmental theory to be applied to contemplative science. A (non-comprehensive) querybased search of papers authored by the 12 major developmentalists listed above confirms that they all rely heavily on hierarchical or growth metaphors (more details in the Supplementary materials). For example, their papers include descriptions of development using: a biological metaphor of growth (Perry, 2014); invariant hierarchical sequence (Loevinger, 1976); hierarchies of skills that organize and transform lower levels (Commons, 2008); terms including unfolding, emergent, and spiraling (Graves, 2002); hierarchical transcend-and-include processes (Wilber, 2017); and transformations in vertical development (Rooke & Torbert, 2005).

A framework that gives equal ontological status to structural release, alongside structural growth, better describes the complexities of wisdom (and related) development.

On structural release as descending maturation

In contrast to ascending (hierarchical) growth, descending release is the loosening, deconstruction, healing, or letting go of cognitive structures. Structural release can be described using three related concepts found repeatedly in the literature on psychological and spiritual maturation: occlusion, release, and recovery. We can sketch this territory with a word-salad associated with each.

Occlusions are learned structures seen as maladaptive from habituating negative behaviors or thoughts, or from inhibiting/hiding positive ones. Related terms include trauma, shadow material, psychic injury, limiting ideas, negative beliefs, frozen narratives, defenses, attachments, identification, illusions, biases, splits, fusions, traps, boundaries, etc. (found in the psychotherapeutic literature, e.g. Van der Kolk, 2014; Maté, 2012; Levine, 2010; Sapolsky, 2004; Porges, 2001, and the contemplative/transpersonal literature, e.g. Shapiro et al., 2006; Dahl et al., 2015).

Occlusions call to be released, ablated, dissolved, let go, healed, deconstructed, decomposed, dismantled, de-centered, dis-identified, laid bare, or seen through. Once released, the occluded material is recovered, exposed, reclaimed, liberated, revealed, revised, remembered, reinterpreted, reintegrated, etc., which are generally ascending movements. 9 For example, researchers have studied learning at the so-called micro-developmental or micro-genetic level between stage transitions, observed over timescales orders of magnitude shorter than development from one stage of hierarchical complexity to the next (Fischer, 2008; Ross, 2014; Dawson-Tunik et al. 2005). Regression or decompensation can also occur at impasses where existing knowledge no longer functions and must be scrutinized or deconstructed. Though some theories mention longer term arcs of release or reintegration (e.g. O'Fallon, 2020; Cook-Greuter, 2005), we are not aware of any empirical studies on the relationships between temporary states and developmental stages.

The domain of "occlusion, release, and recovery" encompasses phenomena such as acceptance, forgiveness, unknowing, humility, post-conventional disidentification from cultural delusion or the "consensus trance," and contemplative accounts of ego-transcendence, ego-death, or the release of egoic identification. Sentiments such as "be here now," returning to home, source, or the sacred, and ensoulment also indicate the release of structural complexity and the blossoming of primordial capacities. Injunctions to attend to the immanent over the transcendent, including appreciating the sanctity of daily life and seeing the sacred within the mundane, are descending notions.

The lifelong journey of development inevitably creates occlusions, the most salient of which may be childhood capacities lost or attenuated during adult maturation, such as playfulness and curiosity, naive spontaneity, un-self-conscious curiosity and openness, raw vitality and enthusiasm, earnest honesty, innocent hopefulness, simple trust and intimacy, perceptual clarity, affinity for the natural world, and creative/imaginal/enchanted imagination. Each individual has a unique set of such occlusions, which can eventually be experienced as limitations calling for release and recovery. Some aspects of wisdom and spiritual maturation, such as psychic wholeness, intimacy with nature, deep interdependence, and the stable experience of "basic goodness," can be framed in descending terms as occluded aspects of the newborn's innate being that are recovered with the application of skillful means, including mature metacognitive awareness and equanimity. Note that other aspects of wisdom are clearly not innate and must be learned (ascending movement), such as emotional regulation, metacognition and executive functioning, self-understanding, multi-perspective-taking, discernment in ethical dilemmas, and curiosity about one's biases and unconscious motivations.

Phenomenologically, ascending structural growth has the sense of gaining a new capacity "that I never had before," whereas descending structural release and recovery have the sense of revelation or insight in which "a part of me always knew this but I forgot." Analogously descending movements are about "learning what is not true" (that one consciously or uncoinsiously believed was true, for example, "my mother did not wish me harm-she was doing the best she could;" or "I believed that the experience of space-time always operated a certain way, and now realize that was false." There are many psychological models explaining these apophatic dynamics-the details of which are beyond our scope (see citations in the occlusions paragraph above)-as our aim is to contrast the phenomenon of structural release with that of structural growth, described in the literature using quite different processes and metaphors (learning, hierarchical complexity, empowerment, skill/capacity building, etc. for the latter). 11 What is important here is that we hypothesize (and suggest for future research) that ascending vs. descending processes may involve different neurological mechanisms, different practice regimes, and require different cautions against unintended and negative outcomes.

For instance, it is understood that in ascending (hierarchical) development one can not "skip levels" any more than one can build the fourth story of a building right on top of the first. 11 Concepts from Complex Adaptive/Dynamic Systems theories can be used to explain descending movements: a system cannot enter a new stable attractor state without first destabilizing or significantly perturbing the existing one, with more radical reorganizations involving deeper shifts in the system's organizing relationships and constraints, allowing new patterns of self-organization to emerge (Thelen & Smith, 1994; Kelso, 1995).

In contrast, one can, e.g., through meditation or psychedelics, release lower levels within the psychic "stack" without needing to release intermediate levels.

Practice methods for ascending and descending processes may differ, and if one conflates ascending with descending, one runs the risk of (1) addressing a problem that requires releasing structures through practices that build structural complexity (without releasing anything); or (2) addressing a problem that requires building complexity, nuance, or sophistication through practices that actually release structures.

All structures create occlusion

In our framework for psychospiritual development (also called the Wisdom Skills model), we maintain that all learning, that is, all cognitive structures (ideas, skills, concepts, assumptions, beliefs, habit patterns, "conditioning," etc.) hide or occlude cognitive content and capacity as well as provide positive new capacities. This contrasts with the common frames in psychotherapy, meditation, and personal growth that focus on the release, deconstruction, or healing of unwanted or harmful cognitive structures. Rather, we argue that all learned structures, by the very nature of being a stable system of interconnections (be it framed as neural pathways, semantic associations, functional rules, etc.) must include aspects that "hold it in place" or defend against alternative or competing ways of being and doing. Mature (automated or habituated) ideas and skills form the solid base upon which other ideas and skills are built-this is the nature of development (i.e. the hierarchical complexity of "one thing building upon another").

Developmental change has been framed in the dialectical terms of thesis (current meaning-making), antithesis (disclosed limitations or negations of the thesis), and synthesis (that transcends and includes)-but this model is overly simplistic. Development is not merely additive-it is selective. New capacities come online while others are backgrounded, suppressed, or rendered phenomenologically inaccessible. Development involves trade-offs: what becomes foregrounded at one level may render prior ways of knowing and doing less accessible, and new solutions create new problems.

All structures must be originally adaptive or useful, given that the organism expends energy to learn/build and stabilize/automate it. But some learned structures become problematic when environmental conditions change, and they no longer serve an adaptive purpose. Yet our working hypothesis (framed as a "transcendental" argument from necessary conditions-see Habermas, 1992; Bhaskar, 1993) is that all learned structures create such occlusions, and any learned structure might later be seen as problematic if changing life conditions render it obsolete or maladaptive.

Release of primordial structures, splits, and confabulations

As learning and development are often understood in terms of processes of differentiation and integration (Piaget, 1971; Kegan, 1994; Fischer, 1980), problematic structures can be understood in terms of splits and mergers (confabulations). 12 We think of structural release as more of a reduction in the habituation, grip, attachment to, or identification with a cognitive structure, rather than its complete erasure or destruction. Some theorists associate the release of cognitive or psychological structures with the liberation of bound-up psychic "energy" that had been used to rigidly maintain the structure (Freud, 1959; Jung, 1966; Reich, 1990). 13 Much of the above sounds familiar within the domains of psychotherapy and basic mindfulness, but what about advanced meditation that includes transcendent or peak states? We can also describe these in terms of the structural release-but of the earliest (primordial) cognitive structures. Cognitive structures for the perception of space, time, bodily boundaries, object permanence, etc., are not normally thought of as part of the personality/ego structure or the shadow material addressed by psychotherapy and basic mindfulness. Advanced contemplative practice and the journey to meditative endpoints including "awakening" and "enlightenment" is sometimes described in terms of undoing primordial splits (dualities) between self and others, self and world, mind and body (or heart), interior and exterior, past/present/future, individuality and unity, thingness (or being) and no-thingness (or emptiness), etc. (Thompson, 2014; Garfield, 1995; Ferrer, 2011; Chowdhury et al., 2024). To release a structure is to experience reality without the constraints, splits, or mergers dictated by that structure, which, for primordial structures, can lead to experiences of timelessness, boundlessness, no-self, non-conceptual thought, cessation of consciousness, etcetera.

Our framework thus unifies, under the metaphor of "descending release," the "middle" psychic strata of narrative ego, infant attachment, personality, psychological "complexes," etc., whose release is the focus of psychotherapy, with more primordial psychic structures that establish the basic apperceptions of time, space, object, self, subjectivity, memory, intention, and attention, whose release are the focus of advanced contemplative practices (and other research into no ordinary states including psychedelics). Learned structures can be ordered quasidevelopmentally, by order of prerequisite or composition, roughly from earliest to latest (with some skills developing concurrently), based on sound cognitive research on children and adults. Assessments of adult stages can be used to test the maturation of wisdom skill (or ego development) resulting from contemplative practices and can be correlated with neurophysiological measures. Research in infant and child development can be used to map release experiences to subsequences in early developmental capacities in spatiotemporal processing, identity/ego formation, reflective thought, and key differentiations including self-vsother, self-vs-world, and inside-vs-outside (e.g. Rochat, 2003).

• Rational: formal operations, autonomous and self-authoring, "modern" values, logical, abstract and critical thinking.

• Post-rational: post-modern or "meta-modern" values, self-aware and self-transforming mind, dialectical and complex-dynamic-systems thinking.

This sketch is not intended to propose a new developmental stratification, but to give a basic sense of the path of development, with additional detail described by theorists including Cook-Greuter (2005), Torbert (2014), O'Fallon (2012), and Kegan (1994). The general trend is that wisdom grows from simplistic "black and white," "us vs. them," parochial meaning-making toward ever more complex, nuanced, inclusive, insightful, and global meaning-making.

Developmental models provide a map of increasing complexity, nuance, abstraction, and depth (which nominally, but not necessarily, mature co-dependently) that coordinates several cognitive phenomena of importance to contemplative science within a single sequence of "action logics": (1) primordial capacities such as spatiotemporal and interoceptive processing; (2) the magical/mythical strata of cognition that blurs into dreamlike imagery, unconscious processes, creativity, and intuition; (3) abstract, logical, and scientific ways of thinking; and (4) postrational and "dialectical" cognition capable of engaging dynamic, ecosystemic principles while also sustaining the "negative capability" required to recognize the emptiness, contingency, and constructed nature of phenomena.

Prior Research Supporting the Proposed Framework

We are aware of only one research program that has combined these three areas: meditation studies, adult developmental models, and neuroimaging. Singleton et al. (2021) used functional MRI to test for correlates between ego development stage and brain structure/functional connectivity in 14 long-term meditators, 16 long-term yoga practitioners, and 16 demographically matched controls. They found that the contemplative groups exhibited higher developmental maturity compared to a matched control group. They also found that developmental maturity correlated with cortical thickness of the posterior cingulate. The relative paucity of such studies, alongside our argument for the importance of combining them, supports the need for frameworks like the one we propose.

While the interdisciplinary treatment of these three areas is sparse, we can cite much in the contemplative science literature that supports or is compatible with the framework we describe in this paper. Specifically, our ascending/descending (growth/release) model can be linked to two recurring themes in contemplative science: (A) stratified models of cognition, in which cognitive capacities are understood as layered or developmentally cumulative; and (B) deconstructive/ablative accounts of cognitive function, in which higher-order constructions are loosened or released, allowing more direct access to primordial or foundational layers of experience. This provides prior foundations and support for our framework and also indicates gaps or unknowns in current research that our framework helps explore.

(A) Stratified models. Stratified hierarchical models are found in both neuroscience and developmental science, with the former focusing on real-time cognitive processing (states) and the latter on extended learning (stages). Still, the theoretical links between the two remain tenuous (later we discuss states vs. stages). Also, both contemplative neuroscience and ego development articulate deconstructive processes as important to advanced states or stages, but again, theoretical links between the two are sparse. Our model is a preliminary attempt to bridge these gaps and support an integration across fields.

Because meditation often involves quieting the mind in order to disclose subtler features of experience, contemplative science has frequently drawn on stratified models of consciousness, cognition, and brain function. In these models, relatively primitive or foundational processes are progressively elaborated into more complex, abstract, and self-reflexive forms of cognition (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2025; Friston, 2010; Laukkonen & Slagter, 2021; Brown, 2002). This layered organization can be understood across at least five partially analogous strata: phylogenetically, in the biological evolution of brain functions from sensorimotor and affective capacities toward symbolic, linguistic, and reflective thought (Donald, 1993; Chin, 2022); developmentally, in the emergence of increasingly complex cognitive structures from childhood through adulthood (Piaget, 1971; Fischer, 2008; Commons, 2008); neurobiologically, in hierarchically organized cortical and subcortical systems that coordinate bottom-up sensory input with top-down predictive control (Panksepp, 2004; Bastos et al., 2012); microgenetically, in the moment-to-moment formation of thought as implicit, affective, and preconceptual processes become articulated as conscious images, meanings, and propositions (Brown, 2002; Thompson, 2014); and phenomenologically, in the meditative investigation of progressively subtler layers of bodily sensation, affect, perception, self-modeling, and awareness itself (Varela et al., 1993; Lutz et al., 2006).

Across these pathways, higher-order structures may be said to build "upward" from more primordial forms of organization, though this should not be understood as a simple linear hierarchy: cognitive and neural systems are recursive, dynamic, and bidirectional, with higherorder processes continuously shaping, constraining, and reinterpreting the lower-order processes from which they emerge. (Most are more complicated than simple linear/hierarchical processes and include top-down and bottom-up feedback loops.) Contemporary predictive-processing and active inference models (Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013; Laukkonen et al., 2024) propose that patterns stored in higher-level structures bias lowerlevel functions to perceive, interpret, or behave according to learned habits or beliefs. Successively higher-level structures model and predict slices of the world at larger scales of time, space, abstraction, context, and counterfactuality. Learning updates the abstracted model to the extent that the perceived world differs from the expected one. Parallel processes are referenced in developmental psychology as assimilation vs. accommodation (Piaget, 1971) and subject-to-object maturation (Kegan, 1994).

Discussion and Future Directions

Scientific studies of contemplative practices are grounded in, or perhaps haunted by, notions of meditative endpoints including awakening and enlightenment born in spiritual and wisdom traditions. This context implicates wisdom, virtue, and ego development within meditative development. As contemplative science enters a third wave of meditation research (Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026) and expands beyond stress reduction, emotional regulation, trauma recovery, and clinical treatment, it would benefit from incorporating measures from developmental psychology, including wisdom skills and cognitive complexity.

In this next stage of the field's development, we want to avoid the vagueness or confusion around the distinction between states and stages that have existed in wisdom traditions and developmental discourses. We also argue that bridging between contemplative science and adult developmental theory is complicated and muddied until growth and release processes are better differentiated and clarified in both fields.

This paper offers a high-level interdisciplinary conceptual framework for integrating developmental science with contemplative science in a way that also supports underpinnings and gestures related to spiritual and transpersonal studies. Much needs to be done to produce a usable model allowing empirical hypothesis testing and practical application. Because so little research exists at the intersection of development and contemplative science, perhaps the most important first step is convening gatherings where researchers in both fields can engage in rich discourse to coordinate language regimes and imagine synergies.

In the paper, first we suggested that the construct of wisdom development, an umbrella term that overlaps with ego development, meaning-making maturity, and some aspects of spiritual attainment, aligns well with the larger motivations and historical roots of contemplative science. We also suggested that for contemplative studies to have greater practical implications, more attention be put on stages (and vantage points) vs. states.

Then we proposed that developmental measures benefit contemplative science as it expands its reach from states to stages, from novice to more experienced mediators, and from basic to advanced contemplative practices. The research implications here are simply to use existing developmental assessments in longitudinal studies, pre-post intervention tests, or stratified analyses by experience or skill level. The basic research questions involve when, where, and how development (of meaning-making, wisdom, hierarchical complexity, etc.) progresses vs. other factors.

The bulk of the paper outlines an ascending/descending framework. For this preliminary work, there are two complementary paths for moving forward, theoretical/conceptual and empirical.

Research Directions

Theoretical/conceptual research. This involves interdisciplinary literature research to expose and coordinate findings, with the aim of supporting insight, clarity, and cross-domain integration. At the very least, we hypothesize that this framework will help create conceptual clarity for meditation theorists and researchers by suggesting that cognitive or neurobiological change be examined from the perspectives of both structural growth and release. We claim that those interested in either cognitive development or contemplative attainments should not conflate increased complexity capacity with increased spiritual clarity by assuming they necessarily track together, and we suggest ways that the two modalities could interact dynamically.

Centrally, the Wisdom Skills framework proposes that structural-release phenomena may be mapped homologously onto developmental sequences, particularly early ones. This shifts the interpretation of meditative processes and outcomes away from a single question of "how high" they are-whether in terms of altitude, transcendence, or presumed developmental superiorityand toward the question of what depth of primordial structure they release, disclose, or make newly accessible. In this way, the framework offers a two-axis model for analyzing contemplative effects and outcomes, distinguishing complexity capacity from release depth, rather than collapsing both into a single measure of developmental altitude.

A conceptual/theoretical research plan would include: A. Research into the early childhood cognitive and sensory development literature to summarize findings on the relative ordering and micro-developmental sequences and key junctures of foundational capacities (including splits and mergers) whose release is implicated in advanced meditation practices, including: spatial reasoning, temporal reasoning, differentiation of imagination/memory from sensory input, somatic self-map (proprioception, interoception, body-schema, etc.), construction of the ego and self-sense, object permanence, synesthesia, differentiation of sensory modalities, and capacities in open and focused attention. B. Research into the contemplative practice, and principally advanced meditation, literature to summarize non-ordinary states referencing the capacities mentioned above, and identifying models suggesting sequences or priorities of states, stages, insights, or practices; or alternative definitions of meditative endpoints. C. Making preliminary attempts to coordinate the findings from (B) with the developmental sequences from (A). We predict that linking child/infant developmental science to contemplative science in this way will clarify some controversies and yield insights for questions involving practice sequences, the definitions of contemplative states, and divergent claims across traditions.

Empirical Research.

A central aim of our framework is to differentiate the growth of cognitive structures from their release. To make this distinction empirically tractable, both processes must be operationally defined and linked to appropriate measures. These measures fall into the broad categories of (1) ascending growth and (2) descending release for (A) psychological measures and (B) neurobiological measures, the former including construct assessments, behavioral indicators, and phenomenological reports. Within each category, multiple candidate measures are possible. Future research will need to determine which of these most closely correspond to structural release, whether different forms of release require distinct measurement strategies, and whether structural release can be modeled as a single latent variable vs. a family of processes. The research agenda would begin with these steps: 1A. Identify psychometrically validated measures of structural or hierarchical complexity, ego development, and/or wisdom. These may include: WU Sentence Completion Test (Loevinger, 1976); Subject Object Interview (Kegan, 1994); Hierarchical Complexity (Commons, 2008); Metacognition (Fleming & Lau, 2014); Dialectical reasoning (Basseches, 1984), Wisdom (Kramer, 2000; Ardelt, 2003; Grossmann, et al., 2020), Mindfulness (Brown & Ryan, 2003; Baer et al., 2006), Cognitive Flexibility (Martin & Rubin, 1995), 1B. Identify neurobiological mechanisms and measures (or correlates) of structural and/or hierarchical complexity. These may include measuring increases in cortical thickness, parietal and prefrontal integration, network controllability, structural connectivity, metacognitive network activation.

2A. Identify psychological measures of structural release, occlusion, and reorganization. Release-related phenomena may be more difficult to measure than complexity capacity, in part because release can occur at any strata, and there is no necessary order of release. Release can be measured by either the disappearance of an occlusion or the (re)emergence of a developmentally primitive capacity. It is most likely that for any given study, the contemplative method will suggest release aimed at a set of strata, for which assessments can then be identified.

There are many validated constructs and assessments related to our concept of structural release, including: Nonattachment and Openness to Experience (Sahdra et al, 2010; Costa & McCrae, 1992), Decentering/Defusion (Fresco et al., 2007; Bernstein et al., 2015), Psychological, Cognitive, Emotional, and Identity Flexibility (Hayes et al., 2006; Dennis & Vander Wal, 2010); Self-transcendence and Ego Dissolution (Reed, 2008; Yaden et al., 2016; Nour et al., 2016); Schema Release (Young & Brown, 1998); Self-protection (Andrews et al., 1993); Post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996); Category/Label Deconstruction (Gillanders et al., 2014); Body Schema/Boundary Dissolution (Dambrun, 2016).

2B. Identify neuro-physiological mechanisms and biomarkers of structural release. First, the possible mechanisms of release must be identified. These include the disattenuation or inhibition, pruning or decay, deconditioning or demyelination, bypassing (routing around) of synaptic connections or pathways. Candidates include neural flexibility/entropy (reduced rigidity), reduced/down-regulated DMN hub activity, greater error integration, increased global connectivity, Amygdala down-regulation, cortical thinning, and synaptic pruning.

Given one or more measures of structural growth and release, we can design studies to address these questions: • What are the best psychological measures of structural growth in hierarchical complexity?

• What are neural markers or correlates of structural growth in hierarchical complexity?

• What are the best psychological measures of structural occlusion and release?

• Can structural release be operationalized as a latent construct distinct from related psychological constructs?

• What are neural markers or correlates of structural occlusion and release?

• What are the mechanistic pathways (e.g., neural networks, interoceptive systems, dynamical brain states) through which release occurs? Does release involve elimination, inhibition, and/or rerouting?

• Do measures of occlusion and release need to be distinct for different developmental stages (or processing strata)?

• Are release and growth quasi-independent, i.e. do they sometimes not track together?

• Are releases (or stable access) at specific levels necessary and/or sufficient for developmental transition into each specific level?

• Does specific pattern release mediate the relationship between contemplative practice and wisdom development?

• Do the latest stages of ego/wisdom development include release at the deepest levels, and conversely, do individuals who show higher structural complexity but low pattern release score lower on wisdom than those high on both axes?

• How do various contemplative practices differ in their support of structural growth and structural release?

• Can measurements of structural growth and/or release be used to improve contemplative training programs?

• Can individual measurements of structural complexity or release be used to determine when a meditative practice or intervention risks negative outcomes; or be used to adapt the intensity or rate of such practices to moderate risk?

• Is there a skill or set of skills corresponding to the ability to intentionally and strategically release problematic mental structures and habits?

Closing Remarks

Some cosmological narratives describe reality in terms of a dance between creation and destruction-birth and death, decomposition and regeneration (Capra, 1975; Narayanan, 2003; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025). Growth, evolution, and transformation processes include hierarchical or emergent growth along with obsolescence, decay, and extinction. We apply this philosophical trope to the interdisciplinary intersection of contemplative science, adult developmental psychology, transpersonal theory, and trauma theory by articulating a model of wisdom maturity that gives equal importance to ascending growth and descending release. Doing so raises questions about the relationship between building complexity capacity versus the "negative capability" (Keats, 2002) of unlearning or tolerating unknowing and uncertainty.

It has become a platitude that we live in a "VUCA" world of Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity, and Ambiguity-that requires society to increase the complexity of our thinking and collaborative problem-solving (Bennett & Lemoine, 2014; Dawson, 2018; Mack et al., 2015). But this is clearly a partial picture. Complex thinkers can be malicious or oblivious to the needs of others. Complex laws, policies, and bureaucracies can be inefficient, oppressive, and exploitative. Complexity can lead to more complexity in a virtue-less cycle (Roy, 2016; Stacey, 2002; Graeber, 2015; Klein & Thompson, 2025; Murray, 2021). An argument could be made that maladaptive beliefs and habits cause most human suffering and that the task of repairing or releasing individual and collective shadow may be as important as increasing the hierarchical complexity of society.

We believe our conceptual framework can help clarify some conundrums encountered in trying to marry contemplative science, wisdom traditions, and adult developmental theories and contribute to the emerging third wave of meditation research. However, it is rife with hypotheses and speculations that require further philosophical consideration and empirical testing, and this essay is a meaningful first step in a longer deliberation that we invite the reader to join.
