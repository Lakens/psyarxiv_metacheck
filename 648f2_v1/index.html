Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

Abstract

In recent years, advances in natural language processing have led researchers to increasingly use text-based data to study various psychological and social phenomena. This usually goes without an explicit measurement model or evaluation of the psychometric properties of text-based indicators. We introduce SEM-WE, which fits measurement and structural models to correlation matrices that are based on word embeddings derived from texts under study. Each construct is represented by several indicator words or phrases. The centered cosine matrix provides the input for estimating loadings, residuals, model fit statistics, reliability indicators, and parameters describing relations between constructs. We use a synthetic corpus, a survey study that combines both textual and Likert-type questions, and real-life posts from X in a multi-study validation exercise. Using simulated persona attitudes, self-ratings, and human coding as benchmarks, we compare six approaches: dictionary stance counts, embedding centroids based on positive indicators alone, embedding centroids contrasting positive and negative indicators, annotations by a local language model, annotations by a frontier language model, and SEM-WE. SEM-WE reproduces benchmark signs in these examples and their ordering at least as well as other methods, matching or outperforming most competitors under the reported configurations. Dictionary and centroid results vary more across reported configurations, while large language models perform well in the synthetic and survey studies; however, at much higher computational cost.

Structural Equation Modeling with Word Embeddings for Text-Based Measurement

Psychological measurement usually represents a construct with several indicators and evaluates their quality before interpreting relations among constructs (Bollen, 1989; Kline, 2023).

Text research often takes a different route. Researchers score open-ended responses, social-media posts, or clinical narratives and analyze those scores without explicitly testing a measurement model or accounting for measurement error (Grimmer & Stewart, 2013; Jacobs & Wallach, 2021).

Methods such as Latent Semantic Scaling relate documents to selected anchors (Watanabe, 2021), but the resulting scores alone do not establish a measurement model. Relations among scores can therefore reflect properties of the scoring procedure as well as the constructs of interest.

Structural Equation Modeling with Word-Embedding Geometry (SEM-WE) extends

Confirmatory Factor Analysis with Word Embeddings (CFA-WE; Pokropek, 2026). Several indicator words or phrases represent each construct. We compute their centered cosine similarities and fit a measurement and structural model to this matrix. The model estimates loadings, residuals, and relations among latent constructs without assigning scores to documents.

Multiple indicators let researchers inspect dimensionality, model fit, and local item misfit.

They do not remove the effects of corpus composition or poor vocabulary coverage; however, they provide a well-established apparatus for testing measurement assumptions and quantifying relations between investigated constructs. SEM-WE changes the unit of analysis. Documents supply the contexts used to learn embeddings, but the variables in the fitted matrix are indicator words. The target of modeling is to recover relationships between words used across the corpus. It does not automatically translate into correlations at the level of respondents or authors. The empirical benchmarks below assess correspondence between these levels, rather than their equivalence.

In the paper, we first define the model and its assumptions. We then test it using different data. Study 1 examines a constructed corpus with simulated data -known persona attitudes. Study 2 examines variation across corpus samples and embedding runs. Studies 3 and 4 compare text estimates with survey self-ratings and human coding. The comparisons include dictionary scores, embedding centroids, language-model annotations, and a supervised classifier.

SEM-WE Workflow

General Overview

SEM-WE uses a corpus to learn word embeddings, indicator words (or phrases) to represent constructs, and a structural model to describe relations among them (Figure 1). A corpus is the collection of texts used for training. A document is one response, post, page in a book, article in a newspaper, student essay, or other text unit.

Indicators should be derived from theory and/or previous studies. In the simplest case, a single word may be sufficient, given that it appears in the studied corpus often enough. For example, vaccination could indicate discussion of vaccines. In other cases, one word is not precise enough, and a short phrase may be preferable: climate change, for instance, is more specific than climate. Whole sentences may also serve as indicators if the embedding method can represent them. In the examples below, the indicators are single words and short phrases for simplicity.

Multi-word indicators are joined before training word2vec, so that each indicator has one embedding vector. These vectors are then centered and normalized to compute their similarity matrix S. The matrix is passed to SEM software, here lavaan, together with a specification of the measurement model. Loadings, residuals, and fit indices allow assessment of the measurement model before interpreting the structural paths. Once the measurement model has been evaluated, a structural model can be specified to represent relations among constructs. Figure 1 presents the overall SEM-WE framework.

Detailed Workflow

Figure 1 describes the overall workflow; here we describe the specific decisions used to implement the workflow for the analysis presented in this paper.

Indicator screening. We begin with candidate words or phrases based on the construct literature and check their use in the study corpus. We join multi-word phrases into single tokens before training and remove candidates with little coverage: an indicator must appear in at least 10 documents and be present in the trained vocabulary. Text is lowercased; URLs and user mentions are removed; hashtags are retained as words; and nonalphabetic characters, stopwords, and onecharacter tokens are removed. These decisions depend on the phenomena under study. For Polish texts, inflected forms are included explicitly because indicators are matched to the vocabulary by exact, case-folded strings.

We train word2vec on the study corpus so that words used in similar local contexts receive similar vectors. The main choices are the embedding dimension, contextwindow size, minimum word frequency, and number of training iterations. In the analyses presented in this paper, we use skip-gram word2vec with 100 dimensions, a context window of 5, a minimum token count of 1, 20 iterations, and a single thread.

Estimation and Inference

Model specification is largely the same as in conventional SEM, but estimation and inference require special care. The process that generates word embeddings does not satisfy the sampling assumptions of normal-theory maximum likelihood. The dimensions of an embedding are not independent observations but coordinates of a deterministic word2vec solution in which the dimensions are jointly learned and hence cross-dependent. A particular dimension lacks a stable substantive meaning comparable to that of a measured variable in a survey. The system is rotation-arbitrary, i.e. the embedding space has no privileged axes: the entire configuration of word vectors can be rotated without changing ordinary cosine similarities, exactly as a factor solution can be rotated without changing its fit; however, the within-vector centering used here can make the resulting similarities rotation-dependent. This is why individual coordinates cannot be treated as measured variables with their own sampling distributions.

We therefore use ML as a minimum-discrepancy estimator (Browne, 1984), not as a fully specified likelihood. Moreover, analytic standard errors and fit indices such as CFI, TLI, and RMSEA require a sample size, whereas S is not computed from independent observations and has no obvious N. Its uncertainty also includes variation from embedding training. In practical terms, ML here selects the parameters whose model-implied matrix is closest to S under the usual ML discrepancy function; it does not imply that the embedding coordinates are normally distributed observations. Inference must therefore rely on resampling the documents and retraining the embeddings. Ideally, a nonparametric bootstrap should be used for this purpose (see Appendix C.1). Also, we propose a procedure for estimating the effective number of observations that can be plugged into SEM estimation based on S to obtain model fit indices. We study properties of this procedure in Study 2b.

The Present Studies

Although we use different data sources, all studies used to exemplify and validate the proposed approach share the same three constructs: Trust in Vaccines, Environmental Concerns, and Acceptance of Immigration. The studies also share the same theoretical structure of relations among these constructs. Trust in Vaccines is regressed on Environmental Concerns and Acceptance of Immigration, with the predictors freely correlated. The paper does not focus on testing specific theories or hypotheses, as it serves methodological purposes. However, the examples presented here have theoretical backgrounds that drive the analysis. Cultural cognition theory proposes that social values influence how people evaluate risks and the credibility of experts (Kahan et al., 2010). The social dominance-environmentalism perspective states that beliefs about hierarchy and inequality apply to two domains: relations between social groups and relations between humans and nature (Graça, 2021). Our model represents these two domains through Acceptance of Immigration (relations between social groups) and Environmental Concerns (relations between humans and nature). Environmental concerns increase attention to preventing harm. This can increase the perceived collective benefits of vaccination and strengthen vaccine trust. Acceptance of immigration reflects openness toward other groups. We propose that this openness reduces rejection of health recommendations seen as part of a cosmopolitan agenda. We therefore expect both predictors to increase Trust in Vaccines.

Previous studies support parts of this account. Bilewicz and Soral (2022) found greater vaccine hesitancy among people who preferred group hierarchy and higher levels of inequality.

This preference can also underlie opposition to immigration and environmental protection (Graça, 2021). Stoeckel et al. (2022) linked hesitancy to cultural closure and anti-elite views. Mosconi et al. (2025) found lower mistrust of vaccine benefits among people with a combination of environmental and altruistic values and connectedness to nature.

Data

We use three text corpora and one survey. The first corpus is synthetic. We generated 8,400 fictional social-media posts on climate, immigration, and vaccines with Claude-Haiku-4-5 (Anthropic). The second corpus includes responses to open-ended questions included in a CAWI survey. Respondents wrote short texts describing their thoughts and emotions about climate change, immigration, and vaccines. They also gave a 1-to-5 self-rating of their readiness to change their lifestyle to combat climate change, openness to immigration, and trust in vaccines (N = 592, yielding 1,776 open responses; see Appendix E for more details). The third corpus includes real X posts on climate, immigration, and vaccines collected weekly between September 2024 and June 2026. For this last corpus, a human gold standard from 18 trained coders provides construct-level stance labels. All data are described in detail in the following sections.

Indicators

Every construct is represented by two lists of indicator words, a pro-list and an anti-list.

The first contains words and phrases that express support for the construct, and the second contains words and phrases that express opposition to it. Contrasting the pro-list with the anti-list helps separate stance from mere topic prevalence. All six models share the same lists of pro-and antiindicators, built by the selection procedure described below. Keeping indicator words and phrases constant across methods helps ensure that differences in the results come from the measurement architecture, not from the vocabulary.

The Six Models for Comparisons M1: Dictionary Stance Count.

Each document is scored as the percentage of pro-list indicators that appear in it minus the percentage of anti-list indicators. This baseline follows established dictionary approaches (Brady et al., 2017; Pennebaker et al., 2015; Stone et al., 1966).

M2: Pro-Only Embedding Centroid.

The word vectors in a document are averaged and then the cosine similarity of the resulting vector to the mean vector of the pro-construct indicators is calculated (Garten et al., 2018). This can be used without an anti-vocabulary, but topic relevance may be mistaken for a positive stance.

M3: Embedding Centroid with Stance Contrast. This is the M2 score minus the cosine similarity of the document average to the mean vector of the anti-construct indicators (Garten et al., 2018; Watanabe, 2021). It requires adequate vocabulary for both poles.

M4: Local LLM Annotation.

A local model, gemma4:26b, rates each text on an anchored 1-to-5 scale at temperature 0. It can return absent or uncertain instead of a score (Halterman & Keith, 2026; Törnberg, 2024).

M5: Frontier LLM Annotation.

Claude Opus (claude-opus-4-8) rates the same synthetic and survey texts with the same prompts. This provides a second language-model comparison (Gilardi et al., 2023; Rathje et al., 2024; Törnberg, 2025).

M6: SEM-WE.

We pass S computed for the selected indicators to the lavaan R package and fit the SEM by ML discrepancy minimization. For Studies 1 and 3, confidence intervals for path coefficients are constructed using a document bootstrap that retrains the embeddings (see replication package). Study 4 reports point estimates with confidence intervals projected from the Study 2b retraining grid rescaled to the X corpus size. The structural specification is the same across studies (Figure 2); indicator sets vary by corpus (see replication package).

Study 4 replaces M5 with a supervised Polish RoBERTa classifier trained on the available human annotations (see replication package). M4 and M5 gave similar results in the first two comparisons.

Study 2b Matching Analytic and Empirical Standard Errors

In classical SEM applications, the sample size N enters the standard error formula directly.

The analytic standard error of a parameter is proportional to one over the square root of N.

However, there is no such natural N in SEM-WE. The input is a cosine matrix S built from word embeddings, not a covariance matrix from independent observations. There are several ways of defining N: the number of documents in the corpus, the embedding dimension, vocabulary size, or something in between. In this study, we ask what N would make the analytic standard error that SEM software reports match the replication-based one, and whether that count has any simple interpretation.

Study 3 Polish Survey Responses and Self-Ratings

Study 3 compares SEM-WE estimates from open-ended responses with self-ratings from the same respondents.

Method

Corpus and Likert questions. The data come from a Polish CAWI survey run in 2025 by Instytut Badań Pollster. The survey had several versions. We used the version in which each respondent wrote a short open-ended response on three topics: climate, immigration, and vaccines.

We keep respondents with complete 1-to-5 ratings on all three target constructs and at least ten characters of open text on each topic. These two criteria resulted in N = 592 respondents.

Each respondent produced three open-ended responses, one per topic, so there are 1,776 documents in total.

M1, M2, and M3 score each document and fit the structural model to the document-level scores. M4 and M5 produce a 1-to-5 rating on each document; these are also averaged within respondent. Study 4 Polish X Posts and Human Coding

Study 4 uses 354,310 real posts from the X platform and an annotation dataset from human coders that allows us to compare methods in real-life situations, typical for NLP analysis.

Method

Corpus. Social media posts were collected weekly on Polish X (formerly Twitter) from September 5, 2024, to June 15, 2026. The collection used the X API. The selection frame was Polish-language, non-retweet posts matching at least one of seven thematic keyword streams: climate, migration, vaccination, health, trust, political agency, and scientists. The final corpus has 354,310 unique Polish posts from about 98,300 authors.

Annotators and quality control. Twenty trained coders read each assigned post and judged what the author expressed. For each topic, the coder first marked whether the post was about that topic.

If yes, the coder then chose one of four options describing the author's position: the author supports or trusts, the author opposes or distrusts, other people hold the stance (the author reports what others think without clearly endorsing it), or the stance is unclear or does not apply. The coders therefore did not report their own views on climate, immigration, or vaccines; they reported what the author of each post appeared to say about these topics. Each coder annotated 5,000 posts plus 250 overlap posts. After a quality review (fast-click rate, over-use of "other", and κ with overlap partners), we excluded two coders. The full human-coded dataset contains 16,432 directional postconstruct labels from 15,901 posts and 9,912 authors. The double-coded dataset consists of 4,081 posts. On this double-coded dataset, topic-presence κ ranges from .71 to .91 across the three modeled constructs, and stance agreement conditional on topic presence ranges from .88 to .98.

Measurement models.

We used a similar set of models as in previous sections. M1 (dictionary stance count), M2 (pro-only embedding centroid), and M3 (pro-anti centroid contrast) score each post, and the per-author score enters the SEM. M4 is a local LLM annotator (gemma4:26b) with an anchored 1-to-5 scale on each construct (similar to previous studies in this paper). Because the human instrument records three directions (support, opposition, or no clear stance), we collapsed the LLM score to the same three categories. We did not re-run the frontier LLM (M5) on this corpus; instead, we used a supervised Polish RoBERTa classifier trained on the 100,000-post annotation pool. The classifier is applied only to posts outside its training split. M6 (SEM-WE) is fit to the cosine matrix built from the indicators selected on this corpus. Additionally, we added a human benchmark (M0) model. The presented method enables social scientists to make better use of the fruits of the Natural Language Processing domain. As word embeddings have already been proven useful, for example for capturing cultural biases in a language community as a whole (Durrheim et al., 2023), researchers came to the conclusion that "statistical contexts of words capture much of what we mean by meaning" (Caliskan et al., 2017, p. 185). At the same time, social scientists need an apparatus to evaluate constructs from textual data beyond classical NLP evaluation metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1. Many research questions concern relations between constructs, making construct validity important alongside predictive performance (Fang et al., 2022). Extracting these properties is central to psychological and sociological theories, and SEM-WE offers a methodological advance suited to this goal.

General Discussion

SEM

Methods that draw on vector representations depend on multiple methodological choices, including corpus composition, model training procedures, and data aggregation. To limit the degrees of scientific freedom it is necessary to anchor new methods in the classical measurement tradition, which SEM-WE does by relying on the traditional structural equation modeling tradition. This also distinguishes SEM-WE from other methods by putting greater emphasis on the theoretical assumptions, instantiated in the iterative process of refining construct representations, inspired by the previous CFA-WE work (Pokropek, 2026), a direction explicitly recommended by the measurement literature (Bender & Koller, 2020; Bringmann & Eronen, 2016) By combining theory-driven measurement models with corpus-based analysis, the presented method offers a framework for investigating not only what patterns can be extracted from text, but also whether and how these patterns support theoretically informed claims about the constructs being measured. Finally, in comparison to the classical questionnaire approaches SEM-WE offers a greater degree of ecological validity by relying on peoples' unprompted utterances to extract the relation between the underlying constructs they discuss by relying on word embeddings to represent their meaning (Harris, 1954; Landauer & Dumais, 1997; Levy & Goldberg, 2014).

In contrast to questionnaire approaches, it is not clear to what extent SEM-WE distinguishes relations at the individual level from those at the level of the entire discourse.

Given that it relies on a pooled embedding model, the present approach does not distinguish between within-subject and between-subject relations. Although the correspondence with persona-and author-level benchmarks suggests that SEM-WE captures associations relevant to individual differences, these results do not establish how clearly such associations can be separated from shared discourse patterns. It is then plausible that shared rhetoric can connect words more strongly than individual attitudes are related. The corpus reflects who writes, what they discuss, and how often they contribute. Moreover, associations between words may reflect individual attitudes, shared cultural meanings, or a combination of both (Caliskan et al., 2017; Garg et al., 2018; Kozlowski et al., 2018).

Interestingly, in Study 3 we included two versions of the training data: a version that treated each response as a separate document, and a concatenated one that combined each respondent's three open-ended responses into one document. R² is higher for the concatenated version (.27 versus .18), showing that document construction can affect the estimated structural relations. This raises another important concern: disjoint communities or topics may provide little cross-construct information. Consider, for example, a situation in which words used to describe attitudes toward vaccination never co-occur with words used to describe attitudes toward immigration. This might result in no estimated relationship between stances mainly because our model cannot learn it, not because it doesn't exist. Combining multiple statements on different topics produced by the same individual, as we did in Study 3, may help address this limitation by introducing additional connections across constructs. Future studies should therefore investigate the outcomes of SEM-WE across different levels of semantic overlap observed in the data. Additional efforts should also be made to assess whether relationships in data sets dominated by only one attitude (consider a data set in which most statements describe anti-immigration attitudes and only a few are pro-immigration) can be properly recovered.

The use of word embeddings in SEM-WE may also propagate one of their inherent limitations: handling antonyms, or reverse relationships (Nguyen et al., 2016). Nevertheless, our results do not clearly show this failure mode: the pro-and anti-indicator pools were separated in Study 1, and SEM-WE reproduced the benchmark path signs across studies. A possible explanation is that estimating constructs from multiple indicators reduces dependence on individual word pairs, potentially limiting the influence of particular antonyms with similar embeddings. Although these findings are encouraging, this explanation remains tentative, and recovering negative structural relationships would benefit from further investigation.

From a measurement perspective, Study 2 shows that document count, embedding dimension, and vocabulary size do not provide a consistent sample-size basis for quantifying uncertainty in structural model parameters. Resampling that includes retraining of the embeddings should therefore be used to assess standard errors and confidence intervals.

Nevertheless, our results suggest that estimates become more stable in larger corpora, although the number of resamplings needed for reliable uncertainty estimates remains to be established.

Further, the "effective number of observations" estimated using the resampling procedure may provide a starting point for calibrating sample-size-dependent model fit indices, subject to further validation. However, given the instability observed in the smallest corpora, the resampling should be repeated where possible to determine corpus-specific uncertainty.

Several limitations are also embedded in the procedures adopted in our four studies. The synthetic corpus was developed iteratively to improve indicator coverage and approximate the survey benchmark, limiting its role as an independent validation sample. Indicator selection and evaluation use the same corpus. The within-and between-subject effects are not directly parseable, and the resulting effects might represent aggregated effects. Furthermore, the X study reports SEM-WE intervals projected from the Study 2b retraining grid rather than from a Twitter-specific retraining bootstrap and uses an annotation set filtered for disagreement, which might limit how well the intervals reflect uncertainty in the X corpus and how well the human benchmark represents posts on which annotators disagree. Row-centered similarities require rotation checks to assess whether the results depend on the arbitrary orientation of the embedding coordinates. Future research should test whether the proposed effective sample-size formulas generalize to other corpora and whether the resulting confidence intervals enclose the true parameter values at the intended rate. The presented studies also cover only one word2vec training regime, two languages, and one three-construct structural model specification.

Future research would benefit from testing how topic segregation, stance balance, and author dependence affect the results while holding the target relation fixed, although controlling these features independently is difficult in non-synthetic datasets. Comparisons on shared authors and documents could clarify when differences arise from scoring, aggregation, or the measurement model. Independent indicator selection and repeated training would help assess stability of the results. Contextual embeddings are a possible extension, although training them from scratch on each study corpus is generally less feasible than training word2vec. They also represent words in particular contexts rather than assigning each indicator a single vector, so their application would require decisions about how to operationalize construct indicators.

Whether this preserves the intended measurement interpretation remains to be established.

Nevertheless, we argue that SEM-WE is a promising approach and that it is possible to advance our understanding of certain issues even if interpreting the outcomes of our measurement still requires additional effort (Bringmann & Eronen, 2016).

Iterative Confirmatory Pruning

For each construct-by-polarity pool that survives the screen with at least three members, a one-factor model is fit to the pool's centred cosine matrix by maximum likelihood (latent variance fixed to 1). If fit does not reach the declared fit threshold, the indicator with the lowest standardized loading is removed and the model refit, iterating until the threshold is met or fewer than three indicators remain. Two thresholds are carried forward throughout the article: satisfactory (CFI ≥ .90, RMSEA ≤ .18, SRMR ≤ .08) and very good (CFI ≥ .95, RMSEA ≤ .16, SRMR ≤ .06). The CFI cutpoints follow Hu and Bentler (1999); the relaxed RMSEA and SRMR floors reflect the higher residual level inherent to cosine-similarity CFA relative to survey CFA (Pokropek, 2026).

While computing the values of fit indices the effective number of observations was set to 2,400 (see Appendix C). Although the pruning is driven by CFI, the index ultimately trusted for the reported models is SRMR, which does not depend on any supplied sample size.

Automatic Residual Covariances

After pruning, each final pool is refit with up to two automatically detected residual covariances. A pair of indicators qualifies if two of their component words share a morphological prefix of at least six characters (e.g., vaccinated / fully vaccinated) or if their raw token cosine exceeds .30; morphological pairs take precedence, and ties are broken by cosine.

F.2 The Coding Instrument

Human coders applied a fixed instrument to Polish tweets. Each tweet is first labeled for which of seven topics it touches -health, trust in strangers, climate, political agency, scientists, immigration, vaccines; multiple labels allowed -and for each detected topic the coder records one of four stance options: the author supports or trusts; the author opposes or distrusts; other people hold the stance; not applicable or hard to say. Irony and sarcasm are coded by the author's intent, not the literal text. For the structural model the mapping is climate → EC, immigration → AI, vaccines → TV; the remaining topics are not modeled because their inter-annotator agreement is materially lower (e.g., agency κ ≈ .25, scientists κ ≈ .42 in the full pool).

F.3 Annotator Exclusions

Twenty coders annotated the corpus; two were excluded after a quality review. Annotator 12 showed inattentive click-through: 26% of annotations under the 3.55-s fast-response threshold, 84.5% "other"-only labels against 67% globally, and Cohen's κ = .19 against overlap partners.

Annotator 18 showed systematic miscalibration: category rates diverging from peers (scientists flagged at 1.5 times and climate at 0.5 times the peer rate) with κ = .23. Annotator 20 was flagged but retained (borderline, κ = .43). Removing annotators 12 and 18 reduces the double-coded overlap from 5,000 to 4,081 tweet pairs.

F.4 Stance Collapse, Overlap Merge, and Author Linking

Per detected topic, the four-way stance is collapsed to a signed own-stance: support or trust = +1; oppose or distrust = -1; "other people hold the stance" is excluded from own-stance; not applicable = missing. Compound annotations resolve to the consistent sign, or missing if conflicting. For double-coded tweets, agreements are kept and disagreements dropped (423 of 32,252 tweet-construct cells, 1.3%). Each annotated tweet is matched to its corpus author by exact text, with a 100% link rate. The final gold standard comprises 16,432 directional tweet-construct stances over 15,901 tweets from 9,912 authors.

Appendix B The Indicator-Selection Procedure

All six models in the comparison ladder draw their indicators from pools built by one fixed procedure which combines a literature-derived starting pool with a statistical screen on the corpus and an iterative confirmatory pruning step.

Candidate Pools

Each construct starts from at least 30 candidate phrases per polarity, taken from the construct's measurement literature (for the synthetic corpus: 30 PRO and 30 ANTI candidates for trust in vaccines, 30 and 31 for pro-environmental concern, and 30 and 31 for acceptance of migration; for the Polish corpora: 33/29, 34/29, and 31/29). For Polish, a heavily inflected language, the pools enumerate the relevant surface forms by hand (e.g., imigrant / imigranci), because indicators are matched to the vocabulary by exact, case-folded surface-form equality.

Preprocessing and the Corpus Screen

Text is lowercased; URLs and @-mentions are stripped; hashtags are reduced to their bare word; only alphabetic characters are kept; snowball stopwords and one-character tokens are removed. Multi-word candidate phrases are then joined into single tokens with the joiner character X before word2vec training, so that each phrase receives a unique embedding. (No selected indicator phrase contains the joiner sequence as a substring of an ordinary word, so the join-thensplit step is lossless.) A candidate survives the corpus screen if it appears in at least 10 documents and its token is present in the trained vocabulary.
