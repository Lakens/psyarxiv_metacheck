Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. 2. Introduction

An out-of-body experience (OBE) is a distinctive alteration of self-consciousness in which a person experiences a felt separation between the self and the physical body, often accompanied by a shift in visuo-spatial perspective, wherein one appears to observe one's own body from an external vantage point (Blanke & Arzy, 2005). Although historically associated with spontaneous, spiritual, or clinical contexts, OBEs have since been studied as a tractable, experimentally inducible phenomenon: for example, illusions of bodily self-location can be reliably manipulated in laboratory settings using multisensory conflict, revealing that the felt sense of "being" in one's body, and of occupying a bounded, located self, is not fixed but can be temporarily reconfigured (Blanke, 2012; Ehrsson, 2007; Lenggenhager et al., 2007). This malleability of bodily self-consciousness is theoretically significant because the sense of being a spatially and temporally bounded self is thought to underlie many of our most basic existential assumptions, including the assumption that the self ends when the body does (Blanke & Metzinger, 2009). OBEs are amongst the most commonly reported features of neardeath experiences (NDEs), which are spontaneous altered states occurring in proximity to death that have long been associated with lasting (and mostly positive) shifts in how people relate to their own mortality, including reduced fear of death (van Lommel et al., 2001). This raises the possibility that the OBE is not merely a striking perceptual curiosity, but a phenomenologically rich state whose specific first-person qualities may be mechanistically significant for how people relate to death. A more granular understanding of what is actually experienced during an OBE -at the level of bodily sensation, spatial perception, and shifting boundaries of self -will not only enhance our understanding of the phenomenon itself, but also provide insight into its potential therapeutic relevance.

In many cases, OBEs are an expression of a broader class of self-transcendent experiences (STEs), which are transient mental states characterized by: (1) a decreased salience of the self, and (2) an increased sense of connectedness with others and with something larger than oneself (Yaden et al., 2017). These two components, sometimes referred to as "annihilational" and "relational", may occur together or independently: the boundaries of the self may attenuate or dissolve, while the felt connection with other people, the environment or a wider whole may deepen (Vidal, Andreu, Fuentes-Ferrada, et al., 2026; Yaden et al., 2017). Several contemporary frameworks suggest a close link between STEs and psychological well-being (Dambrun & Ricard, 2011; Hanley et al., 2018), which is increasingly supported by empirical research across domains. In contemplative science, the attenuation of self-related processes has been proposed as a central mechanism underlying the benefits of meditation (Britton et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2018), and in psychedelic science, STEs have emerged as key predictors of positive psychological outcomes (R. Griffiths et al., 2008; Kałużna et al., 2022; Orłowski et al., 2022; Zeifman et al., 2025). In patients with cancer, psychedelic-assisted therapies have produced sustained reductions in depression and anxiety, together with improvements in spiritual well-being (R. R. Griffiths et al., 2016; Ross et al., 2016), and STEs have been proposed as a central process mediating these effects (Millière, 2017; Roseman et al., 2018). By temporarily loosening the boundary between self and body, the OBE may therefore offer a direct experiential route to self-transcendence, which may be particularly beneficial for people confronting their own mortality.

OBEs and STEs are typically assessed using standardized, closed-ended questionnaires that quantify the presence or intensity of specific pre-defined dimensions of experience. While useful for group-level comparisons, these instruments constrain what participants can report to categories which are decided in advance by researchers, and therefore poorly suited to capturing the fine-grained, pre-reflective, and often difficult-to-verbalize textures that arise during altered states of consciousness. Qualitative and phenomenological approaches have proven valuable precisely where closed-ended measures fall short. For example, in-depth interviews with cancer patients undergoing psilocybin-assisted therapy have surfaced psychological processes, such as the renegotiation of relationships and the reworking of personal narratives around death, that were not captured by standardized outcome measures alone (Belser et al., 2017; Malone et al., 2018), and a systematic synthesis of qualitative studies across psychedelic treatments has identified therapeutic processes, including altered self-perception and transcendental experience, that recur across substances and diagnoses (Breeksema et al., 2020). Within this broader qualitative tradition, micro-phenomenology (Petitmengin, 2006; Petitmengin, Remillieux, et al., 2019) offers a particularly fine-grained approach, using a structured evocation process to guide participants back to a specific lived moment and elicit a moment-by-moment description of its sensory, bodily, and affective texture, rather than a retrospective summary or interpretation of the experience. This method has been used to characterize the structure and temporal unfolding of other non-ordinary states of consciousness, including psychedelic-induced states of immersion, presence, and self-dissolution (Sanders et al., 2026; Timmermann et al., 2025), as well as the fine-grained dynamics of meditative states and the dissolution of self-boundaries in experienced meditators (Andreu et al., 2025; Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2020; Petitmengin, Van Beek, et al., 2019), demonstrating its capacity to reveal complex experiential structures that standardized questionnaires are not designed to access. For example, analyses of phenomenological interviews with experienced meditators have captured fine-grained features of self-boundary dissolution, such as shifts in attention, agency, and bodily sense that culminate in states of openness, boundlessness, and non-duality (Andreu et al., 2025; Nave et al., 2021). Applying this same approach to induced OBEs offers a comparable opportunity: to characterize, in participants' own terms, how this altered state of bodily self-consciousness is actually experienced.

Immersive virtual reality (VR) is particularly well suited to reliably inducing OBE-like states because it allows precise, independent control over the perceptual factors known to shape bodily self-consciousness, namely visual perspective and the synchrony between seen and felt bodily signals, within a fully controlled and repeatable environment. Building on the classical rubber hand illusion, in which synchronous visuotactile stimulation of a visible fake hand and a hidden real hand induces a sense of ownership over the fake hand (Botvinick & Cohen, 1998), researchers extended this logic to the whole body using head-mounted displays: when participants view a life-sized virtual body from a first-person perspective while receiving synchronous visuotactile or visuomotor stimulation, they often experience a strong illusion of ownership over that virtual body (Petkova & Ehrsson, 2008; Slater et al., 2010). Building on this full-body ownership illusion, researchers have shown that gradually shifting the visual viewpoint upward and outward, so that participants come to see their (former) virtual body from behind and above, reliably elicits a felt separation between self and body characteristic of an OBE, accompanied by a shift in the perceived location of the self toward the new, elevated viewpoint (Ehrsson, 2007; Lenggenhager et al., 2007). This paradigm has been validated using a range of measures beyond self-report, including physiological indices such as heart-rate deceleration in response to a threat directed at the virtual body, taken as an index of ownership (Slater et al., 2010), and behavioral indices such as the fragmentation of episodic memory for events experienced from an out-of-body viewpoint compared to a normal, embodied one (Bergouignan et al., 2014).

Building directly on this methodological toolkit, Bourdin and colleagues (2017) combined a first-person full-body ownership illusion with a subsequent shift to a third-person, out-of-body viewpoint, embedded within a brief guided narrative, and found that this virtual OBE not only reproduced the expected shifts in self-location and body ownership, but also produced an immediate reduction in participants' fear of death and in ratings of anticipated pain, an effect not observed in a matched non-OBE control condition (Bourdin et al., 2017). Together, this body of work demonstrates that immersive VR can reliably reproduce key structural features of a naturally occurring OBE, and that at least one brief, tightly controlled virtual OBE paradigm can shift how healthy individuals relate to death, though the psychological effects reported come almost entirely from closed-ended questionnaire measures administered before and after the experience.

Qualitative approaches have started to be applied to VR experience more broadly. For example, a recent micro-phenomenological study of VR embodiment found that participants' sense of inhabiting an avatar was more dynamic and tolerant of imperfection than suggested by closed-form embodiment questionnaires. Moreover, this study identified experiential processes, such as the effortful adaptation involved in entering and exiting VR, that questionnaires do not capture (Hu et al., 2025). However, to the best of our knowledge, such qualitative approaches have not yet been extended to VR-induced OBEs. As such, there is an open question as to what participants actually experience, moment by moment, while immersed in this altered state of bodily self-consciousness.

The present study addresses this gap, comparing a sample of healthy volunteers (HVs), who served as controls, with patients facing a life-threatening illness, during a single session of Clear Light Essence (CLE), a contemplative 'numadelic' VR intervention whose OBE segment is designed to simulate the phenomenology of OBEs reported by those who have experienced NDEs, as described by Glowacki (Glowacki et al., 2020; Glowacki, 2024). The visual aesthetics (i.e., look and feel) within the 'numadelic' VR environment used in this study differ significantly from the photorealistic look and feel used in the vast majority of VR research studies. In stark contrast to the photorealistic approach, the numadelic visual environment is deliberately minimal: participants appear not as photorealistic avatars but as diffuse, dynamic 'energy bodies', with their hands represented as concentrated points of energetic luminosity (a short video representing the OBE phase is available at Andreu et al., 2026). This 'weakly representational' approach avoids exposing participants to exogenous 'top-down' visual phenomenology which reaffirms pre-existing cognitive priors. The 'numadelic' environment is minimal and unfamiliar, offering an open-ended space into which participants can project their own endogenous meaning. Within CLE, participants are encouraged to contemplate a fundamental idea from modern physics: namely, that all material bodies can also be described as energy. Participants are invited to extend this idea to their own physical bodies, and in so doing imagine their bodies as energy, which cannot be created or destroyed, but can only change form. This approach invites participants to reimagine death, not as an end to be feared and avoided, but as a moment of transition and insight for which one can prepare through reflection and contemplation. Contemplative science has begun to support this idea: brief mindfulness-ofdeath practices have been shown to reduce fear of death and dying relative to an active control condition in a randomized trial (Anālayo et al., 2022), suggesting that engaging with mortality directly, rather than avoiding it, can change how people relate to their own death. CLE builds on such ideas, using the weakly representational 'numadelic' VR aesthetic to evoke phenomenological qualities associated with non-ordinary states of consciousness related to psychedelics and near-death experiences (Glowacki, 2024; Glowacki et al., 2022). Numadelic VR experiences have been shown to reproducibly induce STEs (Bonnelle et al., 2026; Vidal, Andreu, Wrzesien, et al., 2026) and to be a feasible intervention for life-threatening illness patients (Kettner et al., 2025). Within this approach, CLE's OBE segment gives participants a first-person encounter with the dissolution of ordinary bodily self-experience. How participants actually experience this OBE sequence, particularly those for whom death is not an abstract prospect but a present reality, is unknown. To date, we are unaware of any prior study which has examined how people phenomenologically experience VR-induced OBEs, and whether these experiences differ for HVs versus patients with a life-threatening illness diagnosis.

The present study examines how participants experience the OBE phase of CLE, a singlesession numadelic VR intervention, and how this experience relates to broader psychological change following the session. We draw on a sample of 58 participants, comprising 19 patients with a life-threatening illness diagnosis and 39 HVs, who completed a single CLE session. We first examine pre-post changes in mental health, death anxiety, non-dual awareness, and selfrelated pictographic ratings across this sample. We then draw on individual, phenomenologically-informed interviews conducted immediately after the session with the same participants, focusing specifically on their pre-reflective experience of the OBE phase: bodily sensations, the perceived boundaries and location of the body, affective states, shifts in thought, and participants' sense of connection with the other group members present during the session. Interviews were analyzed following the experimental phenomenology methodology, from which we derive the main experiential categories and the underlying structure of the OBE experience, and compare these between patients and HVs. Finally, we examine how these experiential structures relate to self-reports of non-dual awareness and a sense of dissolution.

3. Methods

>Methods

3.1 Study Design

This study employed a qualitative, phenomenologically-informed design to explore participants' lived experience of the OBE phase of a single-session numadelic VR intervention combining guided contemplative absorption, structured breathing, and a virtual OBE designed to temporarily suspend the identification of self with body. This phenomenological study was nested within a parent study comparing the effects of CLE on patients and HVs, including quantitative measures of self-report questionnaires and physiological data (available at Bonnelle, 2026). The current study approach adopts a phenomenological research perspective, allowing to understand the nuances of the OBE experience in depth. Following the CLE VR session, participants took part in an individual phenomenological interview focused specifically on the OBE phase, recorded using an audio device for subsequent transcription. This approach aimed to explore participants' lived experience in depth, capturing the nuances of their subjective engagement with this phase of the intervention.

Phenomenological interviewing seeks to uncover pre-reflective aspects of experience that are often inaccessible through standard self-report measures, providing richer insight into the subjective dimensions of the OBE. Data collection took place between May and November 2025, at the Intangible Realities Laboratory (IRL) of the University of Santiago de Compostela.

3.2 Participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Santiago de Compostela (approval number USC 51/2024) and by the Research Ethics Committee of Santiago-Lugo (approval number 2024/506). All procedures adhered to the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All participants provided written informed consent before taking part in the study.

A total of 65 participants were recruited: 20 patients with a life-threatening illness diagnosis, recruited from the oncology unit of the Hospital Clínico Universitario de Santiago de Compostela and from patient associations, and 45 HVs, recruited from the University networks and social media. Eligibility criteria required participants to be between 18 and 80 years old, fluent in Spanish, and free of neurological impairment or psychotic disorder; patients were additionally required to have a confirmed medical diagnosis of a life-threatening illness and to be physically able to complete a VR session. Participants took part in one of 16 group VR sessions, with a maximum of 4 participants per session. One patient and one control did not attend the experimental session and were therefore not interviewed. Of the remaining 44 controls, 5 were not interviewed due to technical issues during the experimental procedure. The final sample for the present study comprised 58 participants: 19 patients (mean age = 55.79 ± 12.21 years, 5 males) and 39 controls (mean age = 48.92 ± 10.65 years, 13 males). Patients were significantly older than controls (t(56) = -2.20, p = .032), but there was no significant difference in gender distribution between the two groups (χ²(1) = 0.29, p = .588).

3.3 Experimental procedure

Experimental sessions were conducted in groups of up to 4 participants and lasted approximately 2 hours in total. Upon arrival, participants signed the informed consent form and individually completed the pre-session questionnaires. Immediately after the VR session, participants individually completed the post-session questionnaires and the interview. Additionally, physiological data (cardiac and breathing) was measured during the whole VR session; these data are not reported in the present article.

The CLE (Clear Light Essence) numadelic VR experience was constructed as a distilled single-session 'taster' of the multi-session English-language Clear Light programme (which we developed to support individuals facing life-threatening illness (Kettner et al., 2025)). Lasting 45 -60 minutes, CLE is an interactive audiovisual group experience which weaves together guided narrative, contemplative absorption, structured breathing, and a virtual OBE.

Constructed within the so-called 'numadelic' aesthetic, the CLE virtual environment is deliberately minimal. Participants appear as diffuse, dynamic clouds of color rather than as physically embodied avatars, with their hands represented as concentrated points of energetic luminosity. This visual design substitutes our typical day-to-day perception of human bodies with an alternative representation as embodied energy (Glowacki, 2024). The minimal and unfamiliar numadelic environment avoids reaffirming participants' pre-existing cognitive priors, instead offering an open-ended space into which participants can project their own endogenous meaning. Within this minimal environment, sound provides an important scaffold for meaning-making. Audio within CLE follows a similarly minimal design principle as the visual content -i.e., CLE attempts to offer as much narrative as required for participants to engage in meaning making, but no more. As such, the CLE audio narrative is punctuated by several periods of silence, alongside a curated ambient soundtrack which we designed to complement the visual and narrative content.

In contrast to the previously published Clear Light study (Kettner et al 2025), the CLE study outlined herein was intended for participants whose mother tongue was Spanish. Given the importance of language for scaffolding meaning during numadelic VR experiences, we worked to construct a Spanish-language version of CLE prior to initiating the studies outlined herein. Translating the English language version of CLE into a Spanish proved difficult for 2 main reasons: (1) Several recurring open-ended words and concepts which are relatively common within the English-language mindfulness discourse (e.g., "awareness", "openness" and "boundless") do not have straightforward Spanish translations; and (2) Spanish often requires more syllables than English in order to express comparable content (Coupé et al., 2019), making it difficult to preserve those moments of reflective silence which are important during numadelic experiences. Given these difficulties, we undertook a wholesale Spanish-language redesign of CLE, whose efficacy we tested on pilot groups prior to initiating the study outlined below.

The results outlined below focus specifically on one particular segment of CLE -i.e., participants' experience of the OBE, in which participants' point of view shifted from a firstperson to a third-person perspective, allowing them to observe themselves and their movements from further back and above. This shift was accompanied by a guided narrative exploring the relationship between consciousness and the corporeal body, inviting participants to reflect on the experience of observing their own body from a distance and to consider their conscious awareness as part of a broader field of awareness. The OBE phase was followed by a guided dissolution phase, in which participants were led, through meditation, to contemplate the progressive dissolution of their virtual body and to reflect on the transient nature of their sense of self. Following this contemplation of dissolution, the virtual body reemerges and returns, closing the sequence. The OBE phase, including the dissolution and return sequence, lasted approximately 8 minutes.

3.4 Questionnaires

Trait-level psychological measures were administered one week before and one week after the experimental session, to capture more stable, non-immediate changes. Mental health was assessed using the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale 21-DASS-21 (Henry & Crawford, 2005), a 21-item scale (0-3 scoring) yielding a total score and three subscales (depression, anxiety, and stress). Death anxiety was measured with the adaptation to Spanish of Templer's Death Anxiety Scale -DAS (Resett et al., 2021), a 15-item scale (1-4 scoring) providing a total score. State-level measures were administered immediately before and after the VR session to capture acute changes. Non-dual awareness was assessed using the state version of the Nondual Awareness Dimensional Assessment -NADA (Hanley et al., 2018), a 3-item scale (1-10 scoring) yielding a self-transcendence subscale, an affective bliss subscale, and a total score. Different aspects related to the meditation experience were assessed using the Meditation Pictographic Scale -MPS (D'elia et al., 2025), a novel pictographic self-report tool.

Items included in this study were those relevant to VR intervention: connection to others, perceived self-expansion, perceived porosity of body boundaries and perceived ego reduction (1-7 scoring).

Finally, subjective experience across the different phases of the VR session was assessed using an ad hoc measure administered immediately after the VR session. Participants rated the dimension of connection and body boundary dissolution on a single-item (0-10 scoring), for the whole OBE phase of CLE. To support recall, participants were shown brief video clips corresponding to the phase before rating it.

3.5 Phenomenological interview

Immediately following the VR session, each participant took part individually in a Spanishlanguage phenomenological interview focused on their experience of the OBE phase.

Interviews were conducted one-on-one, in a private setting, and audio-recorded for subsequent transcription. Interviews lasted between 20 and 30 minutes and were guided by a set of topics of interest while remaining open, allowing the interview to follow the structure of each participant's own account. To ensure methodological consistency, interviews adhered to a procedure inspired by micro-phenomenological guidelines (Petitmengin, 2006). The interview began with a brief introduction outlining its objective and the way questions would be asked and responded to, focused on examining participants' experience of the OBE phase specifically. The interviewer briefly recalled the sequence of phases in the session and situated the OBE phase within it, so that the participant could locate the moment of interest. Participants were then invited to evoke this phase. The use of the evocation principle was central to the interview, aiming to elicit participants' pre-reflective descriptions and vividly reaccess their recent experience, following the principles outlined by Petitmengin and colleagues (2019). Evocation was initially supported by a prompt inviting participants to return to the moment they began to see their body move away (e.g., "If you agree, I suggest you make yourself comfortable, close your eyes, and bring back into your body-mind the moment when you start to see your body move away in the OBE phase. Let me know when you are ready… What did you experience at that moment?"). Signs of evocation (e.g., use of the present tense, slowed speech, gaze unfocused or directed away from the interviewer) were monitored, and when participants drifted towards generalisations or commentary, the interviewer gently guided them back to the specific moment.

The interview combined a diachronic and a synchronic exploration of experience. The diachronic dimension, which concerns how an experience unfolds over time, was used only in a general way: to map what happened before, during and after the OBE phase (e.g., the onset of the phase, the separation of the virtual body, the pulsing body lights of the other participants, and the return of the body), using questions such as "What happened just before that?" or "What happened just after that?". Its purpose was not to describe the whole sequence in detail, but to help participants locate the specific moment to be explored. Once this moment was identified, the interview "zoomed in" through a synchronic exploration, that is, a detailed description of how the experience was configured at that particular moment. This synchronic exploration covered several experiential domains: (i) bodily sensations and the perception of the body itself; (ii) the perceived boundaries of the body and the sense of the space it occupied; (iii) affective-bodily states, that is, how emotions were felt and manifested in the body; (iv) thoughts and attention; (v) the sense of connection and interaction with the other participants present in the group session; and (vi) any spontaneous actions or inner gestures performed during the phase. When a participant named a state (e.g., "calm", "sadness", "love"), the interviewer asked how that state was recognised and felt, rather than accepting the label as a final description. Interviewers also used reformulation, repeating the participant's own words to check understanding and deepen the description, and attended to spontaneous gestures, inviting participants to put into words what they were expressing with them. Examples of questions used for each domain are provided in Table 1.

Phenomenological interviews were conducted by 3 researchers who followed the same protocol. The interviewers had extensive experience conducting phenomenological interviews (more than 100 previous interviews), with specific training in phenomenological and/or microphenomenological interviewing. The interviewers were blinded to which group each participant belonged to.

3.6 Phenomenological analysis

To conduct the comprehensive phenomenological analysis of our data, we employed the Experimental Phenomenological Analysis (EPA) method (Martínez-Pernía et al., 2023; Troncoso et al., 2025 Troncoso et al., , 2026)). This methodology focuses on synchronic analysis of global aspects of experience at critical moments, discerning distinct phenomenological dimensions among individuals and integrating shared aspects as distinct experiential structures. The overarching objective was to phenomenologically describe the complex organization of lived experience at a specific critical moment of the session (the OBE phase), across multiple layers of experience, then examine correspondences between this description and established selfreport measures (NADA, pictorial scales) and explore ordinal relationships between phenomenological dimensions ordered by depth of experience, while examining betweengroup differences in the experiential organization.

To describe this phenomenological experience, the EPA method involves two iterative cycles. Following data collection, all interviews were transcribed verbatim.

Cycle I. An initial subset of 20 interviews was independently analyzed by three internal coders using Atlas.ti (Computer-Assisted Qualitative Data Analysis Software). Each analyzer adopted a phenomenological attitude and systematically selected only statements directly referring to phenomenological features of lived experience, isolating experiential content while avoiding satellite information such as biographical details, temporal markers, or technical descriptions unrelated to the structure of lived experience. Each coder identified synchronic dimensions and categories emergent from selected participant descriptions for the OBE phase. This process generated over 100 categories across coders. Through iterative collaborative triangulation, the three researchers refined the definitional structure through multiple cycles. A comprehensive phenomenological framework was established, organized hierarchically across four analytic levels, consisting of 6 primary categories, 19 secondary subcategories, 38 tertiary subcategories, and 31 quaternary subcategories. Theoretical saturation, that is, the point at which analyzing additional interviews no longer produces new categories, was reached within this subset of 20 interviews.

Cycle II. This framework was then applied across the complete sample (N=58) by two coders from a randomly assigned group of three (one external) using Atlas.ti. Following the same phenomenological attitude and selection procedure, each coder analyzed the interviews independently, identifying phenomenological categories and experiential structures. Coding results were exported into Excel and processed via an R environment to generate HTML reports enabling systematic identification of disagreements among all subjects in each category. Through iterative consensus, researchers reached agreement for each subject, with an average inter-rater agreement of 94.6% and complete agreement in the experiential structures.

3.7 Quantitative analyses

To first examine changes in psychological outcomes across the CLE session in both groups, we conducted a series of two-way mixed ANOVAs, with Time (pre, post) as a within-subjects factor and Group (patients, controls) as a between-subjects factor. Separate models were run for the following dependent variables: the three DASS-21 subscales (depression, anxiety, and stress); the DAS total score; the NADA total score and its bliss and self-transcendence subscales; and the four pictographic scale dimensions (connection, boundary dissolution, selfexpansion, and ego reduction). Post-hoc comparisons were Holm-corrected. Partial eta squared (η²p) is reported as the effect size for effects. Assumptions were checked with standard techniques, no violations were observed. Analyses were conducted using JASP (JASP Team, 2026).

All quantitative analyses using phenomenological data were conducted in an R environment using the exported Atlas.ti coding data. Consensual coding results were used as the basis for quantitative, descriptive comparison between groups. For each category and subcategory, the number of participants coded was tabulated as a percentage of each group's sample size. Because participants could receive more than one code within multi-coding categories, subcategory percentages do not necessarily sum to 100%. Given the reduced sample size (19 patients and 39 controls), between-group comparisons are reported descriptively as percentages, without formal inferential testing.

A correlational analysis was performed using four categories that allow a natural ordinal ordering according to the depth of experience-Experiential Structure, Boundaries, Attentional Dynamics, and Somatic Quality-treated as continuous ordinal scales, to explore the phenomenological relationships between these core dimensions. When participants received multiple codes within a scale, their score corresponded to the mean. Spearman's rank-order correlations were computed between these four scales, and between each scale and group (coded as binary). P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Experiential Structure was additionally correlated (Spearman) against nine post-session variables using raw scores to examine correspondences between the phenomenological structure and established state self-report measures: the NADA total score and its bliss and self-transcendence subscales; two single-item of connection and body boundary dissolution during the OBE phase; and the four pictographic scales (connection, boundary dissolution, self-expansion, and ego reduction). P-values for these nine comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure.

All procedures were conducted with prior anonymization of interviews and randomized assignment of coder pairs.

4. Results

>Results

4.1 Self-report results

The self-report measures summarized below are part of the main parent study (Bonnelle, 2026); here, we present the results for the sub-sample that was interviewed (Figure 1). Across all measures, we observed a significant main effect of Time (all ps < .01), with relevant effect sizes throughout, reflecting robust pre-post changes following the CLE session. DASS-21 depression (F(1, 56) = 14.73, p < .001, η²p = .21), anxiety (F(1, 56) = 22.27, p < .001, η²p = .28), and stress (F(1, 56) = 19.19, p < .001, η²p = .25) all decreased from pre to post, as did death anxiety (DAS total; F(1, 56) = 9.87, p < .01, η²p = .15). Non-dual awareness increased significantly across the session, as reflected in the NADA total score (F(1, 56) = 110.5, p < .001, η²p = .66) and its bliss (F(1, 56) = 66.95, p < .001, η²p = .55) and self-transcendence (F(1, 56) = 99.28, p < .001, η²p = .64) subscales. Pictographic ratings showed the same pattern: connection (F(1, 56) = 20.44, p < .001, η²p = .27), boundary dissolution (F(1, 56) = 40.28, p < .001, η²p = .42), and self-expansion (F(1, 56) = 66.89, p < .001, η²p = .54) all increased significantly from pre to post, while ratings of the ego decreased significantly showing perceived ego reduction (F(1, 56) = 11.90, p < .001, η²p = .18).

No significant main effect of Group emerged for any variable (all ps ≥ .06), indicating that patients and controls did not differ in their overall average scores across time points. The Time x Group interaction was non-significant for all variables (all ps ≥ .11), with the exception of pictographic connection ratings, which showed a significant interaction with a relevant effect size (F(1, 56) = 11.48, p = .001, η²p = .17). Post-hoc comparisons indicated that this interaction was driven by a significant pre-post increase in connection ratings among controls (4.08 to 5.97; t = -6.91, p < .001), whereas the pre-post increase among patients (5.00 to 5.63) did not reach significance (t = -0.69, p = 1.000). Patients and controls did not show significant differences at pre-session (t = -1.85, p = .418) and post-session (t = 2.67, p = .059).

Taken together, these results indicate that a single CLE session was associated with broad, comparable improvements in mental health, death anxiety, non-dual awareness, and selfrelated pictographic ratings in both patients and HVs, with connection to others being the one dimension along which the two groups responded differently: controls showed a significant increase in felt connection to the group, while patients showed a comparable but statistically non-significant increase. To explore in greater depth the subjective experience of OBE and the possible differences in experience between the two groups that may not have been captured by the questionnaires, we present the results of the phenomenological analysis in the following section.

4.2 Phenomenological results

Immediately after the CLE VR session, each participant took part individually in a phenomenological interview focused on their experience of the OBE phase. The iterative phenomenological analysis of these interviews, conducted with the patients (n=19) and control-group participants (n=39; N=58 in total), produced a total of 91 codes, with an intercoder agreement of 94.6%. These codes were organized hierarchically into four general categories: Self-Environment Structure, which comprises the bodily boundaries with the environment, corporality, and interconnection; Presence, which documents the temporal dynamics of presence and the nature of its interruptions; Affective-Bodily Tone, which includes somatic quality and affective quality; Ways of attunement, which groups the elements that facilitate or hinder the unfolding of the experience. The complete operational definitions and the full development of each category and subcategory, is available on the codebook in the supplementary materials.

Beyond these categories, four experience structures were identified: holistic, integrative configurations that condense the dimensions above into four ways of experiencing the OBE phase: (1) rational disconnection; (2) distressed connection; (3) calm connection; and (4) attenuated self. These structures are presented at the end of this section, after each category has been developed separately, and are revisited in the closing correlational analysis.

Given the reduced sample size (Controls n=39, Patients n=19), descriptive proportions are reported; each category is accompanied by its corresponding figure and a space for the corresponding illustrative quote from the interviews.

4.2.1 Experiential Categories

4.2.1.1 Self-Environment Structure

This general category brings together how participants experienced the boundaries of their body with the environment, their relationship to their own body, and the interconnection (Figure 2). All participant quotations below have been translated from Spanish.

Boundaries: Permeability of the Bodily Boundary with the Environment

Participants experienced their bodily boundaries in relation to the environment in distinct ways. Some felt the contours of their own body clearly against the environment, a sharp bodily demarcation coded as "present boundaries"; this configuration appeared with similar frequency in both groups, somewhat more present in patients (control: 38.5%; patients: 42.1%). Others reported a more diffuse bodily sensation, partially blending with the environment without disappearing entirely, "diffuse boundaries"; here the difference between groups is more marked, with a higher frequency among patients (control: 30.8%; patients: 42.1%).

"The body boundary was more diffuse, yes-it was as if a little bit of me belonged to them and a little bit of them belonged to me; I mean, it was as if we were made of the same stuff-not literally, but of the same emotion." (P13)

A third group described the complete dissolution of that demarcation, where the body no longer felt delimited from the environment, "absent boundaries"; this configuration was more frequent in the control group than in patients (control: 35.9%; patients: 21.1%).

"It was as if I couldn't feel my physical body […] but that body had no limits. […] It was vast; it wasn't tiny, but rather it was the entire space. It was as if I suddenly dissolved into the entire space." (P34)

The control group described the complete dissolution of the bodily boundary somewhat more often, while patients more frequently reported partial permeability or a present boundary.

Given the reduced sample size -particularly in the patient group (n=19) -these percentage differences should be read as descriptive tendencies rather than a firm conclusion about a lesser capacity for bodily dissolution among patients. Present or attenuated boundaries are not necessarily an indicator of lesser experiential depth, but of a different pattern of relation between the body and the environment.

Embodiment: Relation to One's Own Body

Participants related to their own bodies during the experience in varied ways. Many reported that their body simply stopped being an object of attention, with no particular bodily sensation coming to the fore, coded as "boundlessness"; this was notably more frequent in the control group than in patients (control: 71.8%; patients: 42.1%; see Figure 2).

"It's just that I had a really hard time relaxing, but once I did, I honestly didn't feel like I had a body." (P1)

Others described observing their own body as if it were an external object, separate from the experience lived in the first person, "disidentification"; this appeared somewhat more frequently among patients (control: 46.2%; patients: 52.6%). "It was me, but at the same time it was like an observer, like someone outside of myself. I was seeing myself as another person, so to speak. I could see myself from the outside." (P27)

A smaller group reported a defensive retention of the body or a tension that prevented the free unfolding of the experience, "attached-to-body"; this was three times more frequent among patients than in the control group (control: 5.1%; patients: 15.8%).

"I don't want my body to go anywhere just yet. And that I want to get better and that I don't want to die. " (P1)

Finally, some participants described the sensation that their body extended beyond its usual limits, "expansion"; this was also more frequent among patients (control: 38.5%; patients: 52.6%). "[…] like floating a little way out of yourself, as if your body and your energy were expanding and you felt more connected to something you perceive, but that's hard to describe-and it gives you a sense of peace and tranquility that makes you feel as if it's something broader than your own body." (P18)

Patients reported absence of body less often and attached-to-body more often than the control group, suggesting a more present, and at times more defensive or retentive, bodily relation. At the same time, they also described greater bodily expansion and disidentification, indicating a more variable relationship with the body: when retention eases, patients access experiences of expansion more often than the control group. This pattern is consistent with the correlational analysis (see section 3.2.3), where corporality was the only significant correlation with group, reinforcing this observation beyond the purely descriptive comparison.

Interconnection: Directions of Relational Connection

Participants described their relational experience in multiple directions. A small number found no identifiable relational bond during the experience, "no connection", with a similar frequency across groups (control: 17.9%; patients: 15.8%).

"I just wasn't really connecting with the experience at the time-like seeing that there's only light left, which is supposed to be you-I just couldn't quite connect with it." (P47)

The large majority reported a strong sense of "being with" the other participants, "connection with others", the most frequent direction in both groups and with almost identical proportions (control: 76.9%; patients: 78.9%; see Figure 2).

"I had the feeling that we were all in tune with each other, as if we were all breathing at the same time, in the same rhythm, in perfect sync." (P15)

Some described an openness toward something exceeding the immediate group relation, "connection with the transcendent", also with a similar frequency (control: 35.9%; patients: 31.6%).

"Even everything I could see in that setting. It was like-I don't know-the cosmos, the sky, infinity, because it was kind of black, too. […] It wasn't limited to the others alone." (P51)

Others reported an experience of non-duality, in which the habitual divisions between themselves and the other dissolved into an integrated whole, "unity"; this was notably more frequent in the control group (control: 48.7%; patients: 36.8%).

"And once again, a sense of being one with many. Belonging to a greater existence-that we all are-but we are just seeing it from different perspectives." (P3)

A smaller group described a feeling of emptiness within their own experience, "void", with a similar frequency across groups (control: 17.9%; patients: 15.8%).

"It was a feeling of emptiness, of void, and of serenity.

And that feeling of emptiness was infinite; it was a permanent, boundless openness. " (P2)

Finally, some reported a sense of connection with their own identity or personal truth, "connection with oneself"; this was the most marked difference in this subcategory, substantially higher in the control group (control: 20.5%; patients: 5.3%).

"[…] actually, the experience had more to do with me than with others, to be honest […]" (P22)

Both groups described similar capacities for interpersonal connection. The most marked difference appears in connection with oneself and, to a lesser extent, in the experience of unity, both more frequent in the control group. This suggests that the control group accessed states of self-reference or full integration more often, while patients maintained a focus directed more toward the other than toward themselves. Given the sample size, this reading should be taken as a tendency rather than a robust difference.

4.2.1.2 Presence

This category documents the temporal dynamics with which relational presence was sustained and the nature of its interruptions (Figure 3).

Dynamics of Presence

Participants described the dynamics of presence throughout the experience in distinct ways. Most reported a sustained immersion in the experience, with no perceptible withdrawal, "fully immersed"; this was the predominant pattern in both groups, with very similar proportions (control: 59.0%; patients: 57.9%; see Figure 3).

"I was completely immersed in what I was experiencing in the virtual environment, with the other people." (P51)

Others described moments of greater and lesser presence, a movement of approach and distancing, "fluctuating", also similar across groups (control: 41.0%; patients: 36.8%).

"Everything I'm telling you isn't something that happened all the time throughout the experience, but rather at specific moments when I felt that way. At other times, I felt completely connected to the material, to the chair, to everything-even to the pressure of the headsets." (P19)

A small number reported a persistent difficulty in sustaining presence, with recurrent disconnection, "constantly distracted"; this was minor and equivalent across groups (control: 5.1%; patients: 5.3%).

"Because you think about it and you know that this is all like a program; it's programmed. […] Well, in a way, it's like when you're watching a movie, and sometimes it occurs to me that certain people are there, but what I see is that some people are filming others-that thought comes to me sometimes; and it's not good for me because when that happens, I kind of lose my connection with the movie. […] I was more focused on these kinds of thoughts than on living the experience." (P7)

Both groups showed very similar patterns in this dimension, with no relevant differences between patients and the control group.

Nature of Interruption

Among participants who reported moments of rupture in presence, the sources of interruption varied. Some described their attention drifting toward third parties unrelated to the interaction, "concern for others", which was more frequent among patients (control: 12.8%; patients: 21.1%).

"Most of the time I was thinking on my own death, but toward the end, I started thinking about the death of my loved ones, what it would be like, and that helped me relax […] I realized that would happen to the people I love, too-or what had happened to the people I've loved." (P13)

Others reported being distracted by the experimental context itself, "environment/execution", which was more frequent in the control group (control: 23.1%; patients: 10.5%; see Figure 3).

"But I did have a sort of pain in my eyes from the headsets, from straining my eyes-I didn't know exactly where-so I did have a little discomfort that kept me from getting even more immersed." (P21)

Several participants described the activation of personal memories or associations emerging during the experience, "re-evocation", with a similar frequency across groups (control: 25.6%; patients: 21.1%).

"That was a source of inspiration, a memory, because if you ever have an experience that you liked and found meaningful, there might be things that remind you of it. And it reminded me of what happened; it brought it back to me and made me realize something I had already felt before." (P48)

Finally, some reported their attention redirecting toward their own situation or state, "concern for oneself", which was twice as frequent among patients (control: 5.1%; patients: 10.5%).

"I was sort of in the present moment of the session, sensing lights-that is, everything associated with the session itself-but I was linking it to the moment of death, wondering what it might feel like if, at some point, I knew that death was imminent, and this was like my preparation for facing that imminent moment. I was sort of there, with that duality-thinking about both the session itself and how this session would feel to me if I were doing it as therapy for myself, and thinking about death." (P43)

The control group described interruptions related to the environment or task execution more often, while patients more frequently reported interruptions due to concern for others and selfconcern. This suggests that, when patients' presence was interrupted, it tended to be for relational or self-referential reasons rather than external distractions from the encounter, although the absolute counts in this subcategory are small and the proportions should be read with caution.

4.2.1.3 Affective-Bodily Tone

This category documents the somatic and emotional tonality of the experience (Figure 4).

Somatic Quality

Participants described the somatic tonality of their experience across a spectrum of bodily sensation. Most described sensations of ease, warmth, or bodily openness, "the relaxing body", which was the predominant tonality in both groups (control: 89.7%; patients: 84.2%).

"Lighter, more like I'm set free, no pressure, no anxiety whatsoever, none. Set free. Well, that's it-this is my body, and it carries no burden at all." (P27)

Others reported sensations of discomfort, heaviness, or tension, "the tensing body", which at times coexisted with the former within the same account. This was somewhat more frequent among patients (control: 33.3%; patients: 42.1%).

"Physically, I felt a little more muscle tension, especially around my mouth; I noticed I was clenching a little more. Restlessness, a racing heart." (P19)

Both groups mostly described sensations of bodily relaxation. Patients, however, reported the tensing body somewhat more often, suggesting a more frequent coexistence of relaxation and bodily tension within the same experience.

Affective Quality

Participants reported a rich spectrum of affective feelings during the experience. Many reported experiences of love, gratitude, familiarity, or compassion, "connection and openness"; this was notably more frequent in the control group (control: 79.5%; patients: 57.9%; see Figure 4).

"I felt so much emotion, and I also felt a lot of love for everyone who was there." (P31)

Others described being overwhelmed, distressed, in pain, afraid of death, sad, or frustrated, "fear of letting go"; this was more frequent among patients (control: 38.5%; patients: 52.6%).

"The memory it brings back isn't pleasant-I mean, it's not a pleasant memory to see myself without a body. It did make me feel a little uneasy, or afraid, I didn't find it pleasant." (P42)

A smaller group reported surprise or bewilderment at what they experienced, "strangeness", which was minor in both groups and somewhat more present in the control group (control: 20.5%; patients: 10.5%).

"It was a very strange feeling-that moment when you are leaving your body. It's as if you feel it more intensely, as if you're seeing it, but you don't understand how it's happening. Well, up until that point, what was happening was kind of fun, and then, as I was saying, it came as a surprise because I didn't expect that to happen, and I just couldn't make sense of it." (P25)

Nearly all participants described, at some point, calm, relief, or hope, "serenity", which was practically universal in both groups (control: 94.9%; patients: 94.7%).

"But I was at peace, I was calm, I was serene." (P34)

Finally, many reported curiosity, joy, or positive activation, "joy/awe"; this was notably more frequent in the control group (control: 94.9%; patients: 57.9%).

"It is a pleasant experience; it was nice because it was an interesting, intriguing experience. Maybe it was more about the excitement of paying attention to what was happening. I was feeling curiosity, admiration. The image itself-it's new things, and they're beautiful, at least to me." (P7)

Serenity was practically universal in both groups, suggesting a shared background of calm independent of clinical condition. The most marked differences appear in connection and openness and in joy/awe, both more frequent in the control group, and in fear of letting go, more frequent among patients. This suggests that, against that shared background of calm, patients experienced nuances of fear or distress associated with the process of "letting go" more often, and states of joy or awe less often.

4.2.1.4. Ways of Attunement

Participants described different internal movements -ways of surrendering or holding back -as their experience unfolded (Figure 5).

Surrendering

Participants identified various ways their experience opened and deepened. Most reported "letting go" into the experience, a confident, non-resistant surrender that allowed the process to unfold without active control on their part; this movement into the experience was the main pathway in both groups, with a higher frequency in the control group (control: 74.4%; patients: 63.2%; see Figure 5).

"I didn't do anything in particular, and it wasn't hard-I just let myself go with the flow. What I visualized helped me a lot-the images and the music in the background-and I visualized in a very natural way how my body might be seen by other people, as if I were a stranger looking at my body from the outside." (P27)

A smaller group described an insight -a sudden, unsought understanding or revelation that momentarily reorganized the meaning of what was being experienced; this appeared as a minority pathway in both groups, also somewhat more frequent in the control group (control: 33.3%; patients: 26.3%).

"I didn't know who I was-from the lights who appeared. But I recognized myself because I was the only one with my arms down. And I thought that wasn't me. Before that, it wasn't the same light I had been following. And then, right then, I think something clicked, and I realized that it doesn't matter-we're all the same in the end." (P31)

Both groups identified "letting go" as the primary way of surrendering to the experience, although the control group did so more often. Patients more frequently reported an experience with no identifiable surrendering pathway (control: 15.4%; patients: 31.6%), which may suggest less consistent access to these internal movements ("letting go" or "insight").

Holding Back

Participants also described movements that held them back or pulled them out. Some reported an internal friction or defense, a closing off against what the experience called for, "resistance", which was notably more frequent among patients than in the control group (control: 7.7%; patients: 26.3%; see Figure 5).

"I didn't want to let go of my body or let it drift away... My arms tensed against the controls, as if in a physical attempt to hold on to my body." (P1)

Others described the intrusion of discursive thought, evaluation, judgment, analysis, "cognitive interference", which displaced them from the immediate experience toward a reflective or distanced stance. This was more frequent in the control group (control: 28.2%; patients: 15.8%).

"And it's true that I usually have a hard time letting go during these experiences… Well, I say 'these experiences' because there are other similar kinds of experiences, and I'm always a bit analytical about the experience rather than just letting myself be carried away by it." (P8)

When held back from surrendering, patients leaned more toward defensive resistance -a closing off against what was being lived -while the control group leaned more toward distraction through discursive thought. Given the small number of cases, this difference should be understood as a descriptive pattern consistent with what was observed in embodiment (attached-to-body).

4.2.2 Experience Structure

From the patterns within these individual categories emerged four distinct experience structures -holistic configurations in which boundaries, embodiment, interconnection, dynamics, and affective-bodily tone moved together as a unified depth of unfolding (Figure 6). What distinguishes them is not the intensity of any single dimension but the way the whole field of experience is organized: where the self is situated, whether the other is reachable, and what the body does while that happens.

In the first structure, "rational disconnection", the participant remains outside the experience while observing it. The body keeps its edges intact against the environment in every case (100%), and no relational bond forms with the other (no connection: 100%) -the encounter is registered but not inhabited. Presence here is persistently broken: two of the three are distracted throughout (constantly distracted: 66.7%), and none sustains immersion at any point (fully immersed: 0%). Attention returns again and again to the setting itself, the headset, the VR environment (interruption by environment/execution: 100%), while discursive thought runs alongside the experience rather than dissolving into it (cognitive interference: 66.7%). Some describe watching their own body as an external object (disidentification: 33.3%) or a residue of strangeness at what they are seeing (33.3%), with the body holding tension (33.3%). Here the experience is never fully entered: the participant stays a spectator of a scene that does not touch them. This configuration, rational disconnection, appeared in only three control participants and in none of the patients (control: 7.7%, n=3; patients: 0%; see Figure 6).

"But I think that almost all the time and everywhere-especially in my mind, let's say, primarily -I'm like an observer. I observe and also participate as if it were a game, but I'm always an observer. Yes, because, well, that's what I'm saying-I see everything more as an observer; I'm not able to get involved or refrain from doing so. […] In other words, it's something that represents me, but it's not me." (P7) "I suppose that… I view it almost as a kind of stage play. In the second structure, "distressed connection", the experience touches the participant but they cannot settle into it. Every participant here reports fear of letting go (100% in both groups) -the experience opens and something in them refuses the opening. The bodily boundary stays present (control 100%; patients 75%) and presence oscillates (fluctuating: 100%; 75%), so contact is made and lost repeatedly rather than sustained. The two groups inhabit this structure differently. Among patients, the refusal is bodily: three of four hold their body defensively (attached-to-body: 75% vs. 0% in controls), all of them report the body tensing (100% vs. 50%), and resistance is the dominant obstacle (75% vs. 50%) -for them the distressed connection is largely a connection with their own bodily unease, since only one of four describes reaching the other at all (connection with others: 25% vs. 100%). Among controls, the same structure is held cognitively: they do reach the other (100%) but analysis intrudes and keeps the experience at arm's length (cognitive interference: 100% vs. 50%). This configuration, distressed connection, was considerably more frequent among patients than in the control group (control: 5.1%, n=2; patients: 21.1%, n=4). In the third structure, "calm connection", participants reach the other while the self stays intact. Every participant describes connection with others (100% in both groups) -and every participant also keeps a present bodily boundary (100% in both groups). This is the finding that most sharply questions the assumption that relational depth requires bodily dissolution: the encounter is complete and warm without the body giving up its edges. It is not, however, a continuous encounter: presence fluctuates for most of them (control 87.5%; patients 75%), with only one participant per group sustaining full immersion (12.5%; 25%). What characterizes this structure is therefore not steadiness but the tone in which the coming and going happens -serenity (87.5%; 100%), affective connection and openness (87.5%; 75%), a relaxing body (87.5%; 100%), and surrender as the entry movement (letting go: 75% in both groups). The same oscillation appears in distressed connection; what separates the two structures is the affective and bodily tone that accompanies it. Within that intact boundary the body still recedes somewhat, and differently by group: half of the controls watch it from a slight distance (disidentification: 50% vs. 25%), while half of the patients describe it extending beyond its usual limits (expansion: 50% vs. 0%). This configuration, calm connection, was similar in overall frequency across groups (control: 20.5%, n=8; patients: 21.1%, n=4).

4.2.3 Correlations Between Categories

To examine whether the phenomenological dimensions identified in the qualitative analysis cohere as shared underlying patterns, we treated four categories as continuous ordinal scales: Structure of experience (from rational disconnection to attenuated self), Boundaries (from present to absent), Dynamics of presence (from constantly distracted to fully immersed), and Somatic Quality (from tension to relaxation). We calculated Spearman correlations between these four scales and group membership (Control=0, Patients=1), correcting p-values for multiple comparisons across the ten resulting tests (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR); when participants received multiple codes within the same scale, their score was the average of those codes.

Results revealed two distinct patterns. First, Structure, Boundaries, and Dynamics correlated strongly with each other (Structure-Boundaries: r=.80; Structure-Dynamics: r=.76; Boundaries-Dynamics: r=.70; all n=58, p_fdr<.001), suggesting these three scales capture a shared dimension of experiential depth: the more the bodily boundary gives way, the more presence is sustained. Second, Somatic Quality joined that dimension only partially, correlating moderately with Structure (r=.40, n=55, p_fdr=.005) and Dynamics (r=.40, n=55, p_fdr=.005) but showing no reliable association with Boundaries (r=.22, p_fdr=.187) -bodily tone accompanies experiential depth without tracking the permeability of the boundary itself. Crucially, none of the four scales differentiated the groups. All four coefficients were near zero and none approached significance before or after correction (Boundaries-group: r=-.13, p_fdr=.49; Somatic-group: r=-.10, p_fdr=.58; Structure-group: r=-.08, p_fdr=.59; Dynamicsgroup: r=.02, p_fdr=.90), indicating that patients and control participants reached equivalent depth on every dimension that admits ordering.

4.2.4 Correlation Between Experiential Structure and Self-Reported State

Results revealed convergent evidence across multiple measurement approaches. Significant correlations with Structure were found for seven of the nine variables after FDR correction (Figure 7). NADA total emerged as the strongest measure of dissolution depth, correlating most closely with Structure (r=.55, p_fdr<.0001). Its two subscales followed: selftranscendence (r=.48, p_fdr=.0005) and bliss (r=.47, p_fdr=.0006). In-session self-reports of connection (r=.38, p_fdr=.0081) and dissolution (r=.35, p_fdr=.0122) during the OBE phase were also significantly correlated, as were the pictographic ego reduction scale (r=-.31, p_fdr=.0258; higher Structure scores corresponded to smaller ego representations) and the pictographic body boundaries scale (r=.30, p_fdr=.0275). Only pictographic connection (r=.24, p_fdr=.073) and pictographic self-expansion (r=.22, p_fdr=.101) did not reach significance.

5. Discussion

The present study examined the phenomenology of the OBE elicited by CLE, a single-session numadelic VR intervention, and its effects on mental health and death anxiety in patients with a life-threatening illness and HVs. The results indicated that the session induced a state of self-transcendence and was followed by reductions in psychological distress and death anxiety in both groups (current study and Bonnelle, 2026). The phenomenological interviews, however, offered a closer understanding of how the OBE was lived and revealed betweengroup differences that the standardized questionnaires had not captured. Although the depth of dissolution was comparable across groups, the relationship with one's own body was not: patients more often held on to their body in a defensive, clinging manner. Lastly, the experiential structures correlated with independently collected self-report measures, supporting their convergent validity.

First, the self-report scales, indicated that the session elicited self-transcendence, as shown by the increase in non-dual awareness and in the pictographic ratings of boundary dissolution and self-expansion, together with a decrease in perceived ego size. These results replicate the effects previously reported for numadelic VR environments (Bonnelle et al., 2026; Glowacki et al., 2022; Vidal, Andreu, Wrzesien, et al., 2026). One week after the session, depression, anxiety and stress decreased, in line with Kettner et al. (2025), who reported similar improvements with an extended version of the Clear Light program for life-threatening illness patients. A single CLE session also reduced death anxiety, which agrees with previous work on virtual OBEs (Bourdin et al., 2017) and on contemplative practices centered on mortality (Anālayo et al., 2022). Taken together, these findings support the broader view that turning towards one's own mortality, rather than away from it, may transform how people relate to death when this encounter takes place within a safe and guided setting. Patients and HVs did not differ on any of these measures, with one exception: pictographic ratings of connection to others increased significantly only in HVs, a difference we address in detail below.

These measures showed that the session was followed by substantial psychological change, but they revealed little about how this change was experienced. Standardized instruments restrict responses to predefined categories and are poorly suited to capturing the pre-reflective texture of altered states of consciousness (Petitmengin, 2006; Petitmengin, Van Beek, et al., 2019). The phenomenological interviews addressed this limitation, yielding four qualitatively distinct experiential structures: "rational disconnection", "distressed connection", "calm connection" and the "attenuated self". The added value of phenomenological methods has been documented in related fields. For instance, in research on VR embodiment, Hu et al. (2025) found that the lived sense of inhabiting an avatar was more dynamic and varied than closed-ended questionnaires suggest. Similarly, in-depth interviews in psychedelic research have revealed processes that standardized outcome measures alone are unable to capture (Belser et al., 2017; Malone et al., 2018).

In the present study, this phenomenological approach showed that patients and HVs reached comparable levels of self-transcendence. Indeed, the "attenuated self" was the most frequent structure in both groups. It was also the only structure in which the bodily boundary became absent or diffuse and in which presence remained continuous for nearly all participants, which suggests that both features belong to the same dimension of experiential depth. The correlational analysis supported this interpretation: the three phenomenological dimensions (Structure of experience, Boundaries and Dynamics of presence) were strongly interrelated and none was associated with the group. Comparable configurations have been described in micro-phenomenological studies of self-boundary dissolution in experienced meditators (Andreu et al., 2025; Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2020; Petitmengin, Van Beek, et al., 2019) and of immersion, presence and self-dissolution under psychedelics (Sanders et al., 2026; Timmermann et al., 2025). Both groups reached the "attenuated self" structure in a similar way, describing a confident, non-resistant surrender rather than an effort to control the experience. However, this comparable depth did not take exactly the same form: within the "attenuated self", controls more often described the bodily boundary disappearing altogether, whereas patients more often rested in a diffuse boundary.

This contrast reflects a broader pattern. Although both groups reached comparable levels of self-transcendence, their relationship with the body during the experience was not the same. Whereas most controls reported that their body simply stopped being an object of attention, patients more often described it as remaining present, whether as something observed from outside (disidentification), as something extending beyond its usual limits (expansion) or, less frequently, as something to hold on to defensively (attached-to-body). These findings extend evidence that the felt sense of bodily location can be temporarily reconfigured (Ehrsson, 2007; Lenggenhager et al., 2007), and suggest that the manner in which this reconfiguration unfolds may depend on the person's current relationship with their body. For people facing a lifethreatening illness, this relationship is particularly charged. The experience of being a bounded self is thought to underlie the assumption that the self ends when the body does (Blanke & Metzinger, 2009), and for these patients this is not an abstract premise but a lived reality, since illness and mortality are experienced in and through the body. This may explain why some patients responded to the virtual OBE by holding on to their body protectively.

This bodily stance was also reflected in how the body felt. As with experiential depth, the bodily and affective tone of the experience was largely shared across groups, with specific points of divergence. Nearly all participants described calm, relief, or hope at some point during the OBE, and most reported sensations of ease, warmth or bodily openness. Against this shared background, patients less often reported activated emotions such as joy or awe, even within the "attenuated self", and less often reported relational feelings such as love, gratitude or compassion. Conversely, they more often described pain, sadness or frustration, together with bodily tension. This profile is consistent with the attached-to-body pattern described above and suggests that, for some patients, the invitation to leave the body behind elicited not only a defensive stance towards the body but also fear of letting go. Notably, this fear did not displace the calm shared by both groups; rather, the two coexisted. This coexistence resonates with the broader emphases within CLE, which encourages participants to imagine death not as an event to be avoided but as a moment that can be prepared for during life (Prude, 2019). From this perspective, experiencing fear of letting go in a calm and guided setting may be a meaningful and therapeutic part of the experience rather than a sign that it failed. At the same time, this raises the question of how patients can become more comfortable with letting go of the body. Since CLE is a single session, repeated sessions with more preparation and a stronger sense of group cohesion, as offered in the full Clear Light program (Kettner et al., 2025), might help patients engage more fully with the experience, which future studies should explore.

Given that the "attenuated self" was the most frequent structure of experience, most participants reached a state of dissolution. However, a substantial subgroup experienced the OBE through "calm connection", in which the bodily boundary remained intact while connection with others was complete, accompanied by serenity, bodily relaxation and a confident, non-resistant surrender to the experience. Connection with others and with something larger than oneself is a core feature of self-transcendent states, constituting their relational component alongside the attenuation of the self (e.g., Dambrun & Ricard, 2011; Hanley et al., 2018; Vidal, Andreu, Fuentes-Ferrada, et al., 2026; Yaden et al., 2017). This pattern has also been observed in numadelic VR environments, where experiences of selftranscendence are accompanied by increased interpersonal connectedness (Bonnelle et al., 2026; Glowacki et al., 2022; Vidal, Andreu, Wrzesien, et al., 2026). Our findings extend this observation to a contemplative VR experience centered on death and show that self-transcendence can also unfold primarily through its relational component, without the dissolution of bodily boundaries. The phenomenological results showed that connection was mainly directed towards others, and was described equally often by both groups. However, some participants also described connection with themselves, that is, with their own identity or personal truth, and this was much more common among HVs. For patients, turning towards oneself may mean facing challenging emotions and physical pain related to their illness. The tendency to avoid such experiences (Davis et al., 2023) may explain why they rarely connected with themselves during the session.

Finally, a small number of participants did not settle into the experience, and this manifested differently in each group. In HVs, it appeared mainly as "rational disconnection", a structure which was absent among patients. These participants observed the experience from outside: the bodily boundary remained intact, no connection with the others emerged, and attention kept returning to the setting, such as the headset or the virtual environment, while discursive thought often ran alongside the experience rather than being absorbed by it. The OBE was thus registered but not inhabited. Among patients, by contrast, this difficulty more often took the form of "distressed connection", in which the experience appeared to have the potential for reaching participants, but something in them refused the opening it invited. Presence tended to fluctuate in a repeated movement of approach and withdrawal, in which contact was made and lost rather than sustained, and the bodily boundary generally persisted. This refusal was lived through the body, which most patients held on to defensively and all of them experienced as tense. Resistance, an internal friction or closing off against the experience, was the main obstacle to its unfolding. This holding was accompanied by a fear of letting go that encompassed distress, sadness, frustration or fear of death. Rather than being drawn outwards to the setting, attention appeared to remain held by this bodily unease. These patients rarely described connecting to the other participants; their connection was instead directed mainly towards their own discomfort. The few controls who showed this structure lived it differently: they did connect to the others, but analysis and evaluation intruded and kept the experience at a distance.

Thus, what held these participants back differed between groups: HVs tended to remain detached from the experience, observing and analyzing it, whereas patients tended to be overwhelmed by it. A likely explanation is that leaving the body was more emotionally charged for patients, as it is through the body that illness is lived (Leder, 1990) and that mortality becomes most immediate (Carel, 2016). For some patients, the OBE was therefore perceived as a threat, and they tended to hold on to their body. However, this pattern was limited to a small subgroup, while the rest of the clinical group lived the experience through the "attenuated self" or "calm connection". Moreover, difficult experiences are a common part of psychological interventions and are well documented in related contexts such as meditation (Lindahl et al., 2017; Lindahl & Britton, 2019) and psychedelics (Barrett et al., 2016; Carbonaro et al., 2016). Rather than indicating failure, they may have transformative potential, and should therefore be carefully scaffolded and further investigated. What appears decisive is that they unfold within a prepared, supported and caring setting, such as that of CLE, so that they can be held safely.

As the preceding paragraphs show, the phenomenological interviews captured a richness of experience that standardized measures alone could not have revealed. This is particularly relevant for states involving a dissolution or attenuation of the self, which are often described as ineffable and difficult to translate into stable linguistic categories (Canby et al., 2024; Dambrun, 2016), and are also multidimensional, involving coordinated alterations in bodily boundaries, self-location, affective tone and connectedness (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2024; Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026). Beyond this descriptive value, the structures derived from the interviews also converged with the independently collected self-report measures. To examine this, the four structures were ordered by depth of experience: (1) "rational disconnection", (2) "distressed connection", (3) "calm connection" and (4) the "attenuated self". Participants whose experience was classified into deeper structures reported higher non-dual awareness after the session, in both its self-transcendence and bliss components, gave higher ratings of dissolution and connection during the OBE phase, and depicted a smaller ego and more porous bodily boundaries on the pictographic scales.

This agreement across qualitative coding, standardized questionnaires, in-session ratings and pictographic scales supports the convergent validity of the phenomenological analysis, indicating that the structures reflect genuine differences in experiential depth. At the same time, each method contributed something distinct: the questionnaires quantified the intensity of the state, the pictographic scales assessed dimensions that are difficult to verbalize, and the interviews showed, with a level of detail that quantitative instruments are not designed to provide, how these dimensions were organized for each participant. Since no single method can capture the complexity of such states on its own, the integration of qualitative and quantitative approaches has long been advocated in consciousness research (Lutz & Thompson, 2003; Varela, 1996), and the present findings illustrate how such triangulation strengthens the coherence and interpretive depth of the results.

These contributions should nevertheless be weighed against several limitations, which concern the sample, the design and the phenomenological approach. Regarding the sample, the practical and ethical complexities of conducting mortality-focused research with patients facing a life-threatening illness resulted in an asymmetrical sample, with a small clinical group. Patients were also significantly older than HVs, which may have influenced the results. This is a common difficulty, since older controls are considerably harder to recruit than younger ones. Regarding the design, the absence of a randomized control condition limits causal interpretation. In addition, because CLE combines contemplative framing, structured breathing, numadelic immersion, and a virtual OBE, the changes observed in the questionnaires cannot be attributed to the OBE phase specifically, whereas the phenomenological findings do refer to this phase, since the interviews focused on it. Outcomes were also assessed only up to one week after the session, so it remains unknown how these changes persist over time. Finally, regarding the phenomenological approach, between-group comparisons were descriptive rather than inferential and should be regarded as exploratory. This applies especially to the less frequent experiential structures, which included only a few participants per group and whose proportions are therefore sensitive to individual cases. Future studies are therefore needed to address these limitations. Randomized controlled trials with larger, age-matched samples would allow the efficacy of the intervention to be tested and group differences to be examined inferentially. In addition, programs of more than one session with longer follow-up would clarify whether these changes persist and how these experiences are integrated over time.

In conclusion, a single session of CLE, a contemplative numadelic VR intervention, induced a state of self-transcendence and was followed by reductions in psychological distress and death anxiety in both patients and HVs. The phenomenological interviews showed that the OBE was lived through a largely shared experiential architecture, in which patients reached a depth of experience comparable to that of HVs. The main differences lay in the relationship of the participant with their body, which more often remained present in patients, at times in a defensive way, and in a less frequent presence of activated and relational emotions, such as joy, awe, love or compassion. The convergence between the experiential structures and selfreport measures supports the value of integrating phenomenological and quantitative methods. Taken together, these findings suggest that numadelic VR experiences can offer a safe and guided space for patients facing a life-threatening illness to approach their own mortality, while highlighting the need to attend to their relationship with the body.
