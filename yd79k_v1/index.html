Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

Précis: Color helps human performance when it carries information that luminance and form do not, and hurts in fine detail, clutter, and color-only codes. Because observers differ widely, human factors engineering must design color for the population, not the standard observer.

1 Introduction

Color marks hazards on signs and pipes, separates alarm states in control rooms and cockpits, encodes data in charts, identifies wires, drugs, and patient risks, and shapes offices, hospitals, and schools. It is also often misused. Some failures come from assuming that every user sees what the designer sees; others come from claims about color that were never well supported or have failed to replicate. This review is organized around a single question: when does color help a task, when does it hurt, and for whom? Each part of the answer draws on established vision science. Color helps when it carries information that luminance and form do not: it guides search, segments scenes, and supports recognition and memory of objects whose color is diagnostic. Color hurts when it competes with or replaces luminance and form. In practice chromatic detail is far less visible than luminance detail, text at isoluminance is hard to read, and too many codes or conflicting codes slow people down.

The question of "for whom" is where applied practice most often fails. Inherited redgreen deficiency affects about 8% of men of European ancestry, and most of them are anomalous trichromats whose severity ranges from near-normal to near-dichromatic. Chromatic discrimination declines with age, most steeply along the blue-yellow axis. Even among observers who pass every clinical test, color appearance and color naming vary substantially. A color code designed for the standard observer is used by a population, and many of its failures happen at the tails of that population. 1), separating robust perceptual findings from weak or failed claims about color, mood, and productivity. I close with design principles for human factors engineering and an agenda for applied vision research. Figure 1 summarizes the framework. Supplementary Material S1 applies the framework to 22 vignettes, ten based on real events and twelve hypothetical, each pairing a design decision with the evidence and the sections that bear on it.

Throughout, I separate four outcomes that are often conflated: detection (is a signal seen at all?), discrimination (are two colors told apart?), identification (is a color recognized as a particular code value and named?), and operational performance (is the task done faster, more accurately, or more safely?). Evidence at one level does not carry over automatically to the next.

A color difference can be discriminable but not identifiable, and a measured loss of discrimination need not raise accident rates (section 16). The test of a color code is therefore not whether its colors can be told apart in principle, but whether the intended information is recovered reliably, quickly, and safely by the people who use it, under the conditions in which they use it. Part I. What color vision is good for 2 Foundations for applied color

Three cone classes, L, M, and S, sample the spectrum. Their signals are recombined into a luminance channel (L+M) and two cone-opponent channels, L-M (red-green) and S-(L+M) (yellow-violet), the cardinal directions of early color vision. These channels differ in spatial and temporal resolution (section 6), and they fail differently: inherited red-green deficiency weakens or removes the L-M signal while leaving luminance and S-cone signals largely intact (section 9).

Two design consequences follow. A code that differs only along the L-M direction is lost to a large minority of users, whereas a code that also differs in luminance survives most deficiencies (Figure 4). And a code's robustness can be checked before deployment by computing its signals in these channels or by simulating deficiency (Machado, Oliveira & Fernandes 2009).

Color differences are specified in device-independent spaces. CIELAB is only approximately perceptually uniform; the CIEDE2000 difference formula and appearance-based spaces such as CAM16-UCS correct many of its errors. All of these were derived for fairly large uniform patches under standard viewing, and discriminability falls for small marks (Szafir 2018).

A color-difference criterion for a code therefore depends on mark size, viewing distance, and the observer.

Color is also categorical. People use a small number of basic color terms, and colors near category centers are named quickly and consistently whereas colors near boundaries are not (Heer & Stone 2012; section 9.5). Codes are spoken and remembered as names (section 14), so a usable code needs colors that are discriminable, nameable, and far from category boundaries for the whole population of users.

3 Discriminating objects and materials

Color vision evolved for tasks that remain everyday human factors problems.

• Fruit and ripeness. Primate trichromacy is well suited to finding fruit against foliage and judging ripeness (Regan et al. 2001; Sumner & Mollon 2000).

• Food judgments under real lighting. Small, near-metameric changes in illumination spectra can change perceived food attributes such as ripeness or freshness (Cebioglu et al. 2025). Lighting choices in shops, kitchens, and inspection stations therefore matter.

• Skin color as signal. Skin color carries information about health and emotion (Changizi et al. 2006).

• Skin color as a measurement problem. In two large cohorts, pulse oximetry missed occult hypoxemia nearly three times as often in Black patients as in White patients (Sjoding et al. 2020). The FDA's 2025 draft guidance calls for device testing across skin tones. Pulse oximetry is optical measurement, not color vision, but it is an instructive analogy: a system validated on one population can fail systematically on another, which is the central concern of this review.

4 Segmentation, recognition, and memory

Color improves rapid scene recognition and memory (Gegenfurtner & Rieger 2000; Wichmann, Sharpe & Gegenfurtner 2002). Its contribution to object recognition is largest when color is diagnostic of the object (Tanaka & Presnell 1999; Bramão et al. 2011). The applied lesson: color helps most when it is meaningful, and arbitrary color can mislead.

5 Search and attention

Color is one of the strongest attributes guiding visual search, but its effect depends on the difference between target and distractors and on how varied the distractors are, not on any particular hue (Duncan & Humphreys 1989; Wolfe 2021, Guided Search 6.0). This explains why grayscale-based control-room displays make alarm colors effective: they keep distractor color variation low. In the periphery, color is better at attracting attention than at being identified (section 6).

Part II. Where color vision is limited 6 Spatial, temporal, and peripheral limits

Chromatic contrast sensitivity is low-pass, whereas luminance contrast sensitivity is band-pass and extends to much higher spatial frequencies (Mullen 1985; Ashraf, Mantiuk, Chapiro & Wuerger 2024). Expressed in cone contrast, chromatic sensitivity is not low; redgreen sensitivity exceeds luminance sensitivity over much of the frequency range (Figure 2A).

The practical deficit comes from what surfaces and displays can produce: an sRGB display can generate only about 9% RMS L-M cone contrast and about 50% S-cone contrast at isoluminance, against 100% luminance contrast. For the modeled sRGB display and viewing conditions, scaling by those limits makes chromatic detail above about 3 cycles/degree roughly 5-10 times less visible than luminance detail (Figure 2B). The exact factor depends on the display gamut, background, and stimulus size. Chromatic signals are also slower, and the central fovea lacks Scones. Text with no luminance contrast is hard to read (Legge et al. 1990; Knoblauch, Arditi & Szlyk 1991). These limits have been exploited deliberately: chroma subsampling in video (4:2:0) and JPEG compression discard chromatic detail people barely see, and foveated rendering in XR reduces peripheral detail. The design rule follows directly: put fine detail and text in luminance contrast, and put color in large areas and coarse structure. extrapolations. The corner in the luminance curve near 0.85 cycles/deg reflects the truncated logparabola used in the model and has no physiological meaning.

Color in the periphery. With stimuli of fixed size, red-green sensitivity falls with eccentricity much more steeply than blue-yellow or luminance sensitivity (Mullen & Kingdom 2002), and red-green opponency is nearly absent beyond about 25-30° (Mullen, Sakurai & Chu 2005). Larger stimuli help: with large enough patches, chromatic detection and discrimination persist to at least 50° (Hansen, Pracejus & Gegenfurtner 2009). Even large peripheral patches look desaturated, however, and their hue can shift (Abramov, Gordon & Chan 1991). The distinction between detection and identification matters here. A colored light in the periphery can attract attention, especially if it also differs in luminance or flickers, but identifying it as red rather than green or amber requires central vision. Peripheral warnings on instrument panels, head-up displays, control-room walls, and AR overlays should carry their meaning in position, shape, or timing, and should grow in size with eccentricity.

Is color fast or slow?

The answer depends on what is timed. Simple reaction times to purely chromatic onsets are longer than to luminance onsets, and longest for S-cone stimuli (McKeefry, Parry & Murray 2003; Parry, Plainis, Murray & McKeefry 2004). Once luminance contrast exceeds roughly 10-20%, reaction times approach the same asymptote whatever the chromatic direction; added color shortens reaction times only when luminance contrast is low (O'Donell, Barraza & Colombo 2010). For warnings, onset matters more than hue. Brake lamps that reach full output quickly (LED or neon) shortened drivers' reaction times by about 166 ms relative to standard incandescent lamps, a larger effect than an eight-fold change in intensity (Sivak, Flannagan, Sato, Traube & Aoki 1994). Drivers with red-green deficiency respond more slowly to red and yellow signals; deuteranopes took about 50% longer than color-normal drivers to respond to red (Atchison, Pedersen, Dain & Wood 2003). Once a scene is visible, however, color speeds what follows: it guides search (section 5) and speeds recognition of natural scenes (Gegenfurtner & Rieger 2000). Personality assessment produced a related claim, that color is taken in quickly and with little effort whereas form must be actively organized. Comparisons of chromatic and achromatic versions of the Rorschach inkblots gave mixed results on response latency (Lazarus 1949; Siipola 1950; reviewed in Peterzell 2026a), and timing each person's responses to chromatic cards against their own achromatic baseline has been proposed as a way to settle the question (Peterzell 2026b). The reconciliation for design is that detection latency is set by luminance contrast and onset, and color pays off after detection, in segmentation, search, and identification. Claims that a particular hue, such as red, is "seen fastest" usually confound hue with luminance and cone contrast.

7 Illumination, night vision, and adaptation

Color codes are seen under real lighting, not under the standard illuminant in which they are specified. Color constancy, the tendency of surface colors to look stable as illumination changes, is good but incomplete; it is weakest when the change in illumination is large, the scene is sparse, or the light is spectrally narrow (Foster 2011). It is also tuned to natural daylight: observers are least sensitive to illumination changes along the daylight axis, especially toward bluer light (Pearce, Crichton, Mackiewicz, Finlayson & Hurlbert 2014), and when the ambient light is already tinted, shifts back toward neutral are the hardest to notice (Aston, Radonjić, Brainard & Hurlbert 2019). Solid-state lighting can produce spectra unlike any natural illuminant and so strains constancy (Hurlbert 2019). Three practical consequences follow. First, the spectrum of a light source changes how safety colors and products look. The long-standing color rendering index (CRI) summarizes this as a single fidelity score; the newer IES TM-30 method adds gamut indices that show whether a source desaturates or oversaturates particular hues. A high average fidelity can hide poor rendering of the reds on which many codes depend. Second, metamerism: two surfaces that match under one light can differ under another, and multi-primary LED sources can generate many lights of identical chromaticity that render object colors differently (Finlayson, Mackiewicz, Hurlbert, Pearce & Crichton 2014), so matched signs, paints, or product colors can come apart at another site or time of day, and small spectral changes can alter judgments of food quality (Cebioglu et al. 2025). Third, narrow-band and dim light defeat constancy altogether. Under low-pressure sodium lighting hue is lost (section 16), and in mesopic light hue discrimination declines while short-wavelength lights look relatively brighter.

Observers also differ in how they discount the illuminant: when cues are ambiguous, as in the 2015 photograph of "the dress," people disagree sharply about surface color (Lafer-Sousa, Hermann & Conway 2015), apparently because they infer different illuminants (Brainard & Hurlbert 2015; Aston & Hurlbert 2017). For design, codes meant to work across lighting should differ in lightness as well as hue and should be checked under the light sources actually installed.

Color at night. As light falls through the mesopic range (roughly 0.005-5 cd/m²), rods contribute increasingly and color vision degrades progressively, first and most along the blue-yellow axis (Walkey, Barbur, Harlow & Makous 2001; Zele & Cao 2015). Reds darken relative to blues and greens (the Purkinje shift), and reaction and search times lengthen (Walkey, Harlow & Barbur 2006). Much of the apparent value of color at night is luminance contrast: the perceived contrast of colored targets at mesopic levels is well predicted by an achromatic measure of effective contrast (Walkey et al. 2005). Mesopic photometry (CIE 2010) was built on peripheral detection and reaction times, not on color identification, so it says nothing about whether colored signals can be named. Self-luminous signal lights against a dark sky are a special case, but even there color-deficient observers misidentify red and white approach-path lights (Cole & Maddocks 2008; section 17). Older observers are effectively more mesopic at the same scene luminance, because a yellowed lens and smaller pupil reduce retinal illuminance (section 9.7), and mild hypoxia at altitude further impairs mesopic color sensitivity (Connolly, Barbur, Hosking & Moorhead 2008). A code that works in daylight may fail at dusk even for color-normal observers.

Adaptation and prolonged viewing. Colors are judged relative to the current state of adaptation. After a change of white point, adaptation is about 90% complete within a minute (Fairchild & Reniff 1995) and essentially complete within two (Rinner & Gegenfurtner 2000).

An operator who moves from daylight to a red-lit cockpit, or between a display in daylight mode and one in night mode, sees codes shifted during that time, and codes near white, such as amber versus white, are most affected. Discrimination is best for colors near the adapting mean (Krauskopf & Gegenfurtner 1992). Prolonged viewing of a dominant hue shifts judgments of other colors and can leave afterimages, which is why standards for visual color assessment control the surround, viewing time, and illuminant. Adaptation also helps: slow normalization partly compensates for individual and age-related optical differences, one reason appearance is more stable with age than sensitivity (Webster 2015; section 9.7). Color stops helping, and starts hurting, in predictable ways: • Too many codes.

Color is strong for search but weaker for absolute identification, and only a handful of colors can be identified reliably across conditions (Christ 1975).

• Conflict. When color and meaning disagree, responses slow, as in Stroop-type interference.

• False structure. Rainbow colormaps create boundaries that are not in the data and hide real ones (Borland & Taylor 2007; Crameri, Shephard & Heron 2020).

• Depth illusions. Saturated red and blue on dark backgrounds can appear at different depths (chromostereopsis). The direction is not the same for everyone: it depends on where each pupil sits relative to the visual axis, so some observers see red in front and others blue. In a sample of 30, about as many saw it one way as the other, and 16 reversed direction when room illumination rose from 10 to 1000 lux (Simonet & Campbell 1990; see also Ye, Bradley, Thibos & Zhang 1991). The popular rule that warm colors advance and cool colors recede (Pawłowski 2026) is therefore not a safe basis for coding depth or priority.

• Color as camouflage. Irrelevant color variation can mask form. Dichromats can detect objects hidden by color camouflage that defeats normal trichromats (Morgan, Adam & Mollon 1992).

Camouflage and conspicuity.

Camouflage is the deliberate use of color and pattern to make detection fail, and its study shows what color contributes to detection. Background matching and disruptive patterning both delay detection by human observers (Cuthill et al. 2005; Troscianko, Benton, Lovell, Tolhurst & Pizlo 2009). Color vision can defeat camouflage or be defeated by it. Dichromats see through camouflage that relies on red-green color noise to hide a shape (Morgan, Adam & Mollon 1992), but in natural scenes real and simulated dichromats are generally slower than trichromats to find camouflaged targets (Troscianko, Wilson-Aggarwal, Griffiths, Spottiswoode & Stevens 2017; Martínez-Domingo et al. 2022). Two widely repeated wartime claims, that armies recruited color-blind observers to spot camouflage and that a vitamin A2 diet gave sailors infrared vision, rest on anecdote or secondary accounts without published data (Supplementary S2). Camouflage for people has also been adopted without adequate testing.

The US Army fielded its Universal Camouflage Pattern in 2004 without completing comparative tests, then replaced it at an estimated cost of up to $4 billion (U.S. Government Accountability Office 2012). Image-based metrics of camouflage explain only a small share of the variance in human search times (Troscianko, Skelhorn & Stevens 2017), so testing with observers remains necessary. High-contrast "dazzle" patterns can distort perceived speed, but the direction and size of the effect vary across studies (Scott-Samuel, Baddeley, Palmer & Cuthill 2011; von Helversen, Schooler & Czienskowski 2013; Hall et al. 2016).

Conspicuity is the inverse problem, and at night luminance and motion matter more than hue. Drivers recognized a cyclist at night 2% of the time when the cyclist wore black, 15% with a fluorescent vest, 50% with a reflective vest, and 90% when reflectors were added at the ankles and knees, where they signal biological motion (Wood et al. 2012). Blaze orange for hunters exploits the dichromacy of deer, which have little sensitivity to long wavelengths (Jacobs et al. 1994; Cohen, Osborn, Gallagher, Warren & Miller 2014), so it is conspicuous to other hunters without being conspicuous to deer. Surveillance data show that most hunters shot after being mistaken for game were not wearing orange (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1996), but no controlled study exists, and trials of visibility aids for pedestrians and cyclists have measured detection rather than crashes or injuries (Kwan & Mapstone 2006).

Part IV. Who sees it 9 Variation among observers

9.1 Why the standard observer misleads design

Color standards and design guidance are written for the CIE standard observer, but codes, displays, and environments are used by a population. That population varies at every stage of color vision: the optical density of the lens and macular pigment, the spectral tuning and ratios of the cones, and post-receptoral gain and categorization (Emery & Webster 2019). The design question is not whether the average observer sees a difference, but how many users miss it and with what consequences. Two observations frame this section. Deficiency is a continuum rather than a category. And the visual system partly compensates for its own limitations, so appearance often varies less than sensitivity does.

9.2 Color vision deficiency

Inherited red-green deficiency affects about 8% of men and 0.4% of women of European ancestry, and 4-6.5% of Chinese and Japanese men (Birch 2012). Tritan and complete deficiencies are rare. The consequences are everyday. About 90% of dichromats and 67% of anomalous trichromats report difficulty with color-related tasks (Steward & Cole 1989). A recent survey of 381 people with color vision deficiency found difficulties with digital displays across most areas of life, from color-coded diagrams to colored text on colored backgrounds (Mazur 2024).

9.3 Anomalous trichromacy as a continuum, and compensation

To a first approximation, anomalous trichromats have three cone classes, two of which have closely spaced spectral sensitivities. The spacing varies between individuals, and optical density, cone ratios, and post-receptoral gain also contribute, so red-green discrimination ranges from near-normal to near-dichromatic. Severity cannot be read from a pass/fail screening test; it has to be measured, for example in the standard normal units of the CAD test.

Suprathreshold appearance is better preserved than threshold sensitivity predicts. Boehm, MacLeod and Bosten (2014) found red-green threshold sensitivity at 38% of normal in deuteranomals and 19% in protanomals, yet the red-green axis of their perceptual color spaces was compressed far less than those losses predicted. Emery et al. (2021) reached a similar conclusion with hue scaling: red-green responses in anomalous observers were only 1.5 times weaker than in controls, although threshold sensitivity was about 6 times lower, roughly a 4-fold gain (Figure 3A). The compensation was partial, and anomalous observers' color categories still differed from normal. For design, compensation cuts both ways. It can preserve the appearance of large color differences, leaving some anomalous trichromats unaware of their deficiency, without restoring normal discrimination of the small differences near threshold at which many codes, charts, and signal lights operate. How far compensation extends, and by what mechanism, is not yet settled.

9.4 When deficiency helps

Deficiency is not only loss. Dichromats can detect objects hidden by color camouflage that defeats normal trichromats (Morgan, Adam & Mollon 1992). Multidimensional scaling has revealed color pairs that only deuteranomalous observers see as different, a dimension normal trichromats lack (Bosten et al. 2005). Color-deficient observers also judge illumination differently: they detect changes along the daylight axis as well as normal trichromats do, and are impaired only for atypical red-green shifts of illumination (Aston, Jordan & Hurlbert 2023).

9.5 Variation among color-normal observers

Observers who pass every clinical test still differ widely in color appearance. The spectral stimuli chosen as unique hues vary substantially, and variations in different unique hues are largely uncorrelated (Webster et al. 2000). Factor analysis of hue-scaling functions points to multiple processes rather than two cardinal opponent dimensions alone (Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell & Webster 2017a). Color naming and hue scaling share some sources of variation but not all (Emery et al. 2017b). Factor-analytic studies of individual differences have a long history in color and spatial vision, and they treat variation between observers as a signal that reveals how many mechanisms underlie a function and how each is tuned (Peterzell 2016; Peterzell & Teller 2000; Peterzell, Chang & Teller 2000; Dobkins, Gunther & Peterzell 2000). The same approach shows that color and motion are represented in fundamentally different ways (Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell & Webster 2023), that simultaneous contrast for color and for brightness depends on separate induction processes (Kaneko, Murakami, Kuriki & Peterzell 2018), and that cortical responses to hues sweeping around the cardinal axes vary between observers in ways that bear on how color is coded in cortex (Kaneko, Kuriki, Andersen & Peterzell 2025). Three levels of evidence should be kept apart. Individual differences in appearance and naming are well documented. From them I predict that codes relying on category boundaries (orange vs. red alarms, teal vs. green status) will be fragile even in a color-normal workforce. But that prediction has rarely been tested in applied tasks, and showing that a particular palette causes operational errors requires task data.

9.6 Carriers and tetrachromacy

About 12% of women carry an X-linked anomalous opsin allele and may have four spectrally distinct cone populations. Whether those populations are expressed and used is a separate question, and most carriers do not show four-dimensional color vision. One of 24 obligate carriers of deuteranomaly behaved tetrachromatically on every test (Jordan, Deeb, Bosten & Mollon 2010). Cone complement and color dimensionality do not map simply onto each other.

9.7 Aging: discrimination declines, appearance holds

Chromatic discrimination improves into early adulthood, peaks around age 30, and then declines in all chromatic systems, accelerating after 60 and most steeply on the tritan axis (Paramei & Oakley 2014; Figure 3B). In their sample of 291 normal trichromats, median tritan thresholds in the oldest group (mean age 82) were about 3.5 times those of the best decade, against about 1.6 to 2 times on the protan and deutan axes. Decline is measurable along the deutan line after 40 (Paramei 2012). The lens yellows progressively. Color appearance, in contrast, is relatively stable: unique blue and yellow and the achromatic locus change little with age (Werner, Delahunt & Hardy 2004). After cataract surgery appearance shifts toward yellow and then drifts back over months. Young observers wearing filters that mimic an older lens partially recalibrate their white point within a day (Tregillus, Werner & Webster 2016).

Compensation can also overshoot: simultaneous color contrast is stronger in older observers and in those with lower chromatic sensitivity, consistent with post-receptoral gain that rises as sensitivity falls (Grasso et al. 2025). An older worker may describe colors normally and still fail to discriminate small blue-yellow differences (Figure 3B), and may see colored surrounds shift a code more than a younger colleague does. 1). Thresholds are lowest near age 30; by the early 80s they are about 1.6 to 2 times the minimum on the protan and deutan axes and about 3.5 times on the tritan axis.

9.8 Acquired deficiencies and visual impairment

Acquired color deficiencies are common in older and clinical populations and are mostly blue-yellow. In population surveys reviewed by Simunovic (2016), acquired deficiency affected 10.1% of adults aged 40-64 and 20.8% of adults aged 58-102, most of it tritan-like. Causes include diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, cataract, optic neuropathies, and drugs such as ethambutol and sildenafil (Simunovic 2016; O'Neill-Biba, Sivaprasad, Rodriguez-Carmona, Wolf & Barbur 2010). Occupational exposure to organic solvents produces mild, dose-related losses (Paramei, Meyer-Baron & Seeber 2004). Köllner's rule, which assigns blue-yellow loss to retinal disease and red-green loss to optic nerve disease, has many exceptions (Simunovic 2016). Three design consequences follow. Codes that rely on blue-yellow differences alone are the most vulnerable to aging and disease. Acquired loss often comes with reduced contrast sensitivity and visual-field loss, so small, low-contrast, or peripheral color codes are doubly compromised. And the Ishihara test, designed for inherited red-green deficiency, misses most acquired loss; periodic testing with a test sensitive to blueyellow loss is warranted for safety-critical roles and exposed workers.

9.9 Implications for design

Sensitivity and appearance come apart, in anomalous trichromacy and in aging alike.

Designers should therefore (1) design for the distribution rather than the mean, using severitygraded data; (2) not rely on self-report or on how colors look; (3) keep codes away from category boundaries and avoid fine blue-yellow distinctions; and (4) remember that variation can help, as when deficient observers see through color camouflage.

10 Lessons from legacy codes and standards

Three histories show what happens when color codes meet real observers. Supplementary vignettes R1, R5, R7, and R9 add further cases, from approach lights and indicator lamps to changed wiring colors and a misleading valve light at Three Mile Island.

• Component color codes. The resistor color code uses ten hues plus gold and silver, more than can be reliably identified, and its most important adjacent pairs (brown, red, orange; green, brown) are classic protan and deutan confusions (Figure 4A). Voke (1976) simulated industrial tasks, including resistor and capacitor coding, with 100 colordefective observers and compared errors with scores on standard tests. Industry eventually routed around the code with meters and printed numeric codes on surfacemount parts.

• Intelligence tests. Kohs's original block-design cubes used red, white, blue, and yellow faces with red-white and blue-yellow diagonals. Wechsler's adaptation uses only red and white, a pair that differs strongly in luminance and so survives most color vision deficiencies (Figure 4B). In a direct comparison, colorized and greyscale versions of the WISC-IV Picture Completion subtest gave no difference in children's scores. For psychometrics, color is a potential source of construct-irrelevant variance, and differential item functioning across color vision types can be tested.

• Aviation and military standards. Screening has failed in both directions. Pilots with color vision deficiency won the right to fly at night in Australia after tribunal decisions in 1987 and 1989, and many have flown since. In the 2002 FedEx Flight 1478 accident, a first officer who had passed Navy color tests 13 times misread the approach-path lights; the NTSB named his deficiency a contributing factor and asked the FAA to evaluate its tests (NTSB/AAR-04/02).

Task-validated, severity-graded testing followed: the CAD test sets limits from performance on safety-critical aviation color tasks (Barbur et al. 2009), and under the UK CAA's adoption about 36% of deutans and 30% of protans are classed as safe to fly. Part V. Color in environments and wellbeing 11 Light versus paint

Evidence for effects of illumination on circadian physiology and alertness is substantially stronger than evidence that particular surface hues reliably improve mood or performance. The two should not be conflated: melanopsin-mediated effects depend on the spectrum and intensity of light reaching the eye, not on perceived color. Melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells drive many effects of light on alertness and circadian timing. These are now quantified in an international standard (CIE S 026:2018) and in expert recommendations for daytime and evening lighting (Brown et al. 2022). Lighting chosen for these non-visual goals also changes perceived illumination and object color, so the two must be designed together (Hurlbert & Yu 2025).

Practical spectral interventions need outcome data: replacing white with red night lighting on old-age psychiatric wards was feasible and well received by staff, but recorded sleep did not measurably change (Martin, Hurlbert & Cousins 2018). Designers should keep these effects separate from claims about wall and furniture colors. Supplementary vignette H4 applies this distinction to a psychiatric unit.

12 Color, mood, preference, and performance

The evidence divides cleanly. Color-emotion associations are systematic across cultures (Jonauskaite et al. 2020), and color preferences are well explained by the objects people associate with each color (Palmer & Schloss 2010). Color preferences differ by sex (Hurlbert & Ling 2007) and change with age into old age (Ling & Hurlbert 2011), on top of components shared across people (Hurlbert & Owen 2015). Causal effects of surface color on performance, by contrast, are weak or have failed to replicate. A meta-analysis found no reliable effect of red on cognitive performance (Gnambs 2020). A direct replication of a reported red/blue effect on detail and creative tasks did not find it (Steele 2014). A careful test found no aggression-reducing effect of "Baker-Miller pink" (Genschow et al. 2015). Field studies of wall color in offices, schools, and hospitals usually change lighting, layout, and color together, so they cannot isolate color; their claims should be held to the same standard.

Red and black in competition.

Claims that athletes in red win more often, or that teams in black play more aggressively, have not held up in later data, although referees' judgments may be biased by uniform color (Hagemann, Strauss & Leißing 2008; Supplementary S2).

13 Visual comfort

Pattern glare and visual discomfort are real phenomena (Wilkins, 1995). The evidence for colored overlays and tinted lenses as reading aids is weak and contested, and blue-light-filtering glasses have not been shown to reduce eye strain. These claims are graded in Table 1.

Patterns underfoot. Visual structure in the built environment can change how people move. Paving patterns whose grout lines were tilted 11-22° from straight ahead made walkers veer by up to about 37 cm over 10 m, consistent with the aperture problem in motion perception (Leonards, Fennell, Oliva, Drake & Redmill 2015). Flat floors printed with illusions of ridges and furrows were rated uncomfortable or destabilizing to walk on by many people, and some reported fear of falling, with large individual differences (Dickson et al. 2021). High-contrast stripes near 0.5-1 cycle/degree slowed walking (Dickson, Anisimova & Leonards 2023). These effects come from luminance contrast and spatial structure rather than hue, and they matter most for older people, who combine reduced contrast sensitivity with a higher risk of falls. Color patterns can also be uncomfortable: discomfort from colored gratings increases with the chromatic difference between their bars (Haigh et al. 2013). Whether floor patterns that differ in hue but not luminance would keep their decorative value with less effect on gait has not been tested. Supplementary vignette H9 applies these findings to flooring in a care home. Part VI. Color in technology 14 Codes, displays, and safety Coding capacity and legibility. Color is a strong code for search and a weaker one for identification (Christ 1975). Sign legibility depends mainly on luminance contrast; black on saturated yellow and white on saturated red work, and yellow on white does not (Wogalter & Vigilante 2006). Polarity matters as well. A light region looks larger than an equal dark one (irradiation; Westheimer 2007), so symbols and strokes tuned for a light background look heavier when the display is switched to a dark one. Dark text on a light background gives better acuity and proofreading in younger and older adults alike, and the advantage grows as characters get smaller (Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund & Buchner 2013; Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner 2014).

Display and alarm standards share perceptual assumptions and rely on redundancy. The highperformance approach to process-control displays, widely applied under ISA-101 and EEMUA 201 as design guidance rather than a mandated palette, uses grey, low-saturation backgrounds and reserves saturated color for abnormal conditions, which keeps distractor color variation low so that alarm colors guide search (section 5). The medical alarm standard IEC 60601-1-8 codes priority by color and flashing together: flashing red (about 1.4 to 2.8 Hz) for high priority, slower flashing yellow (about 0.4 to 0.8 Hz) for medium, and steady cyan or yellow for low. US airworthiness rules for transport aircraft reserve red for warnings and amber or yellow for cautions, exclude red and green for advisories, and limit other uses of red, amber, and yellow on the flight deck (14 C.F.R. § 25.1322); FAA guidance on electronic flight displays calls for consistent use of color and for coding that does not rely on color alone (Federal Aviation Administration 2014). WCAG 2 requires that color not be the only visual means of conveying information. None of these documents specifies its codes in terms of the discrimination of observers with color vision deficiency or age-related loss; they cover those users through redundancy (flashing, position, text, shape). That is sound practice, but it means the standards do not test whether color itself works for those users. WCAG 2 contrast ratios are minimums, not optima, and their formula has known limitations; proposed alternatives such as APCA still need independent psychophysical validation.

Naming: the verbal interface of codes.

Codes are spoken as well as seen. When meanings differ across sites, names become hazards: in a 2005 Pennsylvania near-miss, a nurse put a yellow wristband meaning "restricted extremity" at one hospital on a patient at another hospital, where yellow meant "do not resuscitate." A statewide survey found little consistency: DNR status was marked with blue in just over half the facilities that marked it, and with purple, yellow, pink, or red elsewhere (Pennsylvania Patient Safety Reporting System 2005). Hospital emergency codes varied so widely that many hospitals have moved to plain-language alerts.

Colors near category centers are named fast and consistently, and naming reliability can be measured (Heer & Stone 2012). Across 110 languages, warm colors are communicated more efficiently than cool colors (Gibson et al. 2017), and naming systems approach informationtheoretically efficient compression (Zaslavsky et al. 2018). Languages also differ in how finely they divide color space. The Colour Spinner now covers six languages, American English, British English, French, Greek, Spanish, and Himba (OtjiHimba), with naming data from online experiments and fieldwork (Sinkeviciute, Stutters, Ahmed, Koliousis & Mylonas 2026). In the published five-language analysis, nine American English names for the blue region (light blue, sky blue, blue, navy blue, teal, dark teal, cyan, turquoise, aqua) all fell under the single Himba term burou, (Mylonas, Ahmed, Sinkeviciute & Koliousis 2026); the number of color names speakers treat as indispensable also differs widely between languages (Supplementary S2). A code that relies on distinctions named in one language, such as teal versus blue, may have no stable names in another, so codes for multilingual users should be checked for consistent naming in each language. Tools of this kind also simulate color vision deficiency and age-related changes (Sinkeviciute et al. 2026), but naming data collected on uncalibrated displays measure naming rather than discrimination, and they complement rather than replace perceptual testing.

Supplementary vignettes R², R7, R10, H6, H10, and H13 show how codes fail when their meanings differ across hospitals, wiring standards, countries, languages, and drug labels. This creates a tension: the warm colors most reliably named by trichromats are those that red-green deficient observers confuse most.

Pills and packages.

Color on medicines serves both as an identification code and as a source of expectation. Patients recognize their pills partly by color, and changes in appearance when a pharmacy switches generic suppliers are followed by patients stopping treatment. A change in pill color raised the odds of discontinuing antiepileptic drugs (adjusted odds ratio 1.27, and 1.53 in patients with seizure disorders; Kesselheim et al. 2013) and cardiovascular drugs after myocardial infarction (1.34; Kesselheim et al. 2014). The absolute effects are small, since only about 1% of refills changed color, but the authors attribute them partly to patients who sort pills into organizers and identify them by appearance. Standardizing color across makers is the converse: the UK National Health Service colors reliever inhalers blue and steroid inhalers brown, orange, or burgundy after users confused the two (as described by Kesselheim et al. 2013). Pill color also shapes expected effects: red, orange, and yellow are associated with stimulation and blue and green with sedation, although evidence that color changes a drug's actual effectiveness is inconsistent (de Craen, Roos, de Vries & Kleijnen 1996). Package color helps and hurts. Multiple-traffic-light nutrition labels are the front-of-pack format that most consistently helps shoppers identify healthier products (Hawley et al. 2013), although their main signal is a red-green code, backed up by text and numbers. Conversely, look-alike labels and packaging are a recognized cause of errors in selecting medicines (Berman 2004; Emmerton & Rizk 2012). Package colors also set expectations about taste, and these correspondences are fairly consistent across countries (Spence et al. 2015; Supplementary S2). Supplementary vignette H14 applies this paragraph to a change in a patient's generic medicine.

Accommodation. Design-side systems work: Color Universal Design in Japan, ColorADD symbol codes, palettes that remain distinguishable with color vision deficiency, and redundant cues such as the striped green-and-yellow earth conductor. Observer-side aids are a different case, and the best evidence on them is more positive than their reputation. Multi-notch filters such as EnChroma lenses remove light where the M-and L-cone sensitivities overlap most, which increases the L-M contrast that broadband surfaces produce for anomalous trichromats, more for deuteranomals than for protanomals (Somers & Bosten 2024); in a comparison of nine commercial aids, an EnChroma lens gave the largest red-green enhancement (Moreland, Dain, Cheung & Westland 2022). Deuteranomalous observers wearing the filters showed the predicted shift in color matches and enhanced red-green appearance (Somers, Franklin & Bosten 2024). After about two weeks of daily wear, anomalous trichromats showed a 71% increase in suprathreshold red-green contrast response, measured without the glasses, that did not occur in normal trichromats or with a placebo lens (Werner, Marsh-Armstrong & Knoblauch 2020). This fits evidence that anomalous trichromats already amplify their weak L-M signals after the receptors (Knoblauch, Marsh-Armstrong & Werner 2020; section 9.3), and shows that the filters can drive that gain further.

The effect built up with a time constant of about 8.5 days, so studies that test only on first wear, or after a single week, are likely to underestimate it (Somers et al. 2024); independent replication over a comparable period is the next step. What the filters do not do is make an anomalous trichromat a normal one: threshold discrimination and scores on clinical tests change little, in studies that tested within about 30 minutes of first wear and often included dichromats (Gómez-Robledo et al. 2018; Patterson et al. 2022; Pattie, Aston & Jordan 2022), and dichromats, who lack one of the two cone classes the filters separate, cannot benefit. EnChroma does not overclaim on these points: its own account of the evidence describes the lenses as enhancing color appearance, not as a cure for color vision deficiency or a way to alter test results, which is what the research supports (EnChroma n.d.). The natural next question is whether these gains carry over to everyday color tasks, and that work has only begun. The filters act on color differences that are already visible, so the tasks most likely to benefit are suprathreshold ones such as search, segmentation, and judging materials and skin. The first tests instead used tasks that depend on fine discrimination near threshold, such as identifying retinal lesions in photographs or discriminating closely similar natural colors, which the filters are not designed to improve, and found no change (Trevino, Tichenor, Kovacich & Cheung 2025; Marques, Gomes, Linhares & Nascimento 2023).

For human factors, the filters are a legitimate aid to color appearance and possibly to adaptation, but not yet a substitute for good design: they are not a way to meet occupational color standards or to remove safety-critical confusions, which remain the designer's responsibility.

Memory and transfer of training.

Training transfers poorly across color conventions and color rendering. Memory for color is categorical and becomes more so with delay (Bae et al. 2015). In successive matching, memory error rather than failure of constancy is the main limit (Ling & Hurlbert 2008), and memory colors of familiar objects are biased, though less so for realistic textured objects (Vurro, Ling & Hurlbert 2013). Operators cannot be expected to retain fine distinctions within a category; codes should use colors near category centers and far apart.

Perceptual learning of color discrimination is specific to the trained hues (Özgen & Davies 2002). Color conventions are learned, not universal. Over 90% of US respondents associate red with stop and green with go (Bergum & Bergum 1981), but in a sample from Yunnan, China, no color-meaning association exceeded about 65% (Courtney 1986; see also Chan & Courtney 2001). Even widely shared meanings can reverse between countries: red marks the political left in most of Europe but the Republican Party in the United States (Pawłowski 2026), so a meaning learned in one setting cannot be assumed to carry over to another. Reversing a learned mapping has a measurable cost: responses slow when red means go (Kubo et al. 2021), and stopping is faster to red than to green stop signals (Blizzard et al. 2017). FAA guidance warns of negative transfer when two systems use the same color to mean different things (Xing 2006), but no peerreviewed study has measured it in pilots, controllers, or clinicians who move between systems. A related risk runs from color to its absence. Learners who studied color-coded material and were tested without color did worst on transfer (Skulmowski 2022). This is a single small study, but it bears directly on training with color simulators for work with monochrome or night-vision displays (section 15).

15 Data visualization, XR, and sensor-mediated color Data visualization. Colormaps for continuous data should be perceptually uniform and monotonic in lightness, so that equal steps in the data look equal and order survives the loss of hue; rainbow maps fail both tests (section 8). Discriminability also depends on mark size: colors distinct as large patches merge as small points and thin lines (Szafir 2018). Palettes such as cividis were designed to give nearly identical interpretation with and without red-green deficiency (Nuñez, Anderton & Renslow 2018). Red/amber/green status coding is common and unsafe for many users (Figure 5).

Pseudocolor in medical and neuroimaging. Medicine relies heavily on pseudocolor: statistical maps from fMRI and PET overlaid on anatomy, perfusion and flow maps, color Doppler ultrasound, and thermal images. Here colormap choice affects diagnosis. Medical students searching images of coronary arteries for regions of low shear stress found about 47% more of them with a diverging colormap than with a rainbow map in 2D views, and 82% more in 3D views; the rainbow was faster, and they rated the two maps alike, so the cost went unnoticed (Borkin et al. 2011). A survey of more than 9,000 published fMRI and PET images from 1996 to 2009 found little standardization of display and frequent poorly explained color scales (Christen et al. 2013). Thresholded overlays add a second problem: opaque, all-or-nothing coloring of voxels that pass a statistical cutoff hides sub-threshold results and exaggerates apparent disagreement between studies, which showing sub-threshold values with transparency reduces (Taylor et al. 2023). Guidelines for neurophysiological maps make the same recommendations as for other data: ordered, perceptually uniform maps for magnitude and diverging maps for signed values (Cooper, Baillet, El Khoury Maroun & Chong 2021). Observer variation matters here too.

Pathologists and laboratory scientists with red-green deficiency scored lower on identifying histopathology slides, and scores fell with severity (Poole et al. 1997), but I found no study of color-deficient clinicians reading Doppler, PET, perfusion, or fMRI maps. Maps and satellite imagery. Geographic displays add learned color conventions to the usual perceptual limits. Satellite images are often shown in false color: in a standard colorinfrared composite, near-infrared is mapped to red, so healthy vegetation looks red, and analysts must learn mappings that contradict natural color. When imagery from different sensors is combined, natural-looking false color supports better performance than arbitrary color (Toet & Hogervorst 2012), and a change of mapping between training and operation is a form of the negative transfer discussed in section 14. Elevation maps conventionally run from green lowlands to brown highlands, which invites readers to treat green as vegetation, so a low-lying desert can look fertile; cartographers have proposed tints that blend with land cover to avoid this (Patterson & Jenny 2011). Rainbow color schemes remain common in geoscience despite distorting the data they show (Light & Bartlein 2004; Crameri, Shephard & Heron 2020). Tools such as ColorBrewer offer sequential, diverging, and qualitative schemes and flag those that remain distinguishable with color vision deficiency (Harrower & Brewer 2003). Public hazard maps, such as heat or flood risk maps, combine all of these problems for a lay audience that includes color-deficient and older readers, and their comprehension has rarely been tested in those readers. palette largely survives, although some pairs converge for dichromats. Displays and device variability.

Identical RGB values do not guarantee identical appearance. Displays differ in primaries, tone curves, peak luminance, high-dynamic-range tone mapping, and reflection of ambient light, and many adjust brightness and white point automatically. Observers differ too: calibration to the CIE 1931 standard observer corrects the device, not the individual. As primaries become narrower, as in laser, quantum-dot, and some OLED displays, color-normal observers disagree more about matches. In one study, observers matching images on an LCD and a laser projector differed by 3-4 ΔE00 on average (Asano, Fairchild, Blondé & Morvan 2014; Asano et al. 2016). Ambient light adds a veiling luminance that reduces contrast and desaturates dark colors. In radiology, observer performance with medical-grade color displays has been comparable to monochrome (Krupinski, Roehrig, Fan & Yoneda 2007). For safety-critical displays, the color code should be specified together with the device and viewing conditions (luminance, white point, gamut, tone mapping, ambient light), automatic adjustments should be disabled or validated, and codes should be tested on the devices actually in use.

Sensor-mediated color. Technology increasingly stands between the eye and the world.

Color can be: • Constrained. Cockpit lighting must be visible to the naked eye without degrading goggles, so MIL-STD-3009 limits it to a small set of colors (NVIS Green A and B, Yellow, Red, White).

• Reconstructed. Headset passthrough (Apple Vision Pro, Meta Quest) shows the world through cameras, with color accuracy degrading in low light; optical see-through AR can only add light, so virtual colors mix with the background (Gabbard et al. 2010). Fusion of thermal and intensified imagery works best with natural rather than arbitrary false color (Toet & Hogervorst 2012). Per-user recoloring in passthrough is a research direction for color vision deficiency.

Orientation in unusual environments. Which way is up is partly a visual judgment (Howard & Hu 2001). Spacecraft designers have used darker floors and lighter ceilings to supply a visual vertical, but no experiment has isolated color or lightness as the cue (Oman 2007).

16 Accidents, investigation, and testimony

This section asks what color-vision evidence can establish about real-world safety, responsibility, and reported observations. Color enters accident analysis as a possible cause, as a property of the vehicles and equipment involved, and as evidence reported by witnesses, and each rests on a different kind of evidence. Supplementary vignettes R3, R6, and H7 illustrate these uses: a toy-gun marking that officers could not see, flight into cloud, and an eyewitness report of a car's color.

Color vision as a cause. Color-vision screening in transport began with an accident.

After two trains collided at Lagerlunda, Sweden, in 1875, Holmgren argued that color blindness among railway staff was to blame, and his wool test was adopted by railways across Europe and North America. Mollon and Cavonius (2012) re-examined the archival record and concluded that the accident cannot be attributed to color blindness alone. Screening that is useful in its own right thus rests partly on a misattributed accident. The FedEx 1478 accident (section 10) is the opposite case, in which deficiency was a documented contributing factor. Road data show how hard such attribution is. Drivers with red-green deficiency recognize signal colors more slowly and less accurately, and protans see red signals and rear lights at shorter distances (Verriest, Neubauer, Marre & Uvijls 1980; Atchison, Pedersen, Dain & Wood 2003). Yet Verriest et al. (1980) found no excess of accidents caused by these drivers. Signal position and intensity, redundancy in the traffic system, and drivers' own compensation appear to absorb much of the deficit. A measured perceptual loss is not a measured risk, and neither should be inferred from the other.

Color as conspicuity.

Vehicle color affects how easily a vehicle is seen. In Australian crash data, black, blue, grey, green, red, and silver cars had higher daytime crash risk than white cars, by up to about 10% (Newstead & D'Elia 2007). A widely cited study of Dallas fire pumpers reported about three times as many visibility-related accidents for red as for limeyellow vehicles (Solomon & King 1995), but it drew on one city and did not adjust for exposure.

The general benefit of high-luminance, high-contrast colors for conspicuity is better supported than any specific crash-rate figure.

Color in hostile viewing conditions. Water absorbs long wavelengths within a few meters, so red markings darken at depth and codes that depend on red fail. In technical diving, divers have died after breathing the wrong gas from cylinders that were color-coded and labeled.

Current practice relies on large printed maximum-operating-depth markings and on physically matching regulators to cylinders rather than on color. These accounts come from incident reports and industry guidance rather than peer-reviewed studies, but the lesson matches that of component codes: color cannot be the only carrier of critical information.

Color as testimony.

Witnesses report the colors of vehicles, clothing, and lights, and these reports enter investigations and trials. Memory for color is largely categorical: people retain the category ("blue") better than the shade, and remembered colors are biased toward category centers even after a delay of seconds (Bae, Olkkonen, Allred & Flombaum 2015).

Testimony involves delays of days or months, far longer than these experiments test. Even for vehicles in view, observers agree best on black and white and least on intermediate colors and fine gradations (Lee et al. 2019). The light source matters. Under low-pressure sodium lighting, which is nearly monochromatic, surface colors cannot be distinguished at all, and other lamps with poor color rendering shift and compress apparent colors. Witnesses differ as well: red-green deficiency is common and often undiagnosed, and color-normal observers disagree about where category boundaries fall (section 9.5).

Decisions to use force.

Color enters use-of-force decisions mainly through skin and objects rather than clothing hue. In first-person shooter tasks, participants shoot armed Black targets faster than armed White targets (Correll, Park, Judd & Wittenbrink 2002). Across 42 studies the latency effect is reliable but small (d ≈ -0.13), and effects on errors are inconsistent (Mekawi & Bresin 2015). Bias is larger for targets with darker skin and more stereotypical features (Ma & Correll 2011; Kahn & Davies 2011) and for Black targets in stereotypically threatening attire (Kahn & Davies 2017). The basic bias replicates in new samples, including work testing whether mindfulness meditation can improve signal detection accuracy in the shooter task (Peterzell 2023). Brief interventions have so far had limited effects on the underlying associations: in 82 White adults, about 70% showed a pro-White bias on the race Implicit Association Test, and neither a body-scan nor a lovingkindness meditation reduced it, although lovingkindness moved scores toward zero in both directions (Korsmo 2019; Korsmo, Dorian & Peterzell 2025). Racial categorization, skin pigmentation, facial morphology, and learned associations are confounded in these stimuli, so the shooter-bias literature is not by itself evidence of a chromatic mechanism; skin tone has not been separated colorimetrically from facial features. In the literature I reviewed, no experiment manipulated the hue of clothing, so claims about gang colors or dark clothing and decisions to shoot are untested. Object color has been tested, with a negative result. In an evaluation for the National Institute of Justice, police officers confronted at 15 and 30 feet, by day and night, could not see the blaze-orange barrel plugs required on toy guns and fired in nearly every encounter; coloring the whole toy brightly was more effective (Carlson & Finn 1989). A small chromatic marker cannot be relied on at a glance, under threat, or in low light where hue is lost. No shooter-task study has manipulated scene illumination, although darkness and ambiguity were central to the cases that motivated the paradigm. , the ability to tell a coded signal from everything else, and the criterion, the willingness to respond (Green & Swets 1966). Color can raise sensitivity by segmenting targets and can shift the criterion through salience and urgency, while frequent false alarms push operators toward conservative criteria that no choice of color will fix. Fuzzy signal detection theory extends the analysis to graded states such as caution and warning (Parasuraman, Masalonis & Hancock 2000). Yet few color-coding studies report sensitivity or criterion; most report only response time and accuracy (Christ 1975).

Studies of color-deficient observers show why validation against the task matters.

Arrangement tests are not pure measures of color discrimination: scores on the Farnsworth-Munsell 100-Hue test are related to nonverbal IQ (Cranwell, Pearce, Loveridge & Hurlbert 2015). Screening plates are sensitive: the 38-plate Ishihara identifies 95.5-99% of red-green deficient observers, depending on the number of errors allowed, which is itself a choice of criterion (Birch 1997). But passing a screening test does not establish fitness for a task. Colordeficient observers made more errors on simulated air traffic control color tasks at every level of severity, and only about 6% performed without error (Mertens & Milburn 1996). Ten colordeficient observers who passed the Farnsworth lantern test still made significantly more errors than color-normal observers in naming simulated approach-path lights (Cole & Maddocks 2008).

Doctors with color deficiency detected fewer signs in clinical photographs (Campbell, Spalding, Mir & Birch 1999), and laboratory scientists with deficiency scored lower on histopathology slides (Poole, Hill, Christie & Birch 1997). A color code should therefore be validated by (1) defining the operational decision; (2) testing representative users, including anomalous trichromats, older users, and users with acquired deficiencies, under worst-case lighting, eccentricity, and display conditions; (3) reporting hits, misses, false alarms, sensitivity, criterion, and response time, with redundant cues present and absent; and (4) setting fitness-for-duty standards from task performance rather than plate error counts (Barbur & Rodriguez-Carmona 2017). Supplementary vignettes R1 and H1 show this protocol applied to approach lights and to an alarm palette for a control room. What an expert can and cannot say. An expert witness can establish what is measurable: the color vision of a witness or driver, graded by severity with an anomaloscope or the CAD test rather than screening plates; the spectral power distribution of the lighting at the scene; the chromaticity and luminance of the signals and surfaces involved; and whether a reported discrimination was physically possible under those conditions.
