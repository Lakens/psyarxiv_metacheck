What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

People often resist innovation. Maintaining the status quo may involve fluency, or subjective ease of processing. Content that feels easy to understand seems true, inspires confidence, and is likable. Across four preregistered experiments (total N = 1002), we investigated whether repeated ideas also seem more creative than new ones. Participants saw uncommon uses for everyday objects (e.g., rolling out dough with a wine bottle; Experiments 1 -3) or literary quotes (e.g., The richest plan is not to have one; Experiment 4). Later, they judged the creativity of these ideas and new ones. Repeated life hacks seemed less creative than new ones, but repeated literary quotes seemed more creative than new ones. When participants evaluated dimensions of creativity separately, repetition made uses for objects seem more useful without impacting perceived originality. Our findings support feelings-as-information theory, as people use fluency flexibly in domains like creativity, where novelty should be valued.

People Use Fluency Flexibly When Judging Creativity

Resistance to innovation limits progress in science, policy, and business. Scientists rejected evidence that misfolded proteins, now called prions, cause neurodegeneration (Zabel & Reid, 2015). Legislators hesitate to pass bills that give out cash payments, even though they reduce homelessness and generate net societal savings (Dwyer et al., 2023). And the founders of Ring -a doorbell camera company that eventually sold for over $1 billion dollars to Amazonleft the popular investment TV show, Shark Tank, without a deal. The most creative ideas are not always perceived as the best ones. Uncertainty (Mueller et al., 2012), perceived risk (Keith et al., 2024), and adherence to norms (Reis & Kunde, 2024) undermine support for unconventional ideas. Do basic cognitive biases also lead people to reject creative ideas?

People spontaneously evaluate the quality of their own solutions during creative cognition. Conceiving new ideas involves two phases -generation and evaluation (Kleinmintz et al., 2019). We first search for relevant concepts and details in memory, then construct mental images of them (Benedek et al., 2023). In the alternative uses task, for example, participants produce as many atypical uses as they can for a specific object, like a brick (Guilford, 1967). A participant might search their memory for sturdy, heavy items and remember that they used a brick in place of a stepstool in the past (Ramey & Zabelina, 2024). People then select among these candidates by rejecting ones that are typical or impractical, a filtering process that requires cognitive control (Chrysikou, 2019). For example, a participant might mentally simulate whether standing on a brick would allow them to reach a kitchen shelf (Orwig et al., 2026).

Monitoring our own creative cognition is imperfect. Participants' ratings of their own solutions correlate with the ratings of independent judges, and more creative individuals are even more likely to identify their best ideas (Silvia, 2008). But when making these inferences, people weigh how the creative process felt. In moments of insight, solutions come to mind suddenly, with exceptional positive affect, ease, and confidence (Topolinski & Reber, 2010). Solving a difficult problem faster than expected results in a metacognitive prediction error, similar to those associated with unexpected rewards (Dubey et al., 2026). These "Aha" moments can be helpful indicators of a solution's actual accuracy (Salvi et al., 2016), but they can also lead to false insights (Danek & Wiley, 2017).

Errors can also arise when we evaluate the contributions of others. People who make accurate judgments of their own ideas do not necessarily make accurate judgments of other people's outputs (Runco & Smith, 1992). More creative individuals are also better at evaluating the creativity of others' insights (Benedek et al., 2016), even though they still judge their own ideas even more accurately (Guo et al., 2022). And more generally, we struggle to appreciate the originality of a highly novel pitch (Licuanan et al., 2007). We also are miscalibrated when selecting which solutions to implement. For example, Blair and Mumford (2007) put participants in decision-making roles for a fictional nonprofit and asked them to choose proposals they would fund. Participants passed over risky and more original approaches in favor of ones that provided quick results, aligned more with social norms, and were easy to understand. This preference for straightforward ideas suggests a role for fluency, or ease of processing. According to feelings-as-information theory, people often make complex judgments by asking 'How do I feel about this?' (Schwarz, 2012). As a result, information that is easy to understand inspires confidence (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973), is better liked (Labroo et al., 2008), feels familiar (Whittlesea, 1993), and seems truer (Fazio et al., 2015). Fluency also shapes our perceptions of beauty (Vogel et al., 2020), fame (Jacoby et al., 1989), ethics (Pillai et al., 2023), humor (Gorenz & Schwarz, 2026), and even our own knowledge (Speckmann & Unkelbach, 2024) and mastery of a subject (Miele & Molden, 2010). When solving tough problems, feelings of ease can make people overconfident. For instance, watching an uninformative video of a pilot landing a plane makes people surer that they could land one themselves, though this skill requires extensive training (Jordan et al., 2022). Similarly, reading brief survival tips (e.g., yell to bystanders) for "worst case scenarios" (e.g., fall onto subway tracks) inflates participants' feelings of preparation for these situations, suggesting that ease experienced during remembering (i.e., retrieval fluency) misleads us about our own abilities (Brashier et al., 2024).

One way to make information feel fluent is by repeating it. For example, repeating statements makes them seem truer than new statements (i.e., illusory truth, Hasher et al., 1977; see Brashier & Marsh, 2020, for a review); just a single previous exposure boosts credibility (Hassan & Barber, 2021). Repeatedly evaluating performance can also inflate judgments. As examples, judges awarded more favorable scores to performances in later seasons of Dancing with the Stars, and university professors assign higher grades after teaching the same course multiple times (O 'Connor & Cheema, 2018). Repetition might also increase perceived creativity -songs with more repetitive lyrics are more likely to top music charts because they are processed more fluently (Nunes et al., 2015). And products that we have seen before also seem more valuable (Greul et al., 2023).

By definition, people should deem novel ideas to be more creative than repeated ones; creativity reflects both novelty and usefulness (Runco & Jaeger, 2012; Diedrich et al., 2015). The current experiments test whether fluency encourages the opposite -ideas that have been encountered before could ironically seem more creative than new ones. Across four experiments (total N = 1002), participants were initially exposed to a set of ideas, then later judged the creativity of those same ideas as well as new ones. We investigated whether conflating fluency and creativity depends on depth of processing at exposure (Experiment 2), the specificity of judgments at test (Experiment 3), or the domain that ideas come from (Experiment 4).

Experiment 1 Method

Participants

One hundred and fifty-one Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) workers (50 women, M age = 43.57 years) participated for monetary compensation. An a priori power analysis determined that we would need 54 participants to detect a medium effect with 95% power. We preregistered and collected a larger sample to account for inattention and noise in online data collection.

Design

This study manipulated repetition within subjects.

Materials

We selected 50 uncommon uses for everyday objects (e.g., roll out dough with a wine bottle, see Appendix A). We divided these ideas into two sets of 25 items. One set repeated across the exposure and evaluation phases, whereas the other appeared for the first time at evaluation. Repetition was counterbalanced across participants.

Procedure

After giving informed consent, participants completed the exposure phase. They saw 25 uncommon uses for household objects and rated how easy it would be to carry out each action on a scale from 1 (very difficult) to 4 (very easy). Immediately after exposure, participants completed the evaluation phase. They evaluated the creativity of 50 uses for common objects on a scale from 1 (very obvious) to 4 (very creative). Half of the ideas had appeared at exposure and half of them were new. To ensure online data quality, we excluded and replaced participants who had duplicate IP addresses (n = 1 address), repeating geolocations (n = 11 locations), or provided a nonsense response to an open-ended question (n = 19 participants).

Results

The alpha level was set at .05 for all statistical tests. We conducted a paired-samples ttest to compare the creativity ratings of repeated and new ideas in the evaluation phase. The relevant data appear in Fig. 1. Contrary to our prediction, repeated life hacks (M = 2.81, SE = 0.03) were rated as less creative than new ones (M = 2.86, SE = 0.03), t(150) = 2.58, p = .011, 95% CI of the mean difference [0.01, 0.09]. This difference was small but significant (d = 0.21). represent standard error of the mean.

Discussion

After repeating some 'life hacks,' participants surprisingly judged them to be less creative than new ones. This pattern reverses typical effects of fluency, which generally inflates judgments in a positive direction (see Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009, and Reber et al., 2004, for reviews). However, asking participants to rate feasibility at exposure could have nudged them to consider a hack's usefulness initially. Since usefulness is a component of creativity, this could help them to reject repeated ideas later. For example, Brashier and colleagues (2020) found that focusing on accuracy when first encountering information reduces illusory truth later. They asked some participants to judge truth, rather than something unrelated like interest, at exposure; at test, people with this initial accuracy focus did not perceive repeated statements to be truer than new ones. Experiment 2 replicates our initial finding with a shallow exposure task.

Experiment 2 Method

Participants

One hundred and fifty Cloud Connect workers (72 women, M age = 35.57 years) participated for monetary compensation. An a priori power analysis determined that we would need 54 participants to detect a medium effect with 95% power. We preregistered and collected a larger sample to account for inattention and noise in online data collection.

Design

Like Experiment 1, repetition was manipulated within subjects.

Materials

We used the same 50 uncommon uses for everyday objects as Experiment 1 (see Appendix A).

Procedure

The procedure was identical to Experiment 1, except that participants made simple grammatical judgments at exposure. While reading each use for a household object, they indicated how many plural words the idea contained (from 0 to 3).

To ensure online data quality, we excluded and replaced participants who had duplicate IP addresses (n = 1 address), repeating geolocations (n = 1 location), or provided a nonsense or automated response to an open-ended question with hidden text (n = 6 participants). We added an initial attention check which all participants passed, and disabled copy and pasting for all questions except the final open-ended check to detect use of large language models.

Results

We conducted a paired-samples t-test to compare the creativity ratings of repeated and new ideas in the evaluation phase. The relevant data appear in standard error of the mean.

Discussion

Experiment 2 replicated our initial result under exposure conditions that encouraged shallow processing. After making low-level grammatical judgments, participants judged repeated 'life hacks' to be less creative than new ones. This effect is consistent but small (ds = 0.18 -0.21). In Experiment 3, we tested whether repetition has opposing effects on two components of creativity judgments: originality and effectiveness. People determine whether an idea is creative by evaluating both how original and useful it is, and typically prioritize effective solutions over original ones (Rietzschel et al., 2010). An idea that is easy to understand could seem more useful, but not necessarily more original.

Experiment 3

Participants

Three hundred Cloud Connect workers (169 women, M age = 42.57 years) participated for monetary compensation. An a priori power analysis determined we would need 216 participants to detect a small interaction between repetition and rating type with 95% power. Again, we preregistered and collected a larger sample to account for inattention and noise in online data collection.

Design

This study manipulated repetition (repeated, new) and rating type (originality, effectiveness) within subjects.

Materials

We used the same 50 uncommon uses for everyday objects as Experiments 1 and 2.

Procedure

The procedure was identical to Experiment 2, except that participants judged two distinct components of creativity separately. They rated both the originality and the effectiveness of each use for a common object on scales from 1 (very unoriginal) to 4 (very original) and 1 (very ineffective) to 4 (very effective).

We discouraged and detected automated and low-effort responses using the same means as Experiment 2. We excluded and replaced participants who had duplicate IP addresses (n = 3 addresses), repeating geolocations (n = 8 locations), failed an initial attention check (n = 3 participants), or provided a nonsense or automated response to an open-ended question with hidden text (n = 2 participants).

Discussion

In line with our prediction, participants judged repeated hacks to be more effective, but not more original, than new ones. Global perceptions of creativity seem to be greater than the sum of these parts, since neither perceived effectiveness nor originality decreased with repetition as in Experiment 1. Indeed, people weight these two components differently depending on the type of idea at hand; for example, usefulness matters more when judging urban planning projects than alternate uses of objects (Lloyd-Cox et al., 2022). In Experiment 4, we tested whether repetition decreases perceptions of creativity in another domain and compared them against ratings of truth, which fluency usually increases (see Ye et al., 2026, for a meta-analysis).

Participants repeatedly encountered literary quotes instead of life hacks, because they can feasibly be judged for both their creativity and truth.

Experiment 4 Method

Participants

Four hundred and one Cloud Connect workers (184 women, M age = 39.14 years) participated for monetary compensation. An a priori power analysis determined that we would need 328 participants to detect a small interaction between repetition and judgment type with 95% power. We preregistered and collected a larger sample to account for online data noise and inattention.

Design

This study had a 2 (repetition: repeated, new) x 2 (judgment: truth, creativity) mixed design. Repetition was manipulated within subjects, while judgment type was manipulated between subjects.

Materials

We selected 50 literary quotes that participants were unlikely to know, like The half-life of love is forever (see Appendix B). We divided these quotes into two sets of 25 items. One set repeated across the exposure and evaluation phases, whereas the other appeared for the first time at evaluation. Repetition was counterbalanced across participants.

Procedure

After giving informed consent, participants completed the exposure phase. Participants read 25 literary quotes and rated their sentiment on a scale from 1 (very negative) to 4 (very positive). Immediately after exposure, participants completed the evaluation phase. Participants rated 50 quotes for either creativity on a scale from 1 (very obvious) to 4 (very creative), or truth on a scale from 1 (definitely false) to 4 (definitely true). Half of the quotes had appeared at exposure and half of them were new.

We discouraged and detected automated and low-effort responses using the same means as Experiments 2 and 3. We excluded and replaced participants who failed an initial attention check (n = 4) or provided a nonsense or automated response to an open-ended question with hidden text (n = 15).

Results

We conducted a 2 (repetition: repeated, new) x 2 (judgment: truth, creativity) mixed ANOVA on participants' evaluations of quotes. The relevant data appear in Fig. 4. As expected, ratings of repeated quotes (M = 2.76) were significantly higher than new ones (M = 2.72), F(1, 399) = 8.27, p = .004, η² p = .02. Quotes received significantly higher truth (M = 2.88) than creativity (M = 2.60) ratings overall, F(1, 399) = 70.60, p < .001, η² p = .15. There was no significant interaction between repetition and judgment type, F(1, 399) = 0.19, p = 0.661.

We also conducted planned paired-samples t-tests to determine whether repetition inflated each kind of judgment. Contrary to our predictions, repeated quotes (M = 2.90, SE = 0.03) did not seem truer than new ones (M = 2.86, SE = 0.03), t(199) = 1.46, p = .147, 95% CI of the mean difference [-0.01, 0.09]. And unlike Experiment 1, repeated quotes (M = 2.63, SE = 0.02) seemed more creative than new ones (M = 2.58, SE = 0.02), t(200) = 3.02, p = .003, 95% CI of the mean difference [0.02, 0.08]. This effect was small but significant (d = 0.21).

General Discussion

We investigated whether fluency explains what we call the novelty penalty, where people reject inventive ideas. Initially we found the opposite; participants correctly judged repeated life hacks to be less creative than new ones. A later experiment revealed that repetition impacts two dissociable components of creativity independently. Fluency inflated participants' perceptions of a life hack's effectiveness but not its originality. When faced with more subjective literary quotes, participants judged repeated items as more creative, and they intriguingly did not exhibit illusory truth for these claims. This content is not goal-directed like life hacks are, potentially leaving people to consider their aesthetic appeal instead. Together, these results suggest that participants used feelings of fluency flexibly when judging creativity, depending on the context.

More broadly, people use heuristics according to their ecological validity (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011), relying on a 'fast and frugal' shortcut like fluency only when it outperforms using more information. We learn the meanings of heuristics from statistical relationships in the world around us. For example, people eventually learn that fluency correlates with truth because we are more likely to hear the one true version of a fact (e.g., Neil Armstrong was the first person to walk on the moon) than any of its many possible falsifications (e.g., Buzz Aldrin or Yuri Gagarin). Crucially, these relationships can be updated with experience. While items presented in high contrast typically seem familiar because they are easy to read, Unkelbach (2006) reversed this heuristic with feedback. Through trial-by-trial learning, participants shifted to indicating that low-contrast names had already been presented. This pattern generalizes beyond recognition to truth, with participants successfully learning to reinterpret fluent items as false (Unkelbach, 2007). In the current experiments, fluency did not inevitably lead to more positive evaluations of ideas, perhaps because people have not learned a clear relationship between creativity and easy processing.

Even when we have learned a given correlation, the source of fluency matters. People may rely on conceptual, but not perceptual, fluency. For instance, repetition makes a bad deed seem less immoral (Effron, 2022), but presenting the same actions in higher contrast has no effect (Nadarevic & Kroneisen, 2020). Fluency and familiarity can even be spontaneously discounted when people intentionally disregard easy processing or prior experience as irrelevant (Oppenheimer & Monin, 2009). As examples, people can ignore stark 'printing errors' (i.e., perceptual disfluency) when deciding the merit of a college admissions essay (Oppenheimer, 2006), disregard the salience (i.e., availability) of famous names when estimating their frequency (Oppenheimer, 2004), and dismiss their ability to recognize the name of a city when judging its size (Oppenheimer, 2003), all when they can attend to a more obvious cue instead. Our results suggest that people can make accurate global creativity judgments in the face of fluency, but this may depend on explicitly remembering that they encountered an idea before.

Literary quotes like 'Grief can be a burden but also an anchor' seemed truer with repetition, potentially because they are less tangible and memorable than life hacks like 'pit cherries with a drinking straw.' Indeed, recollection increases with information's concreteness.

As examples, concrete noun pairs (e.g., diamond/ring; Paivio et al., 1994), adjective-noun pairs (e.g., flaming/forest; Begg, 1972), and short texts (Sadoski et al., 2000) are all better remembered than abstract ones. Participants likely recalled reading life hacks like 'rolling out dough with a wine bottle' when they saw them a second time. Literary quotes are more abstract, potentially leaving people unable to discount in the absence of explicit recollection later. This content is also more subjective, which reduces the influence of prior exposure. Repeating socialpolitical opinions like 'Election Day should be a national holiday' does not make them seem more credible (Riesthuis & Woods, 2026). Our result identifies another domain in which subjectivity reduces illusory truth.

While fluency is generally an automatic, positive experience that inflates judgments (Winkielman & Cacioppo, 2001), it can also amplify existing judgmental biases in either a positive or negative direction (Landwehr & Eckmann, 2020). For instance, while people like abstract patterns the longer they look at them, they ironically like faces less the longer they see them, even though extended exposure increases fluency (Gerger et al., 2017). We demonstrated that people use feelings of ease situationally rather than habitually when judging creativity, with small effects of fluency reversing in some contexts. Our work complements the finding that intuitive assessments of creativity outperform more deliberate ones (Zhu et al., 2017) and suggests that transformative ideas are not devalued due to 'fast and frugal' heuristics. If anything, people recognize that an idea that is easy to understand is not necessarily more inventive, so long as it serves a tangible goal.
