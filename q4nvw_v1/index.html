To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The RetractionWatch Check module compares the reference list against studies in the RetractionWatch database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that the study was retracted, has an expression of concern, or a correction.

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the authors are already aware that a study was retracted, but the module can’t evaluate this.

The database can be manually updated with the rw_update function. For more information, see https://gitlab.com/crossref/retraction-watch-data.

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. No reminder is given when the paper already cites that replication or reproduction (matched by its DOI).

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the original study was cited for other reasons than the empirical claim tested, or that the replication/reproduction in the FLoRA database is for only one of the studies in the paper, and not the study the authors discuss.

The database can be manually updated with the FLoRA_update() function. For more information, see https://forrt.org/FLoRA/.

Abstract

Accumulating evidence suggests associations between ADHD, negative body image, and disordered eating; however, relatively little is known about how body image has been conceptualised, assessed, and experienced. This mixed-methods scoping review aimed to map and synthesise existing research examining body image in relation to ADHD and ADHD traits. Seven databases (Embase, CENTRAL, CINAHL, MEDLINE, PubMed, PsycINFO, and Scopus) and two grey literature sources (PsyArXiv and ProQuest) were searched from inception to September 2026. Data were charted iteratively and synthesised descriptively and narratively. Twenty-seven studies (25 quantitative, two qualitative) published between 1995 and 2026 met the inclusion criteria. All studies were based in Westernised contexts, with most employing cross-sectional designs. ADHD was predominantly assessed using symptom-based measures and/or diagnostic status, while body image was most commonly assessed using subscales of the Eating Disorder Inventory. Quantitative studies primarily examined associations between ADHD and negative body image, with some evidence of greater negative body image in ADHD. However, findings were inconsistent across studies, particularly following adjustment for covariates (e.g., co-occurring psychopathology). The literature predominantly adopted pathology-oriented conceptualisations of body image, with no studies examining positive body image and limited exploration of lived experiences. Further gaps were identified in methodological approaches, the conceptualisation and measurement of body image and ADHD, and the representation of diverse populations. Reliance on predominantly neurotypical-derived conceptualisations and measurement tools not validated among ADHD samples raises concerns about their validity. Future research should prioritise participatory approaches to advance an inclusive, neuro-affirming understanding of body image among the ADHD population.

1. Introduction

Body image is a multidimensional, multifaceted construct that refers to an individual's attitudes, perceptions, and cognitions concerning how their body looks, feels, and functions, alongside how they treat their body (Cash & Smolak, 2011). Historically, body image research predominantly adopted a pathology-oriented perspective, with body image disturbance as a core feature of eating disorders (Bruch, 1978). Consequently, constructs such as body dissatisfaction, appearance preoccupation, and drive for thinness were frequently investigated in relation to disordered eating symptomatology (Garner et al., 1983; Grogan, 2021; Stice & Shaw, 2002). This focus is understandable, given prospective evidence that body dissatisfaction is a precursor of eating disorder symptomatology and adverse physical and psychological outcomes, more broadly (Bornioli et al., 2019 (Bornioli et al., , 2021;; Costantini et al., 2026; Stice, 2002; Stice & Shaw, 2002). However, scholarly understandings of body image have advanced considerably over the past two decades, with increasing recognition of its multidimensional nature, encompassing both negative and positive aspects that are distinct from one another (Tylka, 2018; Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015).

Alongside these developments, body image scholars have increasingly sought to recognise the diverse ways body image is conceptualised and experienced among populations historically underrepresented in research (Swami & Tylka, 2026). Although body image experiences share commonalities across diverse populations (e.g., body dissatisfaction, body acceptance), growing evidence suggests nuances in how individuals across different social identities and sociocultural contexts understand and relate to certain aspects of their bodies (Swami & Tylka, 2026; see also Tiggemann, 2015). One population receiving growing attention in this regard is the neurodivergent community, specifically people with ADHD 1 (for a discussion, see Longhurst, 2026).

1 Throughout this review, we refer to ADHD but avoid its full diagnostic expansion due to its deficit-oriented framing and medicalised origins (French et al., 2025). We also adopt personfirst language (e.g., "person with ADHD"), reflecting emerging evidence of terminology preferences among the ADHD population (Cooper et al., 2026).

ADHD is a form of neurodivergence characterised by differences in attention, activity, and impulse regulation, which can vary considerably across individuals (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; see also Campbell, 2020; van der Oord et al., 2026). Indeed, ADHD is increasingly conceptualised dimensionally, with traits distributed across the general population rather than present only among those meeting diagnostic thresholds (e.g., Larsson et al., 2013; Salum et al., 2014; Wootton et al., 2022). Moreover, many people with ADHD remain undiagnosed or are diagnosed late, particularly women and girls, whose presentations are often less externalised and more likely to be overlooked (e.g., Attoe & Climie, 2023; Babinski, 2024). Research examining ADHD traits may therefore capture experiences that diagnosis-based research alone would miss. Beyond differences in attention, activity, and impulse regulation, people with ADHD may also experience differences in sensory and perceptual processing (e.g., interoceptive awareness) and emotional regulation, which are described as shaping how they perceive, experience, and relate to their bodies (Longhurst, 2026; Longhurst et al., 2026; Makin, Meyer, et al., 2025; Makin, Zesch, et al., 2025b).

Existing research has identified potentially distinctive underlying processes that may shape people with ADHD's body image. For example, systematic reviews have identified associations between ADHD and disordered eating, such as linking impulsivity and rewardseeking with bingeing and purging, inattention with reduced hunger and satiety awareness, and, among men, hyperactivity with overexercising (Auch et al., 2025; Kaisari et al., 2017; Makin et al., 2025a Makin et al., , 2025b;; Ptacek et al., 2016). Beyond these individual-level factors, research has documented stigma, anticipated discrimination, and negative social evaluations among people with ADHD (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026; Makin et al., 2025a). The minority stress model (Meyer, 2003), which has been extended to autistic people (e.g., Botha & Frost, 2020), may therefore offer a useful lens for understanding body image concerns among people with ADHD, particularly where individuals encounter culturally prescribed appearance ideals alongside neuronormative expectations concerning how people experience the body and its functional aspects (Longhurst et al., 2026). Finally, a substantive body of literature examining the association between ADHD and weight raises questions about how weight stigma may shape body image among some people with ADHD (e.g., Cortese, 2019).

Although still nascent, research concerning ADHD and body image-related experiences has predominantly been situated within medicalised and pathology-oriented frameworks (for a discussion, see Cameron, 2024). Such approaches may provide only a partial understanding of body image among people with ADHD, particularly where attention is directed towards negative body image and disordered eating, rather than the broader ways people with ADHD experience body image from a holistic and adaptive point of view (Longhurst, 2026; Tylka, 2018). This distinction is important as positive body image represents a distinct dimension of body image that extends beyond the absence of negative body image (Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015). Positive body image is broadly defined as "the overarching love and respect for the body" (Wood-Barcalow et al., 2010, p. 112) and involves appreciating, respecting, and expressing gratitude toward one's body (Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015). A substantive body of research has associated positive body image with greater physical and psychological well-being, over and above negative body image (for a review, see Linardon et al., 2022). Emerging research among certain neurodivergent populations, particularly autistic and AuDHD (autistic and ADHD) people, has begun to explore how positive body image may be conceptualised and experienced in ways that are specific to their neurodivergent identities (Longhurst, 2026). For example, qualitative research has highlighted the relevance of sensory experiences, masking, and (neuro)affirming relationships and social contexts in shaping how autistic/AuDHD people relate to their bodies and eating (Longhurst et al., 2023 (Longhurst et al., , 2026)). However, the extent to which these experiences have been considered within the ADHD population remains unclear.

Taken together, these developments highlight important uncertainties concerning the existing literature on ADHD and body image. Although research has increasingly examined associations between ADHD and disordered eating, comparatively little is known about how body image itself has been conceptualised, assessed, and experienced in relation to ADHD.

More specifically, it remains unclear to what extent body image has received scholarly attention in this population, or how body image experiences may differ among people with ADHD. Moreover, the extent to which research has considered both negative and positive dimensions of body image, including the perspectives of people with ADHD themselves, has yet to be systematically established. To our knowledge, no review has yet synthesised this literature; existing reviews have focused on ADHD in relation to disordered eating (e.g., Kaisari et al., 2017), while body image has been reviewed only among autistic people (Longhurst, 2023). Mapping the existing literature is therefore an important step towards identifying conceptual and methodological gaps and informing more inclusive, neurodiversity-affirming approaches to body image research.

1.1. The present study

To address these gaps, the present study aimed to provide the first systematic mapping and synthesis of existing evidence on body image in relation to ADHD and ADHD traits, to better orient further research in this area. As work on body image within the context of ADHD, and the wider neurodivergent population, is still emerging (Longhurst, 2026), a scoping review was considered most appropriate. This approach accommodates diverse study designs, populations, and ways of conceptualising and investigating body image, and enables the identification of gaps in the literature, without restricting the synthesis to narrowly defined criteria (Arksey & O'Malley, 2005; Colquhoun et al., 2014; Levac et al., 2010).

2. Methods

The current scoping review was designed and conducted according to the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2015; 2020; see also Colquhoun et al., 2014) and reported in accordance with the Preferred Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR; McGowen et al., 2020; Tricco et al., 2018). A review protocol was registered with OSF Registries on October 1 st , 2026: https://osf.io/z6emj.

2.1. Author positionality

Although positionality statements are not typically included in recommended practice for scoping reviews, we recognise that researchers are not neutral and that knowledge production is neither value-free nor untied from social and cultural ideologies (Nagel, 1989; Teo, 2010). We further challenge neuronormative assumptions of objectivity and recognise lived experience as a valuable source of knowledge, rather than positioning people with ADHD solely as subjects of research (Bertilsdotter Rosqvist et al., 2025; Botha, 2021).

Accordingly, we sought to remain reflexive about how our identities and theoretical perspectives informed our research processes and decision-making (Finlay, 2002; Lather, 1993 Lather, , 2001)).

All four authors are based in the United Kingdom (UK), identify as cisgender women and were in their late twenties to late thirties at the time of the review. Three authors identify as White, while one identifies with a minoritised racial identity within the context of the UK.

All authors identify as having ADHD, encompassing both formal diagnosis and selfidentification, with PL also identifying as autistic. PL, FT, and HLS also have lived experience of an eating disorder or disordered eating and negative body image, alongside experiences of cultivating a more positive body image. LM's understanding of body image is informed by previous experiences of disordered eating and negative body image, as well as her experience as a queer, masculine-presenting woman, which have shaped her relationship with appearance, attractiveness, and desirability.

These intersecting positionalities have informed our feminist and neurodiversityaffirming approach to understanding ADHD (for a discussion, see Huijg, 2026). Specifically, we recognise the historical marginalisation and under-recognition of women and girls within ADHD research, assessment, and diagnostic practices (for reviews, see Attoe & Climie, 2023; Babinski & Libsack, 2025), and challenge deficit-oriented conceptualisations of neurodivergence (Botha, 2021; see also Kapp, 2020). Taken together, our positionalities reflect the duality of knowing through lived experience and observing the experiences of others, while recognising how our own perspectives and biases shape the processes of meaning-making (Corbin Dwyer & Buckle, 2009).

2.2. Identifying the review question

Following the JBI methodology, this review addressed a clear scoping review question using the "PCC" mnemonic (population, concept, and context; Peters et al., 2020).

The population comprised individuals of any age with ADHD (self-reported via measurement, self-identified, or formally diagnosed), or studies that explicitly assessed ADHD symptoms or traits. Body image was the main concept, defined as a multidimensional (i.e., having negative and positive dimensions), multifaceted construct that encompasses one's attitudes, perceptions, and cognitions concerning how their body looks, feels, and functions, alongside how they treat their body (Cash & Smolak, 2011; Tylka, 2018). No restrictions were placed on context, including setting, country, or socio-cultural context.

The overarching research question of this review is: What is known about body image in relation to ADHD or ADHD traits across different populations and contexts? In addition, we sought to address the following questions: (1) What are the characteristics of the existing research (e.g., study design, sample population, and setting)? (2) How has body image been conceptualised and assessed in extant research? (3) How do people with ADHD describe and experience their body image, including how these experiences shape their behaviours and attitudes? (4) What is known about the relationships between ADHD or ADHD traits and different dimensions of body image?

2.3. Eligibility criteria

Both academic and grey literature sources were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) included individuals of any age with formally diagnosed, self-reported (using psychometric measures), or self-identified ADHD, or individuals from general population samples in which ADHD symptoms or traits were explicitly assessed; (2) either (a) quantitatively assessed at least one body-image-related construct and examined its relationship with ADHD diagnoses, symptoms, or traits, or (b) qualitatively explored body image or related bodily experiences in relation to ADHD; and (3) employed an empirical quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods research design. For qualitative studies, body image was required to constitute an explicit focus of the research aims, data collection, or analysis, or emerge as a substantive theme or finding within a broader qualitative investigation. Studies employing multidimensional eating-disorder measures were eligible where body-image-related dimensions (e.g., body dissatisfaction, shape concern, or weight concern) were separately analysed or substantively interpreted in relation to ADHD, rather than reported solely as part of a global eating-disorder symptom score. ADHD could be assessed using multidimensional measures or measures of specific symptom dimensions (e.g., inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity), and clinical verification of ADHD was not required.

Studies were excluded if they: (1) assessed broader constructs associated with ADHD (e.g., impulsivity or executive functioning) without explicitly identifying or assessing ADHD diagnoses, symptoms, or traits; (2) examined ADHD and body image independently, without analysing or substantively interpreting their relationship; (3) included mixed neurodivergent or neurodevelopmental samples without identifiable or extractable ADHDspecific body image findings; (4) mentioned ADHD and body image-related experiences only incidentally, without substantive analysis or interpretation; (5) examined constructs such as general self-esteem or self-confidence without an identifiable body image-related component; (6) focused on body schema or body awareness (e.g., interoception, proprioception, or body size estimation), without substantively examining body image in relation to ADHD; (7) focused exclusively on body mass index (BMI), body weight, "ob*sity", body composition, weight management, or other anthropometric or biomedical characteristics without assessing subjective body image; (8) were non-empirical publications, including reviews, editorials, commentaries, and opinion pieces; or (9) comprised grey literature with insufficient methodological detail or outcome data to support meaningful data extraction. No Language or date restrictions were applied during the search process.

2.4. Identifying relevant sources

To identify relevant sources, both academic and grey literature search strategies were used. First, a systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Embase (via Ovid), MEDLINE (via Ovid), APA PsycInfo (via EBSCO), CINAHL (via EBSCO), and Scopus to identify peer-reviewed literature (see Supplementary Materials). Given the anticipated availability of interventionist research in the context of ADHD, we further searched the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) to identify sources such as randomised controlled trials, feasibility and pilot studies, and trial reports containing evidence. Second, searches were conducted on ProQuest and PsyArXiv via OSF to identify grey literature, such as dissertations and theses, conference papers and proceedings, and preprints. Scopus was also searched to identify relevant conference papers and proceedings. In addition, the reference lists of pertinent review papers and eligible studies were screened manually to identify additional articles meeting the inclusion criteria.

Searches were conducted for studies published from database inception to 28 September 2026, with no language restrictions applied.

To ensure a comprehensive and targeted search, Boolean operators and truncation were used to combine search terms reflecting PCC domains: the population (ADHD) and concept (body image). The complete search string was as follows: ("attention deficit hyperactivity disorder" OR "ADHD" OR "ADD" OR "attention deficit disorder" OR "ADHD trait*" OR "ADHD symptom*" OR "attention deficit symptom*" OR "attention deficit trait*") AND ("body image" OR "body dissatisfaction" OR "body satisfaction" OR "body appreciation" OR "body esteem" OR "body confidence" OR "body acceptance" OR "body shame" OR "body surveillance" OR "body evaluation" OR "body compassion" OR "body comparison" OR "appearance evaluation" OR "appearance distress" OR "appearance concern*" OR "appearance anxiety" OR "appearance investment" OR "appearance shame" OR "appearance satisfaction" OR "appearance dissatisfaction" OR "appearance confidence" OR "shape concern*" OR "shape satisfaction" OR "shape dissatisfaction" OR "weight concern*" OR "weight dissatisfaction" OR "weight satisfaction" OR "weight and shape concern*" OR "shape and weight concern*" OR "function* satisfaction" OR "function* dissatisfaction" OR "functionality appreciation" OR "body functionality"). Search terms were adapted for each database to accommodate differences in indexing, search fields, and syntax, with searches restricted to titles, abstracts, and keywords.

2.5. Selecting studies for inclusion

All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan, a web-based screening tool, where duplicates were removed prior to screening. Pilot screening of 25 sources was conducted by three authors (PL, FT, LM) to assess consistency in the application of the eligibility criteria, with pairwise percentage agreement meeting 80%. Discrepancies were discussed to identify and resolve differences in the interpretation of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Titles and abstracts were then independently screened by two reviewers, with PL screening all records and FT or LM acting as the second reviewer, such that each record received two independent screening decisions. Next, prior to full-text screening, a percentage of full-text sources were pilot screened (20%; k = 12) by the second reviewers, with pairwise agreement exceeding 80% (83.3%). Full texts of potentially eligible sources were then independently screened by PL and cross-referenced by a second reviewer (FT or LM). The reviewers discussed the criteria regularly to clarify sources that warranted further interpretation or consideration for inclusion or exclusion. At both stages, discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, where required, reviewed by a third reviewer. The study selection process followed PRISMA-ScR recommendations, with a record of all inclusion and exclusion decisions maintained for transparency (McGowen et al., 2020; Tricco et al., 2018).

2.6. Data charting process

Data were charted (i.e., extracted) using a standardised data charting form developed in accordance with the JBI guidance for scoping reviews and informed by the review questions and eligibility criteria. In addition, the form was designed iteratively, with input from all reviewers to ensure comprehensiveness and consistency. Data charting was conducted by one reviewer (PL) and independently checked by a second reviewer (FT, LM, HLS), with discrepancies resolved through discussion. The following information was charted from each study: (1) study details: author(s), year of publication, country, study design, and study aims; (2) sample characteristics: sample size, age, sex/gender identity, racialised status/ethnicity, and ADHD identification; (3) measures used to assess ADHD symptoms or traits and body image; and (4) key findings concerning body image in relation to ADHD (e.g., quantitative associations or qualitative themes). Due to the lack of specification across included studies, we use sex/gender identity as a combined concept. This may be explained by changes in attitudes and awareness toward differentiation between sex and gender identity within research (Quintana & Pfaus, 2024). For non-English studies (n = 4), generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT) were used to translate papers during the screening and extraction processes.

Extracted data were synthesised using a narrative and descriptive mapping approach. Study characteristics, including methodological approaches, sample characteristics, and ADHD and body image conceptualisation and assessment, were summarised descriptively. Quantitative findings were synthesised narratively, with studies grouped according to study design and objectives. Qualitative findings were summarised descriptively to capture participants' reported experiences of body image.

Findings across both methodological approaches were subsequently considered together to identify patterns, inconsistencies, and gaps in the existing evidence base. In line with the exploratory aims of a scoping review (Arksey & O'Malley, 2005; Peters et al., 2015 Peters et al., , 2020)), no formal quality assessment of the included studies was undertaken. Instead, emphasis was placed on mapping the breadth of available evidence and identifying gaps to inform future research.

3. Results

A total of 2,223 records were identified through searching databases as of 28 September 2025. A further 10 sources were identified via citation searching of relevant literature. Following the removal of duplicates, 1,343 unique records remained for screening. Titles and abstracts were reviewed for eligibility, resulting in 70 articles being retrieved for full-text review (see Fig. 1). After full-text screening, a total of 27 studies were included in the current review.

3.1. Characteristics of included studies

Of the included studies, 17 were cross-sectional, six longitudinal, two interventionist, and two qualitative. Studies were published between 1995 and 2026 and included a total of N = 35,403 participants 2 . Sample sizes varied substantially, ranging from 6 to 6,844 participants across all studies. All studies were conducted in Western contexts, wherein the majority of studies were conducted in the US (k = 10) and published in English (k = 23), with only four studies published in Spanish (see Table 1 for a full overview).

3.1.1. Demographic characteristics of included studies

Participants ranged in age between 6 and 92 years, with 15 of the quantitative studies (60%) focusing on or involving children and/or adolescents. Gender identity or sex composition varied considerably across studies: four included exclusively female participants and one study included exclusively male participants. Based on the demographic information reported, 17,334 participants were classified as female, 12,288 as male, and 38 within other sex/gender identity categories (i.e., "other", non-binary, or intersex; de Vries et al., 2019; Giordano et al., 2015; Goldschlager et al., 2026; see also Janus et al., 2026). Sex/gender identity was unavailable or could not be reconciled with the reported sample sizes for the remaining 5,744 participants. 3.2.1. Between-group differences in body image

Studies comparing body image across ADHD and comparison groups reported mixed findings, although several identified greater body image-related concerns among individuals with ADHD, particularly in larger community-based and longitudinal samples (see Table 2). In a five-year prospective study of 228 girls (ADHD-combined, n = 93; ADHDinattentive, n = 47; without ADHD, n = 88), Mikami et al. (2008) found that those with a childhood ADHD-combined presentation reported greater adolescent desire to lose weight, assessed using a composite of body dissatisfaction, drive for thinness, and perceived-ideal body-size discrepancy, than girls without ADHD (p <.01, d = 0.63). However, girls with predominantly inattentive ADHD did not significantly differ from either group. Childhood impulsivity also predicted greater desire to lose weight when controlling for inattention and hyperactivity symptoms (partial r = .17), and peer rejection predicted greater desire to lose weight after accounting for ADHD diagnosis (β = .23).

In a later prospective longitudinal cohort study of 696 adolescents (with ADHD, n = 432, Mikami et al. (2010) found that adolescents with ADHD reported significantly greater body dissatisfaction than adolescents without ADHD (p = .040, d = 0.21), although the effect size was small. There was no significant ADHD-by-sex interaction (p = .320). Furthermore, childhood impulsivity, but not inattention or hyperactivity, uniquely predicted subsequent body dissatisfaction. Similarly, in a retrospective longitudinal cohort study, Bisset et al. (2019) found that children with elevated ADHD symptoms had greater odds of reporting body dissatisfaction at ages 8-9 (OR = 1.5, p = .003, 95% CI [1.0, 2.2]) and 12-13 years (OR = 1.7, p = .02), 95% CI [1.2, 2.4]), but not at 10-11 years (OR = 1.3, p = .150, 95% CI [0.9, 1.9]), after adjustment for BMI, gender, and maternal education, suggesting that the relationship between ADHD symptoms and body dissatisfaction may vary across developmental periods. Two recent cross-sectional studies also identified elevated body image-related concerns among individuals with ADHD. In a US sample of 13,844 children aged 6-17 years (children with ADHD, n = 4,819; without ADHD, n = 9,025), Limbers et al. (2026) found that caregivers reported concerns regarding weight, body shape, or body size more frequently for children with ADHD than those without ADHD (p < .001), regardless of medication status. Furthermore, 34.1% of children with ADHD taking medication and 34.4% of those not taking medication were reported to have at least some body image concerns, compared with 20.6% without ADHD. (2019) identified ADHD in 1 of 17 adolescents with BDD (5.9%), compared with 7 of 28 adolescents without BDD (25.0%), although the small sample size (n = 45) limits statistically meaningful comparison.

Two studies compared body image-related concerns across neurodevelopmental characteristics or medication-use groups. Karjalainen et al. (2016) examined eating pathology among 228 Swedish adults diagnosed with ADHD (n = 109), autism (n = 74), or co-occurring ADHD and autism (n = 45). While there were no significant differences in overall eating disorder pathology, autistic adults presented lower odds of endorsing a single EAT-26 item concerning preoccupation with body fat compared with those with ADHD (OR = 0.49 [95% CI = 0.26, 0.92], p = .026), after adjusting for education and age. Meanwhile, Giordano et al. (2015) examined appearance-contingent self-worth among 3,038 US undergraduate students, comparing groups according to whether they reported having a prescription for ADHD medication. 4 Appearance-contingent self-worth scores were descriptively higher among participants reporting stimulant misuse (M = 26.32) than nonusers (M = 24.62) and those using prescribed medication appropriately (M = 24.87).

Appearance-contingent self-worth contributed to differentiating medication-use groups, although the overall discriminant effect was small (R² = 4.3%).

3.2.2. Associations between ADHD and body image

Across ten studies (see Table 2), findings concerning associations between ADHD and body image were mixed. Nine studies identified at least one statistically significant association between ADHD or ADHD-related characteristics and body image, although the magnitude and direction of these associations varied. More specifically, findings differed according to ADHD symptom dimension, sex, or developmental period.

Notably, several associations were no longer significant after adjusting for relevant covariates, including demographic characteristics (e.g., age, BMI), parent-/teacher-reported impulsivity, childhood predictors, and eating disorder-related symptomatology.

Population-based and longitudinal studies provided evidence of associations between ADHD and body dissatisfaction, including differences according to sex and ADHD presentation (Ek et al., 2008; Neumark-Sztainer et al., 1995; Yao et al., 2019; Yilmaz et al., 2017 Yilmaz et al., , 2023)). More specifically, in a cross-sectional, population-based study of 3,530 adolescents aged 12-20 years in the United States, Neumark-Sztainer et al. (1995) found that participants with ADD (n = 689) had significantly greater odds of weight dissatisfaction than adolescents without a chronic illness, among both girls and boys. ADD was also associated with greater concern about body development across sexes, although lower body pride was significantly associated with ADD only among girls. These associations were identified after adjusting for age, race, socioeconomic status, and BMI.

Among 94 Swedish children (aged 10-11 years; 61 boys, 33 girls) with ADHD, subthreshold ADHD, and milder attention, behavioural or learning difficulties, Ek et al. (2008) found that higher teacher-rated impulsive-restless behaviour (r = -.31) and emotional lability (r = -.25) were associated with less favourable physical appearance self-perceptions.

Similarly, Baraskewich and Climie (2022) examined associations between different ADHD symptom dimensions and body dissatisfaction and drive for thinness. In unadjusted analyses, both body image outcomes were positively associated with inattentive symptoms (rs = .22-.24) and overall ADHD symptom severity (rs = .30-.32), but not hyperactive/impulsive symptoms. However, when accounting for age, gender, and inattentive symptoms, a significant negative association emerged between hyperactive/impulsive symptoms and body dissatisfaction (β = -.26), suggesting that associations may differ depending on the extent to which overlapping ADHD symptom dimensions are accounted for. In a separate multiple regression analysis examining overall ADHD symptom severity as the outcome, neither body dissatisfaction nor drive for thinness contributed significant unique variance when entered alongside bulimia symptoms.

Longitudinal studies provide further, albeit mixed, evidence concerning associations between ADHD and body dissatisfaction and drive for thinness, respectively.

Using growth mixture modelling, Yilmaz et al. (2017) identified four distinct symptom trajectories among 2,315 participants from the Swedish Twin Study of Child and Adolescent Development, assessed across three waves spanning approximately eight years.

Adolescents with persistently elevated inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity reported greater body dissatisfaction and drive for thinness at ages 16-17 years than those with consistently low symptoms (p < .01), including after adjustment for BMI or anxiety/depression symptoms. However, predominantly inattentive and hyperactive/impulsive trajectories were not significantly associated with body dissatisfaction.

Similarly, in a prospective longitudinal community cohort, Bufferd et al. (2022) found that ADHD diagnosis at age 6 was weakly associated with greater body dissatisfaction at age 12 (r = .12, p < .05), but not at age 15. However, the association at age 12 was no longer statistically significant following adjustment for childhood demographic, temperament, and psychopathology factors.

Cross-sectional analyses of longitudinal twin cohorts also identified associations between ADHD-related characteristics and body Image. In an overlapping cohort (N = 1,575) using genetic modelling, Yilmaz et al. (2023) found positive correlations between ADHDrelated symptom dimensions and body dissatisfaction (rs = .11-.29), alongside genetic correlations involving cognitive/inattention problems among girls (rₐ = .41) and boys (rₐ = .35). Similarly, Yao et al. (2019) found that greater ADHD polygenic risk was associated with body dissatisfaction (b = .042) and drive for thinness (b = .032) among 5,679 adolescents; however, these associations explained only 0.13% and 0.10% of the variance, respectively.

Studies involving smaller, clinical populations (i.e., those receiving ED treatment), further report mixed findings. Among 191 women with eating disorders in Spain (136 women receiving treatment for eating disorders in Spain, Fernández-Aranda et al. (2013) found that neither body dissatisfaction (p = .286) nor drive for thinness (p = .476) was significantly associated with ADHD symptom severity once adjusting for age, BMI, and other eating disorder symptomatology. However, in a subsample of the same cohort (N = 136; screened positive for ADHD, n = 46), Testa et al. (2020) found positive correlations between body dissatisfaction and overall ADHD symptoms (r = .42), inattention (r = .36), and hyperactivity (r = .282). These findings were consistent across diagnostic subgroups, with no significant associations observed among participants with anorexia nervosa or those with bulimia nervosa, eating disorder not otherwise specified, or binge eating disorder (all ps ≥ .230).

Such findings further suggest that differences in analytical procedures, including the adjustment for eating disorder symptomatology, may partly account for the contrasting results.

Finally, de Vries et al. (2019) examined associations between body satisfaction and ADHD symptoms among 1,022 individuals with differences of sex development across six European countries. In adjusted regression analyses, sex-neutral body characteristics were associated with ADHD symptoms among participants with Turner syndrome (p <.05), while genital and secondary sex-characteristic satisfaction were associated with ADHD symptoms among female participants with XY-DSD without androgenization (p <.05). Body image dimensions were also identified as significant individual predictors among female participants with partially androgenized XY-DSD and male participants with XY-DSD (both ps <.05), although the overall regression models for these groups were not statistically significant.

3.2.3. Body image as a mediator, moderator, or intervention outcome

A total of four studies examined body image as a mediator, moderator, or intervention outcome in the context of ADHD. Two cross-sectional studies investigated the mediating or moderating role of body image outcomes in associations between ADHD and psychological or behavioural outcomes. Among 220 children aged 7-12 years with higher weight, recruited to a weight management programme, Gowey et al. (2017) found that body dissatisfaction significantly moderated the association between ADHD symptoms and disordered eating (B = 0.16, p = .01), with stronger associations observed at higher levels of body dissatisfaction. The interaction accounted for an additional 2.51% of the variance in disordered eating. Consistent with these findings, Van Eck et al. (2018) examined associations between ADHD symptoms and five dimensions of body image among 627 university students in the United States. Greater ADHD symptom severity was significantly associated with more negative appearance evaluation (β = .117, p = .005), greater overweight preoccupation (β = .148, p < .001), and greater body-area dissatisfaction (β = .098, p = .021), after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics. Furthermore, negative appearance evaluation, overweight preoccupation, and body-area dissatisfaction significantly mediated the association between ADHD symptoms and depressive symptoms (standardised indirect effects = .032, .022, and .028, respectively), whereas appearance orientation and self-classified weight did not.

Two intervention studies evaluated body image as an intervention outcome among children with ADHD. Among 65 preadolescents with ADD/ADHD, Frame et al. (2003) evaluated a four-week, school-based support-group intervention that aimed to enhance selfperceptions across six domains, including self-perceived physical appearance. Following the intervention, participants receiving the support group reported significantly more favourable perceptions of their physical appearance than those in the control group, after adjusting for baseline scores (p = .001; no effect size reported). In a smaller, single-group, pre-post study, López Sánchez et al. (2015) investigated changes in body-size dissatisfaction following a 12-week physical activity programme among 12 boys aged 7-12 years with diagnosed ADHD. Although a statistically significant change in body-size discrepancy was observed (p = .017), changes in body-size preferences were mixed. Specifically, the proportion of participants satisfied with their body size increased from 8.3% to 41.7%, while the proportion wishing to be thinner also increased from 33.3% to 50.0%. The increase in body-size satisfaction therefore appeared to reflect a reduction in the proportion wishing to be larger, rather than a uniform improvement in body image. Furthermore, the small sample and absence of a control group limit conclusions regarding intervention efficacy.

3.2.4. Qualitative experiences of body image among people with ADHD

Two qualitative studies referred to body image-related experiences among adults with ADHD, highlighting how negative body image intersected with eating behaviours, internalised stigma, and self-perception. Using a hermeneutic-phenomenological approach, Zwennes and Loth (2019) explored the lived experiences of six adults (female, n = 4; male, n = 2; aged 23-41 years) from the Netherlands, all of whom have been diagnosed with ADHD or ADD, a circadian sleep-wake disorder, and binge eating disorder or "ob*sity".

Negative body image emerged as an explicit qualitative theme, with participants describing longstanding negative perceptions of themselves and their bodies that were intertwined with eating behaviours, weight-related concerns, and difficulties managing everyday routines.

Participants described turning to food for emotional comfort, with one explaining that they sought comfort in food during periods of negative thinking -"Whenever I feel uncomfortable in my skin, I make unhealthy food choices to gain instant comfort" (p. 189). These experiences were often accompanied by recurring feelings of shame, guilt, and perceived failure, which the authors conceptualised as part of an interconnected and cyclical relationship between ADHD-related difficulties and negative self-perceptions and bodyrelated distress.

Extending these findings to 15 adults (12 female, 1 male, 1 non-binary; aged 18-46 years) with co-occurring ADHD and anorexia nervosa (AN), Goldschlager et al. (2026) explored several ways in which ADHD-related experiences shaped their body image concerns and eating disorder behaviours. Participants described how ADHD-related intense interests and hyperfocus often became directed towards their bodies, weight, and weightcontrol behaviours, contributing to the development and maintenance of AN. For example, one participant described, "I like to plan my weight loss and stuff. And I spend, like, hours on it. So, the ADHD can kind of… help me focus on that" (p. 22). Body dissatisfaction was also described in relation to stimulation seeking and eating behaviours, with one participant reflecting, "In my experience, a lot of the bingeing that I did as a teen and a child was associated with, like, boredom and under stimulation. And then how that then turned into the anorexia was because I became dissatisfied with my body" (p. 23-24).

Within the theme of Shame & Self-Perception, Goldschlager et al. (2026) reported that participants described how longstanding experiences of criticism, perceived underachievement, and difficulties associated with ADHD negatively influenced their selfworth. For some, changing their appearance or losing weight became a means of attempting to improve their self-perception or achieve a sense of mastery. As one participant explained, "With ADHD, you kind of struggle to do things, then that impacts your self-worth, and then you think that you can improve your self-worth by improving how you look" (p. 30). Similarly, another participant described how restriction and weight loss provided a sense of accomplishment that temporarily improved their self-perception -"The restricting made me feel good about myself, in a way" (p. 30). Yet, in the theme, Understanding Matters, participants went on to describe how receiving an ADHD diagnosis helped them reinterpret longstanding difficulties, reducing self-blame and fostering greater self-understanding -"Awareness helps a lot, because it really takes the shame away of why you struggle" (p. 32).

Although these latter experiences concerned broader self-perception rather than body image specifically, they provided context for understanding how appearance-related self-worth and shame could become intertwined with ADHD-related experiences.

4. Discussion

Although body image among neurodivergent populations has received increasing scholarly attention in recent years, little is known about how body image is conceptualised, measured, and experienced among people with ADHD. This scoping review sought to map and synthesise existing evidence concerning body image in relation to ADHD and ADHD traits, identifying key findings and methodological limitations to inform future research. A total of 27 studies were identified, comprising 25 quantitative studies and two qualitative studies.

Most quantitative studies employed cross-sectional designs, and both qualitative studies explored body image experiences in the context of disordered eating and sleep-related difficulties. Overall, findings provide some evidence of associations between ADHD and facets of negative body image, although the strength and consistency of these relationships varied across studies. Importantly, the evidence base remains limited in its conceptual scope, with no study examining positive body image, as well as in its methodological approaches and representation of diverse populations.

Our review suggests that, broadly speaking, two approaches have dominated quantitative research examining ADHD and body image. First, studies have investigated direct associations between ADHD and negative body image, most commonly, body dissatisfaction and drive for thinness, particularly through between-and within-group comparisons and correlational analyses. Collectively, these studies suggest that ADHD and ADHD-related characteristics may be associated with greater negative body image, although findings were inconsistent, particularly following adjustment for covariates. Second, a smaller body of research has examined more complex relationships, including body image as a potential mediator or moderator of associations between ADHD and other outcomes, or as an outcome of intervention. Although these studies extend beyond direct associations, the diversity of relationships investigated and the limited number of studies make it difficult to identify consistent patterns. Intervention research was particularly scarce, with only two small studies (Frame et al., 2003; López Sánchez et al., 2015), neither of which specifically targeted body image. Furthermore, as the mediation and moderation analyses were crosssectional, conclusions about temporal relationships and potential explanatory mechanisms are limited. Taken together, existing findings suggest that the associations between ADHD and negative body image may vary according to how ADHD is assessed, sample characteristics, the dimensions of body image assessed, and the broader psychosocial contexts. Future research should therefore move beyond examining whether ADHD and negative body image are associated to investigate the conditions and underlying processes that may explain these relationships.

An important development in body image research is recognising body image as a complex, multidimensional construct, comprising distinct negative and positive dimensions that each encompass multiple facets (Tylka, 2018; Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015).

However, no study included I the present review examined positive body image; all adopted a pathology-oriented conceptualisation of body image, with research predominantly examining body dissatisfaction and drive for thinness in relation to ADHD, to the exclusion of positive body image. This may, however, partly reflect the relatively recent growth of positive body image, particularly over the past decade (Swami & Tylka, 2026), although this does not explain its absence studies published within this period. Relatedly, studies frequently drew upon associations between ADHD, "ob*sity", and eating disorder symptomatology to justify investigating body image, reflecting a predominantly medicalised framing of both body image and ADHD. This represents a significant conceptual limitation of the existing literature. An exclusive focus on reducing negative body image means that, at best, research can inform efforts to reduce body image concerns, while overlooking the how people with ADHD experience and cultivate acceptance, appreciation, and respect for the body (Tylka, 2018; Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015).

The predominance of quantitative research, with only two qualitative studies, further illustrates the limited attention given to how people with ADHD themselves understand and make meaning of their body image experiences. The emphasis on ADHD as an individuallevel correlate or risk factor for negative body image has left broader socio-cultural processes comparatively unexplored. For example, Goldschlager et al (2026) described how lifelong ADHD-related criticism and underachievement eroded participants' self-worth, with some seeking to restore it through changing their appearance. Research with other neurodivergent groups (i.e., autistic/AuDHD people) similarly suggests that experiences of stigma, masking, and pressures to conform to neurotypical expectations may shape body image (Longhurst et al., 2026; Makin et al., 2025a Makin et al., , 2025b)), consistent with a minority stress perspective (Both & Frost, 2020; Meyer, 2003). Notably, Goldschlager et al. (2026) highlighted how shame was particularly related to late diagnosis of ADHD, with a diagnosis easing shame by providing an explanatory framework for self-understanding. This resonates with broader research suggesting that receiving a diagnosis may shape how neurodivergent people understand and evaluate themselves (e.g., Frick et al., 2026; Makin, Zesch, et al., 2025), with implications for body image (e.g., Longhurst et al., 2023). Nonetheless, the role of these sociocultural experiences in shaping body image remains insufficiently understood.

Critical to understanding the relationship between ADHD and body image is how both constructs are operationalised and measured. Across the included studies, considerable heterogeneity was evident in the assessment of negative body image, with measures encompassing different facets, including body dissatisfaction, drive for thinness, body dysmorphic concerns, weight and shape concerns, and appearance-related self-evaluations.

This variability makes it difficult to determine whether ADHD is meaningfully associated with negative body image more broadly or with particular facets of the construct. Importantly, to our knowledge, none of the measures used were developed with people with ADHD in mind, and their psychometric properties have not yet been adequately established within this population. This reflects an overarching limitation within the field, whereby neurodivergent populations have historically been excluded from the development of body image theory and commonly used measurement tools (for discussion, see Longhurst, 2026; see also Longhurst et al., 2024). Consequently, it remains unclear whether existing instruments adequately capture how people with ADHD understand and experience their bodies, particularly where cognitive, emotional, sensory, and interoceptive experiences may differ from those of the populations in which these measures were originally developed.

Similarly, considerable heterogeneity was evident in how ADHD was operationalised and assessed, ranging from diagnostic status (e.g., self-or parent-reported, clinicianconfirmed) and measures of ADHD symptoms or traits, to proxies such as medication use and polygenic risk (for discussions, see Alarachi et al., 2024; Harrison & Edwards, 2023). This is particularly important as, although ADHD is increasingly understood dimensionally (Larsson et al., 2013; Salum et al., 2014), studies examining ADHD symptoms (e.g., hyperactivity) and their association with body image in general populations may not be directly comparable with those involving individuals with a formal ADHD diagnosis. Taken together, differences in the operationalisation and measurement of both constructs may have contributed to inconsistencies across findings, limiting the conclusions that can be drawn from extant evidence.

4.1. Limitations

Despite the novelty of the current review, several limitations warrant consideration.

First, our findings must be interpreted in the context of epistemological hierarchies that privilege certain forms of knowledge production and dissemination, while marginalising other ways of knowing (Teo, 2010). Consistent with conventional scoping review methodology, eligible sources were identified through searches of academic and grey literature databases, supplemented by citation searching. Although no restrictions were imposed in terms of language or geographic location, the use of English-language search terms may have limited the identification of relevant sources published in other languages. These considerations are particularly important given that neurodiversity and the neurodivergent literature remain predominantly situated within White, Western and Eurocentric frameworks, shaping whose knowledge is produced and legitimised (Nair et al., 2026). Such epistemological and methodological constraints do not operate independently of broader socio-political, economic, and academic power structures, which may reproduce inequities in knowledge visibility and reinforce neuronormative assumptions or ideals (see Longhurst et al., 2026; see also Botha, 2021; Reddy & Amer, 2023). These dynamics may be reflected, at least in part, by the evidence included in this review.

Second, a related limitation is that all included studies originated from countries in the Global North (i.e., Australia, Canada, Europe, and the United States). Although geographic differences in ADHD prevalence estimates are reported, these may partly reflect variations in sociocultural understandings of ADHD, access to healthcare, and diagnostic practices (Cortese et al., 2026; Tuncer-Ebetürk et al., 2026). Accordingly, the absence of studies from the Global South should not be interpreted as evidence that ADHD is less prevalent in these regions; instead, this may highlight enduring inequalities in whose experiences are represented in the global literature. It may also reflect broader, structural and institutional barriers that impact research funding and publication opportunities (Chan et al., 2025). This includes, but is not limited to, the availability of culturally adapted tools and resources relating to ADHD and body image across cultures, potentially impacting how these are experienced, recognised, and addressed (Landor et al., 2024; Shalaby et al., 2024). Even within these contexts, race and ethnicity were reported in only 10 of the 27 studies, with samples predominantly comprising white participants, limiting understanding of how race, ethnicity, and experiences of racism may intersect with ADHD and body image.

Third, the included studies were limited in their representation of diverse social identities, particularly regarding gender minorities (e.g., non-binary or transgender people).

Across studies, it was largely unclear whether demographic information referred to gender identity or biological sex, with reporting predominantly restricted to binary categories. This is particularly important given evidence that ADHD is more prevalent among transgender and gender-diverse populations (for a review, see Goetz & Adams, 2024), while research has highlighted the complex relationships between body dissatisfaction, gender dysphoria, and efforts to modify or align the body with one's gender identity (e.g., Burstall et al., 2024).

Gender-diverse people may therefore have distinctive body image experiences that are not adequately captured by existing research, including experiences shaped by the intersection of neurodivergence and gender identity. As a result, it remains unclear to what extent the findings reflect the experiences of gender-diverse people with ADHD. Moreover, several studies were conducted exclusively with women and girls in clinical settings, predominantly eating disorder services (e.g., Fernández-Aranda et al., 2013; Testa et al., 2020). Given that ADHD is frequently underdiagnosed in women and girls (Attoe & Climie, 2023; Babinski, 2024), it is difficult to disentangle whether findings from these samples reflect ADHD, gender, or the clinical context. These limitations therefore constrain the applicability of findings to the wider ADHD population and highlight the need for more inclusive representation and clearer, more consistent reporting in future research.

Fourth, some studies drew on overlapping samples (i.e., Fernández-Aranda et al., 2013, and Testa et al., 2020; Yilmaz et al., 2017 Yilmaz et al., , 2023)), such that some participants are represented more than once. Our broad inclusion criteria also meant that studies using single-item measures of body image (e.g., Karjalainen et al., 2016) were included, which may further limit comparability across studies. Finally, the methodological quality of included studies was not formally appraised, consistent with the exploratory and evidence-mapping objectives of scoping reviews. Consequently, we did not evaluate the methodological robustness of the existing evidence or the extent to which study quality may have influenced the reported findings. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as a synthesis of the nature and extent of available evidence, as opposed to an assessment of its methodological quality or certainty.

4.2. Future directions

Moving forward, future research should build upon the present findings in several ways. First, thoughtful consideration around conceptualising and measuring body image is necessary. As discussed, there remains limited research explicitly exploring the ways in which people with ADHD themselves understand and experience body image, particularly outside the context of eating disorders. Moreover, the existing literature has predominantly adopted a pathology-oriented approach, focusing on negative body image to the exclusion of positive body image and its multiple facets (e.g., body appreciation, functionality appreciation, embodiment, body acceptance by others). Emerging research has begun to address similar limitations among other neurodivergent populations, particularly autistic people, by exploring how positive body image is conceptualised and experienced (Longhurst et al., 2023). Given growing evidence of the protective and health-promoting role of positive body image for physical and psychological well-being (Linardon et al., 2022), future research should investigate whether such findings extend to ADHD populations, including across intersecting identities (e.g., among racialised minoritised and gender-diverse people).

Importantly, none of the included studies reported participatory approaches; participatory research should help establish whether existing conceptualisations and measurement tools adequately capture body image among people with ADHD, or whether adaptations are needed (e.g., Longhurst et al., 2024). Such work would provide a stronger conceptual and practical foundation for identifying potentially modifiable processes that could inform future prevention and intervention efforts.

Second, given emerging, albeit inconsistent, evidence of associations between ADHD and negative body image, greater attention should be directed towards understanding the processes underlying these relationships. As noted above, existing research has emphasised ADHD symptoms as predictors or correlates of body image concerns, with comparatively limited consideration of the sociocultural contexts in which these experiences arise. This emphasis risks reinforcing deficit-based framings by locating body image difficulties primarily within the individual, while overlooking social and structural barriers (e.g., ableism, stigma; Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026) that may constrain opportunities for people with ADHD to develop and maintain a positive relationship with their bodies. Future research should therefore move beyond predominantly symptom-focused approaches by examining potential mediating and moderating roles of such sociocultural factors in links between ADHD and body image. Doing so may inform more contextually sensitive and neuroaffirming theory and practice.

A final priority for future research is to examine the temporal processes shaping body image among people with ADHD. Given the predominance of cross-sectional studies in the current review, relatively little is known about how body image develops or changes over time, or the extent to which these processes are shaped by interpersonal and sociocultural factors. Longitudinal studies employing repeated assessments could help identify temporal relationships between body image and relevant processes, such as perceived social acceptance, internalised stigma and masking, and appearance-related pressures. Relatedly, incorporating measures of ADHD and both negative and positive body image into existing and future population-based cohort studies could provide valuable opportunities to examine these constructs and their associations in larger, more diverse samples. Analytical approaches such as cross-lagged panel modelling could be used to investigate prospective associations, while random-intercept cross-lagged panel models could help distinguish between-person differences and within-person changes over time. In addition, more intensive longitudinal approaches such as ecological momentary assessment could further elucidate fluctuations in body image in everyday life and how these changes relate to contextual influences. Such research could help identify who is most at risk, the processes that mediate this risk, and potential points of intervention at both individual and structural levels.

4.3. Conclusion

This scoping review provides, to our knowledge, the first synthesis of research on body image and ADHD. Findings provide some, albeit inconsistent, evidence of associations between ADHD and negative body image. The available literature is limited in the range of topics, methodologies, and study populations, and is dominated by a pathology-oriented Body Image and ADHD conceptualisation of body image and a symptom-focused examination of its association with ADHD. Moreover, studies were conducted exclusively in Western contexts, with predominantly White samples (where reported), and relied on body image theories and measures developed with neurotypical populations, reflecting broader neuronormative assumptions. Overall, this review highlights clear gaps in the existing evidence base. Little is known about positive body image among people with ADHD, how they conceptualise and experience body image, or the processes underlying its associations with ADHD. We urgently call on researchers to prioritise participatory approaches to better understand body image in this population, the development and psychometric evaluation of measurement tools, and longitudinal and mechanistic research examining the impact of sociocultural influences on the body image of people with ADHD.
