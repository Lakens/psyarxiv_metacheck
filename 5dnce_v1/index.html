Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

On each trial, participants were shown a rocket and made four sequential accept/reject judgements, one for each rocket-product (RP) pair, about whether that product was associated with the rocket, testing their knowledge of the full top-to-bottom chain (Figure 1c).

The remaining two were no-feedback (no-FB) rockets, where no outcome was shown and participants had to rely on their internally updated structural model to make correct predictions.

Participants were told that such changes could occur at either level, but not when.

After the experiment, participants completed a set of psychiatric questionnaires.

Only one change occurred at a time, and participants were informed of this possibility, but not of the time when they might occur.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Impaired cognitive flexibility, the ability to form and update latent structural associations, is a hallmark of psychiatric conditions such as obsessive-compulsive (OC) disorder, yet its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Using a novel structural inference task, we dissociated three candidate mechanisms: (i) initial acquisition of associative structures, (ii) belief updating following structural changes, and (iii) inference about unobserved associations. In a large general-population sample (N=313), transdiagnostically characterised OC symptoms were linked to impaired updating following structural change and reduced generalisation to unobserved associations, whilst initial learning remained intact. Using fMRI (N=51), we found this was driven by overrepresenting structurally irrelevant associations in medial orbitofrontal cortex, and impaired formation of novel latent associations in posterior hippocampus. These findings reveal that OC-related cognitive inflexibility is grounded in dissociable fronto-hippocampal representational failures that impair structural model revision, rather than the capacity to acquire latent associations in the first place.

To dissociate three candidate mechanisms of cognitive inflexibility: (i) the initial formation of stimulus-outcome associative beliefs, (ii) the updating of those beliefs following unsignalled structural changes, and (iii) the ability to infer unobserved but structurally related associations in the absence of direct feedback, we developed a novel structural inference task and administered it to a large general-population online sample recruited via Prolific with a preregistered design (N=313; UK-based; aged 18-45 years; 39.6% female; M=32, SD=6.7; see Methods for exclusion criteria).

The task required participants to learn two parallel hierarchical structures, each spanning three levels: rockets at the top were linked to planets in the middle, which were each associated with two out of four possible products at the bottom (Figure 1b). On each trial, participants were shown a rocket and made four sequential accept/reject judgements, one for each rocket-product (RP) pair, about whether that product was associated with the rocket, testing their knowledge of the full top-to-bottom chain (Figure 1c).

Two of the four rockets were assigned to a feedback (FB) condition: after choices involving these rockets, participants received explicit information about which products were correct, allowing them to update their structural beliefs. The remaining two were no-feedback (no-FB) rockets, where no outcome was shown and participants had to rely on their internally updated structural model to make correct predictions. Because the two structures were fully interconnected, feedback about FB-rocket associations could be used to infer the correct associations for no-FB rockets. No-FB trials therefore served as the key measure of structural inference. To ensure that complete structural information was available before the inference moment, trials were organised into mini-blocks (MBs) each constituting a complete run-through of both structures, with the two FB rockets always probed before the two no-FB rockets (Figure 1d).

Structural changes were introduced to probe belief updating. Once a participant exceeded a 70% accuracy on FB trials, the underlying structure was probabilistically modified at either the top or bottom level: two rockets (level 1) or two products (level 3) swapped positions between structures (Figure 1e). Participants were told that such changes could occur at either level, but not when. Since a change could only be detected through feedback during FB trials, changepoints directly tested whether participants used incoming evidence to revise their beliefs and adjust their subsequent choices, and whether this updating carried over to the latent associations probed on no-FB trials.

Introduction

Adaptive behaviour depends on the ability to form internal representations of environmental structures and to flexibly update these representations when contingencies change 1 . Humans tend to build complex latent structural models of their environment 2- 3 which enables them to generalize and make inference about associations that have never been observed directly [4] [5] [6] [7] . Critically, such latent representations must remain adjustable to cope with dynamic, ever-changing environments: new associations need to be added, while associations that are no longer valid require pruning. This is particularly challenging when it affects unobservable associations, as they cannot be guided by direct feedback but rely on a correct extrapolation from the limited observable associations [8] [9] [10] [11].

Deficits in flexibly forming and adjusting such representations are hallmarks of mental disorders. Cognitive inflexibility is a key feature of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) and related conditions [12] [13] [14] . This inflexibility manifests as executive dysfunction, including poor model-based learning, where dynamic maintenance and integration of an internal task structure is essential [15] [16] [17] [18] . However, the precise mechanisms underlying this impairment remain unclear. Standard model-based paradigms explicitly instruct participants on the environmental structure 19, 20 while other tasks lack relational complexity between different structures 21, 22 . Thus, it remains unknown whether the deficit lies in the initial formation of latent associations, the adaptation of behavior when these change, or the ability to infer unobserved but structurally related associations. Because most existing paradigms conflate structural acquisition, belief updating, and inference, our ability to isolate the precise mechanisms has thus far been limited.

We have thus developed a novel structural inference task that teases apart the formation, flexible updating, and generalization of latent structural associations. Using a large-scale online study, we found that obsessive-compulsive (OC) traits were linked to impoverished updating of latent associations following structural change, and to a corresponding failure to generalize these updated associations to unobserved, inferred associations, whilst initial latent structural learning remained intact. Using fMRI, we found this was driven by lingering representations of irrelevant associations in medial orbitofrontal cortex and hippocampus whilst hippocampal representations of novel latent associations remained under expressed. Our findings thus show that OC-related cognitive inflexibility is driven by inadequate updating of latent associations, linked to aberrant fronto-hippocampal representations. Successful structural adaptation and generalisation, with marked individual variability

We first confirmed that participants were able to use their structural knowledge to make correct predictions, with performance clearly above chance across all choice trials, even when including the transient below-chance dip that follows a structural change by design (one-sample t-test vs. 0.5: t(312)=22.37, p<0.001, mean=67.9%, 95% CI [66.3%, 69.5%]; Figure 2a). Because structural changes were only triggered by sustained high performance on FB trials (>70% accuracy), they directly reflected how effectively participants used feedback to revise their structural beliefs. With a median of 4 (maximum 6) structural changes, and 96.8% of participants (303 of 313) experiencing more than one, most participants demonstrated successful and repeated adaptation (Figure 2b,c).

Given that structural changes were triggered exclusively by FB performance, participants could in principle have solved the task through FB-based learning alone, without inferring unobserved but structurally related associations. Above-chance no-FB performance demonstrates that this was not the case (mean=70%; Figure 2c), and participants who encountered more structural changes also showed better no-FB performance (ρ=0.606, p<0.001; Figure 2d), indicating that successful structural adaptation supported the inference of unobserved but structurally related associations.

Whilst FB and no-FB performance were positively related, FB performance explained only 27% of the variance in no-FB performance (r=0.52, p<0.001; Figure 2e), indicating that the two were partially dissociable. There was substantial variability across participants in how well they generalised structural knowledge to unobserved associations, over and above what could be predicted from their FB performance alone. This individual variability is a prerequisite for the analyses that follow, in which we asked whether OC traits were specifically linked to difficulties in adapting to structural changes, inferring unobserved but structurally related associations, or both. Diminished explicit belief updating in high-OC individuals

Next, we tested whether behavioural adaptation after a structural change varied with OC scores, focusing on FB trials where associations had explicit feedback.

Critically, high OC participants completed significantly fewer structural changes than those with low OC scores (ρ=-0.144, p=0.011; Figure 4a). This could reflect two distinct mechanisms: more stochastic decision-making irrespective of learning, or a specific inability to adapt swiftly to structural changes. To dissociate these, we split trials into three phases relative to each structural change: the mini-block immediately preceding the change (MBt-1), the mini-block during which the change occurred (MBt), and the subsequent mini-block (MBt+1; Figure 4b). Each MB constitutes a complete run-through of both structures, allowing us to directly quantify participants' associative beliefs about both structures. Participants recovered after a structural change had occurred, as indexed by a significant improvement in performance between MBt and MBt+1 (N=297; t(296)=11.58, p<0.001; mean MBt=55.2%, mean MBt+1=69.9%).

By MBt+1, all information needed for correct rocket-product predictions had been observed, so errors reflect a failure to update. If OC traits reflect a structural updating deficit specifically, we would expect an effect at MBt+1 rather than uniformly lower performance across all MBs. Consistent with this, OC scores were unrelated to performance before the change (MBt-1: ρ=-0.089, p=0.121, BF01=4.2) or at MBt (ρ=0.040, p=0.493, BF01=11.0) but significantly predicted lower performance at MBt+1 (ρ=-0.138, p=0.017; Figure 4c), favouring a specific updating impairment over generally noisier decision-making.

To characterise the underlying mechanism, we tested whether OC-related performance differences could be explained by weaker integration of explicit prediction errors to update associative beliefs. Using a logistic random intercept model, focused only on trials where feedback was given, we predicted whether participants changed their accept/reject choice on a given RP pair, relative to their previous encounter with the same pair, as a function of the associative-belief prediction error (ab-PE) on that prior encounter, a binary signal coding whether feedback had shown their choice for that pair to be wrong (1) or right (0). Blunted integration of structural prediction errors during latent inference along the OC dimension

Next, we tested whether the OC dimension also relates to inference about unobserved associations, examining no-feedback (no-FB) trial performance, where associations could only be inferred, not observed. All participants showed a transient drop in no-FB performance after a structural change (MBt), recovering by MBt+1 (Figure 5b); the degree of recovery varied with OC: a significant interaction between mini-blocks since change and OC scores (β=-0.016, p=0.003; Figure 5a) showed high-OC participants recovered more slowly and incompletely, though OC scores alone were not significant (β=-0.014, p=0.236). At MBt+1, no-FB performance showed a negative trend with OC (ρ=-0.106, p=0.068; Figure 5c).

To characterise the trial-level mechanism driving this impairment, we examined whether OC traits attenuated the use of structural prediction errors (s-PE) to guide inference choices on no-FB trials. The s-PE was computed as the complement of mean FB accuracy within each mini-block (1 -mean FB accuracy). A high s-PE therefore indicates that feedback is broadly inconsistent with the participant's current structural model, signaling that a structural change has likely occurred and that inferred associations should be updated accordingly (Figure 5d). We modelled trial-wise switch/stay decisions (whether participants changed their choice on a no-FB pair relative to their previous encounter with that pair) using a logistic random intercept model with s-PE, whether the no-FB pair itself had changed at the most recent structural change (changed pair), OC scores, and their interactions as predictors (Figure 5e). These analyses included participants who experienced more than one structural change (N=303), ensuring at least one post-change inference window.

Higher s-PE increased switching overall, confirming that FB-derived structural evidence appropriately guided inference (β=0.455, p<0.001), but this sensitivity was significantly attenuated in high-OC participants (OC×s-PE: β=-0.043, p=0.002; Figure 5e). Switching increased specifically for no-FB pairs that had themselves changed (s-PE×changed pair: β=0.146, p<0.001), and the OC-related attenuation was strongest for these pairs (s-PE×changed pair×OC: β=-0.029, p=0.037). This was specific to structural change rather than general FB performance: the interaction replicated when restricting s-PE to directly changed pairs (β=-0.044, p<0.001; Figure 5f). High-OC individuals could thus use FB evidence to guide choices but showed reduced sensitivity to structural prediction errors, diminishing their ability to transfer updated knowledge to unobserved pairs.

Representational mechanisms underlying OC-related impairments in structural updating and inference

Having established these behavioural deficits, we next asked what neural mechanisms underlie them, collecting an independent in-lab fMRI sample (N=51; age=25.6±5.6 years, range=18-40; 23 male) that completed a matched task version with novel everyday-object stimuli, focusing here on OC-related representational effects (structural representations themselves are reported in a separate article) 29 . Behavioural analyses used a larger sample (N=59) and replicated the core adaptation pattern (above-chance performance, a post-change dip with partial recovery, and above-chance no-FB inference) and, more weakly given reduced power, the OC-related effects (Supplementary Note 1).

To probe structural neural representations independently of choice behaviour, we introduced fMRI suppression blocks (SBs) interleaved with the task's choice blocks (CBs) (the imaging equivalent of the online mini-blocks), in which participants made the same FB and no-FB rocket-product judgements described above. In SBs, all possible stimulus pairs were presented in rapid succession and pseudorandom order, without a behavioural response, allowing representational strength to be measured independently of choice. This leveraged the principle of repetition suppression: the more strongly two stimuli are associated, the more their neural co-occurrence suppresses the regional response, providing an implicit, behaviour-independent measure of representational strength relative to unassociated pairs 30, 31 . We defined four pair categories by their structural relevance before and after the change: Stable pairs (associated both before and after), Novel pairs (newly associated only after), Outdated pairs (associated before but no longer valid after), and Irrelevant pairs (never associated at any recent point; Figure 6a). All contrasts were computed relative to the Irrelevant pairs, which served as a proxy for the absence of any representational relationship. We focused our analyses on mOFC and hippocampus, based on prior literature on structural coding in mOFC 4, 32 and hippocampal encoding of novel associations 33, 34 , corroborated by our in-lab study 29.

First, we tested how selective latent structural representations were. People with higher OC scores showed a reduced Stable-vs-Irrelevant representational contrast (Figure 6b) in medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC). To aid interpretation of this effect, we visualised condition-wise parameter estimates within the significant cluster, showing the relationship between OC traits and responses to Stable and Irrelevant pairs (Figure 6b2-b3). This pattern suggests that the contrast effect is primarily driven by decreased responses to Irrelevant pairs with increasing OC severity (lower responses indicating stronger representations), whereas responses to Stable pairs show no clear relationship with OC traits. Given that mOFC normally functions as a selective structural map maintaining valid associations while dropping invalid ones 29 , this suggests OC is linked to overrepresentation of irrelevant associations, a less precise code that could explain the explicit updating impairment observed behaviourally.

Because the OC factor was primarily linked to adjusting after change, we next asked whether incorporation of novel associations was altered. In posterior hippocampus, high-OC scores were associated with reduced encoding of Novel-vs-Irrelevant associations (Figure 6c), consistent with the hippocampus' established role in integrating newly valid associations 29, 33, 34 , an impairment that would constrain both explicit belief revision and no-FB inference. All effects held when controlling for behavioural performance and switching (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Together, these findings reveal two dissociable representational failures; imprecise structural coding in mOFC and attenuated incorporation of novel associations in hippocampus, providing mechanistic grounding for the behavioural updating and inference impairments. Discussion

Cognitive flexibility depends on forming and updating latent structural associations. Using a novel task, we found that obsessive-compulsive (OC) traits impair structural processing selectively at later stages: initial learning was intact, but higher OC scores were linked to diminished integration of evidence after structural change and reduced sensitivity to structural prediction errors during inference, mirrored by increased mOFC representation of irrelevant associations and reduced hippocampal representation of novel associations. These findings identify altered structural-representation adaptation, not basic associative learning, as a core mechanism of OC-related cognitive inflexibility.

Earlier studies often blur initial learning with subsequent rule updating, making it difficult to isolate where OC-related deficits occur 12, 13, 35 . By separating these phases, we found high-OC individuals performed as well as others on comprehension checks and pre-change trials, echoing prior evidence that OCD individuals accurately learn initial associations before contingency changes 15, 36, 37 , and ruling out generalised explanations such as attentional deficits or degraded associative encoding. Evidence from volatility paradigms, which rarely demand relational structure, is mixed, with some studies reporting altered belief updating in OCD [38] [39] [40] and others not [41] [42] [43] . Together, this suggests that OC-related inflexibility is not a general deficit in learning or updating, but emerges specifically when relational structures must be revised. Indeed, OC-related deficits only emerged after a structural change: high-OC individuals completed fewer changes and recovered more poorly, despite comparable performance in stable periods. Trial-level analysis attributed this to reduced sensitivity to associative-belief prediction errors, i.e. reduced revision after disconfirmatory feedback, consistent with Bayesian accounts linking OCD to impaired response to contingency shifts 44, 45 and broader reversal-learning alterations 12, 40, 46, 47 . Interestingly, prior reports of hyperactive outcome-related prediction-error signals in OCD 48, 49 align with these findings, suggesting that a neural hyperactivation might be related to a behavioural blunting of these prediction errors.

Reduced sensitivity to structural prediction errors in OC individuals was also found to drive the impaired generalization to unobserved associations. This hints at a broader impoverishment of internal models. Whilst both hint at a common mechanism, individuals greatly differed in the impact of prediction errors on these two separate behaviours (Supplementary Note 3), suggesting that high-OC individuals may be heterogeneous, with some showing one of the two prediction-error deficits but not necessarily both.

Neurally, these prediction-error deficits were mirrored in structural representations. In high-OC individuals, mOFC, a selective encoder of hidden task states 4, 32, 50 that normally holds a consolidated store of stable relational structure 29 , over-represented irrelevant pairs. This suggests that prediction errors failed to sharpen the structural code, leaving irrelevant information unfiltered 51 , consistent with attenuated mediofrontal tracking of evidence updates in high-OC individuals 52 . Complementarily, posterior hippocampus, central to building cognitive maps 2, 53 , showed attenuated en-coding of novel associations, a signal that tracks behavioural adaptation in this paradigm 29 , suggesting that prediction errors were also insufficiently used to incorporate the updated structure. Though our data cannot establish directionality, OFC-hippocampal co-dependency in maintaining structural models 11, 54 and the abolition of structure-based inference by hippocampal stimulation 55 suggest that both regions jointly limit the model available for belief revision.

Together, these results refine existing accounts of OC-related cognitive inflexibility. Compulsivity has classically been characterised as reliance on habit systems due to impaired goal-directed control [15] [16] [17] 36 , stemming from impoverished internal-model representations 18, 56 . Our results clarify that this impoverishment reflects not a basic failure to acquire the model but an impaired capacity to calibrate it under structural uncertainty, so rigidity is most pronounced in post-change, evidence-sparse, inference-dependent contexts. Extending this paradigm to clinically diagnosed OCD samples is an important next step.

In sum, OC-related cognitive inflexibility reflects not a failure of control or learning capacity but a representational precision issue that compounds when structural demands are highest. Fronto-hippocampal circuits supporting structural model maintenance and revision emerge as a mechanistic target for understanding and addressing the inflexibility characterising OCD and related disorders.

Methods

Online study

Participants

Participants were recruited via Prolific (https://www.prolific.co/); eligibility was restricted to UK-based individuals aged 18-45 years. An exploratory online study (N=111) tested task feasibility, defined hypotheses and exclusion criteria, and determined the sample size for a pre-registered confirmatory study (https://osf.io/eq56y/).

A total of 432 participants enrolled in the confirmatory study. Exclusions were applied for insufficient attention during the practice phase (N=118), insufficient attention during the questionnaire phase (≥2 incorrect attention-check responses; N=1), or belowchance task performance (accuracy<50%). The final sample comprised 313 participants (124 female, 1 preferred not to say; age range 18-45 years; M=32, SD=6.7).

The target sample size was determined a priori using G*Power, based on the correlation between OC scores and choice accuracy in the exploratory study (ρ=-0.2, p=0.03); a power analysis targeting 95% power at α=0.05 yielded N=313, and data collection stopped once this target was reached. Ethical approval was granted by the UCL Research Ethics Committee (15/LO/1361); all participants gave informed consent.

Experimental procedure -overview

The study comprised an instruction phase, comprehension check, two practice phases, the main experimental session, and a questionnaire battery (Figure 1a), with attention/comprehension checks throughout to control task admission and inform posthoc exclusions.

Main task overview

The structural inference task dissociated three components of structural behaviour: initial formation of associative beliefs, updating after unsignalled change, and inference about unobserved associations without direct feedback. Participants learned two parallel hierarchical structures (rockets-planets-products) and made sequential rocket-product predictions.

A subset of trials provided explicit feedback (enabling direct belief updating), while the remainder withheld feedback, requiring reliance on the internally maintained model. Unsignalled structural changes required detecting change from feedback evidence and propagating updated knowledge to untested associations. Points were awarded for correct choices and converted into a monetary bonus.

Structures and choice trials

The task comprised two parallel three-level hierarchical structures: a rocket linked to a planet, each planet associated with two of four possible products (Figure 1b). On each trial, participants viewed a rocket and made four sequential accept/reject judgements about candidate products (Figure 1c); 'accept' indicated a belief that the rocketproduct pair (RP) belonged to the same structure.

Each rocket was permanently assigned to a feedback (FB) or no-feedback (no-FB) condition. On FB trials, the two correct products were revealed after choices, alongside points awarded; on no-FB trials, a fixation cross appeared instead. Because the two structures are fully interconnected (including anti-correlational associations between rockets and products from different structures), FB-trial information can in principle be used to infer correct no-FB associations, requiring participants to maintain and deploy an updated internal model. Points from both trial types contributed to the performance bonus.

Mini-blocks

Choice trials were organised into mini-blocks (MBs; Figure 1d). Each MB comprised 20 trials: four rockets × four products, plus one unanalysed planet-predictor trial (no feedback). Rocket order was randomised within condition; the two FB rockets always preceded the two no-FB rockets, ensuring complete structural information was available before no-FB inference. Because each MB probed the full set of associations, beliefs could be quantified directly from trial-level choices without computational modelling. Participants completed 14 MBs in the main experiment.

Structural changes

Structural changes were triggered when participants exceeded 70% FB accuracy (Figure 1d, dotted line). To prevent anticipation, a change occurred with 50% probability at threshold crossing; if not triggered, it was forced at the next crossing. A minimum of two MBs separated successive changes (except the first, permitted after MB 1, since both structures had already been instructed). The maximum number of changes was six (constrained by trial count); the minimum was one.

There were two types of change: two rockets swapped positions between structures, or two products swapped positions (Figure 1e); only one change occurred at a time, and participants were informed of this. Swaps always involved either two FB rockets or one FB and one no-FB rocket (never two no-FB rockets), preserving complete structural inferability. Change-type order (rockets vs. products) was fixed across participants; the number of changes experienced was performance-dependent.

Experimental Procedure

During the instruction phase, participants viewed an explicit display of the task's hierarchical structure and learned the stimulus-level assignments (Figure 1b), encoded via a card-matching game in which structures were shown with items missing and participants selected correct completions from six options. Before proceeding, participants completed multiple-choice comprehension questions on the structural hierarchy, FB/no-FB distinctions, and change rules; all had to be answered correctly, with errors triggering repetition of the full question set.

Participants then completed two practice phases, each containing one structural change: the first with FB trials only, the second alternating FB and no-FB trials as in the main task. Each phase comprised 3-5 MBs; participants needed 50% FB accuracy by the fourth MB, or were shown the full structure and given one further MB to reach threshold, else excluded. The main-task threshold was 70%; criteria were disclosed to participants beforehand.

Additional post-hoc exclusions applied predefined criteria: failed attention checks or incomplete/incorrect comprehension responses during practice, ≥2 failed attention checks during the questionnaire phase, or below-chance main-task performance (accuracy<50%).

After the main task, participants completed a questionnaire battery: the Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R) 27 , Short Scales for Measuring Schizotypy (SSMS) 57 , Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS) 25 , Apathy Motivation Index (AMI) 26 , Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES) 58 , Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale-42 (DASS-42) 24 , Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS) 59 , and the International Cognitive Ability Resource (ICAR) 28.

The study lasted approximately 80 minutes. Participants were compensated £8.00/hour plus a performance-dependent bonus (£0-£2), with partial compensation of £4.50 for incomplete sessions.

Behavioural analyses

Performance analyses

Performance was measured as choice accuracy, assessed across the entire experiment (Figure 2a), separately for FB and no-FB trials, and time-locked to structural changes. Because the experiment was structured into MBs, we compared performance in the MB before the change (MBt-1, reflecting participants' maximum FB accuracy), the MB during the change (MBt), and the subsequent MB (MBt+1, reflecting performance recovery once full structural information was available). These timepoints were of particular interest for comparing the formation of associative beliefs (MBt-1) against belief updating following new feedback (MBt+1). Reaction times were recorded but not reported, as uncontrolled variability from online testing (hardware, browser rendering, network latency) rendered them unsuitable for meaningful inference.

Factor analysis

We used a transdiagnostic approach to define latent psychiatric dimensions across seven questionnaires (Supplementary Figure S3) 16 , applying factor analysis to 22 subscales while accounting for intra-and inter-questionnaire correlations. The number of latent factors was determined via the Catell-Nelson-Gorusch (CNG) scree test on eigenvalues (Supplementary Figure S3b) 60 . The first factor was labelled anxious-depressive, the second social unease, and the third (our factor of interest) obsessivecompulsive. Factor scores were used to relate individual differences to structural acquisition, belief updating, and inference.

The same set of questionnaires was used across both exploratory and confirmatory study, while the reported results show a replication of factor classification.

Logistic random intercept models

We used two logistic random-intercept models to quantify how evidence integration drove behavioural change on FB and no-FB trials. The first tested whether explicit action-outcome evidence (FB trials) updated associative beliefs; the second tested whether structural evidence observed on FB trials informed inference about unobserved but structurally related associations (no-FB trials). Separating these models allowed us to dissociate belief updating from inference over latent associations.

FB trials: associative belief update

We tested the degree to which evidence was integrated to update associative beliefs and direct behavioural change (Figure 4d-e), focusing on FB trials, which capture explicit stimulus-outcome updates. We used a logistic random-intercept model predicting trial-wise switch (=1) or stay (=0) decisions for the current pair relative to the previous encounter of the same pair, with a random intercept for subject ID. Stay/switch was predicted by: (a) associative-belief prediction error (ab-PE): whether the choice on the previous encounter of the same pair was incorrect (1) or correct (0), (b) obsessive-compulsive trait (OC) scores: derived from the previous factor analysis, (c) and the interaction between both variables, ab-PExOC.

Regressors were normalised (mean = 0, SD = 1) within person; OC scores were normalised across participants. The interaction effect quantified how ab-PE integration changed with OC severity. Model: switch ~ ab-PE + OC + ab-PE×OC + (1|subject). Choices for the first predictor within each mini-block were included, ensuring belief updating had not yet occurred via feedback within that block.

No-FB trials: structural inference

Next, we tested whether structural information from FB trials, indicative of potential change, informed inference about related but unobserved associations on no-FB trials, using the same logistic random-intercept approach (trial-wise switch/stay for both no-FB predictors, random intercept for subject ID). Stay/switch was predicted by (a) structural prediction error (s-PE): 1 -mean accuracy on the FB trials preceding the no-FB trials within the same MB, so that a higher coefficient reflects a stronger impact of s-PE on switching, (b) obsessive-compulsive (OC) scores: derived from the previous factor analysis, (c) changed pair: whether the current no-FB pair had itself changed at the most recent structural change, together with all interactions between s-PE, changed pair and OC (s-PE×changed pair×OC).

Regressors were normalised as above. Inference analyses included participants who experienced more than one structural change (N=303), ensuring at least one postchange inference window. The interaction effect quantified how s-PE integration changed with OC severity. The model was refined by restricting s-PE to FB pairs that had changed relative to the prior structural change, testing whether the most relevant information was underused on inference trials. Models: switch ~ s-PE×changed pair×OC + (1|subject), and switch ~ sPE_change×OC + (1|subject). The pre-registration specified separate prediction errors for changed-structure (sPE_change) and same-structure (sPE_NOchange) pairs; entered simultaneously, the OC interaction was specific to sPE_change (β=-0.056, z=-3.51, p<0.001), with no interaction for sPE_NOchange (β=-0.005, z=-0.33, p=0.743), justifying the focus on sPE_change.

As pre-registered, all trial-level models (Figs 4d/e, 5a, 5e, 5f) were repeated controlling for cognitive ability (ICAR; Supplementary Note 2); all two-way OC interaction effects survived (all p≤0.007), as did the three-way s-PE×changed pair×OC interaction (p=0.037). Non-significant correlations were complemented with Bayesian analyses to distinguish inconclusive results from evidence for the null. All analyses were repeated on the neuroimaging behavioural dataset (Supplementary Note 1).

Neuroimaging study

Participants and study rationale

A neuroimaging study (Study 2) was conducted in an independent sample to investigate the neural representations underlying the behavioural components identified online. Study 2 characterises how these components are instantiated at the level of latent neural representations and how OC-dimension differences relate to representational updating; primary results characterising structural formation, updating and deprecation in fronto-hippocampal networks are reported separately in a dedicated manuscript 29.

That paper focused on representational dynamics during structural learning and switching, independent of psychiatric traits, and is not reproduced here. We instead use the same task and representational framework to address a distinct question: how OC-dimension differences modulate neural representations of structural associations, and how these relate to the online behavioural alterations.

51 neurologically and psychiatrically healthy adults participated (age=25.6±5.6 years, range 18-40; 23 male), all with normal/corrected vision and meeting MRI safety criteria, drawn from 124 online screening completers (89 eligible, 61 invited, 10 excluded before analysis: 5 inattentive, 3 technical errors, 2 excessive motion, 1 anatomical anomaly).

The study received approval from the UCL Research Ethics Committee (14261/002), and all participants gave informed consent.

Task design

The core task structure closely mirrored the online paradigm. Participants first completed the online study; those exceeding a performance threshold (>2 structural changes successfully inferred) were invited to the MRI session, where they learned hierarchical stimulus-outcome structures and made choice-trial predictions, with some associations accompanied by feedback and others requiring inference. Structural changes occurred without warning, requiring participants to update their beliefs.

Choice-block and structural-change procedures matched the online study: each of 14 choice blocks (CBs) presented every FB and no-FB trial once, plus a middle-layer predictor trial (20 trials/CB); unsignalled changes swapped two first-or third-layer items once FB accuracy exceeded 70%. Participants experienced 1-6 changes (me-dian=5; all≥2).

Compared to the online study, the imaging task used novel everyday-object stimuli to avoid familiarity effects. Explicit feedback was provided for both higher-and lowerlevel associations, ensuring sufficient information for representational updating. The key extension was dedicated stimulus-pair presentation blocks (suppression blocks) interleaved with choice blocks, probing latent representations independently of overt decision-making: stimulus pairs were presented in rapid succession without a behavioural response, leveraging repetition suppression (reduced neural response to repeated or overlapping information reflects shared representations) 30 , similar to prior stimulus-outcome work 10 . The logic of feedback-based learning and inference remained conceptually identical across studies. Further paradigm details are in the Supplementary Methods.

Representational categories and operationalisation

Neural representations were operationalised at the level of pairwise stimulus associations, derived from the underlying task structure. Stimulus pairs presented during the suppression blocks were categorised into four classes: 1.

Stable associations: stimulus pairs that belonged to the same structure both before and after a structural change.

2.

Novel associations: stimulus pairs that became structurally related only after a structural change.

3.

Outdated associations: stimulus pairs that were structurally related before a change but no longer valid afterwards.

4.

Irrelevant associations: stimulus pairs that were never part of the same structure.

These categories dissociated representations that should be maintained, newly formed, or deprecated following structural change; all were defined by task structure alone, independent of participants' choices or trial-level performance.

fMRI analysis

Details of MRI data acquisition, preprocessing, and ROI definition are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Representational contrasts

An event-related general linear model (GLM) was estimated to characterise neural responses to the different classes of stimulus pairs presented during suppression blocks. Each stimulus pair was modelled as a single epoch regressor spanning from the onset of the first stimulus to the offset of the second stimulus. Separate regressors were included for stable, novel, outdated, and irrelevant pairs.

Repetition suppression principles were used to index representational overlap: stimulus pairs that were jointly embedded within a latent structure were expected to evoke reduced neural responses relative to structure-irrelevant pairs. Accordingly, the main contrasts of interest compared: • Stable vs Irrelevant pairs, • Novel vs Irrelevant pairs,

•

Outdated vs Irrelevant pairs.

The GLM additionally included regressors modelling task events unrelated to representational probing (predictor onset, choice phase, feedback, suppression block onset and offset), as well as motion, CSF and physiological nuisance regressors (see Supplementary Methods). All regressors were convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function and high-pass filtered.

Group-level analysis and region-of-interest definition

Imaging data were analysed using FSL. Subject-level contrasts were combined across runs with fixed-effects models and entered into mixed-effects group analyses. Statistical inference used threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) with permutationbased family-wise error correction (p<0.05). All ROIs and contrast-ROI pairings were defined independently of OC traits, selected from statistically significant effects in a prior analysis without behavioural covariates 29 , corroborated by literature on structural and relational representations (Supplementary Figures S5) 3, 4, 7, [32] [33] [34] 61 . Higher-level analyses were restricted to these predefined contrast-ROI combinations.

Obsessive-compulsive dimension: derivation and integration

To relate neural representations to OC traits, we applied the same transdiagnostic factor-analytic framework used online. Factor analysis was conducted on a larger behavioural dataset including online and Study 2 participants (n=374), yielding robust dimension estimates; OC factor loadings were extracted for the neuroimaging subsample and entered as continuous group-level covariates. Supplementary Figure S4 illustrates how factor loadings from the imaging subsample replicate the same factors as the online dataset (Supplementary Figure S3). OC scores were tested only within predefined contrast-ROI combinations, with separate contrasts for positive and negative associations between OC loadings and stable, novel, and outdated representations, avoiding circularity and overfitting.

Post-hoc analyses and control for behavioural performance

For contrast-ROI combinations showing significant OC associations, beta estimates were extracted per participant and representational category (stable, novel, irrelevant) for post-hoc visualisation only. Post-hoc analyses additionally controlled for behavioural indices (choice accuracy, number of switches; Supplementary Figure S6), using Bonferroni-corrected Pearson correlations. Behavioural analyses were conducted in R.

Imaging behavioural task: further details

Training and initial structure learning.

Participants received a shortened recap of the online instructions and completed multiple-choice comprehension questions covering the three-layer hierarchy, the FB/no-FB mapping, and the rules governing structural changes (which layers could change; one change at a time). All questions had to be answered correctly, with the full set repeated on any error. Structures were then learned via a card-matching game: a partially completed diagram was shown with items missing, and participants selected the correct completion(s) from six candidates, with feedback. A distinct, randomly generated set of everyday-object stimuli was used for each participant (never overlapping with the online session) to avoid contaminating repetition-suppression effects. Training continued until ≥80% accuracy was reached; no participant required more than one repetition.

Main task

Trial structure (predictor phase, four binary predictor-product choices, outcome phase) followed the main Methods. Each predictor was deterministically linked to exactly two of four products; no-FB predictors always followed sufficient FB exposure to permit inference of the full mapping. Because each predictor belonged to only one structure, products from the other structure were implicit rejections, so no-FB choices reflected both direct knowledge and cross-structural inference. Points were converted linearly into a monetary bonus.

Choice blocks

Each of 14 choice blocks (CBs) presented all FB/no-FB trials once (predictor order randomised within condition; FB/no-FB block sequence fixed across participants), plus a middle-layer trial (predictor = middle node; four accept/reject judgements spanning both structures, no feedback). Jittered intervals (gamma distribution: min 2s, max 6s, mean≈4s) preceded predictor onset, followed the choice phase, and preceded the next trial, to decorrelate BOLD responses.

Structural changes

At each changepoint, either two first-layer predictors or two third-layer products swapped structural assignment, constrained so that swaps always involved at least one FB stimulus (two FB predictors, or one FB + one no-FB predictor; never two no-FB predictors), guaranteeing the new structure was inferable. The set of possible swaps was fixed across participants; timing was performance-dependent: a change became eligible once FB accuracy exceeded 70% within a CB, was implemented with 50% probability at that point (else forced at the next crossing), and was followed by ≥2 CBs before the next change was possible (except the first change, permitted after CB 1). Participants experienced 1-6 changes (median=5, all≥2; Supplementary Fig. 2); the change sequence differed from the online task to prevent anticipation.

Suppression Block

Each CB was followed by a Suppression Block (SB; opening/closing green fixation), presenting all 45 unordered pairs of the 10 structure stimuli as sequential two-image presentations (1.4 s/image, 0.6 s fixation), pseudorandomised so a pair's second stimulus could open the next pair (~53 images/SB), with pair order regenerated per CB and participant. Ten astronaut catch trials per SB (appearing ≥0.5 s post-onset) required a speeded button press and contributed to the bonus, to maintain engagement.

MRI data acquisition

Imaging used a 64-channel head coil on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma Fit. Functional scans: multiband GE-EPI, 60 axial slices (2.4 mm, interleaved), 2.4×2.4 mm in-plane resolution, multiband factor 2, TR=1.5 s, TE=20 ms, flip angle 65°, phase-encoding anterior-posterior, GRAPPA=2, FOV 216×216 mm, matrix 90×90; first 5 volumes/block discarded. Sequence parameters were selected in-lab to minimise signal dropout in OFC, our a priori ROI. Anatomical: T1-weighted MPRAGE, sagittal, FOV 256×256×176 mm, 1 mm³ isotropic, TR=2530 ms, TI=1100 ms. Field map: dual-echo GRE, transverse, FOV 192×192×191 mm, 3.0×3.0×2.0 mm voxels, 64 interleaved slices (2 mm), TR=1020 ms, TE1/TE2=10.00/12.46 ms, flip 90°, phase-encoding R≫L. Pulse and respiration were recorded throughout functional runs.

Preprocessing

Preprocessing used fMRIPrep 23.2.1 (RRID:SCR_016216) 62 , built on Nipype 1.8.6 .

63,64

Fieldmaps were phase-unwrapped with prelude (FSL) 65 . The T1w image was bias-corrected (N4BiasFieldCorrection, ANTs 2.5.0), skull-stripped (ANTs ants-BrainExtraction.sh, OASIS30ANTs target), and segmented into CSF/WM/GM (fast, FSL). Volume-based normalisation to MNI152NLin6Asym and MNI152N-Lin2009cAsym (TemplateFlow 23.1.0) used nonlinear antsRegistration. For each of 2 BOLD runs/subject, a reference volume was generated; head motion was estimated pre-filtering with mcflirt; the fieldmap was rigidly co-registered to the EPI reference and its coefficients mapped onto it; BOLD-to-T1w co-registration used mri_coreg followed by boundary-based flirt (6 DOF). Predefined ROIs (hippocampus, OFC), were derived from Neuroquery meta-analytic maps based on prior literature, and tested at an uncorrected cluster-defining threshold of p<0.001 with peak-level FWE small-volume correction (p<0.05). Supplementary Note 1 -Behaviour in imaging task

To confirm that the behavioural effects characterised in the online study (N=313) generalised to the independent fMRI cohort, we applied the same analyses to the behavioural data collected in the scanner (N=59). This behavioural sample was larger than the neuroimaging sample (N=51), as eight participants were excluded from imaging analyses due to excessive movement (Ν=6) or imaging artefacts (Ν=2) but retained for behavioural analyses.

Basic task performance was comparable to the online sample. Participants performed above chance on both FB and no-FB trials, and FB performance was significantly higher than no-FB performance (0.914±0.014 vs. 0.887±0.020; paired t-test: t(58)=2.07, p=0.043), confirming that structural inference without feedback remained the more demanding condition. Participants encountered a mean of 4.85±0.13 structural changes, and both FB and no-FB performance showed the expected pattern around changepoints: performance was high prior to the change (MBt-1: FB=0.977±0.006, no-FB=0.922±0.016), dropped at the change (MBt: FB=0.891±0.019, no-FB=0.870±0.023), and partially recovered in the subsequent mini-block (MBt+1: FB=0.910±0.022, no-FB=0.883±0.028; Supplementary Fig. S1a-c).

The OC traits and the performance measures appeared to have the same direction in their effects as the online study, but they did not reach significance in this smaller sample. OC scores were unrelated to the number of structural changes encountered (ρ=-0.007, p=0.956), FB performance at MBt+1 (ρ=-0.057, p=0.667), or no-FB performance at MBt+1 (ρ=-0.198, p=0.133; Supplementary Fig. S1e-f). The structural prediction error (s-PE)×OC interaction on no-FB switching also did not reach significance, either using overall s-PE (GLMM estimate =-0.021±0.052, p=0.691) or the change-pair-specific s-PE (GLMM estimate=-0.102±0.101, p=0.315). By contrast, the feedback-based prediction error (ab-PE)×OC interaction replicated in both the MB t+1-restricted model (GLMM estimate=-0.439±0.151, p=0.004) and the full model across all mini-blocks (GLMM estimate=-0.110±0.048, p=0.022; Supplementary Fig. S1g-j).

No OC-related effect reversed in direction relative to the online study. The nonreplication of the OC performance correlations and s-PE model is most plausibly attributable to insufficient statistical power: the fMRI cohort is approximately five times smaller than the online sample, and the expected effect sizes (ρ≈-0.10 to -0.20) require N≥100-150 to reach α=0.05 with adequate power. The replication of the ab-PE×OC interaction (the strongest and most theoretically central finding of the online study) across both timepoints provides direct behavioural support for the neuroimaging analyses that follow. We therefore interpret the fMRI behavioural data as confirming the key prediction-error-based mechanism linking OC traits to impaired structural updating, while acknowledging that the smaller sample was insufficient to detect the weaker correlational effects observed in the online cohort.

Supplementary Note 2 -ICAR robustness analyses

Supplementary Note 3 -Insight into OC-related impairments

To test whether the same individuals drove both OC-related impairments, we estimated subject-level sensitivity to each prediction error signal separately. For ab-PE sensitivity, we quantified how strongly each participant's switching behaviour on MBt+1 predictor trials was driven by the pair-level prediction error from MBt (1 -feedback accuracy on previous encounter of same pair). For s-PE sensitivity, we quantified how strongly each participant's switch/stay decisions on no-FB trials were driven by the MB-level structural prediction error (1 -mean FB accuracy within the preceding MB). We used two estimation approaches: (1) per-subject logistic regression betas (glmfit on each subject's trials independently), and (2) best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs) from random-slope generalised linear mixed models (switch ~ PE + (PE|subject)), which apply partial pooling and are more stable when trial counts are low (median 16 trials/subject for ab-PE). Spearman correlations and Bayesian correlation tests (uniform prior on ρ) 69 were used to assess the relationship between the two sensitivity measures and with OC scores.
