To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 21 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
ps > .42 Under the strict classification, performance and learning effects did not differ statistically significantly, F(1, 11.70) = 0.25, p = .629, and this conclusion remained unchanged under both alternative classifications (both ps > .42).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 10 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

When a statement is expected

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

The rapid emergence of ChatGPT in education was accompanied by an equally rapid proliferation of meta-analyses synthesizing its effects on educational outcomes. We conducted a systematic epistemological audit of the first wave of these meta-analyses, evaluating both their methodological and reporting quality and the primary evidence they synthesized. We included meta-analyses published after the release of ChatGPT that quantitatively synthesized its effects on educational outcomes. Five meta-analyses comprising 87 unique primary studies met our inclusion criteria. We also reconstructed their quantitative evidence base and conducted exploratory three-level random-effects metaanalyses to examine effect magnitude, heterogeneity, and robustness, and how these varied with theoretically and methodologically relevant characteristics. The five meta-analyses generally searched multiple databases, specified eligibility criteria, and examined heterogeneity and potential publication bias, but showed limitations in independent screening and coding, preregistration, open data and code, and transparent reporting. The underlying primary evidence likewise showed methodological and reporting limitations and substantial conceptual heterogeneity. Among 69 classifiable primary studies, 79.7% assessed performance exclusively, whereas only 11.6% assessed learning exclusively. The reconstructed meta-analysis yielded a positive average effect (g = 0.83, 95% CI [0.68, 0.97]), but heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 94.06%; 95% prediction interval [-0.75, 2.41]), and estimates were markedly attenuated in sensitivity analyses addressing small-study and selection effects. Although positive effects remained among stronger studies assessing learning, increasingly stringent quality criteria rapidly reduced the evidence base, limiting the certainty and generalizability of conclusions about learning effects. Overall, our findings caution against interpreting a single pooled estimate as a stable general effect of ChatGPT on learning and highlight the need for more rigorous, conceptually precise, transparent, and reproducible research.

A Critical Epistemological Audit of Early Evidence Syntheses in Generative AI

Educational Research: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Introduction

The release of ChatGPT in November 2022 marked a pivotal moment in the public and scientific discourse surrounding generative artificial intelligence (genAI), particularly in education (Fütterer et al., 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023). As large language models (LLMs) became widely accessible, researchers rapidly began investigating their potential effects on educational outcomes. Meta-analyses soon followed, synthesizing these early findings and drawing broader conclusions about ChatGPT's educational effects.

Such syntheses carry considerable responsibility because meta-analytic findings can shape subsequent research and inform educational practice and policy. They are also often interpreted as providing robust, generalizable evidence. For example, Deng et al. (2025) reported a large effect (g = 0.71) of ChatGPT on academic performance, operationalized as academic achievement. The study subsequently received considerable scholarly attention and has been repeatedly cited as evidence that ChatGPT can improve academic performance and learning. This rapid uptake illustrates how early meta-analytic findings can quickly become part of the evidentiary basis for broader claims about genAI in education.

Yet the speed with which this evidence base emerged raises questions about its maturity. Rigorous empirical studies require time for study design, ethical approval, recruitment, data collection, analysis, and publication. Nevertheless, many primary studies included in early ChatGPT meta-analyses were already published in 2023 (see Table A), and several meta-analyses synthesizing this literature were submitted by mid to late 2024. Thus, within approximately two years of ChatGPT's public release, primary evidence had not only been generated but had already been quantitatively synthesized into general claims about educational effectiveness. This compressed timeline is particularly relevant given two established challenges in meta-analysis: the garbage-in-garbage-out problem and the apples-to-oranges problem (e.g., Borenstein et al., 2021; Harrer et al., 2021). Even a methodologically rigorous meta-analysis cannot compensate for weak or inadequately reported primary studies. Likewise, heterogeneity is not inherently problematic, but pooled estimates become difficult to interpret when substantively different outcomes, interventions, or assessment methods are combined without adequately examining these differences. Early evidence may additionally be susceptible to publication bias (Ferguson & Heene, 2012), particularly in a rapidly developing and highly visible research area such as genAI. Under these conditions, quantitative synthesis may convey greater certainty than the underlying evidence warrants.

Against this backdrop, we provide a systematic epistemological audit of the first wave of meta-analytic evidence on ChatGPT in education. We evaluate the methodological quality, reporting, and transparency of the identified meta-analyses and assess the methodological quality of their underlying primary studies. We further reconstruct the quantitative evidence base by verifying and, where possible, recalculating effect sizes and independently recoding the outcomes assessed. Of particular importance, within cognitive outcomes we distinguish immediate performance from learning, because successful task performance, especially when ChatGPT remains available, does not necessarily demonstrate a durable change in learners' knowledge or competence. Finally, we conduct an exploratory multilevel meta-analysis of the reconstructed evidence base to examine the magnitude, heterogeneity, and robustness of estimated effects and whether they vary with theoretically and methodologically relevant study characteristics. Together, these analyses address not only how the early evidence was synthesized, but also what evidence was synthesized and what conclusions it can reasonably support.

We deliberately focus on ChatGPT rather than the broader and increasingly heterogeneous range of genAI systems. This narrower scope provides a more reproducible basis for tracing meta-analyses, primary studies, and effect sizes and for evaluating whether ostensibly similar studies examine comparable interventions. We therefore treat the early ChatGPT literature as a historically bounded case of evidence formation in a rapidly developing field, rather than as a characterization of the continuously expanding literature on genAI in education. Examining this first wave lets us investigate how quickly emerging primary findings were aggregated and translated into broader claims about educational effectiveness before the evidence base fully matured.

Theoretical Background

Meta-Analyses as a Core Element of Evidence-Based Research

Whereas traditional empirical studies focus on individuals, specimens, or other units as the basis for statistical analysis, meta-analyses use primary studies as their units of analysis (Glass, 1976). Meta-analyses systematically synthesize the available evidence about a clearly defined research question by combining and statistically summarizing results across studies (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001). They provide a comprehensive overview of current research and are especially valuable for estimating intervention effectiveness. When conducted rigorously, meta-analyses are a cornerstone of cumulative science (Cooper et al., 2019). They can inform research, guide policy decisions, and shape educational and clinical practice, especially when the meta-analysis is methodologically strong and the results are interpreted with care (Higgins et al., 2019). As such, meta-analyses are often highly influential, as they are frequently cited and serve as a basis for evidence-based recommendations in science, policy, and practice (Harrer et al., 2021). For a prominent example of the broad reach and impact of meta-analyses in education, see Hattie (2009 Hattie ( , 2023)).

Meta-Analyses on genAI Under Pressure: Ensuring Quality and Scientific Integrity

The current academic climate creates strong incentives for producing meta-analyses, given their high citation rates and perceived scientific prestige (Ioannidis, 2016; Patsopoulos, 2005). At the same time, journals often strongly prefer publishing meta-analyses, even when their methodological rigor or scientific contribution may be limited (Harrer et al., 2021).

These pressures are amplified in emerging fields (Ioannidis, 2016) such as genAI, where researchers seem to strive to be first movers and tend to publish rapid syntheses without sufficient theoretical grounding, robust evidence, or methodological rigor (Weidlich et al., 2025; Yarkoni, 2022). The increasing accessibility of AI-supported tools for literature screening and data extraction may further fuel this trend (Fütterer, Campos, et al., 2026), giving the illusion of simplicity while undermining the core principles of meta-analysisnamely, providing a careful, theory-informed synthesis of robust empirical evidence (Moreau & Gamble, 2022; Pigott & Polanin, 2020).

When meta-analyses are conducted under (time) pressure, whether due to funding deadlines, publication expectations, or hype surrounding a topic, there is a risk that researchers may cut corners and compromise methodological quality. For instance, to save time, they may skip coding the quality of primary studies, omit double-coding procedures, forgo preregistration, or fail to fully adhere to established reporting standards. In the worstcase scenario, such shortcuts not only reduce methodological rigor but may also violate principles of good scientific practice and neglect core elements of the open science framework, such as transparency and reproducibility.

To get an initial sense of potential concerns about the reliability of the broader evidence base, we conducted a systematic bibliometric analysis of publications on AI in education from 1990 to 2025 and matched these records against the Retraction Watch database. This period encompasses both pre-generative-AI research and the rapid expansion of genAI research following the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022. Among the complete corpus of publications identified before screening, 1.27% had been retracted. After screening for substantive relevance to AI and education, the proportion of retracted publications increased to 2.09%. These proportions are noteworthy when considered against broader patterns in the scientific literature. For context, a recent Nature editorial reported that approximately 0.2% of articles published in 2022 had subsequently been retracted, already three times the rate observed a decade earlier (Nature, 2025). Although these estimates are not directly comparable because they differ in publication populations, time periods, and observation windows, the comparatively high prevalence of retractions in our corpus further indicates that the reliability and traceability of the rapidly expanding AI-in-education evidence base warrant close scrutiny. Importantly, retractions typically occur with a considerable delay; recent bibliometric analyses of retracted AI publications reported median times to retraction of approximately 17-18 months (e.g., Sridharan & Sivaramakrishnan, 2026). Consequently, the most recent genAI literature has had substantially less time to undergo post-publication scrutiny than earlier research. This issue is directly relevant to the present audit: Wang and Fan's (2025) meta-analysis, which forms part of the first wave of ChatGPT meta-analyses examined here, was subsequently retracted following concerns about discrepancies in the meta-analysis. Taken together, these observations do not establish that research on AI in education is generally less reliable than research in other fields. Rather, they underscore the importance of rigorous quality appraisal when synthesizing evidence in a rapidly expanding literature.

Rigorous evidence generation takes time. Even under ideal conditions, common errors occur: about 5.5% of relevant primary studies typically go unidentified during screening (Waffenschmidt et al., 2019; Z. Wang et al., 2020), and effect sizes are sometimes extracted incorrectly from original studies (Lakens et al., 2016). These general concerns may be particularly consequential in the rapidly emerging genAI literature. Weidlich et al. (2025) characterize parts of the early ChatGPT literature as "fast science", pointing to poorly specified and heterogeneous interventions, inadequately described comparison conditions, and outcome measures that do not necessarily provide evidence of learning. Relatedly, Bauer et al. (2025) caution against research driven primarily by current AI hype and argue for moving beyond whether AI works toward theory-driven investigations of why, for whom, and under which instructional conditions it supports learning. Concerns also extend to the design and measurement of primary studies. Lawson et al. (2026) show that experimental comparisons may confound ChatGPT use with other instructional features, complicating the causal attribution of observed effects specifically to ChatGPT. Similarly, Bardach et al. (2025) argue that claims about the effects of AI applications in education are sometimes overstated and risk misrepresenting the strength of the available evidence. Drawing on educational intervention research (e.g., Kraft, 2020; Slavin & Madden, 2011) Quality issues in meta-analyses frequently stem from the selection and characteristics of the included primary studies. Two well-established methodological problems are particularly relevant in this context: (1) the garbage-in, garbage-out problem and (2) the apples-and-oranges problem (Borenstein et al., 2021; Greco et al., 2013; Harrer et al., 2021).

First, the garbage-in-garbage-out problem emphasizes that the quality of the evidence a meta-analysis generates is limited by the quality of the primary studies it includes (Greco et al., 2013). Even a methodologically rigorous meta-analysis cannot compensate for biased, poorly designed, or inadequately measured primary research. Importantly, methodological characteristics can systematically affect the magnitude of estimated effects. In educational intervention research, for example, effect sizes vary by study design, sample size, publication status, and whether outcomes were assessed using researcher-developed or independent measures (Cheung & Slavin, 2016; see also Bardach et al., 2025). Thus, documenting methodological shortcomings is only a first step. The critical empirical question is whether estimated effects vary with the quality and measurement characteristics of the underlying studies. Accordingly, in this audit we assess multiple dimensions of primary-study quality separately and examine theoretically relevant study and assessment characteristics as potential moderators of effect sizes.

Second, the apples-and-oranges problem concerns synthesizing studies that differ in conceptually or methodologically meaningful ways without adequately accounting for these differences. This issue is especially relevant in emerging fields such as genAI, where study designs, outcome measures, and theoretical frameworks often vary widely. Although a metaanalytic estimate can be computed across heterogeneous studies, its substantive interpretation depends on whether the included studies address a sufficiently coherent research question and whether they adequately characterize and examine theoretically meaningful differences (Harrer et al., 2021). As Harrer et al. (2021) point out, "the goal of meta-analyses is not to heedlessly throw everything together that can be combined" (para. 1). Instead, the scope and specificity of a meta-analysis should be guided by a practically relevant and well-defined research question that meaningfully transcends the findings of individual studies (Borenstein et al., 2021). Heterogeneity is therefore not inherently problematic; rather, interpretability becomes limited when substantively different studies are combined into a single estimate without adequately accounting for relevant sources of variation, for example through theoretically informed moderator analyses. Therefore, both the methodological quality of the meta-analysis and the rigor of the primary studies it summarizes must be high to produce meaningful, trustworthy insights.

Theoretical Framing of Educational Outcomes in Generative AI Research

Distinguishing Outcome Constructs and Their Assessment

Evaluating claims about the educational effects of ChatGPT first requires clarity about what constitutes an educational outcome. We distinguish broad outcome domains based on an adapted taxonomy inspired by Plass and Pawar (2020): cognitive and achievement outcomes, motivational-affective outcomes, and behavioral outcomes. Within these domains, we can distinguish more specific constructs, including declarative and procedural knowledge, skills, learning strategies and metacognition, higher-order cognitive abilities, interest, self-efficacy, motivation, emotions, attitudes, behavioral engagement, academic achievement, and transfer. These distinctions matter because an effect on one outcome cannot automatically be generalized to another. For example, improved task performance, increased self-efficacy, and greater transfer may all represent valuable educational outcomes, but they support different substantive conclusions about what ChatGPT affects.

Importantly, the construct being assessed must be distinguished from the method used to assess it (Kane, 2001; Strauss & Smith, 2009). The same broad outcome domain can include self-reported measures, standardized or researcher-developed tests, behavioral observations, and assessments of produced artifacts. These measurement approaches are not interchangeable, as the assessment method shapes the inferences that can be drawn about the underlying construct. Self-reported metacognitive strategy use, for example, provides different evidence than a performance-based measure of metacognition, just as the quality of a genAI-assisted written product provides different evidence than performance on an independently completed knowledge test. Moreover, researcher-developed assessments may be more closely aligned with an intervention than independent assessments, potentially yielding different effect estimates (Bardach et al., 2025; Cheung & Slavin, 2016). We therefore treat outcome construct and assessment method as separate dimensions and examine whether estimated effects differ across assessment methods (Figure 1).

Performance, Learning, and Human-AI Hybrid Performance

A second distinction concerns performance and learning. Performance refers to observable success during or immediately following task execution, whereas learning implies a relatively durable change in knowledge or competence that remains available beyond the conditions under which it was acquired (Yan et al., 2025). Consequently, strong performance does not necessarily indicate learning. This distinction matters for genAI because the technology can contribute directly to the performance being measured. A high-quality essay written with ChatGPT, for example, provides evidence about the performance of the human-AI system, but does not by itself establish that the learner has acquired the knowledge or skills required to produce a comparable essay independently. Conversely, assisted performance is not an uninformative outcome: it captures an important potential benefit of genAI. The crucial issue is that effects on assisted performance and effects on independently available human competence answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable evidence of learning (Yan et al., 2025). This distinction can be understood in terms of how cognitive work is distributed between the learner and AI. GenAI can reduce demands on learners by retrieving information, generating explanations, structuring text, or producing candidate solutions. Such offloading can help when it frees resources for learning-relevant cognitive activity, but it can harm learning when AI replaces the constructive processes through which knowledge and skills are acquired. From a cognitive-load perspective, reducing processing demands therefore does not necessarily imply deeper learning; empirical work similarly suggests that LLM support can reduce experienced mental effort while accompanying shallower scientific inquiry (Stadler et al., 2024). More broadly, distributed-cognition and cognitive-offloading accounts suggest that what matters is not simply whether cognitive activity is externalized, but which processes learners delegate and which they keep under their control (Kuhn et al., 2026). This creates a particularly important distinction between human performance and human-AI hybrid performance. AI assistance can improve the quality of the jointly produced output without necessarily producing corresponding gains in the learner's independent capability. Recent research accordingly points to potential dissociations between AIsupported task performance and metacognitive accuracy (Fernandes et al., 2026), whereas other work emphasizes designing AI support to prioritize learning over immediate performance (Fütterer, Gerjets, et al., 2026). Whether an outcome was assessed while AI support remained available is therefore theoretically relevant.

Learning, in contrast, is more strongly supported when changes in knowledge or competence persist beyond immediate AI-supported task execution, for example, through retention, transfer, delayed assessment, or independent application after support is withdrawn (Yan et al., 2025). This does not mean every immediate post-test lacks information about learning, nor that only delayed tests can indicate learning. An immediate post-test using an appropriate competence measure can provide evidence of learning, but the inference becomes stronger when the acquired competence persists or generalizes beyond the immediate instructional and support conditions. Treatment alignment constitutes a related but distinct issue: highly intervention-aligned measures may capture proximal gains more readily than distal or independent assessments (Bardach et al., 2025). Accordingly, performance versus learning, assessment method, and degree of treatment alignment should not be collapsed into a single distinction (Figure 1).

That means, combining heterogeneous educational outcomes is not inherently problematic, provided researchers theoretically identify, code, and examine the dimensions along which they differ. A pooled estimate across declarative knowledge, self-efficacy, writing quality, and transfer may provide a broad descriptive summary, but its substantive interpretation depends on whether researchers investigate differences among these outcomes rather than obscure them. The same applies to differences in assessment method and in the conditions under which performance is measured.

The Present Study

The swift increase in research on ChatGPT in education has been accompanied by growing concerns about the quality and interpretability of the emerging evidence. Recent conceptual and methodological critiques have pointed to limitations in study design, intervention and control conditions, outcome measurement, and the synthesis of heterogeneous evidence, raising questions about whether the available empirical literature provides a sufficiently robust basis for broad conclusions about the educational effectiveness of genAI and ChatGPT in particular (Bardach et al., 2025; E. Bauer et al., 2025; Lawson et al., 2026; Weidlich et al., 2025).

Despite these important contributions, the discussion has remained largely conceptual or has focused on isolated methodological issues. To our knowledge, no study has yet provided a similarly comprehensive and integrated examination of ChatGPT meta-analyses while simultaneously evaluating the methodological quality of both the meta-analyses and their underlying primary studies. This study addresses this gap by systematically auditing the early meta-analytic evidence base for ChatGPT. Specifically, we examine the first wave of meta-analyses identified through our systematic search conducted in March 2025, evaluate their methodological quality, assess the methodological quality of the primary studies on which they relied, verify and, where possible, recalculate reported effect sizes, and conduct an independent multilevel meta-analysis based on the reconstructed evidence base. Rather than providing an exhaustive assessment of the continually expanding ChatGPT literature, our analysis provides an empirical case study of how evidence was produced, synthesized, and interpreted during the initial period following the introduction of a rapidly adopted In exploratory analyses, we further examined whether effect sizes varied by theoretically and methodologically relevant characteristics of the primary studies and outcomes.

Coding

We conducted coding at three levels: the meta-analysis level, the primary-study level, and the effect-size level.

At the meta-analyses level, we coded, in addition to study characteristics (e.g., publication year) and reported effect sizes, the methodological and reporting quality. We developed a comprehensive coding manual and a corresponding coding scheme (accessible at Zenodo). To assess the methodological and reporting quality of the five meta-analyses, we developed a structured coding scheme based on established recommendations and appraisal frameworks for research syntheses. Specifically, our coding was informed by the AMSTAR framework for assessing the methodological quality of systematic reviews (Shea et al., 2007), methodological recommendations for conducting and reporting meta-analyses (Moreau & Gamble, 2022; Pigott & Polanin, 2020), and recent adaptations of AMSTAR for educational meta-analyses (e.g., Emslander et al., 2025). Because no single instrument fully captures both methodological quality and contemporary standards of transparent, reproducible metaanalytic practice in educational research, we integrated and extended these established frameworks rather than relying on a single checklist. Consequently, our coding scheme comprised 26 dichotomously coded indicators covering conceptual clarity (e.g., clearly defined research questions and hypotheses), methodological rigor (e.g., comprehensiveness of the literature search, independent screening and coding, assessment of study quality and risk of bias, heterogeneity analyses, moderator analyses, and justification of the effect size model), reporting quality (e.g., characteristics and list of included studies, adherence to reporting standards), and transparency and reproducibility (e.g., preregistration, data availability, availability of analytic code). We made the coding manual and complete coding scheme publicly available at Zenodo. The first author coded all variables for the five metaanalyses, and the second author subsequently reviewed all coding decisions for accuracy and consistency. Thus, the second author verified the meta-analysis-level coding, but it was not independently double-coded. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

At the primary study level, we coded standard study characteristics (e.g., year of publication; Card & Little, 2016), methodological and reporting quality, outcome types (e.g., cognitive, affective), ChatGPT activities, targeted learning activities, cognitive support, and effect sizes. We operationalized primary-study quality using eight binary indicators spanning three dimensions: sample quality (representativeness of the sample), measurement quality (reported reliability coefficients and validity evidence), and study design and reporting quality (group assignment, baseline equivalence, study design, clearly defined research questions, and clearly defined hypotheses; see Scherer & Emslander, 2025). Following Scherer and Emslander (Scherer & Emslander, 2025), we first evaluated whether the primary study quality indicators could be represented as a reflective latent construct before deriving composite quality measures. Because all quality indicators were binary, we conducted the analyses using tetrachoric correlations. We first performed an exploratory factor analysis using the psych package (v2.5.6; Revelle, 2025) in R v4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025). We smoothed the tetrachoric correlation matrix because it was not positive definite, and parallel analysis did not support a substantively interpretable factor solution. We subsequently specified a confirmatory factor analysis using the lavaan package (v0.6.21; Rosseel, 2012), with the weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator for ordered categorical indicators. The hypothesized reflective measurement model showed insufficient fit, indicating that the quality indicators did not represent a common latent construct.

Therefore, we used a model-free representation of study quality rather than a model-driven composite score (Scherer & Emslander, 2025). We computed an overall quality index for each primary study as the arithmetic mean of the eight available binary quality indicators. All quality indicators contributed equally to the average score, and we ignored missing values when calculating the mean.

At the effect-size level, we additionally coded several theoretically relevant characteristics derived from the distinctions introduced in our theoretical framework (Figure 1). First, we distinguished effects on performance from evidence more indicative of durable learning. Second, we distinguished performance assessed while learners continue to receive AI assistance, which represents human-AI hybrid performance, from performance attributable to the learner without concurrent AI support. Third, we distinguished outcome constructs from their assessment methods, particularly because self-reports, researcherdeveloped assessments, standardized tests, behavioral observations, and produced artifacts differ in the inferences they support and may yield systematically different effect estimates.

Coding these characteristics at the effect-size level allowed us to examine whether effect estimates varied across these theoretically meaningful sources of heterogeneity.

To ensure consistent and valid ratings for the primary studies, we distinguished between low-and high-inference variables. We coded low-inference variables such as publication year, journal name, or whether data availability was mentioned once by a single trained rater. Before coding the full sample, two raters independently coded a random sample of 18 primary studies (26.5% of those included in Deng et al., 2025) to assess initial interrater reliability for high-inference variables, such as targeted learning activities. Across 17 coded variables, the mean Cohen's κ was .80. After this calibration phase, two raters independently double-coded all high-inference variables across all primary studies. We resolved discrepancies through discussion or, when necessary, adjudicated them by an author. Effect sizes were coded as Hedges' g. When possible, we calculated effect sizes directly from reported raw data (i.e., group means, standard deviations, and sample sizes). If these data were unavailable or insufficient, we derived effect sizes from other reported test statistics (e.g., t-values, F-values, or equivalent statistics). All formulas used to calculate or transform effect sizes are provided in the Online Supplement. We coded effect sizes so that positive values consistently indicated a desirable effect in favor of the experimental group.

Accordingly, we determined the direction of each effect size based on the substantive meaning of the outcome. For example, we coded higher levels of achievement or motivation as positive effects, whereas higher levels of undesirable outcomes (e.g., boredom) were reverse-coded so positive values always reflected beneficial intervention effects.

Metanalytical Analyses

We followed the recommendations of Harrer et al. (2021) and estimated meta-analytic models in R (v.4.5.0) using the metafor (v4.8.0; Viechtbauer, 2010), clubSandwich (v0.6.0; J. Pustejovsky, 2024), and meta (v.8.5.0; Balduzzi et al., 2019) packages. Prior to model estimation, we conducted preliminary data analyses following the PRIMED framework (J. E. Pustejovsky et al., 2026). These analyses examined the amount and dependency structure of the available data; the distributions, variability, and missingness of potential moderators; sampling-error and precision characteristics; and the marginal and within-study distributions of effect sizes. Because primary studies frequently contributed multiple effect sizes, we fitted three-level random-effects models that distinguished sampling variance, within-study heterogeneity, and between-study heterogeneity. Compared with a two-level model, the three-level model showed a statistically significantly better fit, χ²(1) = 9.99, p = .002. We estimated models using restricted maximum likelihood (REML); for likelihood-ratio comparisons of the two-and three-level specifications, we re-estimated models using maximum likelihood. For statistical inference, we used CR2 cluster-robust variance estimation with small-sample adjustments, clustering effect sizes at the primary-study level.

The primary dataset included each effect size appearing in at least one examined metaanalysis only once, preventing effect sizes reused across meta-analyses from receiving disproportionate weight. We identified potential outliers from an initial three-level model using externally studentized residuals and a two-sided Bonferroni-adjusted critical value. The primary model excluded effect sizes exceeding this threshold, whereas sensitivity analyses retained these effects. We report the pooled standardized mean difference (Hedges' g), its 95% confidence interval, and the 95% prediction interval. We decomposed heterogeneity into within-and between-primary-study components and summarized these components using multilevel I² statistics.

Moderator analyses used mixed-effects, three-level meta-regressions while retaining the primary model's random-effects structure and CR2 cluster-robust inference. We generally examined moderators in separate models; we entered categorical moderators as factor variables and standardized continuous moderators. For the multidimensional primary-study quality model, we entered the three quality-domain scores jointly. We based statistical inference for moderators on cluster-robust coefficient tests and omnibus tests of moderator effects. Pairwise contrasts between outcome categories were examined following the corresponding omnibus moderator analysis, with p values adjusted for multiple comparisons using both the Holm (1979) and Benjamini-Hochberg (1995) procedures.

We examined the robustness of the primary findings across alternative analytic samples and dependency specifications. These analyses included models retaining Bonferroni-identified outliers; models retaining repeated representations of the same effect sizes across the source meta-analyses; and models including all effect sizes identified in the reconstructed evidence base, irrespective of whether they had been quantitatively synthesized in the original meta-analyses. When we retained repeated effect-size representations, we included an additional meta-analysis-level random effect to account for their nesting within source meta-analyses.

We examined small-study effects and potential dissemination bias using complementary approaches. We visually inspected standard and contour-enhanced funnel plots and ran an Egger regression (Egger et al., 1997) regressing the standard normal deviate (Hedges' g/SE) on precision (1/SE), with CR2 inference clustered at the primary-study level.

We additionally conducted the precision-effect test (PET), regressing effect sizes on their standard errors, and the precision-effect estimate with standard error (PEESE), regressing effect sizes on their sampling variances, using multilevel models with CR2 inference. For the PET-PEESE procedure, we selected the PEESE limit estimate when the PET intercept was statistically significantly positive in a one-sided test at α = .05; otherwise, we retained the PET estimate. We interpreted funnel-plot asymmetry and these regression-based analyses as diagnostics of small-study effects rather than as direct evidence of publication bias. As an additional model-based sensitivity analysis for dissemination bias, we fitted robust Bayesian meta-analytic (RoBMA) models that averaged across models differing in the presence or absence of an effect, heterogeneity, and publication-bias adjustment. We conducted RoBMA analyses on the full dataset and repeated them after excluding the Bonferroni-identified outliers. Because the primary data contained statistically dependent effect sizes, we also compared multilevel RoBMA specifications that accounted for primary-study clustering with a single-level specification that ignored this dependency.

Results

General Observations and Descriptive Statistics

All five meta-analyses explicitly or implicitly aimed to synthesize evidence of the effects of ChatGPT on educational outcomes and, in many cases, especially student learning outcomes (e.g., academic achievement) as assessed in quasi-experimental and experimental studies, although their stated outcome scopes differed.

For instance, Deng et al. (2025) state that they included only studies that "focuses on the impact of ChatGPT on student learning" (Table 3). He and Cordie (2024) defined their inclusion criterion as studies that "focused specifically on the effects of ChatGPT on learning outcomes" (p. 51). Heung and Chiu (2025) focused their analyses on student engagement (i.e., behavioral, cognitive, and emotional), whereas Liu et al. (2025) specified as an inclusion criterion that "studies included should examine the impact of ChatGPT on students' academic achievement" (p. 5). Wang and Fan (2025) reported excluding studies "unrelated to the impact of ChatGPT on student learning" (p. 4) and further stated that they included studies that "investigate how ChatGPT affects student performance, learning perception, or higherorder thinking" (p. 4). However, none of the meta-analyses focused on a specific domain (e.g., mathematics), educational level (e.g., primary education), or outcome type (e.g., achievement, motivation).

We observed considerable overlap between the meta-analyses. Of the 87 unique primary studies, 45 (51.7%) appeared in at least two of the five meta-analyses; 21 in two, 18 in three, five in four, and one in all five. Quality of Meta-Analyses (RQ1)

To address RQ1, we examined adherence to 26 transparency and quality criteria across the five included meta-analyses (Figure 4). Overall, the meta-analyses consistently implemented several established meta-analytic procedures. All five conducted literature searches across multiple databases, reported inclusion and exclusion criteria, assessed heterogeneity and potential publication bias, reported confidence intervals, and explained their choice of effect-size model. At the same time, important limitations emerged regarding procedures intended to reduce researcher-dependent decisions and facilitate reproducibility.

None of the five meta-analyses combined independent screening with independent coding by at least two raters. Across the available criteria, the proportion fulfilled ranged from .52 to .68 (M = .62, SD = .07).

In addition, the selection of primary studies was relatively restrictive. None of the five meta-analyses included grey literature, and they excluded non-English-language studies, which runs counter to a core quality criterion of systematic literature reviews: identifying the available evidence as comprehensively as possible (see Pigott & Polanin, 2020). Moreover, none of the meta-analyses used community calls or comparable strategies to identify unpublished studies. Open science practices were similarly limited. None of the five metaanalyses was preregistered, data were available only upon request, if at all, and analytic code was not publicly available.

Beyond these methodological and transparency criteria, we identified concerns regarding the integrity of parts of the underlying evidence base. As of August 4, 2026, the primary report by Chan et al. (2022), which was included in the meta-analyses by Liu et al. (2025) and Wang and Fan (2025), had been retracted. Moreover, during the preparation of the present manuscript, the meta-analysis by Wang and Fan (2025) was itself retracted after the editor identified discrepancies that undermined confidence in the validity of the analyses and resulting conclusions (see retraction note here https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-07310-z).

These developments occurred against the backdrop of several transparency and reporting issues that we identified when reconstructing the evidence base of the included meta-analyses. This retraction is consistent with several transparency and reporting issues we identified across the included meta-analyses. For instance, in Wang and Fan (2025), incomplete documentation of included primary studies required manual reconstruction of the study sample, including adding previously unlisted studies. Furthermore, inconsistencies among the main text, tables, and reference list; incorrect author identification (e.g., first names used instead of surnames); and inconsistent reporting of publication years complicated the identification of primary studies and raised concerns about the potential propagation of citation errors across reviews. Similarly, Deng et al. (2025) reported including 62 of 69 identified studies but did not specify which studies were synthesized, making it necessary to infer the analyzed sample from supplementary figures. In addition, discrepancies between textual descriptions and graphical results (e.g., regarding the treatment of outlier studies) reduced the traceability of the analyses. Overall, inconsistent reporting of study identification, publication details, and inclusion decisions substantially limited transparency and reproducibility, complicated cross-meta-analytic comparisons, and increased the risk of study misclassification and duplicate coding.

Quality of Primary Studies on ChatGPT (RQ2)

To address RQ2, we examined eight methodological and reporting criteria across the 87 unique primary studies (Figure 5). Adherence was highest for study design: 98.9% (86/87) met the criterion for a higher-quality study design. In contrast, only 6.9% (6/87) met the criterion for use of a representative sample. For the remaining criteria, 59.8% (52/87) met the reliability criterion, 52.9% (46/87) the group-assignment criterion, 49.4% (43/87) the validity criterion, 49.4% (43/87) the criterion for clearly defined hypotheses, 48.8% (42/86) the baseline-equivalence criterion, and 44.8% (39/87) the criterion for a clearly defined research question.

Across the eight criteria, the formative primary-study quality score averaged M = .51 (SD = .18, Mdn = .50, range = .13-.88), indicating substantial variation in methodological and reporting characteristics. The three constituent quality domains also differed descriptively: design quality was highest (M = .67, SD = .33), followed by reporting quality (M = .47, SD = .29) and measurement quality (M = .39, SD = .26).

Open-science practices were limited. Data were reported as available for 39.1% (34/87) of the primary studies, whereas only 3.4% (3/87) were preregistered and only 1.1% (1/87) made analytic code available. Among the 86 primary studies for which all eight quality criteria could be evaluated, four studies met only one of the eight criteria. At the other end of the distribution, one study met seven of the eight criteria, and none met all eight.

Aggregation of Outcomes (RQ3)

To examine whether comparable outcomes were aggregated, we first examined the distribution of broad outcome domains across the five meta-analyses (Figure 6). Cognitive outcomes constituted most effect sizes in every meta-analysis, ranging from 55.6% to 64.8%.

However, all five meta-analyses combined cognitive outcomes with motivational-affective outcomes (19.7%-33.3%) and, with few exceptions, behavioral (0%-6.6%) and other or mixed outcomes (1.5%-11.5%). Thus, although the relative distributions were broadly similar across meta-analyses, none of the quantitative syntheses was restricted to a single homogeneous outcome domain.

The meta-analyses differed in how they handled heterogeneity in outcome constructs.

Several meta-analyses estimated separate pooled effects for broad outcome domains. Deng et al. (2025), for example, distinguished academic performance, affective-motivational states, higher-order thinking propensities, self-efficacy, and mental effort, whereas Wang and Fan (2025) separately synthesized learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking. Heung and Chiu (2025) distinguished behavioral, cognitive, and emotional engagement in addition to an overall engagement estimate. In contrast, He and Cordie (2024) pooled different learning outcomes, including learning achievement, motivation, and skills, into a single overall estimate. Thus, outcome heterogeneity was partly addressed by estimating domain-specific effects, but outcome aggregation differed considerably across the five meta-analyses.

We next examined whether the outcomes quantitatively synthesized matched the outcome scope explicitly defined by each meta-analysis. Across the reconstructed quantitative syntheses, 75.4% (202/268) of effect sizes fit the respective meta-analysis's stated outcome scope, whereas 24.6% (66/268) fell outside that scope. The degree of alignment differed substantially across meta-analyses: the proportion of fitting effect sizes ranged from 47.6% to 96.9%. Specifically, 71.7% of effect sizes fit the specified scope in Deng et al. (2025), 47.6% in He and Cordie (2024), 96.9% in Heung and Chiu (2025), 77.3% in Liu et al. (2025), and 88.5% in Wang and Fan (2025).

The nature of the mismatches also differed across meta-analyses. For example, among the effect sizes classified as outside the stated scope in Deng et al. (2025), learning-strategy and metacognitive outcomes constituted the largest category, followed by other or unclear outcomes and academic-achievement outcomes. In He and Cordie (2024), most non-fitting effect sizes represented academic achievement. These findings indicate that the quantitative evidence synthesized in some meta-analyses extended substantially beyond their explicitly defined outcome scope.

We additionally distinguished between outcomes reflecting performance and outcomes intended to capture learning. Under our strict classification, of the 69 primary studies we could classify at the study level, 55 (79.7%) assessed performance outcomes exclusively, eight (11.6%) assessed learning outcomes exclusively, and six (8.7%) included both. Because the distinction between performance and learning depends partly on how immediate post-use outcomes are conceptualized (i.e., learning is operationalized), we additionally examined two alternative classifications. The proportion of learning-only studies ranged from 8.7% to 20.3% across these classifications, whereas performance-only studies consistently constituted the majority (71.0%-82.6%). Thus, the conclusion that the literature predominantly assessed performance rather than learning held across alternative operationalizations. This distinction is illustrated by studies in which students completed tasks with versus without ChatGPT and then evaluated the resulting task products. For instance, the study by Ahmed Moneus and Al-Wasy (2024), which was included in the meta-analysis by Deng et al. (2025), compared university students who completed the same legal translation task either with ChatGPT or without AI support and evaluated the resulting translations using expert ratings of accuracy, content, language, style, and overall translation quality. Although the study was synthesized as evidence of ChatGPT's educational effectiveness, it assessed immediate and AI-assisted task performance rather than learning outcomes. Another example is the study by Xue et al. (2024), which was also included in the meta-analysis by Deng et al. (2025) and which conducted a controlled experiment with undergraduate students enrolled in an introductory Java programming (CS1) course, comparing an experimental group with access to ChatGPT 3.5 during a Unified Modeling Language (UML) design and programming assignment to a control group without ChatGPT access, followed by a closedbook post-evaluation. Although the authors referred to these measures as learning outcomes, the meta-analysis used students' task performance on the UML design, Java programming assignment, and immediate post-evaluation as the primary outcome, rather than longer-term learning. Such designs provide information about performance with access to ChatGPT but do not necessarily establish whether students acquired knowledge or skills that persisted independently of ChatGPT support. Consequently, describing all such outcomes as evidence of learning risks conflating performance during ChatGPT-supported activity with learning as a relatively durable change in knowledge or skill.

We also examined whether primary-study quality differed by whether studies assessed performance, learning, or both (Figure 7). Under the strict classification, mean quality scores were similar for performance-only studies (M = .49, SD = .17), learning-only studies (M = .47, SD = .15), and studies containing both types of outcomes (M = .56, SD = .22). A Welch ANOVA indicated no statistically significant differences among these groups, F(2, 9.53) = 0.38, p = .691, η² = .017. The standardized difference between performance-only and learning-only studies was correspondingly small (d = -0.12).

To address RQ4, we examined the targeted learning activities (Figure 8) and forms of cognitive support represented across the five meta-analyses. Exploratory: Meta-Analytic Results

Overall Effect and Preliminary Inspection of the Evidence Base

Given the substantial conceptual and methodological heterogeneity identified in RQ1-RQ4, we did not interpret a pooled effect as a single estimate of ChatGPT's effect on learning. Instead, we used the reconstructed evidence base as the starting point for a series of exploratory stress tests. Specifically, we examined how the apparent overall effect changed when accounting for effect-size dependency, influential observations, differences in outcome conceptualization and methodological quality, and potential small-study and selection effects.

Preliminary inspection of the quantitatively synthesized evidence base identified 203 unique effect sizes from 77 primary studies. Studies contributed an average of 2.64 effect sizes (SD = 2.48, Mdn = 2, range = 1-13), with 55.8% contributing more than one effect size.

The effect-size distribution was highly dispersed and positively skewed (M = 1.17, SD = 2.60, Mdn = 0.76, range = -3.32 to 25.01). As shown in the density plots (Figure A), including the zoomed view of the central portion of the distribution, most effect sizes were concentrated in a much narrower range, whereas a small number of very large positive estimates produced a pronounced right tail. These characteristics underscored both the dependency and heterogeneity of the reconstructed evidence base and motivated the use of multilevel models and cluster-robust inference.

During the coding and verification of the reported effect sizes, we identified numerous inconsistencies and implausible statistical values in several primary studies. These included impossible or highly unlikely effect-size estimates (e.g., a reported correlation of r = 3.93, despite correlations being bounded between -1 and 1; REF), implausibly large standardized mean differences (e.g., Cohen's d = 17.81; REF), and impossible test statistics such as a negative F value (F = -29.041; REF). We also identified unusually large reported varianceexplained estimates (e.g., η² = .88; REF), which, although mathematically possible, are exceptionally rare in educational research and therefore warranted careful verification. Furthermore, recalculating standardized effect sizes from the reported descriptive statistics yielded extremely large Hedges' g values, in some cases exceeding 20 (REF).

Although many of these effect sizes were ultimately excluded from the published metaanalyses, these findings raised concerns regarding the accuracy and consistency of statistical reporting in several primary studies. Consequently, we systematically checked all reported effect sizes against the original study reports and, when possible, recalculated them independently from the available raw descriptive statistics before including them in the present analyses.

We identified five effect sizes as influential outliers using the Bonferroni-adjusted outlier criterion and excluded them from the main model. The resulting analytic sample comprised 198 unique effect sizes nested within 75 primary studies. A three-level randomeffects model with CR2 cluster-robust inference yielded an average effect of g = 0.83, 95% CI [0.68, 0.97], p < .001. Heterogeneity was substantial, Q(197) = 2579.59, p < .001. The estimated variance components indicated heterogeneity both between primary studies (σ² = 0.124) and, particularly, between effect sizes within primary studies (σ² = 0.500).

Decomposing the total variability, 18.74% was attributable to between-study heterogeneity, 75.32% to within-study heterogeneity, and only 5.94% to sampling variance, corresponding to an overall I² of 94.06%. Consistent with this pronounced heterogeneity, the 95% prediction interval was wide, ranging from -0.75 to 2.41. Thus, although the average estimate was positive, effects varied substantially both across and within primary studies, and the model implied that effects in individual settings may range from negative to strongly positive. A likelihood-ratio test further supported the three-level specification over a reduced two-level model, χ²(1) = 9.99, p = .002.

Robustness Across Outcome Conceptualizations and Study Characteristics

We next examined whether the large average effect could be attributed to aggregating substantively different outcome types. Pooled estimates were positive for performance (g = 0.91, 95% CI [0.74, 1.09]), learning (g = 0.80, 95% CI [0.48, 1.12]; Figure 9), and affective outcomes (g = 0.73, 95% CI [0.49, 0.97]). However, the corresponding prediction intervals were wide and included zero for all three categories. Moreover, differences among these outcome categories were not statistically significant, F(2, 21.46) = 2.08, p = .149.

Similarly, a broader four-category classification distinguishing cognitive, motivationalaffective, behavioral, and other or mixed outcomes did not significantly moderate effect sizes, F(3, 10.22) = 2.04, p = .171.

We further examined the distinction between performance and learning using increasingly liberal definitions of learning. Under the strict classification, performance and learning effects did not differ statistically significantly, F(1, 11.70) = 0.25, p = .629, and this conclusion remained unchanged under both alternative classifications (both ps > .42).

Likewise, among performance outcomes, effects did not differ statistically significantly according to whether performance was assessed independently or while participants continued to receive AI assistance, F(1, 15.96) = 0.57, p = .460. Thus, the positive average effect was not exclusively attributable to studies measuring immediate AI-assisted performance.

Overall primary-study quality was negatively, but not statistically significantly, associated with effect size, F(1, 26.23) = 3.19, p = .085, and the three quality domains did not jointly moderate effects, F(3, 29.35) = 1.43, p = .253. Most individual methodological indicators likewise showed no statistically significant association with effect magnitude. One notable exception was the binary study-design classification: effects were smaller among studies classified as using stronger designs, b = -0.33, 95% CI [-0.48, -0.19], F(1, 57.87) = 20.61, p < .001. This finding motivated a more restrictive analysis focusing specifically on evidence for learning.

Progressive Refinement of the Evidence for Learning

Because the preceding analyses showed that the reconstructed literature frequently conflated short-term performance with learning and varied substantially in methodological quality, we conducted an additional exploratory evidence-refinement analysis. Rather than treating the pooled estimate of g = 0.83 as a definitive summary, this analysis progressively restricted the evidence base to studies that more closely addressed the substantive question of whether ChatGPT supports learning.

The main analytic sample contained 198 effect sizes from 75 primary studies.

Restricting the analysis to outcomes meeting our strict definition of learning reduced the evidence base to only 13 effect sizes from 11 primary studies. Importantly, all 11 of these studies were already classified in the stronger study-design category. Thus, further restricting the sample to stronger designs did not reduce the evidence base. Despite this substantial reduction in evidence, the pooled learning effect remained positive, g = 1.00, 95% CI [0.56, 1.44], p < .001.

The evidence base became markedly smaller when we additionally imposed methodological quality criteria. Requiring studies to meet at least four of the eight predefined quality criteria left six effect sizes from six primary studies and yielded g = 0.82, 95% CI [0.09, 1.56], p = .035. Requiring at least five criteria reduced the evidence base to only four effect sizes from four studies, with an estimated effect of g = 0.45, 95% CI [-0.26, 1.16], p = .134. The same four studies met at least six of the eight criteria, yielding the same estimate. Although cluster-robust inference remained computationally available for these analyses, the latter estimates were based on only four independent primary-study clusters and are therefore highly uncertain.

This progressive refinement provides an important qualification to the preceding findings. The positive signal in the literature was not confined to immediate performance outcomes or to weaker study designs: studies that strictly assessed learning and used stronger designs continued to show positive effects. At the same time, only 11 primary studies met these two conditions, and imposing more stringent multidimensional quality requirements reduced the evidence base to as few as four studies. Consequently, the available literature provides substantially less evidence about effects on learning than the large number of published effect sizes and meta-analyses might initially suggest.

Sensitivity to the Analytic Sample and Influential Effects

The average effect was also sensitive in magnitude to the analytic sample composition.

Retaining the five influential outliers increased the pooled estimate from g = 0.83 to g = 1.11, 95% CI [0.79, 1.43], p < .001. Conversely, expanding the analysis to all 355 unique effect sizes reconstructed from the primary literature, regardless of whether they were included in the quantitative synthesis of the original meta-analyses, yielded g = 0.93, 95% CI [0.74, 1.13], p < .001. Models retaining repeated representations of the same evidence across the original meta-analyses yielded similar estimates. Thus, positive average effects emerged across alternative specifications, but their magnitude was sensitive to the inclusion of extreme estimates and the precise composition of the evidence base.

Small-Study Effects and Selection-Bias Sensitivity Analyses

Finally, we examined whether the conventional pooled estimate was robust to potential small-study and selection effects. A multilevel extension of Egger's regression indicated pronounced funnel asymmetry, with a cluster-robust intercept of 2.10, 95% CI [0.69, 3.51], p = .005. This pattern indicates a systematic relationship between effect magnitude and sampling precision but, by itself, does not establish publication bias.

Bias-adjustment procedures substantially attenuated the estimated average effect. PET yielded an adjusted limit estimate of g = 0.10, 95% CI [-0.35, 0.55], p = .647, whereas PEESE produced an intermediate estimate of g = 0.44, 95% CI [0.18, 0.70], p = .001.

According to the prespecified PET-PEESE decision rule, we retained PET because the PETadjusted intercept was not significantly positive.

A robust Bayesian model-averaged multilevel analysis provided a complementary sensitivity analysis. For the main dataset after exclusion of the five influential outliers, convergence was satisfactory (R ̂ = 1.00-1.01 for the principal parameters). The modelaveraged estimate was close to zero, μ = 0.01, 95% credible interval [-0.26, 0.28]. The posterior inclusion probability for an overall effect was .247 (inclusion BF = 0.33), whereas the posterior inclusion probabilities for heterogeneity and publication-bias components both exceeded .999. The corresponding Bayesian analysis including the extreme outliers did not converge adequately and was therefore not interpreted.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a systematic epistemological audit of the first wave of meta-analytic evidence on the effect of ChatGPT on educational outcomes. Rather than asking only whether the available meta-analyses produced positive pooled effects, we examined how they generated these estimates, what evidence they synthesized, and what conclusions that evidence can reasonably support. Several findings are particularly important.

First, although the five examined meta-analyses used many established meta-analytic procedures, important limitations emerged in transparency, reproducibility, and safeguards against researcher-driven decisions. Second, the underlying primary evidence base showed substantial methodological and reporting limitations. Third, the five meta-analyses drew heavily on overlapping primary evidence, so multiple syntheses should not be interpreted as multiple independent replications of the same conclusion. Fourth, much of the evidence described as concerning learning actually assessed immediate performance, and a nontrivial proportion assessed performance while AI assistance remained available. Finally, our reconstructed meta-analysis produced a positive average effect under conventional multilevel modeling, but this estimate was accompanied by extreme heterogeneity, was sensitive in magnitude to analytic sample composition, and was substantially attenuated in analyses designed to examine small-study and selection effects.

Taken together, these findings substantially qualify the interpretation of a single pooled effect as evidence for a general educational benefit of ChatGPT. Although a positive signal remained when analyses were restricted to stronger studies assessing learning, increasingly stringent methodological restrictions rapidly reduced the evidence base. The central limitation of the early meta-analytic literature is therefore not the absence of a positive signal, but uncertainty about what this signal represents, how large it is, and to which educational outcomes and conditions it generalizes.

Quality, Transparency, and Reproducibility of the Early Meta-Analyses

The five meta-analyses were not uniformly methodologically weak. All searched multiple databases, specified inclusion and exclusion criteria, reported confidence intervals, examined heterogeneity and potential publication bias, and explained their choice of metaanalytic model. This is important because the central conclusion of our audit is not that early meta-analyses on ChatGPT were inherently inappropriate. Rather, the findings reveal a mix of established meta-analytic practices and procedural weaknesses that matter for transparency, reproducibility, and protection against researcher-dependent errors.

Most notably, none of the five meta-analyses combined independent screening with independent coding by at least two raters; none was preregistered; analytic code was unavailable; and data were available only upon request, if at all. In several cases, insufficient reporting made it difficult to reconstruct which primary reports or effect sizes had actually entered the quantitative synthesis. These limitations matter because systematic reviews involve a long sequence of consequential decisions: identifying records, applying eligibility criteria, linking reports to studies, extracting statistics, selecting among multiple outcomes and time points, calculating effect sizes, and specifying statistical models. Human error in screening and extraction is well documented (Waffenschmidt et al., 2019; Z. Wang et al., 2020), and errors in effect-size extraction and calculation are not unique to the present literature. Lakens et al. (2016), for example, demonstrated that even published meta-analyses can contain effect sizes that cannot be independently reproduced and emphasized that reproducibility requires sufficient access to the decisions and data underlying a synthesis.

Our reconstruction illustrates why these safeguards matter. We encountered inconsistent study identification, unclear inclusion decisions, incomplete reporting of synthesized samples, and statistical values in primary reports that were impossible or highly implausible, including a correlation exceeding 1 and a negative F statistic. Such observations do not imply misconduct, nor are they unique to research on genAI. Meta-analysis is a complex process in which errors can enter at multiple stages. However, when data, code, extraction decisions, and study mappings are unavailable, detecting and correcting such errors becomes unnecessarily difficult. Open science practices therefore function not merely as indicators of good reporting but as practical safeguards for cumulative science.

The absence of preregistration should also be interpreted in the context of broader meta-analytic practice. Sandoval-Lentisco et al. (2025), for example, found that only 27% of 1,403 psychology meta-analyses published in 2021 were preregistered, and preregistrations themselves frequently omitted key decisions recommended by PRISMA-P. Thus, the practices observed here partly reflect broader methodological challenges in research synthesis rather than a problem unique to ChatGPT research. Nevertheless, the consequences may matter more in a rapidly developing and highly visible field. As Ioannidis (2016) warned more generally, the proliferation of meta-analyses can produce redundant or misleading syntheses when incentives favor rapid publication over scientific necessity and methodological rigor. In emerging fields, where relatively few primary studies are repeatedly synthesized and the literature changes rapidly, these risks become especially salient.

The subsequent retraction of Wang and Fan (2025), as well as the retraction of a primary report included in two of the examined meta-analyses, further illustrates the importance of traceability. Retractions do not retrospectively invalidate an entire research area, and their occurrence should not be used as a general indicator of genAI research reliability. They do, however, demonstrate why a cumulative evidence base must remain auditable after publication. Transparent mappings between primary reports, studies, effect sizes, and analytic decisions make it possible to update a synthesis when a source's status changes. Without such traceability, errors or retracted evidence can continue to propagate through subsequent reviews and citations.

The Primary Evidence Base as the More Fundamental Constraint

The limitations identified at the meta-analytic level represent only one part of the problem. More fundamentally, the reliability and interpretability of any synthesis are constrained by the primary evidence it aggregates. This is the essence of the garbage-ingarbage-out problem: sophisticated statistical models cannot compensate for primary studies that provide weak causal identification, inadequate measurement, incomplete reporting, or outcomes that do not correspond to the claims subsequently made about them.

Our findings indicate substantial room for improvement. Among the 87 reconstructed primary studies, only 6.9% met our criterion for sample representativeness; about half provided sufficient evidence of validity, group assignment, baseline equivalence, or clearly defined hypotheses or research questions; and none met all eight predefined quality criteria.

Open-science practices were similarly uncommon: only 3.4% were preregistered and 1.1% provided analytic code. At the same time, the pattern was not uniformly negative. Almost all studies met our stronger-study-design criterion, and more than half met the reliability criterion. The evidence base should therefore not be characterized simply as low quality; rather, its methodological strength varied substantially across dimensions. This distinction is also important for interpreting our exploratory moderator analyses.

The overall quality index was negatively but not statistically significantly associated with effect size, and the three quality domains did not jointly explain variation in effects. These null findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating that methodological quality is irrelevant. Moderator analyses require sufficient variation and adequate numbers of independent studies, and several characteristics showed limited variation in the present evidence base. Notably, the study-design indicator, the methodological characteristic for which our analyses did reveal a clear association, was related to substantially smaller effects, with stronger designs producing estimates approximately one third of a standard deviation smaller (b = -0.33). This finding aligns with the broader concern that methodological characteristics can systematically influence effect-size estimates (Cheung & Slavin, 2016), although it should be regarded as exploratory rather than causal evidence that any particular design feature produces smaller effects.

The statistical inconsistencies encountered during reconstruction further reinforce the importance of inspecting primary evidence rather than treating published effect sizes as errorfree inputs. Recalculation yielded individual Hedges' g values exceeding 20 in some cases, and we identified five effect sizes as influential outliers in our primary model. Retaining these five estimates increased the pooled effect from g = 0.83 to g = 1.11. The issue is therefore not merely that unusual values exist. In a literature consisting of relatively small and heterogeneous studies, a small number of extreme estimates can materially affect the magnitude of the apparent average effect.

Five Meta-Analyses, but Not Five Independent Evidence Bases

A further epistemological issue concerns the degree of overlap among the five syntheses. Of the 87 unique primary studies in the reconstructed evidence base, 45 (51.7%) appeared in at least two meta-analyses, and one appeared in all five. Consequently, the rapid accumulation of meta-analyses can create an impression of repeated independent confirmation that is not warranted when the syntheses substantially recycle the same primary evidence.

Overlap among systematic reviews is neither unusual nor intrinsically problematic.

Researchers addressing similar questions should often identify many of the same primary studies. However, Goodyear-Smith et al. (2012) demonstrated that reviews addressing essentially the same question can reach different conclusions because reviewers make different decisions about study eligibility, outcome selection, quality assessment, and statistical synthesis. Meta-analysis is therefore not a mechanically neutral transformation of a fixed literature into a single estimate. It contains numerous decision points, and these decisions require transparency and justification.

Our reconstruction illustrates this issue empirically. The primary analysis, which included each effect size synthesized in at least one of the examined meta-analyses only once, produced g = 0.83 after exclusion of influential outliers. Retaining the five outliers increased the estimate to g = 1.11, whereas expanding the evidence base to all 355 reconstructable unique effect sizes produced g = 0.93. These differences do not demonstrate that the original meta-analysts intentionally selected evidence to obtain particular results. They do show that decisions about which outcomes and effect sizes enter a synthesis can materially affect the magnitude of the resulting estimate. Such decisions are especially consequential when primary studies report multiple outcomes, measures, time points, or analyses from which a meta-analyst could extract an effect.

The proliferation of meta-analyses should therefore not be mistaken for independent replication of an effect when those syntheses rely substantially on the same primary evidence.

Future meta-analyses in rapidly developing areas should make corpus overlap with previous syntheses explicit and explain what new evidence, conceptual distinction, or methodological advance justifies an additional synthesis. This point is particularly relevant given longstanding concerns about the mass production of overlapping and potentially redundant meta-analyses (Ioannidis, 2016).

Performance is not Synonymous With Learning

Perhaps the most important conceptual finding of the present audit concerns what the primary studies actually measured. The meta-analyses frequently framed their research questions and conclusions in terms of student learning. Yet, under our strict classification, 79.7% of classifiable primary studies assessed performance outcomes exclusively, only 11.6% assessed learning outcomes exclusively, and 8.7% included both. Although these percentages varied under alternative operationalizations, performance-only studies constituted the clear majority under every classification. This distinction is not semantic. Performance during or immediately following an activity and learning as a relatively durable change in knowledge or competence are related but not interchangeable constructs (Yan et al., 2025). This distinction matters especially for genAI because the technology can directly contribute to the product being assessed. If students write an essay, solve a programming problem, or translate a text while using ChatGPT, improvements in the resulting product may represent an important educationally relevant benefit. However, they primarily show what the human-AI system can accomplish under those conditions, not necessarily what the learner can accomplish independently afterward.

Our coding of human versus human-AI hybrid performance makes this problem visible. Among classifiable performance effect sizes, approximately one quarter assessed outcomes while AI support remained available. Such outcomes should not be dismissed; the ability to work effectively with AI may itself be an increasingly important competence.

However, the inference differs from a claim that AI use produced a durable change in the learner. From a hybrid human-AI perspective, cognitive work can be allocated dynamically between the learner and the external system, and educational consequences depend on which processes the learner retains and which are delegated to AI (Kuhn et al., 2026). Offloading may support learning by freeing cognitive resources for productive processing, but it may also improve immediate output while bypassing processes required for durable knowledge acquisition.

Importantly, our exploratory analyses did not demonstrate that performance effects were statistically larger than learning effects. The estimated effects were g = 0.91 for performance and g = 0.80 for learning, and the difference was not statistically significant.

Primary-study quality also did not differ statistically significantly between performance-only and learning-only studies. The conceptual problem is therefore not that our data establish that performance effects are inflated relative to learning effects. Rather, much of the literature provides evidence about performance, while meta-analytic conclusions are sometimes articulated more broadly in terms of learning. Epistemic calibration requires that conclusions remain aligned with what the underlying measures can establish.

The restricted analyses further illustrate this point. When we limited the evidence to outcomes meeting our strict definition of learning, only 13 effect sizes from 11 primary studies remained. Their pooled estimate was positive and substantial (g = 1.00), indicating that the positive signal in the literature was not confined to immediate performance outcomes.

However, applying additional quality restrictions rapidly reduced this evidence base to six and ultimately four primary studies. At the threshold of five quality criteria, the estimate declined to g = 0.45, and its confidence interval included zero. The important finding is therefore neither that ChatGPT has been shown not to improve learning nor that it has been established to do so by a particular magnitude. Rather, the amount of evidence directly supporting durable learning is far smaller than the overall number of effect sizes might suggest.

Future research should consequently place greater emphasis on assessments that permit stronger inferences about learning, including independent posttests, retention measures, transfer tasks, and assessments conducted after AI support has been withdrawn or reduced.

Immediate posttests can provide evidence of learning when they assess independently available competence, but delayed and transfer assessments strengthen the inference that observed gains represent durable changes rather than temporary performance improvements or support-dependent success.

Beyond "Does ChatGPT Work?": Heterogeneous Outcomes and Interventions

The performance-learning distinction represents only one dimension of heterogeneity.

The examined meta-analyses also synthesized cognitive and achievement, motivationalaffective, behavioral, and other outcomes. Approximately one quarter of reconstructed effect sizes did not fit the outcome scope explicitly stated by the respective meta-analysis, although this proportion varied substantially across syntheses. An overarching pooled effect can therefore combine effects on achievement, task performance, motivation, engagement, selfefficacy, metacognition, and other constructs that have different theoretical meanings.

Again, heterogeneity itself is not a methodological failure. A broad synthesis can be scientifically useful when its scope is theoretically justified and meaningful differences are examined rather than obscured. Our own moderator analyses did not identify statistically significant differences between performance, learning, and affective outcomes, nor among the broader outcome domains. These results provide no basis for claiming that outcome domain systematically explains the observed heterogeneity in the present data. At the same time, statistical nonsignificance does not make the constructs conceptually interchangeable. A pooled effect across motivation, achievement, writing quality, and engagement remains an average across substantively different outcomes and should be described accordingly.

The same argument applies to the intervention side of the evidence base. "ChatGPT" was not a single treatment. Across the primary studies, it was used for feedback and guidance, information and knowledge support, text production, revision, instructional design, programming, and other activities. Learners differed in how they interacted with the system, which tasks they delegated to it, and the guidance surrounding its use. Moreover, generative AI is inherently interactive and partly nondeterministic: nominal access to the same system does not guarantee an equivalent learning experience. From a control-allocation perspective, the relevant instructional treatment is therefore better characterized by how cognitive and metacognitive activity is distributed among learner, AI, and teacher than by the mere presence of an AI tool (Kuhn et al., 2026). This suggests that the familiar question "Does ChatGPT work?" is underspecified. A more informative question is which configuration of AI support affects which outcome, for which learners, through which mechanisms, and under which instructional conditions. Future syntheses should therefore code and analyze the configuration of AI-supported activity rather than treating tool access as a homogeneous intervention. This requirement grows more important as successive versions of ChatGPT differ in capability and as educational uses of the technology become more sophisticated.

What Does the Positive Pooled Effect Actually Tell Us?

Our reconstructed meta-analysis demonstrates why a statistically precise average can coexist with considerable epistemic uncertainty. The primary model yielded g = 0.83, 95% CI [0.68, 0.97], but total heterogeneity was extremely high (I² = 94.06%), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from -0.75 to 2.41. Thus, the mean effect was estimated with considerably greater precision than the effects expected across individual settings. In practical terms, the same statistical model that produces a clearly positive mean is compatible with true effects ranging from negative to very large positive values. This distinction matters especially for communicating meta-analytic findings. A narrow confidence interval around a pooled effect can create an impression of settled evidence, particularly when the estimate is subsequently summarized in abstracts, reviews, or policy documents. Yet the confidence interval primarily quantifies uncertainty about the estimated mean; it does not imply that the underlying effects are homogeneous or that a similar effect should be expected in a particular educational context. The precision of a pooled estimate should therefore not be confused with the maturity or consistency of the evidence base from which it was derived.

The sensitivity analyses add a second layer of uncertainty. The conventional multilevel models consistently produced positive average effects across alternative analytic samples.

However, the magnitude changed when we included influential observations or additional reconstructable effect sizes. More importantly, we detected pronounced funnel asymmetry, and methods intended to examine small-study and selection effects produced substantially attenuated estimates. PET yielded g = 0.10 with a confidence interval including zero, whereas PEESE yielded g = 0.44. The model-averaged RoBMA estimate after removal of the extreme outliers was approximately zero, with stronger evidence for heterogeneity and publicationbias components than for an overall effect.

These procedures rely on different assumptions and should not be interpreted as revealing the uniquely correct effect concealed beneath the published literature. Funnel asymmetry can arise for reasons other than publication bias, particularly in heterogeneous literatures, and bias-adjustment models can themselves be sensitive to model specification.

Their value here is therefore diagnostic. Taken together with the extreme heterogeneity, influential observations, and methodological variability of the primary studies, they show that g = 0.83 should not be treated as a stable estimate of a general "ChatGPT effect". The The issue is therefore not merely statistical accuracy. It is calibration between the scope of the evidence and the scope of the claim.

Implications for Research and Evidence Synthesis

Several implications follow from these findings. First, genAI research should move beyond undifferentiated questions about whether ChatGPT works. Primary research should specify the causal and theoretical question more precisely: how AI is used, by whom, for which learning activity, through which cognitive or motivational mechanism, and under which instructional conditions. Access to ChatGPT is not itself a sufficiently specified educational intervention. The relevant treatment includes the task, prompting and interaction structure, instructional guidance, degree of learner autonomy, control condition, and allocation of cognitive activity between learner and AI.

Second, future studies should distinguish learning from AI-assisted performance. This requires outcome measures that assess independently available knowledge and competence and, where theoretically appropriate, retention and transfer after AI support has been withdrawn. Researchers should also study human-AI hybrid performance explicitly rather than treating it as an inferior outcome: the capacity to use AI effectively may be educationally valuable. The key requirement is conceptual precision about whether a study is evaluating the learner, the human-AI system, or changes in the learner resulting from interaction with that system.

Implications for Practice, Policy, and Scientific Communication

The present findings do not imply that educators should avoid ChatGPT or that genAI cannot support learning. They imply that broad claims about educational effectiveness should remain proportionate to the evidence from which they are derived. In particular, a pooled standardized effect should not be translated directly into a general statement that ChatGPT improves learning when the underlying synthesis combines different outcomes, instructional uses, assessment conditions, and substantial amounts of AI-assisted performance.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present audit warrant consideration. First, we deliberately focused on ChatGPT rather than the broader range of LLMs and genAI systems. This restriction provided a comparatively coherent and historically important case for examining early evidence formation, but it limits generalization to other models and applications.

Moreover, ChatGPT itself has changed substantially since its introduction, further underscoring that our findings concern a historically bounded evidence base rather than a timeless intervention.

Second, we completed our systematic search in March 2025. The present study should therefore not be interpreted as an audit of the current evidence base on ChatGPT or genAI in education. Since our search, numerous additional meta-analyses and even second-order syntheses have appeared (e.g., Bartoš et al., 2026; Dong, 2026; İrfan Ayhan, 2026; Liu et al., 2025; Ma & Zhong, 2025; Mo et al., 2026; Ünal et al., 2026; X. Wu et al., 2026). This continued proliferation reflects how quickly the literature is developing: second-order syntheses are now being conducted across an already extensive set of first-order metaanalyses. For example, Ünal et al. (2026) explicitly motivate their second-order meta-analysis by pointing to inconsistent findings, methodological heterogeneity, variable quality, and overlapping primary studies across existing AI meta-analyses. Bartoš et al. (2026) similarly synthesize 1,840 effect sizes from 67 meta-analyses and report pronounced heterogeneity and evidence of publication bias, illustrating that concerns about interpretability extend beyond the specific first wave examined here. Mo et al. (2026) Third, our own meta-analytic analyses were primarily diagnostic and exploratory. They were intended to stress-test the reconstructed evidence base, not to establish a definitive causal estimate of ChatGPT's educational effectiveness. Some moderator analyses relied on relatively few independent primary studies or strongly unbalanced categories, which limited statistical power to detect potentially meaningful moderator effects. This is a general challenge in meta-regression, where statistical power depends not only on the total number of effect sizes but also on the number of independent studies, the distribution of studies across moderator categories, and the amount of between-study heterogeneity (Harrer et al., 2021). This limitation matters for the performance-learning comparison because substantially fewer studies assessed learning than performance. Consequently, the absence of statistically significant moderation should not be interpreted as evidence that these outcome types-or other moderator categories-produce equivalent effects. Rather, some of our moderator analyses may simply have lacked sufficient statistical power to reliably detect differences of plausible magnitude.

Fourth, our quality assessment necessarily depended on information reported in the available primary reports and meta-analyses. Some methodological safeguards may have been implemented but not documented. Consequently, our indicators capture the methodological and reporting quality of the scientific record available for evaluation and cannot always distinguish between the absence of a procedure and the absence of information about that procedure. This limitation does not make reporting quality inconsequential: if readers and subsequent meta-analysts cannot determine what was done, the evidence remains difficult to evaluate and reproduce. A5. Inclusion of Studies not Examining ChatGPT as a Learning Intervention

Across several meta-analyses, including Deng et al. (2025) and He and Cordie (2024), some primary studies do not directly examine ChatGPT as a learning intervention. Instead, these studies focus on other AI-based tools (e.g., Duolingo, Jasper, Shortly AI), custom-built chatbots (e.g., Flow XO), or ChatGPT-generated materials without active learner interaction.

In some cases, the primary focus is on assessment or content generation rather than learning processes or learning outcomes.

A6. Potentially Incomplete Literature Coverage Due to a Narrow Search String

The meta-analysis by Heung and Chui (2025) employed a highly specific search string ("ChatGPT and engagement") to identify relevant primary studies. Given the rapidly evolving and heterogeneous terminology in the emerging literature on generative AI in education, this narrowly defined search strategy raises concerns about the completeness of the retrieved corpus of studies.

A7. Inclusion of Retracted or non-Peer-Reviewed Studies

At least one primary study included in a meta-analysis has been formally retracted, without explicit acknowledgment or sensitivity analysis addressing its inclusion. In addition, several included studies are preprints or unpublished manuscripts, with no clarification regarding peer-review status, version changes, or potential effects on reported outcomes.

A8. Ambiguous or Confounded Intervention Definitions

Many primary studies included in the reviewed meta-analyses combine ChatGPT use with additional instructional elements, such as game-based learning environments, supplementary digital tools, or increased instructional time in the experimental condition.

These design characteristics make it difficult to attribute observed effects uniquely to ChatGPT.

A9. Conceptual Mismatch Between Outcomes and Claimed Learning Effects

Several included primary studies report only post-test performance, task completion metrics, or assessment-related outcomes, yet are treated as evidence of learning gains in the meta-analyses. In some cases, studies focusing on test construction or evaluation rather than learning are included in syntheses of learning outcomes, raising concerns about conceptual alignment.

A10. Implications for Transparency and Reproducibility

Taken together, the identified issues across Wang and Fan (2025), Deng et al. (2025), He and Cordie (2024), and related reviews substantially limit transparency, reproducibility, and interpretability. Reconstructing the actual analytic samples often requires manual crossreferencing and inferential judgment, increasing the risk of misclassification and error propagation in secondary research.

A11. Conflation of Distinct Publications and Inappropriate Inclusion of a Meta-Analysis as a Primary Study

In Wang and Fan (2025), Wang et al. (2024a) and Wang et al. (2024b) are both cited as primary studies. However, an inspection of the coding table indicates that the variables attributed to Wang et al. (2024a) correspond exclusively to those reported in Wang et al. (2024b). Audit of Documentation and Inclusion Issues in Existing Meta-Analyses on ChatGPT in Education

The following appendix documents systematic irregularities identified during the audit of existing meta-analyses on the effects of ChatGPT in educational contexts. The goal of this audit was to assess transparency, traceability, and comparability of the reported syntheses.

All observations are based on a systematic cross-check of inclusion tables, reference lists, appendix figures, and reported methods sections.

A1. Incomplete and Inconsistent Reporting of Included Studies

Several meta-analyses provide insufficiently transparent documentation of the primary studies included in the quantitative synthesis. The meta-analysis by Wang and Fan (2025) shows multiple inconsistencies between the narrative description of included studies, the reference list, and the tabulated overview of primary studies (Table 3). Specifically, several studies mentioned in the text or marked with inclusion indicators in the reference list do not appear in the main inclusion table, preventing an unambiguous reconstruction of the analytic sample. One prominent example is Niloy et al. (2023), which is cited repeatedly in the text and marked with an asterisk in the reference list, yet does not appear in the table listing the included studies.

A2. Misidentification of Primary Studies due to Incorrect Author Attribution

Across multiple meta-analyses, primary studies are occasionally referenced by authors' first names rather than surnames, which substantially complicates correct identification. In Wang and Fan (2025), the study listed as "Donald et al. ( 2024)" refers to a publication in which Donald is the first author's first name; the correct citation is Johnson et al. (2024).

Similarly, the study listed as "Qi et al. ( 2024)" corresponds to Lu et al. (2024), again reflecting the use of a first name rather than a family name. Notably, the same mislabeling of 2024a) is itself a meta-analysis, rather than a primary empirical study. Its inclusion as a primary study within the meta-analysis by Wang and Fan (2025) is therefore methodologically questionable. Including a meta-analysis as a primary study introduces a risk of conceptual misclassification and non-independence of evidence, particularly if overlapping primary studies are synthesized across levels of analysis.

The combination of (a) conflating two distinct publications and (b) treating a metaanalysis as a primary study further complicates the reconstruction of the analytic sample in Wang and Fan (2025).

Third, genAI interventions need substantially more precise description. Researchers should report the model and version used, relevant access dates, prompting procedures, the degree and type of AI interaction, teacher guidance, learner autonomy, duration of exposure, and the comparison group's activities. Without this information, researchers cannot meaningfully compare or reproduce ostensibly similar interventions. Fourth, standards for research synthesis should rise alongside the increasing quantity of primary evidence. Meta-analyses should preregister consequential analytic decisions where feasible (see J. When multiple syntheses already address similar questions, authors should additionally report corpus overlap and explain the incremental contribution of a new meta-analysis. More meta-analyses do not necessarily produce more cumulative knowledge if they repeatedly aggregate substantially the same evidence without resolving its conceptual and methodological limitations.

Scientific communication needs the same calibration. Abstracts, press releases, reviews, and policy documents can inadvertently strengthen claims as findings travel from a narrowly defined empirical result to a general statement about the effectiveness of AI in education. Meta-analytic precision does not eliminate the need to communicate uncertainty, heterogeneity, and limits to generalization. The implications are therefore neither a call for technological enthusiasm nor for technological skepticism. Decisions about genAI in education involve potential benefits and potential costs, and withholding potentially useful technologies until perfect evidence exists would itself be consequential. Conclusion This systematic audit shows that the first wave of meta-analyses on ChatGPT in education provides a considerably less settled evidence base than positive pooled effects alone might suggest. The five meta-analyses relied substantially on overlapping primary evidence characterized by methodological and reporting limitations and considerable heterogeneity in outcomes and interventions. Most importantly, much of the evidence framed as concerning learning assessed performance instead, sometimes while AI support remained available. Our reconstructed meta-analysis yielded a positive average effect under conventional multilevel modeling, and a positive signal remained when analyses were restricted to stronger studies specifically assessing learning. However, the wide prediction interval, the rapid reduction of the evidence base under stricter quality criteria, and the substantial attenuation of estimates in sensitivity analyses addressing small-study and selection effects indicate considerable uncertainty about the magnitude and generalizability of this effect. Thus, g = 0.83 should not be interpreted as a stable estimate of a general effect of ChatGPT on learning. The central question is therefore not simply whether ChatGPT "works," but what it supports, for whom, under which instructional conditions, and with what durability. Future research should distinguish learning from AI-assisted performance, specify genAI interventions more precisely, employ designs and measures capable of demonstrating durable learning, and strengthen transparency and reproducibility in both primary research and evidence synthesis. Meta-analysis remains essential for cumulative science, but the precision of a pooled estimate should not be mistaken for the maturity of the evidence base from which it is derived.
