To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 16 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Mental health shapes the progression and content of thought. Studies have linked depression, anxiety, and stress with repetitive negative thoughts, but it remains unclear whether these disturbances are reflected in the structure of an individual's speech. We introduce an approach to quantify the structure of thought as expressed in language, applying it to memory recountings from 82 individuals across six psychotherapy sessions. We model each recounting's semantic progression with contextual embeddings, and use persistent homology to identify loops of meaning, as recountings circle back upon themselves. More loops are associated with depression, anxiety, stress, and negative affect, and with negative self-referential language, implicating them as geometric markers of negative fixation and rumination. Semantic content also relates to mental health. However, neither semantic structure nor content mediate therapeutic improvement. These findings reveal that the semantic structure of speech offers a quantitative window into thought disturbances in depressive and anxiety disorders.

Introduction

Mental health is tied to the content and dynamics of mind. For example, depression, anxiety, and stress have all been linked to blunted experiences of pleasure 1, 2 ; thoughts relating to worry and negative cognitive biases [3] [4] [5] ; and rumination 6 , which may be experienced as recursive loops of negative self-referential thoughts 7 . However, the capacity to identify aberrant cognitive processes and associated psychopathology is presently limited by a broad reliance on self-reported surveys and ratings. These measures collapse rich mental dynamics into post hoc static summary judgements that can be confounded by demand characteristics, response bias, the cognitive biases associated with anxiety or depression, and participants' understanding of the questions being asked [8] [9] [10] . These issues leave the moment-to-moment quality of cognitive disruptions in mental health conditions opaque.

There is increasing evidence that disrupted cognition can be studied through language. For instance, research examining spontaneous speech collected during online therapy has found that depression and anxiety are associated with increased usage of personalizing language (e.g., first person pronouns), as compared to depersonalizing language (e.g., third person pronouns) 11, 12 . During autobiographical recollections, however, individuals with depressive symptoms tend to assume a third-person perspective 13, 14 . Furthermore, social media research has found that depression severity is associated with greater use of words indicative of cognitive distortions (e.g., catastrophizing and magnification of negative experiences) in an individual's posted content 15 . More recent studies using language models to score the content of texts have found that the sentiment of an individual's language can be used to predict their anxiety 16 and future changes in their depression severity 17 . Finally, neurophysiological studies have provided evidence for the neural coupling between cognition and language by successfully predicting a speaker's brain activity from language model encodings of their speech's meaning (semantics) [18] [19] [20].

Whilst ample attention has been paid to how the content of an individual's language relates to their mental health, relatively little research has been directed towards studying how that content is structured. Clinical accounts suggest that individuals with depression and anxiety experience their thinking as circular, stuck, or repetitively returning to the same negative themes; this may be reflected in the structure of their speech 7, 21 . Indeed, some research employing speech graphs, which model linguistic structure as networks of words linked by their sequential ordering, has found that speech exhibiting more repetitive, looping word patterns than expected by chance is associated with dysphoric mood, social anhedonia, and perseveration in individuals at high-risk for psychosis, possibly reflecting ruminative patterns in speech 22 . Furthermore, other studies employing computational linguistics have found that the syntax of language produced by individuals with depressive disorders is less structurally complex than that of controls, as evidenced by shorter sentences 23, 24 and a greater proportion of single-clause sentences, relative to multi-clause sentences 25 . However, present methods for directly characterising semantic structure model language as sequential transition sequences between individual words [26] [27] [28] [29] . These approaches ignore the broader discursive context from which words obtain their meaning 30, 31 and, by focusing upon discrete transitions, fail to capture the dynamic and continuous semantic flow of narrative arcs as well as the geometries that they trace. Hence, there remain lacunae in our understanding of how the semantic structure and affective progression of an individual's thought, as reflected in their speech, relates to their mental health.

We address this question by examining in-session transcripts from a cohort of 82 individuals. Each individual received 6 sessions of a psychological therapy for low positive affect, depression, anxiety, or stress; this therapy (called Positive Affect Treatment) 2, 32, 33 is designed to increase positive emotions by asking individuals to recount recent positive experiences. To understand how the structure and content of an individual's speech relates to their mental health, we first cast each recounting as a continuous semantic trajectory by using a language model (Sentence-BERT) 34 to embed sliding windows of text throughout the recounting; each semantic trajectory represents the progression of meaning across an individual's recounting. We then identify loops of meaning within each of these semantic trajectories by utilising a tool known as persistent homology, which is capable of identifying topological features (loops) from high-dimensional point clouds. Loops arise as recountings take circuitous paths that tend to return back to previously discussed themes. After counting the number of semantic loops within each recounting, we examine how the number of semantic loops in patient recountings relates to their mental health over the course of treatment. Second, we ask if semantic loops are related to rumination by examining their association with content reflecting negative fixation. Third, we test if mental health is marked by changes in the semantic content of patient recountings across key dimensions of hedonic, eudaimonic, and interpersonal joy. Building upon this, we interpret how the thematic composition of patient recountings relates to each of these dimensions of joy by utilising a recent approach for modelling the topic content of small datasets 35, 36 . Finally, we examine the role of these linguistic properties as markers or mediators of mental health and its improvement over the course of therapy. Our study provides evidence that the meanings expressed in speech and the geometries that they shape during the organisation of discourse can be used as quantitative markers of mental health and associated thought disturbances.

Results

To examine the relationship between mental health and language use during psychotherapy, we analysed data from 82 individuals with clinically severe low positive affect, anxiety, depression, or stress symptoms; the data are drawn from two multisite randomized controlled trials of Positive Affect Treatment (PAT) 32, 33 (see Methods for detail on sample selection). PAT is designed to increase positive affect through behavioral, cognitive, and mindfulness interventions. Across the time period analysed in this study, participants engaged in six weekly hour-long therapy sessions and were encouraged to engage in positive activities in the intervening weeks. In the present study we analyse individual mental health outcomes and language during the first module of treatment (sessions 2-7). During this component of treatment, participants performed imaginal recountings ("savoring") in which they were guided by therapists using memory specificity techniques that promote first-person, present-tense re-experiencing of positive events. This exercise is designed to enhance reward processing and increase positive affect. It mitigates overgeneral memory, reduced positive imagery, and reward devaluation by evoking detailed sensory and emotional content [37] [38] [39] . Therapists prompt clients to describe particular physical sensations or positive emotions to deepen their experiences, and are instructed to interrupt participants when they move into a more narrative style, shift to a different topic, or redirect their attention towards negative aspects of the situation 40.

At the start of each therapy session, participants completed the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21), a 21-item self-report instrument that measures symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress over the previous week on a 4-point scale (0 = did not apply to me,3 = applied to me most of the time). The DASS-21 yields three subscale scores corresponding to depression, anxiety, and stress domains, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity (i.e., worse depression/anxiety/stress). Additionally, participants completed the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS), a 20-item measure assessing positive and negative affect. The PANAS yields two subscales-positive affect and negative affect-each comprising 10 items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = very slightly or not at all,5 = extremely), with higher scores indicating greater levels of the respective affective dimensions. Semantic loops are markers of negative mental health

To test if mental health is tied to the semantic structure of speech, we first model each imaginal recounting as a trajectory through semantic (meaning) space (Fig. 1A). We use a fine-tuned language model (Sentence-BERT all-MiniLM-L6-v2) to encode sliding context windows of text (90 words per context window) as high-dimensional vectors (embeddings). Embeddings can be thought of as coordinates, whose position corresponds to the overall meaning of the context window under consideration. For example, the embedding for the phrase "I love apples" would be semantically close to the phrase "I like eating apples" and would be comparatively distant from the embedding for the phrase "I like dogs". Context windows are sampled throughout each recounting (10% word stride in between windows) such that the unfolding of each recounting traces out an embedding trajectory that represents the semantic progression of the recounting over time (Fig. 1A, Fig. 2

Loops co-occur with semantic content relating to negative fixation Semantic loops are not necessarily valenced, but prior research indicates that loop-like dynamics in natural language may structurally instantiate fixation upon negative thoughts in speech 22 . Thus, even though PAT is designed to encourage savoring of positive events, it is conceivable that semantic loops relate to temporary fixation on negative content. To further test this, we quantify the negative fixation of each recounting and then test if loops and mental health are associated with semantic content relating to this construct. We do this by creating a latent semantic dimension for negative fixation (Fig. 3A); following well-established techniques 35, 44 , we first embed representative phrases (e.g., "I keep thinking about what went wrong."), average those embeddings, and then subtract the averaged embeddings of phrases that represent a non-fixated progression of thought (e.g., "I focus on what I can do next."). This dimension represents the meaning of negatively fixated cognition in text; text embeddings that have more positive projections onto this latent dimension encode meanings related to fixation upon negative events. By projecting each point in a given semantic trajectories onto this dimension and averaging the maximum 5% of loadings (i.e., projection) over the recountings, we obtain a measure of negative fixation according to recounting content (Fig. 3A) . We utilise the maximum loadings for our analyses to focus upon the most affectively salient components of each recounting [45] [46] [47] ; however, similar results are obtained when using the average projection across the recounting.

We find that the presence of loops in narratives is associated with a marked increase in content related to negative fixation (Fig. 3B, LMM, 𝜒2 =15.99, df=1, p=0.0040) and that anxiety is significantly positively associated with content related to negative fixation (Fig. 3C, LMM, 𝜒2 =8.43, df=1, p=0.016). In contrast, positive and relative affect are significantly negatively associated with negatively fixated content (Fig. 3C, LMM, positive affect: 𝜒2 =6.36, df=1, p=0.029; relative affect: 𝜒2 =5.77, df=1, p=0.035). However, we find no significant associations between depression, stress, or negative affect with negatively fixated content (Fig. 2C, LMM, all 𝜒2 ≤1.98, df=1, p≥0.20). In essence, these results provide evidence that semantic loops are coupled to narrative content relating to more negative fixation which, in-turn, is associated with panic-fear-like anxiety symptoms and less positive affect. Together, these structural and semantic properties converge to detect the phenomenology of poorer mental health in speech.

Recounting affective content reflects mental health

Positive affect treatment 32, 48, 49 is designed to increase capacity to anticipate and enjoy rewarding, positive events, in order to improve positive mood and mental health. Therefore, we next examine patients' mental health is associated with the extent to which they express joy through the content of their language. To do this, we load semantic trajectories onto three embedding dimensions that represent the latent meanings of eudaimonic, hedonic, and interpersonal joy (Fig. 3A, See Methods for dimension creation). Eudaimonic joy is a durable sense of fulfilment that arises from living in line with one's values-pursuing purpose, growth, and contribution beyond the self. Hedonic joy is the immediate pleasure or relief felt from enjoyable, comfortable, or gratifying experiences. Interpersonal joy is the positive feeling and meaning derived from warm, supportive connection and belonging with others; see Table 1 for a summary [50] [51] [52] [53] [54] [55] . If a narrative loads more positively onto one of these dimensions, it indicates that its content relates more positively to that construct of joy. For example, a highly positive loading for hedonic joy indicates that the language is expressing highly enjoyable and gratifying themes.

We predict that all three dimensions of joy will be negatively associated with depression, anxiety, stress, and negative affect, but will be positively associated with positive and relative affect.

Sensitivity analysis

To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted five sensitivity analyses (Fig. S4-5). First, we tested whether our results were sensitive to the semantic scale at which narratives were analysed by varying the context window sizes used to extract semantic trajectories (50, 70, 90, 110 words). Nearly all significant effects were recovered across every scale (Fig. S4). Second, we confirmed that the associations between loop number and mental health were consistent across different persistence thresholds (Fig. S5A). Third, we tested that the reported associations between loop number and mental health were robust to language model fine-tuning (Fig. S5B). Fourth, we evaluated the robustness of our content findings (Fig. 2 3) by using a large language model (LLM) to repeatedly find alternative seed phrases for constructing each latent semantic dimension and compared the strength of the associations measured with the LLM-derived seeds to those reported in our study (Fig. S5B). In general, rephrasing had almost no influence on our reported associations; the average difference in standardised β values between original and rephrased latent dimension seed was less than 0.01. The association between relative affect and hedonic joy was the only result that was substantially stronger with the original seed phrases and there were two associations that were actually stronger when rephrased seeds were used (Fig. S5C, standardised increased by ≥0.10 with β LLM-derived seed phrases for anxiety versus interpersonal content and positive affect versus negative fixation, as compared to original seed phrases). Fifth and finally, we assessed the robustness of our findings to measurement error by injecting increasing levels of noise into our data and refitting models (Fig. S5D). Most associations were highly robust (at least to 30% of their standard deviation), though the interaction between loops and depression showed weaker robustness to noise injection, and so should be considered more tentatively.

Narrative properties predict, but do not mediate, mental health So far, our results provide evidence that mental health is reflected in narrative by semantic structure (Fig. 1), cognitive and affective content (Fig. 3 4), and affective dynamics (Fig. 4). To further test this, we ask whether narrative properties mediate the therapeutic effect of PAT (Fig. 5B: therapy → narrative properties → mental health). To do this, we first reduce all measured narrative semantic properties (Table 1) into a single composite variable (latent component) for each target rating of mental health by using Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression. The latent component is the linear combination of predictors (narrative properties) that maximises their covariance with the outcome rating of mental health, and the loadings of narrative properties onto this component are highly similar to their previously modeled associations. For example, if a narrative scores more positively for the latent component created for anxiety, this tends to indicate high loop number and volatility but low interpersonal and hedonic joy (Fig. 5A, Fig. S6). We then test the narrative latent component's role as a predictor of an individual's current mental health (Fig. 5A; Fig 5B: b-path, narrative effect) and as a putative mediator for treatment-induced improvements in mental health (Fig. 5B: a×b) in concurrent mediation models. To avoid overfitting and ensure generalisability, the latent component was iteratively trained on individuals not included in the mediation model in a cross-validation procedure (See Methods).

We find that the structural and semantic properties of speech explain a modest proportion of the variance in an individual's mental health, beyond that which is explained by the number of sessions of positive affect therapy that an individual has participated in (depression, ΔR²=0.01; anxiety, ΔR²=0.10 ; stress, ΔR²=0.07; negative affect, ΔR²=0.07; positive affect, ΔR²=0.02; relative affect, ΔR²=0.07). Indeed, the resulting PLS coefficients confirmed the associations previously evidenced in this study (Fig. 5A) and the narrative latent component is significantly associated with all ratings of mental health except for positive affect (Fig. 5B-C: narrative effect; Table S3). However, we do not find any evidence that narrative properties act as mediators for any outcome mental health rating (Fig. 5C; Table S3). Discussion

Language is a promising window into mental health and cognition 11, 15, 56, 57 . Most quantitative studies investigating this so far have focused upon the content of texts-sentiment classification; computing the occurrence of different lexical items or themes within texts; or LLM-derived ratings of psychological constructs [57] [58] [59] . In this study, we treat language as continuous semantic trajectories to provide evidence that mental health is mirrored in speech by semantic structure (semantic loops), semantic content, and affective dynamics. Importantly, these linguistic properties had no bearing on the effectiveness of PAT over time, showing that they are not reflective of the quality of therapeutic practice or the mechanism of PAT-induced improvements in mental health. By combining natural language processing with topology, these results provide evidence that an individual's mental health is not only manifested in the meanings of their expression, but also in how that meaning is structured.

Within the therapeutic exercise designed to savor positive experiences, we identified semantic loops and found that the number of loops was associated with more negatively fixated content. Whilst we cannot establish the causal relationship between semantic loops and negative content (as these features of narrative could be generated by a range of cognitive and affective processes) our findings do align with existing studies investigating the relationship between mental health and the character of thought. For example, our finding that depression, anxiety, and stress were associated with more loops resonates with previous work identifying rumination and persistent negative thoughts as transdiagnostic properties of negative mental health 21, 41, 60 as well as accounts of the phenomenology of repetitive thought 7, 21 . However, we found that only anxiety, and none of the other clinical outcomes (i.e, depression, stress, and negative affect), was associated with both loops and content relating to negative fixation. These findings for anxiety dovetail with the negative association between anxiety and interpersonal joy; previous studies have found that anxious rumination often focuses upon stressful social settings [61] [62] [63] . These results provide support for the idea that linguistic properties, now including semantic structure, may be indicative of mental health. Such indicators may be used in future work to identify individuals at risk for worsening depression, anxiety, or stress 17.

The patterning of expressed semantic content across mental health dimension points towards shared and specific disruptions to affective experience. Depression was found to be marked by significant reductions in hedonic and interpersonal joy, consistent with its clinical profile of anhedonia and social withdrawal 2, 64 . Anxiety showed a specific deficit in interpersonal joy. This suggests that-even when recounting ostensibly positive experiences-anxious individuals may struggle to access or articulate social reward; this may be a consequence of threat-related appraisal of past social situations [65] [66] [67] . Stress was found to be associated with diminished hedonic and eudaimonic joy, potentially reflecting depleted capacity for both savouring pleasure and constructing meaning in memory recollections 68, 69.

Although semantic properties of imaginal recountings offer a window into an individual's present mental health, they do not mediate PAT-associated improvements in mental health over time. This result suggests that the act of recounting positive events can drive improvements in mental health, independently of the content or structure of that recounting. Alternatively, the effect of PAT on mental health may hinge more upon the extratherapeutic activities that clients are encouraged to engage in as part of treatment. Future work may disentangle this by collecting activity engagement data to test as a putative mediator of mental health improvements.

The methods presented here offer broad promise for characterising the form and content of the abundant narratives arising both from clinical and non-clinical (e.g., social media) contexts. Discursive form, as quantified by persistent homology, could signal a range of other mental health conditions. For example, impaired cognitive control in conditions such as autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, and dementia is associated with perseveration-persistent topic repetition-that may take form in language as loops [70] [71] [72] [73] [74] [75] . Furthermore, in conversational contexts, the collective meaning of each interlocutor's dialogue could assume a combined form, which may be perturbed by individual or collective psychological states 76 , potentially offering a structural-linguistic perspective on phenomena such as social cohesion and emotional contagion.

Our approach for quantifying the semantic content of text-combining language model embeddings with latent semantic dimensions and DATM-offers a highly flexible and interpretable means of characterising the content of clinical texts across abstract psychological dimensions. This technique offers several advantages over commonly used approaches for characterising the content of an individual's language, which either use pre-defined affect ratings for individual words, count the frequency of different words/word-sequences that fall within pre-defined categories of interest, or-increasingly-use language models to rate psychological constructs 57, 58, 77, 78 . On the one hand, lexical approaches ignore the surrounding contextual information from which words gain their meanings 30, 31 . On the other hand, language models, though context-aware, carry their own constraints: tuned classifiers are typically limited to broad sentiment ratings (i.e., is this language positive or negative?), and LLMs yield opaque outputs that are uninterpretable and, given their domain-general training, may fail to capture the unique semantics of specialised clinical therapeutic transcripts [78] [79] [80] [81] [82] . Furthermore, LLM-derived ratings of psychological constructs are, in a sense, just an automation of human annotation and fail to discover some of the genuinely interesting aspects of textual structure, including latent semantic and structural dimensions, which can be revealed by sophisticated machine learning and NLP approaches. By contrast, our technique, drawing from computational social science 35, 83, 84 , derives interpretable psychological dimensions directly from the embedding space of the transcripts themselves, making it both domain-sensitive and granularly interpretable via DATM.

Our study has several limitations. First, each imaginal recounting is of individual experiences, unique to each person, which may have true differences in structure and content (although this should be somewhat controlled for by the longitudinal design of our study). Second, mental health may influence the overall expressivity of an individual's language, limiting the extent to which speech is a direct reflection of thought and impairing our ability to detect strong differences in speech semantics. Third, we do not possess extra-linguistic ratings of putatively loop-generating cognitive processes that would further evidence a mechanism for this aspect of semantic structure, such as rumination. Fourth, it is possible that loops may also be generated by subject-therapist interactions, as individuals are prompted by their therapist to re-engage with a topic following their diversion to a negative theme and may thus represent an interesting marker of the structure of therapy, rather than the participant's speech (although we tried to control for this). Fifth, we did not quantify instances of positive fixation due to challenges in defining terms that could be used to create such a dimension and our primary interest in the psychopathological feature of worry and rumination-like repetitive content patterns. Finally, whilst we found that linguistic properties reflect mental health, it is unclear if language, in turn, exerts a simultaneous influence on present mental health. Future research could thus build upon our work by studying shared experiences, collecting additional ratings across a more diverse range of psychological dimensions, and testing if mental health outcomes are modified by recounting pre-specified texts.

Overall, we provide evidence that an individual's mental health is reflected in changes to the semantic structure and content of their language. Our finding that narrative loops are signatures of depression, anxiety, stress and negative affect is striking as a potentially geometric account of negative fixation and rumination in speech. Furthermore, our analyses of recounting semantic content provide salient evidence that the clinical presentation of depression, anxiety, stress, and affect is mirrored in the meaning that individuals express in spoken language. This paper illuminates the shape of meaning in language as a reflection of mental health and thought disturbance. Our approach offers broad opportunities for the naturalistic assessment and identification of mental health from speech, with many potential clinical applications.

Methods

Sample and details of imaginal recountings

Participants (N=82) were adults with clinically severe symptoms of anxiety or depression and functional impairment. They were recruited from the community through postings advertising a psychological treatment study targeting depression, anxiety, and stress. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 60 years (M=33.81, SD=11.94). 63% of participants were assigned female at birth and 37% were assigned male at birth. Participants identified their ethnicity as white (54.1%), asian (22.4%), black (5.9%), multiracial (5.9%), native american/alaskan (3.5%), and "other" (8.2%). Elevated symptom levels were assessed using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) and the Sheehan Disability Scale. Additional inclusion criteria included stabilized psychotropic medication (or medication-free), willingness to refrain from starting other treatment before the six month follow-up visit, English-speaking, and being between the ages of 18 and 65. Exclusion criteria included serious medical conditions, history of Bipolar I or II disorder, history of schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, substance use disorder within the past six months, past-year suicide attempt, self-harm, or active suicidal ideation, intellectual disability, and current pregnancy. Participants were recruited as part of two randomized-controlled, multisite studies 48, 49 Participants were randomized to receive either Positive Affect Treatment (PAT) or Negative Affect Treatment (NAT). Only participants in PAT were included in the present study, all having completed at least one session of imaginal recounting (study 1, N=35, study 2, N=47). Participants who completed NAT were not included in our sample as NAT does not involve the same exercise of recounting positive memories, that is the focus of this study. PAT was designed to increase positive affect through behavioural (module 1, sessions 2-7), cognitive (module 2, sessions 8-10), and cultivating positivity (module 3, sessions 11-14) exercises. Participants engaged in 15 sessions of hour-long weekly individual therapy with a trained graduate-level therapist, who followed manualized session procedures and received ongoing supervision by licensed clinicians. Participants were assigned homework to complete between sessions. After an initial psychoeducation session, the second through seventh therapy sessions in PAT focused on augmented behavioral activation; these six sessions were included in the current analyses. Between sessions, participants were assigned to engage in positive activities, which included both pleasant activities (e.g., going to a favorite cafe) and activities to move towards mastery (e.g., practicing a new skill) or accomplishment (e.g., cleaning the kitchen). Participants were particularly encouraged to engage in social activities. During therapy sessions, participants were guided in imaginal recounting exercises, in which they recounted the details of their positive activities, with particular focus on sensory details and positive cognitions and emotions. Participants were instructed to close their eyes and envision a specific pleasant activity from the past week, slowly recounting the details out loud in the first-person, present-tense (e.g. "As I walk into my daughter's bedroom, I feel my heart pounding in excitement"). Participants spent approximately 10-25 minutes per session engaging in the recounting exercises, with guiding questions from the therapist (e.g., "stay there in the positive emotions, what are you feeling?", "What does that feel like in your body?") and gentle redirection in response to negative details; patients rated their mood on a scale of 0-10 before and after the exercise 40, 49.

Sessions were audio recorded, manually transcribed, and de-identified. We analysed the text produced by clients only, and not their therapists, and removed back-channel communication from the analysed text (e.g. "Yeah.", "Sure.", "Um.") by creating a custom script that uses the Backbiter algorithm 76 to remove short utterances that contain vocabulary items typically comprising back-channel communication. To ensure individual confidentiality, our analyses were conducted locally with techniques that do not necessitate the use of LLMs or any other online programs that could store and distribute individual data.

Semantic trajectory extraction

To extract semantic trajectories we use the masked language model Sentence-BERT (S-BERT) all-MiniLM-L6-v2 34 . The model is based upon the BERT-base-uncased model, which was trained on the Toronto book corpus and English Wikipedia >3 billion words, and has been fine-tuned on a further >1 billion sentence pairs. For context, a training set size of 100 million tokens has been shown to produce sentence embeddings that yield near maximal performance for predicting human brain responses to language 18 . This model uses bidirectional encoding to provide contextual embeddings of words, or word-sequences, within a dense 384-dimensional semantic space, derived from the model's final hidden representations. S-BERT embeds windows of text by performing mean-pooling over the contextual embeddings of the words within the window. With only six transformer layers and a 384-dimensional output space, the model strikes an excellent balance between representation quality, computational efficiency, and resilience to over-fitting on small data. Furthermore, its compact size and low computational cost ensure that our pipeline remains accessible to clinicians interested in deploying our approach.

Topological Data Analysis

Before performing persistent homology , we reduced the dimensionality of our embeddings to 100 by fitting Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the full dataset of embeddings and then projecting each vector into the resulting 100-dimensional subspace 87, 88 . This step concentrates the semantic variance into the leading components (explaining 82% of the original variance) and dramatically reduces the computational burden of subsequent computations.

To identify semantic loops, we use persistent homology with the Vietoris-Rips construction 43 . Approximately, persistent homology is performed as follows. First, persistent homology initializes 𝓃-dimensional spheres ("balls") that are centred upon each semantic embedding in the trajectory; connections (simplices) are formed between points that share balls with overlapping radii. The resulting set of points and inter-connecting simplices forms a structure that is similar to a network and is known as a simplicial complex; loops can be identified in a simplicial complex (Fig. 1B-D).

However, certain features may be resolved at different spatial resolutions (i.e., ball sizes; Fig. 1B-C). For example, if there are small distances between successive trajectory points, a loop can be identified with small ball sizes; if there are larger distances between trajectory points, greater ball sizes will be required to identify a similarly sized loop (Fig. 1B-C). Persistent homology addresses this issue by performing filtration. In a filtration, balls are progressively grown from each point and the ball size at which features are born and die (the filtration value) is tracked. In our analysis, the final result of persistent homology is a persistence diagram (Fig. 1E), that is generated for each imaginal recounting. In the persistence diagrams that we analyse, each point identifies a unique loop, and the coordinates of points on the persistence diagram indicate the filtration value at which the loop is born (x-axis) and dies (y-axis). Generally, very short-lived loops are considered noise 89 . So, we exclude these short-lived loops from our subsequent analyses and, for each narrative, count the number of remaining loops that persist across a broad range of spatial scales (Fig. 1E).

We created latent semantic dimensions for rumination and three dimensions of joy. Modelling affective dynamics

To test if the fluctuation of joy over the course of a narrative is an indicator of mental health, we applied an established approach rooted in dynamical systems theory 91 that considers the geometric progression of eudaimonic and hedonic joy over the course of a narrative. In this approach, we take the trajectory of hedonic or eudaimonic joy over the course of the narrative and represent the geometry of its unfolding by using time-delay embedding (Fig. S9). Here, time-delay embedding simply samples three neighbouring points in a trajectory of joy and uses these, respectively, as x, y, and z coordinates. By doing this for each 3-window of neighbours in a trajectory, the progression of joy is cast into a three-dimensional geometry. We assessed the choice of embedding dimension using the false-nearest-neighbours criterion 92 . The proportion of false nearest neighbours (FNN) increased with embedding dimensions greater than three (embedding dimension, m=3, FNN=8%; m=4, FNN=9.1%), supporting our parameter choice. Time delay embedding enables us to measure how unpredictably joy unfolds over the narrative, whilst respecting its temporal structure, by the Lyapunov exponent, which we measure by the Rosentein method 93 . The Lyapunov exponent measures how rapidly nearby points in the now three-dimensional trajectory diverge. High values indicate volatile and unpredictable affective dynamics. We note that the Lyapunov exponent is classically measured over noise-free deterministic dynamical systems and interpreted as a measure of chaos; since affective trajectories in text are noisy and unlikely to be deterministic, we cautiously interpret the exponent as a measure of dynamical volatility/unpredictably. After measuring the exponent for both eudaimonic and hedonic joy within a narrative, we collapse this into a single measure of affective volatility by averaging the two exponents. We did not incorporate interpersonal joy into this measure since we expect it to vary depending upon whether or not the recounting is discussing other people, which is not always the case in our data.

We used a recent approach for modeling topics using language model embeddings to interpret constructed semantic dimensions 35, 36 . In contrast to classical DATM 35 , which models topics from static word embeddings (requiring large corpora to train), Contextual-DATM (CDATM) learns topics from contextual embeddings produced by our fine-tuned S-BERT model.

CDATM is performed as follows. First, we create a semantic space, , that represents the set of 𝒱 contextual word meanings within a corpus. For each window, we use S-BERT to encode the contextual embeddings of each token, , in the window. The contextual 𝑡 embedding of a term depends upon the other terms that co-occur with it in its context window. As such, a given term that is found in context windows across a corpus will be represented in 𝑛 our semantic space times, given that it does not co-occur with an identical set of terms, in the 𝑛 exact same sequencing, multiple times.

A high-quality topic model should identify semantically rich topics that cluster a range of semantically similar, but distinct, terms across a semantic space. To ensure this, CDATM performs two pre-processing operations on the semantic space. First, to model only the most semantically rich terms as topics, we remove all vectors that relate to stop words or padding tokens from the embedding matrix. Second, for each token in our semantic space, we average over its redundant contextual representations, whilst ensuring that sufficiently semantically distinct uses of the word are indicated by correspondingly distinct embeddings. This problem can be illustrated by the polysemous word "bank". "bank" conveys highly distinct meanings in the utterance "I went to the river bank" as compared to the utterance "I took money out of the bank"; in these two cases S-BERT will encode two distinct and distant embeddings for the term. In contrast, "bank" conveys essentially identical meanings in the two utterances "I strolled to the river bank" and "I walked to the river bank"; nevertheless, since the words that co-occur with "bank" differ between the two utterances, S-BERT will encode distinct (but highly similar) embeddings for the term's use in each utterance. For this polysemous word, it is desirable to obtain a single embedding for all uses of the term "bank" in the context of river and a single embedding for all uses of the term "bank" in the context of money. Statistics: linear modelling

To analyse the relationships between mental health and narrative properties (loop number, ruminative content, joy dimensions, or affective volatility), we used Linear Mixed-effect Models (LMMs) in R (package: lme4). Each LMM included a mental health rating (anxiety, depression, stress, negative affect, positive affect, or relative affect) as a response and a measured topological/semantic variable as a predictor. We additionally included session, the length of client transcripts (number of context windows sampled from the narrative), and the number of interruptions by the therapist during the recounting as additional main effects. Subject identity was included as a random effect.

For LMMs testing for the effect of loops, negative fixation, and volatility, we initially included an interaction term between session and the focal predictor of interest and compared this interaction model to one without the interaction term by using likelihood tests. Since the relationship between loop number and negative fixation was non-linear, for the analysis testing the relationship between loop number and negative fixation we included the presence/absence of loops as a categorical predictor of negative fixation. For all other tests, loop number was considered as a continuous variable. Since our findings for loop number and negative fixation found little evidence of strong interacting effects, we mitigated the interpretive complexity of our later analyses on semantic content by exclusively fitting models that did not consider interacting effects between each latent dimension and mental health outcomes. We tested that LMM assumptions were met by measuring kurtosis and absolute skewness of model residuals. Kurtosis should be below 4 and absolute skewness should be less than one. Additionally, we visually inspected for homoscedasticity, for normal distribution of residuals, and for linearity by using qq-plots, histograms of residuals, residuals-versus-fitted plots, and partial residual plots. If model assumptions were initially violated, we transformed predictors and/or responses in the models. We note that there was a linear relationship between loop number and narrative length.

For each LMM, we used the R package effectsize 95 to predict the standardized β value-size of predictors on ratings of psychological well-being, whilst holding session constant. The absolute value of standardized β value sizes corresponds to the strength of the effect, whilst the sign of the effect corresponds to the direction of the association (~0.1=weak effect; ~0.2=medium effect; ~0.3=strong effect) 96.

We tested the significance of LMMSs including an interaction term by using type-three ANOVA; if no significant interaction was detected we used type-two ANOVA. To protect against multiple testing, we applied study-wide Benjamini-Hochberg correction across all p-values collected in the study 97 . All reported p-values are after Benjamini-Hochberg correction

Statistics: PLS and mediation models

For each outcome (DASS anxiety, depression, and stress subscales; PANAS negative and positive affect; and relative affect), we used Partial Least Squares regression (PLS) to construct a single latent component from the narrative predictors. PLS constructs weighted latent components that maximise covariance between predictors and the outcome variable while accounting for correlations among predictors. This approach allows individual narrative features to contribute with differing magnitudes and directions of association, rather than assuming that all features reflect a single uniformly valenced construct. The resulting latent component therefore represents a multivariate pattern of narrative organisation associated with each psychological outcome. Predictors and outcomes were z-scored within each individual to target within-person variation. In this analysis, to interpret the effect of loop number independently of narrative length, we divided the metric by the narrative length. To validate our earlier findings, we first fitted a single PLS to the whole data and extracted the PLS coefficients, which we normalise for each response between -1 and 1. The sign of PLS coefficients corresponds to the direction of their influence on the outcome variable and the absolute value corresponds to the relative strength of that property's influence.

We then test the combined influence of narrative properties as mediators for session-by-session improvements in mental health by deploying a nested cross-validation procedure. For each outcome variable, we proceed as follows. First, at each round of cross-validation, ten individuals are held out (test individuals) and predictors and outcomes are de-meaned within each subject, to accommodate the nested design. A PLS model is then fitted on the remaining individuals (training individuals). Second, we extract the first latent component (T1) of the PLS (which has similar relative, but not equivalent weights to the PLS coefficients; Fig. S6), which maximises the covariance between the predictors and the response. Third, T1 is computed for each test individual using the PLS model fitted on the training individuals; this ensures that the test subject's outcome (Y) is not used to define T1. Fourth, for each held-out participant (with at least three sessions) we fit three ordinary least squares (OLS) models on that participant's sessions: (i) T1∼1+session, (ii) Y∼1+session+T1, and (iii) Y∼1+session. Model i estimates how the putative mediator changes with session; model ii is used to estimate the direct effect of session on the outcome, that is not a consequence of T1; model iii is used to capture the overall effect of session on the outcome, without mediators. We measure the mediating effect as the coefficient of session in model i multiplied by the coefficient of T1 in model ii; the R² of session is simply that of model iii; and the R² of T1 (narrative R²) is the R² of model ii subtracted from that of model iii. Effects and R² values are computed at each round of cross-validation and averaged. To quantify uncertainty, we used a subject-level nonparametric bootstrap-resampling participants with replacement (2,000 replicates), recomputing the mean effects each time, and taking the 2.5th-97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution as 95% CIs. This entire procedure is repeated independently for each outcome variable.

Sensitivity analysis

To test the robustness of our results, we performed several sensitivity analyses. In the first, we tested the influence of context window size on measured narrative associations. In this sensitivity analysis, we finetuned independent S-BERT models at context window sizes of 50, 70, and 110 and used these finetuned models to extract narrative semantic trajectories by using its respective window size and a stride of 10% of the window size. Each model was finetuned according to the exact same general procedure as our main model (context window size of 90). After fine tuning models and extracting semantic trajectories, we then re-examined each presented analysis (Fig. S4-5). We find that all significant effects measured at a context window size of 90, except for eudaimonic/hedonic volatility, are recovered across each of these semantic scales. The associations between eudaimonic/hedonic volatility and anxiety or negative affect were measured to differ with smaller window sizes (50 and 70), but to still be recovered with the larger window size (110).

In our fourth sensitivity analysis, we evaluated the robustness of our results against our selection of seed phrases for latent semantic dimension construction. To do this, we offered Claude Opus 4.6 the prompt "Find an alternate phrasing for each of these seed phrases and substitute them into this script: [list of original seed phrases]", to generate alternative seed phrases. We constructed a latent dimension for each set of alternative seed phrases and tested outcome measures of mental health against narrative loadings onto these dimensions, as we did before. We repeated this procedure ten times and measured the median standardised β values for these associations across the ten rephrasings. As shown in Figure 5C, rephrasing generally had little influence on the directionality and absolute strength of our reported associations. Only the association between relative affect and hedonic joy was substantially stronger according to our originally defined latent dimensions, whereas the associations between positive affect and ruminative content and between anxiety and interpersonal content actually tended to increase in strength upon rephrasing.

In our final sensitivity analysis, we tested the robustness of our findings to noise injection. In this analysis, we injected noise of increasing magnitudes into the response and predictor in our data-from 0.1 to 1✕ their standard deviation (𝜎)-and re-fitted LMMs, and extracted the p-value of the main/interacting effect. We recorded the 𝜎 threshold at which the effect was no longer recorded as significant. We found weak robustness (𝜎≤0.1) for the associations between loops and depression and between eudaimonic content and relative affect (Fig. S5D). All other associations were measured to be relatively robust, withstanding at least 0.2𝜎 of noise.

Glossary

• Semantic (content): The meaning conveyed by language in a portion of text/speech.

• Embedding: A high dimensional coordinate that represents the semantics of a portion of text.

• Semantic trajectory: A sequence of embeddings taken throughout a recounting, which provides a spatial representation of the semantic progression of that recounting.

• Semantic dimension: A continuous axis within a semantic space that represents a particular conceptual property, e.g., happy↔sad.

• Topology: Describes the properties of an object (here, a semantic trajectory) that are preserved under continuous deformation (e.g., bending, twisting, stretching).

• Topological feature: A property of an object that remains unchanged under continuous deformation. In this study, the topological features are loops and the objects are semantic trajectories.

• Latent component: An underlying, unobserved factor inferred statistically from observed variables that captures shared variance or structure in the data. Here, latent components are used to describe the linear combination of linguistic properties that describe the most variance in an outcome rating of mental health.
