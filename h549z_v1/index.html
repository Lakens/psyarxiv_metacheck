Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p > .99 This did not significantly worsen model fit, χ²(9) = 0.40, p > .99.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

What we found

We found 14 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Generating errors during learning benefits subsequent recall, particularly when corrective feedback is presented immediately. Feedback in existing research conflates notification of error commission with provision of the correct target. Across two experiments, we examined the effects of immediate feedback, delayed feedback, and delayed feedback with an immediate "error" warning on cued and error-cued recall. In Experiment 1, warnings did not mitigate the detrimental effect of delayed feedback on cued recall, indicating that optimal pretesting requires immediate, answer-containing feedback. This challenges two hypotheses of errorful learning: that suppressing errors at encoding facilitates error correction (the error-suppression hypothesis), and that error commission promotes context binding which enables episodic reinstatement during feedback and at test (episodic reinstatement hypothesis). Immediate feedback improved cued recall performance but not participants' ability to recall the accuracy of their initial responses, further contradicting the possibility of episodic reinstatement at test facilitating target recovery.

In Experiment 2, delaying feedback improved error-cued recall, undermining mediator-based accounts which assume that errors function as additional retrieval cues, increasing the likelihood of successfully retrieving the target. We propose that error commission increases attention to the target at encoding, strengthening the cue-target retrieval route.

Error Correction Necessitates Immediate, Answer-Containing Feedback: Challenging Error Suppression, Episodic Reinstatement, and Semantic Mediation Accounts

Identifying optimal studying conditions is crucial for improving learning and achievement outcomes (Hartwig & Dunlosky, 2012). Despite common misconceptions (Karpicke et al., 2009; McCabe, 2011), considerable research demonstrates that retrieval practice (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006) and spaced practice (Cepeda et al., 2006) are effective strategies for improving retention (Carpenter et al., 2022; Geller et al., 2018). Retrieving information from memory results in increased accessibility later (Rowland, 2014), and students can leverage the testing effect by implementing self-testing into study sessions (e.g., via flashcards or practice quizzes; Carpenter et al., 2022). Spacing out study sessions over time, rather than engaging in fewer longer sessions, also boosts retention (Latimier et al., 2021). However, both testing and spacing increase the likelihood of committing errors during studying relative to rereading materials and massed practice (Pashler et al., 2003).

Should errors during learning be avoided or encouraged? Attitudes towards errorful learning in the classroom vary across cultures (Stevenson & Stigler, 1994). Behaviorist theories of learning predict that errors during learning might reinforce ineffective approaches or incorrect answers (Ausubel, 1968; Guthrie, 1952; Skinner, 1968). Interference theories suggest that initial errors may compete with the target during later recall (Melton & Irwin, 1940). It has also been argued that errors, when less frequent than correct responses, capture attention due to their relative novelty, prompting a reorientation of attention away from the task (Notebaert et al., 2009). According to these perspectives, errors during learning should be avoided to reduce the likelihood of repeating them at test (e.g., via more precise initial instructions; Bandura, 1986).

Some evidence seems to substantiate that making errors during learning can impair memory (Marsh et al., 2007; Cunningham & Anderson, 1968). For example, Pashler et al. (2003) had participants learn foreign language terms (Experiment 1) or obscure English vocabulary (Experiment 2). In both experiments, participants completed an initial study session and then a cued recall practice test, in which they were prompted to enter the target given the cue.

Participants either successfully retrieved a term or committed an error, and were presented with the correct answer. The authors found that items answered incorrectly in the initial practice test were more likely to again be guessed incorrectly at a later test relative to items that were initially guessed correctly. This result persisted across both stimulus types, suggesting that errors are likely to be repeated at test, even when immediate corrective feedback is provided.

However, studies like those implemented by Pashler et al. (2003) provide only correlational evidence, as the authors merely measured error generation rather than manipulating it. Therefore, the detrimental effects of errors on recall may be attributable to differences in item difficulty, with increased difficulty leading to higher likelihood of errors during both learning and test sessions. Additionally, researchers have used diverse types of stimuli, including number pairs (Cunningham & Anderson, 1968), multiple choice questions (Butler et al., 2008), foreign language terms (Potts & Shanks, 2014), and trivia questions (Butowska-Buczyńsk et al., 2025).

Variability within the item set in semantic relatedness between the cue and target may also account for mixed results. It is difficult to draw conclusions from these studies given potential confounds.

In laboratory environments in which these item effects are controlled, unsuccessful retrieval attempts benefit learning relative to read-only conditions. For example, Kornell et al. (2009) isolated the effect of initial guess accuracy from item difficulty by introducing "read-only" (read the cue and target) and "test" (guess the target, given the cue, before reading the correct target) conditions. They used fictional trivia questions (e.g., "What is the last name of the person who panicked America with his book Plague of Fear?" ; p. 990), which could never be initially guessed correctly, and weakly associated word pairs (e.g., "skyscraper-tower"; p. 991) selected to be guessed accurately about 5% of the time. Test conditions, in which participants made erroneous guesses during initial learning, outperformed read-only conditions at cued recall. This result transpired in both stimulus types and all experiments in which encoding time was consistent across conditions (five of six experiments).

The distinction between omission and commission errors also explains some of the apparent inconsistency within the errorful learning literature. Omission errors occur when a learner fails to retrieve an answer, while commission errors involve retrieving the wrong answer.

Commission errors, of which Kornell and colleagues (2009) conducted "the first definitive study" (Metcalfe, 2017), improve memory for the correct answer relative to omission errors (Slamucka & Fevreiski, 1983). Substantial evidence suggests that committing errors during initial learning sessions can increase retention under the right circumstances (Huelser & Metcalfe, 2012). The benefits of unsuccessful retrieval attempts persist when corrective feedback is provided (Huelser & Metcalfe, 2012; Bangert-Drowns et al., 1991), when studied word pairs are semantically related (Grimaldi & Karpicke 2012; Knight et al., 2012), and when erroneous guesses are committed by the learner rather than observed (Metcalfe & Xu, 2018).

Notably, feedback timing modulates the effect of committing errors when studying related word pairs. Employing the procedure developed by Kornell and colleagues (2009), Grimaldi and Karpicke (2012) demonstrated that introducing a brief delay before presenting feedback erased the advantage of erroneous guessing over read-only studying. Similarly, Vaughn & Rawson (2012) found that generating an erroneous guess given the cue resulted in higher recall performance than reading when the full pair was presented immediately, whereas reading outperformed guessing when feedback was delayed. Hayes and colleagues (2013) found, again, that the memory benefit of errorful learning disappeared when presentation of the full word pair was delayed by about 9.5 minutes. We found the same pattern of results with a delay of only approximately 5 minutes (Hausman et al., under review). (Conversely, when the studied materials are more semantically rich and complex, such as general knowledge questions and answers, errors improve learning even when feedback is delayed; Kornell, 2014.) Effects of feedback timing are of particular practical and theoretical importance. Practically, this research enables students to establish study conditions and habits that are maximally effective. Furthermore, any theoretical explanation of error correction should be able to account for the detrimental effect of delayed feedback.

A few studies have examined the impact of error warnings on memory for erroneously guessed information. Results vary by stimulus type, as is the case with feedback timing effects (Kornell, 2014). Warnings do not seem to facilitate error correction in the learning of mathematical concepts relative to feedback (Kuklick et al., 2023; Roper et al., 1977). Warnings were also not helpful in correcting initial errors when stimuli were sentences (Wang & Yang, 2021), but did enable error correction for multiple-choice questions (Marsh et al., 2012).

Warnings alone, unlike feedback, do not enable error correction more than no-feedback conditions for simpler stimuli such as foreign language terms (Pashler et al., 2005). Pashler and colleagues (2005) had participants study Luganda-English word pairs and complete two cued recall tasks. Participants who received feedback containing the correct answer during the initial cued recall task were better able to correct their errors on the final test than participants in the warning-only and no-feedback conditions, the final test scores of which were not different from one another. Later work using similar stimuli and protocols also found that, while feedback facilitated error correction, warnings did not (Fazio et al., 2010). Intuitively, this is plausible.

Although the correct answers were studied before initial guessing and thus were theoretically available, warnings would only prove helpful if errors resulted from an inability to discriminate between correct and incorrect answers, rather than an inability to retrieve the target. However, if errors resulted from inaccessibility of the target during guessing, participants would not have been able to update their memory even if they were warned of their error.

The above research does not address whether warnings might be useful in mitigating the effect of delayed feedback. Fazio and colleagues (2010) employed a study phase, an initial test, a review period in which participants viewed the correct answers without time constraints, and a final cued recall test following a delay. Feedback type during the initial test (no feedback vs warning vs feedback) and review (review vs no review) were manipulated between subjects.

Averaged across review conditions, feedback containing the right answer improved error correction relative to no-feedback and warning conditions, and there was a positive main effect of review, which facilitated error correction across all three feedback types. In the no-review conditions, only participants who received immediate feedback showed improvement on questions originally guessed wrong. These results suggest that warnings can facilitate error correction when combined with delayed feedback. However, they do not illuminate whether warnings in combination with delayed feedback might mitigate the feedback timing effect. The authors did not disclose comparisons of the warning/review and no-feedback/review conditions, which map onto the delayed-warning and delayed-no warning conditions in the present study. Furthermore, initial guessing accuracy was not manipulated, so item difficulty was not controlled for, and because the initial test was preceded by a study phase, the experiment does not provide insight into how warnings impact error correction for material that is tested before study.

Experiment 1

The purpose of Experiment 1 was to determine whether notification of error commission accounts for some or all of the feedback timing effect. Using a variation of the paradigm introduced by Kornell and colleagues (2009), we manipulated error commission (errorless vs errorful) and feedback timing (immediate vs delayed) within subjects. Critically, we also manipulated whether or not participants in the delayed feedback block received an immediate warning that they had committed an error. Thus, Experiment 1 examined whether receiving an immediate warning could attenuate the detrimental effect of delayed feedback on subsequent recall. Pragmatically, these findings will inform whether students should consider incorporating warnings into their study practice. As Fazio and colleagues (2010) point out, they will also determine whether teachers can substitute less time-intensive right/wrong feedback (referred to henceforth as "warnings") for answer-containing feedback (referred to henceforth as "feedback") without lessening its corrective impact.

Experiment 1 evaluates warnings as a way to attenuate the effect of delayed feedback. This experiment represents the first evaluation of the effect of warnings on errorful learning of associated word pairs when feedback is delayed. In line with previous work, we expected that cued recall performance would be higher in the immediate feedback condition relative to the delayed feedback condition (Grimaldi & Karpicke, 2012; Vaughn & Rawson, 2012).

Method

Participants

With the approval of Colorado State University's Institutional Review Board, 139 undergraduates were recruited to participate in this experiment in exchange for Introductory Psychology course credit. The undergraduate population at Colorado State University were 52% female, 71% White, 14% Hispanic/Latino, 4% Multiracial, 3% Asian, 2% Black or African American, and 1% unknown race/ethnicity when the data were collected (National Center for Education Statistics, 2019). Participants were excluded from analysis if technical issues prevented the experiment from running properly or if they indicated inattention by failing to correctly type presented words 80% of the time (final sample: n = 129). A power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1 indicated that, with a sample size of 128, we would be powered to detect between-subjects medium-sized effects (dz = 0.5, α = 0.05, 1-β = 0.8; Faul et al., 2007).

Materials

Stimuli.

Consistent with previous research (Kornell et al., 2009; Kornell, 2014; Vaughn and Rawson, 2012), participants studied English-English word pairs (e.g., picnic-park, swim-float) with a forward associative strength of .05 (Kucera & Francis, 1967), indicating that individuals initially guess the target (second word) roughly 5% of the time when given the cue (first word). For the purposes of this study, weakly related word pairs were optimal for experimental control (correct initial guesses are unlikely), theoretical investigation (the present study aims to examine theoretical accounts of errorful learning for related information), and ecological validity (in real study sessions, most studied answers are semantically associated with their corresponding questions but not easily answerable). Seventy-two word pairs were used, and 48 were randomly selected for each individual.

Measures. In the cued recall task, the studied cues were displayed sequentially and in randomized order. The survey prompted participants to type the target corresponding to each cue.

There was no time limit. After providing each target, participants answered a binary choice question indicating whether they correctly guessed the target when it was originally presented ("memory for initial performance").

Procedure

The study employed a mixed, partially factorial design. All participants completed both an immediate feedback block and a delayed feedback block. Block order was counterbalanced across participants to ensure order effects did not confound the effect of feedback timing. In both blocks, trials were split equally between errorless and errorful learning. In the delayed feedback block, participants were randomly assigned to either a warning or a no-warning group. Thus, feedback timing (immediate vs. delayed) and error condition (errorless vs. errorful) were manipulated within subjects and warning condition (warning vs. no warning) was between-subjects. Each block consisted of a study phase, a distractor phase, and a test phase. See Figure 1 for a graphic depiction of the procedure.

Before starting the blocks, participants were told they would learn a set of word pairs in three phases: a study phase, a short break, and a test. They were informed that this process would occur twice, and that their goal was to remember the correct targets.

Study Phase.

In the study phase, the screen displayed 24 randomly selected word pairs sequentially. For each pair, the cue was presented and participants were instructed to type their guess for the corresponding target into the text field. This was self-paced.

Error Condition.

The survey software randomly assigned half of the word pairs in each block to the "errorless learning" condition: that is, the participant's initial guess was internally deemed the correct target. In the "errorful learning" condition, comprising the rest of the items, each cue had a predetermined correct target. Errorful learning trials in which participants coincidentally entered the correct target (expected to occur in about 5% of these trials) were excluded from analysis.

Feedback Timing Condition.

The feedback component of the survey displayed the full word pair beneath text indicating that this was "the correct pair." Participants had nine seconds to type the target word, confirming they attended to it. In the immediate feedback block, feedback was presented immediately after each guess. In the delayed feedback block, feedback was provided after all 24 guesses had been completed (about 5 minutes after each guess was generated, on average).

Warning Condition.

The warning component displayed the text "INCORRECT!" in red or "CORRECT!" in green. Participants randomly assigned to the warning condition received a warning after each guess in the delayed feedback block. Participants in the no-warning condition did not receive warnings.

Distractor Phase.

Participants completed mental math problems in which they added up to five natural numbers (e.g., "6 + 3 + 5 + 9 + 1"). After five minutes, the survey automatically progressed to the test phase.

Discussion

Manipulating feedback timing and warning conditions in an errorful learning paradigm, we found that immediate feedback increased recall accuracy on the final test but warnings did not. These results suggest that warnings do not mitigate the effect of delayed feedback on error correction. Instead, answer-containing feedback must be provided immediately for optimal learning. Delaying feedback increased the likelihood of repeating one's error at test on trials in which participants committed test errors, but did not impact discriminability, which was high overall. This suggests that learners who received delayed feedback could remember their original accuracy but were unable to retrieve the correct target, so they resorted to generating a new erroneous guess.

General Discussion

This study represents the first test of a combined immediate error warning and delayed answer-containing feedback approach to pretesting.

Across two experiments, we examined the effects of feedback timing (immediate vs delayed) and error warnings (warning vs no warning) on error correction. Experiment 1 replicated past work indicating that delaying feedback impairs errorful learning and revealed that warnings did not mitigate this effect. Immediate feedback decreased the likelihood of making new (rather than repeated) errors when participants made errors at test, and discriminability was high overall but unaffected by feedback timing. Experiment 2 demonstrated that, when cued at test with their original erroneous guesses, immediate feedback impaired learning, increasing the number of repeated errors relative to both new errors and target recovery at test. These findings indicate that, for optimal learning from pretesting, feedback must be presented immediately following erroneous guesses and must contain the correct answer. Immediate error warnings are not sufficient to prevent or even alleviate the detrimental effect of delaying feedback on learning. Teaching and studying should therefore incorporate immediate, answer-containing feedback to maximize the benefits of errorful guessing. Importantly, these findings also yield theoretical insights into the cognitive mechanisms underlying the mnemonic benefits of error commission during learning. Multiple theoretical accounts, some of which are not mutually exclusive, propose explanations of error correction.

The prediction error hypothesis, also referred to as the error correction theory (Grimaldi & Karpicke, 2012), suggests that errors represent expectancy violations, prompting an attention shift (Mera et al., 2022). According to this account, the discrepancy between erroneous guesses and correct answers produces an error signal, increasing attention during learning and resulting in stronger encoding (Grimaldi & Karpicke, 2012). The hypothesis is consistent with multiple models of learning. Classical conditioning models (e.g., the Rescorla-Wagner model; Wagner & Rescorla, 1972) propose that learning is proportional to the difference between the predicted outcome and the actual outcome, with larger differences benefiting learning (Esber et al., 2025).

Similarly, connectionist approaches such as parallel distributed processing (Rumelhart et al., 1986) adjust the weighting of connections according to the difference between the actual and desired output vectors. Adaptive neurocognitive theories propose that committing an error elicits the activation of conflict monitoring mechanisms aiming to resolve competition between the error and target (Botvinick et al., 2001). The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), which evaluates ongoing performance, monitors response conflict (e.g., competition between candidates) in order to detect errors and then, according to this framework, signals this to other brain regions responsible for attention allocation and cognitive control (Decker et al., 2020; Kerns et al., 2004; Yeung et al., 2004).

Converging evidence from cognitive psychology and neuroscience literatures supports the prediction error hypothesis. Error commission prompts increased physiological arousal and slower responding (Houtman & Notebaert, 2013), suggesting neural and behavioral shifts.

Furthermore, increased activation of the prefrontal cortex, involved in cognitive control, is associated with slower responding following errors, providing a direct link between an indicator of increased attention and a behavioral adjustment (Kerns et al., 2004). High-confidence errors, which theoretically should correspond to greater expectancy violation, are more likely to be corrected than low-confidence errors, a phenomenon known as the hypercorrection effect (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001). Accordingly, high-confidence errors elicit greater activation in brain areas associated with attention, surprise, and error detection (Metcalfe et al., 2012). More surprising feedback produces better recall of contextual episodic details as well as better item memory, indicating an increase in attention during encoding (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001; Fazio & Marsh, 2009), and pupillometry evidence indicates that expectancy-violating outcomes following guesses produce surprise responses, the strength of which correlate positively with memory performance (Brod et al., 2018 (Brod et al., , 2022)).

While considerable theoretical and empirical work is consistent with the prediction error hypothesis, the mechanisms by which this attentional increase facilitates error correction remain unclear. The present findings inform four possibilities which may arise individually or in combination.

Mediated Retrieval Routes

First, the error signal may result in the creation or strengthening of mediated retrieval routes, in line with mediator-based accounts of error correction. The mediator effectiveness hypothesis has been frequently cited in the error correction literature as a possible explanation for how errors facilitate learning (Bridger & Mecklinger, 2014; Carpenter, 2001; Grimaldi & Karpicke, 2012; Pyc & Rawson, 2010). This account aims to explain the broader testing effect but also accommodates the benefits of failed retrieval attempts and is therefore a useful account of error correction (Mera et al., 2022). The mediator effectiveness hypothesis posits that retrieval attempts, even unsuccessful ones, engender the generation of mediators between the cue and target that are more effective than those generated during read-only studying. The authors parse mediator effectiveness into two components: mediator retrieval (accessibility of the mediator at test) and mediator decoding (efficacy of the mediator at eliciting the target; Pyc & Rawson, 2010). These components represent the cue-mediator and mediator-target connections, respectively. Mediators in this context are defined functionally rather than structurally (Pyc & Rawson, 2010). Theoretically, the mediating information may be a word, concept, or a more abstract representation. There is evidence consistent with the mediator effectiveness hypothesis.

When learners are instructed to generate mediating keywords during study, retrieval practice produces mediators that are more accessible and more likely to elicit the target at test relative to read-only trials (Pyc & Rawson, 2010). Furthermore, participants who learned word pairs through guessing rather than reading were more likely to recover the target given a semantically related term, indicating that semantically related words not explicitly generated by the learner may be activated during encoding and function as effective mediators during later retrieval (Carpenter, 2001; Coppens et al., 2016).

The errors-as-mediators hypothesis (also referred to as errors as stepping stones; Cyr & Anderson, 2015; and the additional cue hypothesis; Grimaldi & Karpicke, 2012) is a derivative of the mediator effectiveness hypothesis relevant specifically to the benefits of unsuccessful retrieval attempts (Huelser & Metcalfe, 2012; Knight et al., 2012; Kornell, 2014; Kornell et al., 2009; Vaughn & Rawson, 2012). According to this account, generating an erroneous guess enables the creation of an additional retrieval route from the cue to the target via the guess. The error serves as a semantic mediator, facilitating retrieval. In support of this view, Knight and colleagues (2012) found that accessibility of initial guesses correlated with target retrieval for related word pairs. Errors-as-mediators also provides an explanation for the detrimental effect of delaying feedback. Within this theoretical framework, feedback benefits learning when provided immediately because the presentation of the target in temporal proximity to the error strengthens the association between the error and the target, facilitating the creation of the cue→error→target retrieval route. When the cue is presented again at test, the error is activated, which in turn activates the target via spreading activation (Collins & Quillian, 1972). By contrast, a procedure in which feedback is delayed creates the cue→error association during guessing and the cue→target association during feedback. Without a strong error→target link, the error competes with, rather than facilitates, retrieval of the target during subsequent recall.

Note that the errors-as-mediators hypothesis is consistent with, but not entailed by, the mediator effectiveness hypothesis.

Theoretically, warnings should not make a difference in strengthening cue-target or mediated retrieval routes. The error signal must be temporally close to the target and/or error to facilitate stronger encoding, and a warning of error commission could not strengthen associations between the target and the cue or error if the target is not presented. Therefore, within this framework, there is no reason to assume that warnings could mitigate the effects of presenting the correct answer after the error signal has faded. This hypothesis is consistent with the absence of a significant warnings effect in Experiment 1, as it cannot account for the benefit of an error signal without concurrent target encoding. Regarding effects on error-cued recall, in which participants are presented with their original guess as a cue rather than the cue presented at encoding, trials for which feedback was provided immediately should outperform those with delayed feedback due to the relatively stronger error-target link. However, immediate feedback decreased on error-cued recall in Experiment 2 and was associated with greater persistence of original errors. These results challenge the mediated retrieval route hypothesis.

Error Suppression

Notification of error commission may enable learners to selectively suppress their erroneous guesses. Robustly replicated research on directed forgetting indicates that forgetting can be induced by instructing participants to selectively forget content (Bjork et al., 1968; Bjork, 1989). The present procedure differs from directed forgetting experiments in several ways: encoding phases in traditional list-method studies involve reading items rather than engaging in retrieval, do not involve updating paired associates, and typically include explicit instructions to forget certain items or lists (Sahakyan et al., 2013). However, implicit cues may be sufficient to induce forgetting. Foster and Sahakyan (2011) found that covert forget-cues (instructions to only remember List 2 items) were not associated with less forgetting than overt forget-cues (explicit instructions to forget List 1 items). Therefore, the error warning may function as a covert forget-cue, given that participants are instructed to remember the correct answers. It has been suggested that testing might elicit selective suppression of the error (Carrier & Pashler, 1992), weakening incorrect retrieval routes (Kornell et al., 2009). An error-suppression mechanism cannot account for increased retention of the target directly, but it may promote target recall by decreasing interference.

A beneficial effect of warnings would be consistent with the error-suppression hypothesis. If error-suppression accounted for some of the feedback timing effect, we would expect that warnings would help suppress the cue-error retrieval route, resulting in better target recall or decreased likelihood of repeating the same error at test. However, inconsistent with this hypothesis, warnings did not significantly improve target recall or even decrease the odds of repeating the error at test in Experiment 1. With regards to error-cued recall, the error-suppression hypothesis predicts that the error signal produced by a warning triggers selective suppression of the erroneous guess, so repeating the error on the final test should be less likely for participants in the immediate and warning conditions. Again, results were inconsistent with this hypothesis, as target recall was worse overall, and when participants committed errors at test, they were more likely to be repeated errors in the immediate feedback condition. Thus, neither experiment produced results that substantiate the error-suppression hypothesis.

Episodic Reinstatement

Although the previous mechanisms describe changes in semantic association strength, stronger episodic reinstatement of the encoding context may also explain the benefits of errors during learning. Increased attention may improve context binding such that the cue is more integrated with its encoding context (Boywitt & Meiser, 2012).

During later recall, the cue would facilitate episodic reinstatement of the context in which it was originally encoded. If the target is presented in close temporal proximity to the cue, as would be the case when feedback is presented immediately, increased salience of the encoding episode should enable recovery of the target. If the target is presented after a delay, the cue would be associated with two different episodes, introducing competition for retrieval at test. Furthermore, there would not be an error signal strengthening episodic encoding. Thus, in a delayed feedback condition, reinstatement of the episode in which the cue was originally encoded at test would not facilitate target recovery.

The episodic reinstatement hypothesis predicts that a warning should still produce an error signal, enhancing the association between the cue and the study episode. This should therefore increase the likelihood of reinstatement of the context in which it was originally presented when the cue is later presented during feedback and, in accordance with findings on recursive remindings (Jacoby & Wahlheim, 2013; Wahlheim & Jacoby, 2013), strengthen recall.

Without the warning, the original study episode would be less likely to be retrieved during delayed feedback, leading to a relatively weaker ability to retrieve the correct answer at test.

Experiment 1 results did not support this, however. Warnings did not affect target recall, and metacognitive evidence substantiates that immediate feedback did not enable learners to better discriminate between their original error and the target at test. The episodic reinstatement hypothesis predicts that immediate feedback and warnings should improve learners' ability to remember whether their initial guess was right or wrong due to enhanced reinstatement of the study episode at test. Signal detection analyses indicated no significant effect of either feedback timing or warnings on discriminability for the metacognitive question on errorful trials. In other words, immediate feedback and warnings did not help participants identify that their original guess was incorrect at test. Furthermore, if error signals during encoding improved discriminability, we would expect that immediate feedback and warning conditions would be associated with decreased likelihood of repeating the same error at test, because learners would be better able to identify that their original guesses were incorrect. However, when participants did make errors on the final test, they were less likely to make a new error in the immediate condition than the delayed condition. Descriptive statistics indicate that this reflects a decrease in new errors in the immediate condition (6.43 on average; delayed feedback trials involved 11.23 commissions on average) relative to a similar number of repeat error commissions (7.57 and 7.77 on average, in the immediate and delayed conditions respectively).

With regards to the effects of warnings on error-cued recall, it may be the case that the context binding of the cue causes reinstatement when the cue is presented again during feedback, facilitating an association between the error (part of the study episode) and the target (part of feedback). This would lead to improved subsequent recall when cued with the error at test.

However, Experiment 2 did not reveal an effect of warnings on error-cued recall, representing a failure to support an episodic reinstatement explanation.

Direct Retrieval Routes

The present experiments provide evidence against mediated retrieval, error suppression, and episodic reinstatement hypotheses of the feedback timing effect. One final possibility is that increased attention may strengthen the direct cue-target association. It follows from the prediction error framework that attention may be directed primarily to the target, causing stronger encoding and increased likelihood of retrieval at test. The answer-containing feedback is the trigger that theoretically evokes the expectancy violation and surprise which subsequently enhances attention. Also, when an error signal is produced, allocating attention to the correct answer is an effective strategy if one's goal is to minimize errors, as per error-correction models (Rescorla & Wagner, 1972; Rumelhart & McClelland, 1986). Finally, it has been suggested that conflict resolution mechanisms, which neuroimaging research indicates are triggered following errors (Mera et al., 2022), amplify neural responses to task-relevant information (Egner & Hirsch, 2005). When learners realize they committed an error, the feedback is the most relevant information to their goal of learning the correct answer.

The direct retrieval route hypothesis does not predict an effect of warnings on cued recall because the error signal would have faded by the time the correct answer is presented, constraining the strengthening of the cue-target retrieval route. There is no reason to expect an effect of warnings on error-cued recall either, as this explanation does not assume any functional role of the cue-error association in retrieval. This hypothesis is consistent with findings from Experiments 1 and 2, in which warnings did not affect cued or error-cued recall. While this hypothesis cannot explain the negative effect of immediate feedback on error-cued recall, it is not necessarily inconsistent with these findings. Therefore, the data from these experiments align most closely with the direct retrieval route hypothesis.

Conclusion

While several studies demonstrate that feedback must be provided immediately for learners to benefit from committing errors during studying, past methods have conflated the notification of error commission with the provision of the correct target. Parsing these two 29 aspects of immediate feedback, we show that error warnings do not mitigate the detrimental effect of delayed feedback on subsequent recall. We present evidence inconsistent with theoretical explanations of the feedback timing effect based on mediated retrieval routes, error suppression, and episodic reinstatement. Instead, we propose that the mnemonic advantage of immediate feedback is attributable to a strengthened association between the cue and the target.

These experiments yield both practical guidance for optimizing learning conditions and theoretical insights into the cognitive mechanisms underlying error correction.

Appendix A Model Selection

The balance of statistical power and type I error in random effects modeling is an ongoing discussion, particularly in cognitive psychology research. Some simulations suggest that fitting the maximally random model is optimal (Barr et al., 2013), while others indicate that one can achieve better statistical power without unduly inflating type I error rate by fitting a more parsimonious model (Bates et al., 2018; Matuschek et al., 2017). We used generalized linear mixed effects models to evaluate target recall and memory for initial performance at the trial level. In our regression models, we started with the maximal random effect structure, including intercepts and slopes for subject and item, and incrementally removed random effects as necessary to identify a model that converged. Then, employing likelihood-ratio tests (α LRT = .2) and the backward-selection heuristic recommended by Matuschek and colleagues (2017), we continued to simplify the random effects structure until reducing the model further would yield a significantly worse-fitting model.

Decisions about the order in which to remove random effects were guided by the following scheme, considering both theory and the observed data. First, we prioritized maintaining random effects for subjects, as it is theoretically plausible that baseline performance (accounted for by random intercepts) and the effects of experimental manipulations (accounted for by random slopes) would vary across individuals more than items, which were selected to have equal forward associative strength. Second, we prioritized random intercepts over random slopes when possible, given that some individuals have better memory than others and some items may be inherently more memorable than others.
