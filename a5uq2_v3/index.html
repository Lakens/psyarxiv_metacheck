Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

In a large, genre-balanced corpus of American English, blue attracts a far wider variety of unique chromatic specifiers than any other basic color term, while brown is the most heavily qualified of all by common, largely achromatic modifiers, suggesting that basicness is gradient.

1. Introduction

Human observers can discriminate an enormous number of colors, yet languages organize this perceptual continuum into a small inventory of named categories (Paramei & Bimler, 2024). In their foundational cross-linguistic survey, Berlin and Kay (1969/1991) proposed that languages draw their color vocabulary from a universal set of at most 11 basic color terms (BCTs), which in English are red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, pink, brown, black, gray, and white. The corresponding categories were argued to recur across languages and to emerge in a partially fixed order, a view later elaborated as the Universals and Evolution model and supported by the World Color Survey (Kay & Regier, 2003; Regier & Kay, 2009).

Following the conventions of the linguistic literature, we distinguish a color word from the region of color space it denotes: italics for the color word and its qualifiers, single quotation marks for an English gloss of a non-English color term, and small capitals for the category or region, so that blue is the English term, ao 'blue' is Japanese, and BLUE the area it names.

Basic color terms are conventionally required to be monolexemic, not subsumed under another color term, applicable across diverse referents, and psychologically salient (Berlin & Kay, 1969 /1991). In practice, these criteria sort terms only imperfectly, and the monolexemic criterion in particular has been questioned on the grounds that "basic" need not equal formal monolexemicity and that putatively non-basic color words are no less analyzable than basic ones (Levisen, 2026). Biggam (personal communication, September 2026) argues that "basicness" is better treated not as a dichotomy but as a continuum of relative basicness running from the most to the least basic terms. Kerttula (2002 Kerttula ( , 2007) ) gives that contention empirical purchase: for British English and Finnish corpora, she estimated basicness from a composite of four linguistic indices: the term's chronological primacy (earliest attestation), its corpus frequency, the breadth of its application across referents, and its derivational productivity.

This shift has been accompanied by further linguistic and psycholinguistic quantitative operationalizations: a cognitive salience index combining a term's elicitation frequency and mean list position (Sutrop, 2001); a category-strength measure that classifies categories as always-basic, potentially basic, or never-basic from naming consensus (Fider et al., 2017; Fider & Komarova, 2018); and a well-formedness criterion for modeling of color category denotata, under which a category is natural to the extent its instances are maximally similar within and dissimilar across categories (Douven & Gärdenfors, 2020). Whether the basic/non-basic boundary is sharp or graded is therefore an empirical question, one to which patterns of everyday usage are directly relevant. 'Blue' is the most prominent challenge to a single universal partition: several languages encode two or more basic-level BLUE categories rather than one, and the recurring partition runs along the lightness dimension. The best-studied case is Russian, in which goluboj 'light blue' is widely regarded as a 12th BCT distinct from sinij 'dark(er) blue' (Paramei, 2005 (Paramei, , 2007)). The two terms behave as basic-level terms rather than as color words with attached modifiers; moreover, they differ in their connotative and affective associations (on the distinction between conceptual color-emotion correspondences and felt affect, see Jonauskaite et al., 2026), and support categorical effects in speeded discrimination near the boundary, although the scope of that perceptual effect is debated (Martinovic et al., 2020 (Martinovic et al., , 2026;; Winawer et al., 2007).

The same lightness-based split of the BLUE area recurs widely: in Modern Greek, which has 12 BCTs including ghalazio 'light blue' alongside ble 'dark blue' (Androulaki et al., 2006); in Italian, where blu denotes the darkest, navy shades and azzurro the lighter shades (Paggetti et al., 2016; Squillante, 2013); for Tuscan (predominantly Florentine) speakers, the BLUE area is partitioned still more finely into three basic terms refined along lightness, celeste 'light/sky blue', azzurro 'medium blue', and blu 'dark/navy blue', each meeting the salience and nonsubsumption criteria for basicness in elicitation, focal-color, and corpus analyses, with all three categories separating predominantly along the lightness (L*) dimension of CIELAB space (Del Viva et al., 2022 , 2023); and in Japanese, where the light-blue term mizu is used by all but one of 57 speakers and is argued to be a 12th basic term alongside ao 'blue' (Kuriki et al., 2017).

Comparable distinctions extend across other Slavonic and circum-Mediterranean languages (Bimler & Uusküla, 2014 , 2017; Paramei & Bimler, 2024; Uusküla & Bimler, 2026).

Cross-linguistically, such refinement of color space is understood to proceed by a few routes (Paramei, 2020; Paramei & Bimler, 2024): the lexical partition (fission) of an existing category into two, as in the BLUE case; the insertion of a new category straddling the boundary of existing ones; and the emergence of a term in a previously under-named region. Color terms are also commonly derived from the objects that bear the color, and this naming after objects proliferates in commercial registers such as cosmetics, interior or car painting, where color names often prioritize evocative connotation over denotational precision (Anishchanka, 2013; Anishchanka et al., 2015; Espinosa-Zaragoza, 2026). English, by contrast, is usually described as having just one basic blue category: laboratory methods such as similarity sorting and semantic-map derivation do not recover a basic-level split within English blue (Uusküla & Bimler, 2016). Yet such conclusions rest largely on constrained elicitation tasks, which may understate distinctions that speakers nonetheless draw in spontaneous usage. It remains possible that American English speakers partition BLUE in everyday usage by attaching modifiers (most obviously "light" and "dark") to blue far more often than to other BCTs, so that light blue and dark blue function in practice as quasicategorical labels even without dedicated monolexemic terms. Bimler (2025) argues that the modifiers themselves fall along a gradient of "basicness" analogous to that proposed for color terms, with "light" and "dark" the most basic (applicable to nearly every term) and specialized modifiers such as "neon" or "dusky" the least basic. Our question is whether English blue is finely differentiated in how speakers modify and specify it, and what that differentiation implies for basicness.

Naturalistic language use offers a direct window onto this possibility. When color-modifier associations are used often enough they become conventionalized collocations, "crystallized metaphors", whose presence in written corpora indexes collective color cognition (Paramei & Bimler, 2024; Steinvall, 2007). The frequency and diversity with which speakers qualify a color term thus reflect how finely that region of color space is lexically differentiated, and large, genre-balanced corpora capture this in production rather than under task instructions.

Corpus methods have been applied to color terms before. Steinvall (2002 Steinvall ( , 2006) ) analyzed how basic color terms behave as type modifiers (as in white wine or red hair) in the Bank of English, concluding that such classifying uses concentrate on the most basic terms and that the color lexicon is best modeled as a radial category centered on them. Philip (2011) traced color-word collocation and connotation across English and Italian; cross-linguistic corpus comparisons of color-term collocations continue (e.g., Spanish versus Chinese; Xu et al., 2026). Constructiongrammar analyses of color words in COCA itself map how they combine with high-frequency prepositions and modifiers (Sandford, 2026). Furthermore, diachronic collocation counts have been used to adjudicate the basic status of competing terms, as for the two terms for 'brown' in Russian (Bochkarev et al., 2023), French (Gréa et al., 2026), and Italian (D'Achille & Grossmann, 2017).

Computational work points the same way: the corpus frequency of color terms tracks the Berlin-Kay order (Mohammad, 2011), the distribution of color terms across Italian idiomatic multiword expressions reproduces that same order (Squillante, 2013), and a term's adjectival-modifier usage predicts how closely its distributional (language-model) representation aligns with perceptual color space (Abdou et al., 2021). Building on this tradition, but inverting Steinvall's focus, we quantify the qualifiers that attach to color terms rather than the color terms that act as classifiers. To our knowledge, the relative use of chromatic specifiers across all 11 English BCTs has not been systematically compared.

Here we use the Corpus of Contemporary American English (COCA; Davies, 2008-) to ask whether "light," "dark," and other achromatic modifiers and chromatic specifiers attach disproportionately to blue. For each of the 11 BCTs, we quantify the relative frequency of light and dark among the most common preceding collocates, and measure both the overall rate of qualification and the number of distinct (unique) specifiers each color term attracts. Because both color words and their qualifiers can be used non-chromatically, we introduce two corrections: one for the proportion of truly chromatic uses of each color term, and a Method of Matched Unambiguous Cases for the qualifiers. If American English speakers differentiate blue in practice, we predict that the term blue will attract "light" and "dark" and similar qualifiers more than other color terms, and that this English inventory will resemble the object-derived "blue" vocabularies documented in other languages (e.g., Griber et al., 2018 Griber et al., , 2026;; Grossmann & D'Achille, 2016). This corpus approach sits at the vision-language interface. Vision and language are often studied as separate systems, yet they interact whenever we use words to describe what we see. Here we examine that interaction directly, treating the structure of linguistic production as an empirical record of how a perceptual continuum is carved into categories. As such, the method is complementary to the behavioral, psychophysical, and neuroimaging approaches more commonly brought to this interface, supplying naturalistic, population-scale evidence that those methods cannot.

2.2. Target terms and collocates.

For each of the 11 English BCTs (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, pink, brown, black, gray, white) we extracted all occurrences together with the 150 most frequent words immediately preceding each term (collocates). Counts for gray combine the gray and grey spellings; the two were analyzed separately in the original pass and are merged here, because grey is the minority spelling in American English and carries a far higher proportion of surnames. Qualifiers were identified residually rather than sought directly. Each collocate was assigned to a linguistic category that would disqualify it as a qualifier: punctuation, determiner, preposition, pronoun, verb, numeral, common noun, proper name, or fixed expression (as in blood orange, where the collocate forms part of a conventional compound rather than qualifying the color term). Proper names were chiefly first names, since several of the color terms are themselves surnames. Every collocate was assigned actively, so that items falling into any of these categories were removed from further consideration; the words that remained unassigned were treated as qualifiers of the color term. Items that were difficult to assign were resolved by discussion among the authors, and the assignments were independently checked by a second author. Throughout, we use the umbrella term "qualifier" for any qualifying word that precedes a color term.

Whether a qualifier carries color content was not a sorting criterion at this stage. That division emerged from the data after the common/unique split described in Section 2.5, and we report it as a result in Section 3. We introduce the terminology here for clarity of exposition: (chromatic) "specifiers" themselves carry color content (e.g., navy, cobalt, lime); (achromatic) "modifiers" adjust lightness or saturation (e.g., light, dark, bright, dull, neon, fluorescent). Every specifier and every modifier is thus a qualifier, though specifiers and modifiers remain distinct. Because the division is a result rather than a criterion, the axes of Figures 6, 7A, 8A and 10 are labeled by the split that was imposed, all qualifiers, common qualifiers or unique qualifiers, and not by the chromatic or achromatic character the Results go on to report. Neither group is pure: the common list contains nine qualifiers that carry color content (royal, cherry, golden, creamy, rosy, reddish, bluish, cadmium and jet), and the unique list contains eleven that carry none (bold, off, dim, hard, true, intense, darker, strange, sleek, flat and pure). Chromatic specifiers are usually non-basic color terms, though a basic term can itself specify another, as in blue-green. . Correction 1: Non-chromatic uses of color terms. Color terms are not used equally often to denote color. Some are surnames (green, brown, grey/gray); orange may denote a fruit; some appear metonymically (yellow press, pink slip) or in idioms (out of the blue, born in the purple); and in fixed expressions (Red Cross, NYPD Blue (a US television series named for the blue uniforms of the New York Police Department), red carpet) or species names (red fox, blue jay, grey whale) the color is conventional or inseparable rather than descriptive.

We therefore estimated, for each basic color term, the proportion of occurrences that are truly chromatic (Figure 2). For each term we drew a random sample of instances (100 per term; 300 for blue, brown and green; 299 for grey/gray and red), and hand-coded each color-plus-noun phrase as chromatic, non-chromatic, or unclear. Pink and purple each rest on 100 coded instances, the same sample size used for black, orange, white, and yellow; blue, brown and green each rest on 300, and grey/gray and red on 299. The pink and purple workbooks were created by copying the blue workbook, which held three sample sheets; the first sheet was recoded for the new term and the two unused sheets were left in place still holding the blue rows. All 200 such rows in each workbook were identified as duplicates of the blue samples and excluded, a reading the coder has confirmed, so pink and purple rest on one sample each. The supplementary material retains those rows, flagged, so the exclusion can be checked. A phrase was coded non-chromatic if it failed any of three tests: a name test (is it a proper or species name?) ; a referent-color test (if the referent's actual color changed, would the phrase keep its meaning? a green card stained purple is still a green card, whereas a yellow water bottle whose paint burns away is no longer a yellow water bottle); and a qualifiability test (can the term take that color's characteristic modifier in context? blue eyes becomes light blue eyes, whereas NYPD Blue does not become *NYPD light Blue; following linguistic convention, an asterisk marks a collocation that is not possible).

Because colors differ in the modifiers they take, the modifier used was each term's most frequent modifier in the corpus (for example pale for yellow). Instances still ambiguous after the tests were coded 'unclear'. Each term's chromatic-use proportion, the chromatic instances as a fraction of all sampled instances, was applied as Correction 1. To assess reliability, a second author independently re-coded a random subset of 165 instances (15 per term) using the same protocol and blind to the original codes; agreement was almost perfect (Cohen's κ = 0.88, 93.9% raw agreement), with disagreements confined to hard cases, chiefly fixed expressions in which the color term is the identifier but the meaning goes beyond it (red carpet, yellow line, White Sox) and technical or domain-specific uses (periaqueductal gray, cadmium yellow). Both coders are native speakers of American English, which the protocol requires: the qualifiability test turns on intuitions about fixed expressions, as in red fox not becoming *light red fox. We therefore treat the coding as reproducible.

2.5. Common versus unique qualifiers.

We distinguished qualifiers that are shared across more than one basic color term (common qualifiers; e.g., dark, light, bright, pale, deep) from those used with essentially a single term (unique qualifiers; e.g., navy, cobalt, cerulean for blue). We name the two groups by how widely they are shared, not by what they denote; the chromatic character of the second group is reported in Section 3 as a finding. Operationally, we counted a specifier as unique to a color term when it occurred with that term and with no other basic color term in the collocate data. This is the strictest available criterion and imposes no further threshold: a specifier that qualifies even one other basic term is counted as common. It does, however, inherit the cutoff at 150 collocates per term, since a specifier can only be seen to be shared if both of its color terms are sampled deeply enough to show it. Section 3.7 addresses that limitation directly, by extending every list to a common frequency floor. On this rule navy, cobalt and cerulean are unique to blue, whereas royal, which qualifies both blue and purple, is not. Three entries of the original list are set aside here as artifacts of the extraction rather than qualifiers: uncle and crumbled under blue and package under yellow. A fourth, dioxazine, was recorded under blue but occurs only with purple on this release and is counted there. Together these move blue's value in Figure 8A from 6.31% to 6.07% and yellow's from 2.33% to 2.26%, and leave every ordering unchanged. The common/unique split is the one we imposed, and it is independent in principle of whether a qualifier carries color content. That the two line up as they do, with common qualifiers predominantly achromatic modifiers and unique ones predominantly chromatic specifiers, is an outcome of the data rather than a design decision, and we report it as a result (Section 3). We summarized the full qualifier-color structure as heatmaps of collocation frequency (Figures 7 and 8) and computed, per basic color term, the overall percentage of qualification by all qualifiers together and the variety of unique specifiers. We refer to the combined weight of these two measures, how many different qualifiers a term attracts and how often those qualifiers occur with it, as the density of qualification. A color term is densely qualified when it both draws many distinct qualifiers and draws them frequently; density is thus a joint function of type count and token frequency, not either alone.

The strict criterion is conservative but sensitive to sampling depth: a specifier overwhelmingly associated with one term is reclassified as common if it occurs even once with another, and whether it does so depends on how deeply the lists are read. We therefore repeated the classification under a dominance criterion, assigning each specifier to the term carrying at least a given share of its total occurrences, using collocate lists read down to a frequency of 2 for all eleven terms on the same pre-2020 release. We report the result at a dominance of 90% and give the full range from 70% to 100% as a robustness check (Section 3.6). This is a follow-up to the main analysis, not a replacement for it: the strict rule and the 150-collocate cutoff remain the basis of Figures 6 to 8, and the dominance analysis asks whether the common and unique division survives when every list is read to the bottom. It re-sorts the specifiers already identified rather than searching for further ones; discovery at a matched sampling depth is the business of the frequency-baseline control of Section 3.7.

Two caveats attach to the unique-specifier counts in particular. First, because collocates were identified by adjacency, a pre-color word is occasionally one that modifies the following head noun rather than the color term itself (as in weathered gray car, where weathered modifies car); the matched-unambiguous-cases device of Section 2.4 removes such cases from the light/dark analysis, but the broader collocate tabulation does not screen them out.

Second, the uniquespecifier tail is heterogeneous: alongside conventional shade terms (e.g., navy, cobalt, snowwhite, shocking pink) it includes idiomatic (mellow yellow), brand-derived (Day-Glo, Cheerful), and technical items (underglaze blue), together with comparatives of achromatic terms (e.g., darker) that arguably belong with their base form. We retain these items as they occur in the corpus rather than curating them by hand; because the central result is distributional (blue carries by far the largest and most varied set of unique specifiers, while brown sits near the floor on that measure), it does not rest on any individual collocation, and the qualitative pattern is unaffected by the status of particular borderline items.

2.6. Frequency-baseline control: procedure.

The collocate extraction of Section 2.2 was carried out on the COCA release available when this project began, which held approximately 560 million words across five registers. COCA has since been expanded as described in Section 2.1. The added material is drawn from the same 1990 to 2019 period rather than from later years, so the two releases differ in size and in register coverage rather than in the span of time they sample. To test the robustness of the central result we repeated the qualifier analysis on the current release. It could not have been run on the earlier one: the COCA web interface serves only the release current at the time of query, so the pre-2020 collocate lists cannot be re-queried and a control built after the expansion necessarily rests on the enlarged corpus.

For each of the eleven basic color terms we retrieved preceding collocates from the COCA web interface (span one word left, zero right, sorted by frequency, minimum mutual information of 3). Rather than taking a fixed number of collocates per term, we extended each list until every term reached a common frequency floor of 37 occurrences. This matters because the propername load differs sharply between terms: an equal-rank cutoff truncates different terms at different frequencies, and at 100 collocates, the depth at which this control's lists were first drawn (the main analysis of Section 2.2 read to 150), brown truncates at a frequency of 73 but blue at 34, so the two lists are not sampled to comparable depth, and any comparison of qualifier variety is biased against the terms that carry many names. The floor of 37 is the deepest level at which all eleven lists can be compared, because the interface returns collocates down only to that frequency for red and green; those two terms therefore reach the floor exactly, while the other nine extend below it. Lists were extended to 221 collocates for brown, 175 for white and 124 for black. Each term's overall corpus frequency was taken from the same interface and used as the denominator throughout.

Collocates were then classified using an explicit rule-based scheme applied without reference to the original sorting, so that the control tests the result against a change of classifier as well as a change of release. Each collocate was assigned to exactly one of three classes: specifier (naming a particular shade or referent), common modifier (a scalar adjustment of lightness, saturation, brightness or surface), or other (proper names, determiners, punctuation, size and shape adjectives, and domain terms). The third class plays the same exclusionary role as the linguistic categories of Section 2.2, so that here too qualifiers are what remains once non-qualifying words are removed. The first two classes, however, encode the chromatic and achromatic distinction as a sorting criterion, whereas in the main analysis that distinction emerges only after the common and unique split. This is a real difference between the two schemes, and a further reason the control's absolute values are not comparable with Figures 6 to 8; it does not bear on the ordering, which is what the control tests. A specifier was counted as unique to a term when it occurred with that term and with no other. Rates were compared using the exact conditional (binomial) test for two Poisson rates, which takes each term's overall corpus frequency as its exposure. To equalize sampling effort we applied individual-based rarefaction (Hurlbert, 1971), reducing every term's collocate pool to 1,315 tokens, the pool size of the smallest term, purple, and recomputing the expected number of distinct unique specifiers each term would then be expected to show. Two terms need qualification under this scheme. Purple's own unique specifiers all fall below the common floor of 37, so its value is computed from its complete list, which runs down to a frequency of 4, and is marked accordingly wherever it is plotted. Red and green reach the floor exactly, so any unique specifiers they carry below it are invisible here and their values should be read as lower bounds; the other nine terms are sampled below the floor and are unaffected.

2.7. Inferential tests. Differences between color terms are tested on rates rather than on raw counts, because the natural exposure for a qualifier is the number of times its color term occurs.

For uncorrected counts we use the exact conditional (binomial) test for two Poisson rates, which conditions on the total of the two counts and needs no large-sample approximation. The corrected rates divide additionally by the Correction 1 proportion, which is itself estimated from a coded sample of 100 to 300 instances per term and carries uncertainty of its own; for corrected comparisons we propagate that uncertainty, drawing the chromatic-use proportion from its Jeffreys posterior and the qualifier count from its Poisson sampling distribution, and reading the rate ratio from 40,000 draws. Intervals are 95% throughout. We report rate ratios with intervals rather than p values, because the question is how far the terms differ rather than whether they differ at all. The same exact test is applied to the unique-specifier rates of Section 3.7.

2.8. Which COCA release underlies which figure. Because the two releases serve different purposes, we set out the provenance of each panel here. Every collocate count in the main analysis comes from the pre-2020 release and from the same 150-collocate lists, and so do records. The frequency-baseline control of Sections 2.6 and 3.7 is on the current release of necessity and supplies Figure 9, whose panel C repeats the specifier heatmap of Figure 8B on that release, using the control's own classification, so that the two can be set side by side. The supplementary material reports the plotted value of every bar and every heatmap cell, so that each panel can be reconstructed from the list it came from. Figure 10 is the one exception, because it is drawn at four candidate dominance thresholds rather than at a single criterion; its counts at every threshold are tabulated separately.

The figures also depart from the original analysis in two respects, both of which affect every term alike. The original figures expressed each qualifier count as a proportion of the tokens recovered in that term's collocate extraction, and used the gray spelling alone. Here every panel instead uses the term's overall corpus frequency as the denominator and merges the gray and grey spellings. The change of denominator makes the bar charts directly comparable with the rate statistics of Section 3.7, which take corpus frequency as the exposure, and merging the spellings is the correctness fix described in Section 2.2. Both changes raise all eleven values by a similar factor and leave the ordering unaffected.

4. Discussion

Three findings stand out. First, blue attracts a far larger and more varied set of unique qualifiers, terms used with essentially only blue, than any other basic color term, and these turn out to be almost entirely chromatic specifiers such as navy, cobalt, and cerulean; this is blue's distinctive signature (Figure 8). Second, brown is also heavily qualified, but by a different route: it draws chiefly on common qualifiers, which turn out to be largely achromatic modifiers such as light and dark, while sitting near the floor on unique specifiers (Figures 4 and 7) -the "brown surprise." Third, qualification is graded across the eleven terms rather than falling into two tiers, a distribution we take up below as evidence for a continuous rather than a sharply discrete basic/non-basic boundary.

The two signatures lie on different axes of elaboration, and the wider literature speaks to each.

The first is a lightness axis -light/dark and related achromatic qualifiers -on which blue is high but not distinctive, since brown and green are qualified at least as heavily (Figures 4 and 7).

Independent naming data track this axis: in a free-naming task embedded in an EEG study, English speakers labeled cool-region samples (blues and greens) largely with hyponyms or achromatic-modified terms (Martinovic et al., 2026), and, in an online experiment spanning the Munsell solid, light blue was among the most frequently produced English color expressions, ahead of the basic terms yellow and red (Jraissati & Douven, 2018). British-English corpus data converge as well: in the BNC, blue mostly occurs unqualified, but when it is qualified the most frequent qualifier is dark (259 instances), ahead of pale (215), deep (129), bright (118), and light (75) (Kerttula, 2002), echoing the dark-over-light ordering we observe for blue in COCA. These qualifiers do not all index the same dimension, however: light and dark concern lightness, whereas bright concerns colorfulness and pale chiefly saturation, so the dark-over-light contrast rests specifically on dark and light, which nonetheless attach densely to blue across both major varieties of English. Brown was the most densely modified of all basic terms by these common, achromatic qualifiers, a point we take up in Section 4. and sapphire), the very items that recur as blue's unique specifiers here, with a further term, aqua, identified as an expanding addition to the family.

Similarly, other specified blue terms (sky blue, dark blue, navy blue) rank among the 30 most frequent color descriptors English speakers offer for the BLUE area (Mylonas et al., 2017), and the referential range of blue shifts with context, with competitors such as navy locally displacing it for darker shades (Anishchanka, 2013; Anishchanka et al., 2015).

Cross-linguistic naming data point the same way: in the five-language study of Mylonas et al. On this reading, English speakers differentiate multiple blues in practice, like speakers of other languages, but through a rich modifier-and-specifier vocabulary, not through one distinct basic term of the kind that, in other languages, has yielded a second basic 'blue' (Paramei, 2005 (Paramei, , 2007 (Paramei, , 2020;; Paramei & Bimler, 2024). Two or more basic 'blue' terms are documented in Russian,

Greek, Italian, Turkish, Maltese, Thai, Lithuanian, and Japanese (Androulaki et al., 2006; Bimler & Uusküla, 2017; Kuriki et al., 2017; Ulusoy et al., 2026). English is not among these. Like Finnish and Estonian, which are sometimes assumed to split BLUE but in fact retain a single basic term, with 'light blue' expressed only by transparent compounds (Uusküla, 2007), English meets the same communicative demand through modification rather than through a new basic term. We therefore read the dense light/dark modification of blue as this modifier strategy at work, not necessarily as a sign that English is moving toward a second basic 'blue'.

That a lighter-blue term can become deeply entrenched even where a darker term is more frequent is shown directly for Italian: in a Stroop task, the secondary basic term azzurro 'lightand-medium blue' produced strong semantic interference and facilitation while the more frequent primary basic term blu 'dark/navy blue' produced neither, indicating greater entrenchment, hence, processing automaticity for the light-blue concept (Paramei et al., 2026).

Tuscan Italian shows the endpoint of such a trajectory: there the BLUE area is lexicalized by three basic terms, celeste, azzurro, and blu, that separate chiefly along lightness, the very dimension that "light" and "dark" index in our English data (Del Viva et al., 2022). Cross-linguistic evidence suggests that such a partition need not erode the primary category: comparing languages with and without a separate light-blue term, Uusküla and Bimler (2026) found that subdividing BLUE leaves the BLUE-GREEN boundary essentially unchanged, and argued that fission is driven less by any general weakening of the primary 'blue' term than by local cultural or historical factors that single out 'light blues' for a dedicated label.

Whether English light blue is on a similar trajectory, and whether the blue category is poised to undergo fission under continued communication pressure, are open questions.

A direct test of American English would be diachronic: tracking whether qualifier-marked "blues" such as light blue or navy drift toward basic status over time. Because the COCA releases that postdate the version analyzed here remain limited (a single expansion, with a shifted genre composition), a fuller diachronic test would still require historical corpora such as the Corpus of Historical American English or the Google Books Ngram corpus, the latter already used to chart the rise of an emergent basic term among the "Russian browns" (Bochkarev et al., 2023). A comparable diachronic-corpus approach has tracked shifts in the dominant uses of a single color term, green, across decades of the Polish press (Stanulewicz & Pawłowski, 2026).

Comparable trajectories have been proposed for teal and lavender in American English (Lindsey & Brown, 2014), and turquoise and lilac in British English (Mylonas & MacDonald, 2016).

Whether such drift is underway for English blue is an empirical question for future work.

4.2. The brown surprise.

Brown was qualified by "light," "dark," and other shared qualifiers at least as strongly as blue. Its density is of a different kind, however: it rests on common, largely achromatic modifiers of lightness and purity, with brown near the floor on the unique specifiers that distinguish blue (Figures 7 and 8). Crucially, brown is standardly treated as the name of a single basic category in English, so dense common qualification cannot, by itself, be read as a signature of an emerging basic category. Whether English brown might itself support a two-term split, as Russian, French and Italian do, is a question our frequency counts do not settle. We treat brown not as a control on blue but as a parallel case: both terms denote regions of color space that are densely lexicalized by qualifiers rather than partitioned by new basic terms.

Brown's exceptional status recurs cross-linguistically, if in different guises. In Italian, for instance, marrone is nearly absent from idioms and falls to the bottom of the Berlin-Kay ordering those idioms otherwise reproduce (Squillante, 2013), yet corpus analysis shows it increasingly attracting both achromatic modifiers and chromatic specifiers (D' Achille & Grossmann, 2017). Mylonas and Griffin (2020) found brown among the most generative English color terms (ranked fifth) in a corpus of Twitter messages. Corpus-derived basicness rankings concur: in Kerttula's (2002) British-English study, brown attains one of the highest relativebasicness scores, on a par with that of blue, and far exceeding its own hyponyms (tan, russet, beige, and the rest), and it heads the largest color-term family she documents, a breadth she attributes to warm colors being lexicalized more readily than cool ones. Because idiom participation and modification rate are different measures, the contrast is suggestive rather than direct, but it reinforces that brown is seldom a typical case.

A related cross-linguistic pattern bears on what those qualifiers might denote: Russian, French, and Italian each designate the BROWN area with two partly overlapping terms, an older broad term for impure, grayish, object-tied browns and a newer term for saturated browns that initially entrenched in dyed (uniformly-colored) goods and has risen toward basic status (Bochkarev et al., 2023; D'Achille & Grossmann, 2017; Gréa et al., 2026). Whether the qualifiers English attaches to brown likewise separate saturated browns from duller, object-tied gray-browns is a question our frequency counts cannot settle, but a natural target for future denotational analysis.

Brown's heavy qualification may also follow from its being a related color. Unlike the other basic chromatic categories, brown exists only in relation to its surround, as a dark appearance seen against a brighter one, so that a yellow or orange stimulus looks brown precisely when luminance contrast renders it dark (Bartleson, 1976; Buck, 2015; Buck & DeLawyer, 2014; Shinomori & Werner, 2023). The perceptual status of brown has been debated: Fuld et al. (1983) raised the possibility that brown is a perceptually elemental sensation not reducible to a mixture of yellow and black, a proposal that Quinn et al. (1988) later attributed to a response-order artifact, reaffirming the six elemental colors of the opponent-process theory (Werner & Wooten, 1979). What is not disputed is that brown is constitutively dark and surround-dependent, so that lightness is an intrinsic, defining dimension of the category rather than an incidental one.

Speakers may therefore reach for "light" and "dark", "duller" versus "intense" etc. to subdivide BROWN more readily than they do for hues that remain recognizable across a wide range of lightness, consistent with the observation that brown's familiar hyponyms, such as tan and beige, largely denote lightness variants (Lindsey et al., 2020), even though lightness is not the whole story: as the two-term BROWN splits noted above suggest, purity and saturation, the grayer, duller browns versus the more saturated ones, are also central to how BROWN is subdivided. (On the salience, associations, and prototypical referents of 'beige' specifically, see Stanulewicz & Komorowska, 2026.).

None of this means that English wants for a brown vocabulary. Nutty, mousy, cinnamon, caramel, cocoa, dirt, sienna, sepia, tobacco, dun and walnut all occur in these collocate data, each of them with brown and with no other basic color term, and they differ in hue and undertone as much as in lightness: chocolate and chestnut are red-shifted, whereas mustard and olive pull toward yellow. What separates them from blue's specifiers is frequency rather than existence. Brown's list of 150 collocates truncates at a frequency of 61, against 42 for blue, because brown is also a common surname and its collocates are heavily loaded with first names; every one of the terms just listed falls below that cutoff, and only chocolate, at 130 occurrences, survives it. That is precisely the sampling bias the frequency-baseline control of Section 3.7 was built to remove, and at a matched depth brown still carries five unique specifiers against eighteen for blue. The contrast is one of degree, then, and not of presence against absence. It also leaves the reading above intact: the brown vocabulary speakers reach for most often is the lightnessbased one, with dark and light at 1,351 and 1,136 occurrences, while the hue-differentiated terms sit in the tail.

This account also reconciles an apparent tension with other measures. Bimler (2025) excluded brown from his set of highly modifiable "core" color terms on the grounds that it is a related color, definable only as a darkened appearance against a brighter surround, which constrains its modification. Communicative-need inferences likewise place browns among the lowest-demand regions of color space (Twomey et al., 2021). These two need-based statements are compatible rather than opposed: brown's referential prominence as an object-and food-anchored color is a different measure from its low absolute demand as a narrow, constitutively dark region of color space. Brown thus looks peripheral on naming-survey and need-based measures yet central in our collocation data as well as in Kerttula (2002) and Mylonas and Griffin (2020), a divergence that resolves once brown's constitutive dependence on lightness is taken into account, and that arguably sharpens rather than weakens the case that its relational status is what drives the pattern.

The dense qualification of brown may reflect this relational structure as much as communicative need and referential demand, the forces that jointly shape color lexicons (Gibson et al., 2017; Zaslavsky et al., 2019); distinguishing the two forces is an open question.

4.3. Elaboration without new basic terms.

The graded distribution of qualification frequencies across color terms, and the qualification along different axes, is consistent with the view that the boundary between "basic" and "non-basic" categories is continuous rather than discrete: the relative basicness continuum attested on independent grounds from corpus data (Kerttula, 2002 (Kerttula, , 2007)), and formalized by measures such as the cognitive salience index (Sutrop, 2001), category strength (including an explicitly potentially basic tier) (Fider et al., 2017; Fider & Komarova, 2018), and the well-formedness of a category (Douven & Gärdenfors, 2020). Rather than a sharp partition between 11 English basic color categories and everything else, basicness may be a matter of degree. Categories differ in a second respect as well -in how finely they are lexically differentiated by their members, and BLUE is among the most finely differentiated in English: terms at its boundary with GREEN, such as teal and turquoise, are named with lower crossspeaker consensus than green or blue, yet cannot simply be replaced by them (Witzel, 2019).

In this context, the dense qualification of blue and brown is best read as rich lexical elaboration within stable single categories, not as a sign that either is acquiring a new basic term. Lexical richness alone does not imply movement toward an additional basic color category: a candidate term can elaborate extensively and still never become basic, as with Japanese kon 'dark blue', which Stanlaw (2010, p. 213) posits as "at least an incipient, if not full-fledged, twelfth color term" on historical and elicitation evidence, but which contemporary naming data do not bear out (Kuriki et al., 2017). The two findings may not be in conflict. Kuriki et al. (2017) acknowledge that the best examples of kon may fall outside their own test palette, which leaves them unable to say whether its status has changed, and they note that Stanlaw reports kon applying to blue samples that are very dark and somewhat desaturated. Each study may therefore be limited by the reach of its stimuli: Stanlaw's darker set recovers kon as basic but does not treat the lightblue term mizu as basic at all, whereas the palette of Kuriki et al. recovers mizu, a distinct lightblue term that all but one of their 57 subjects used, and reaches kon for only 37% of them. Were the full lightness and saturation range of blue sampled, Japanese might prove to be clothed in "triple blues" as well, with mizu, ao and kon dividing the range (cf. Bimler & Uusküla, 2014; Del Viva et al., 2022).

Cluster analyses of English naming recover several lexically important subregions without carving a new one inside BLUE apart from teal (Lindsey & Brown, 2014). On corpus evidence from British English and Finnish, Kerttula (2002 Kerttula ( , 2007) ) identified the BLUE and PURPLE areas as those most prone to such elaboration, and English shows it in hard-to-name warm regions as well, in beige, salmon, tan and peach (Mylonas & MacDonald, 2016). Our data fit this picture: blue and brown sit at the differentiated end of a continuum of basicness, elaborated by qualifiers rather than by new basic terms. Whether that elaboration ever hardens into a lexical split is not a question synchronic corpus frequencies can answer, and we do not claim that it does. The more secure and, we think, more interesting contribution is what the elaboration shows about basicness itself: that it is graded rather than all-or-none, as Kerttula (2002) proposed and Biggam (personal communication, September 2026) likewise maintains.

Throughout, these patterns are facts about linguistic usage and production. Usage need not mirror perceptual category structure or mental representation one for one; whether the boundary structure implied by these qualifier patterns is reflected in perception is a separate, empirical question, better settled psychophysically than by corpus frequencies. A constructivist account that treats chromatic stimuli as multidimensional physiological and psychological events (Peterzell, 2026a) further motivates reading a densely qualified region as elaborated along several dimensions at once rather than as one splitting into a new basic category.

Several literatures bear on how such elaboration relates to lexical change. The lexical-splitting route has a worked semasiological mechanism, in which a new term enters in a restricted set of artefact-anchored collocations and gradually widens its referential range (MacLaury, 1997; Rakhilina & Paramei, 2011; Vejdemo, 2018), the very kind of distributional signature that corpus collocations such as ours can register. The enrichment of both the "blue" and the "brown" vocabularies has been linked to the dyeing trade, with new terms emerging first on dyed goods and artefacts before spreading to natural objects (for 'blue' in Italian, see the Addendum in Paramei et al., 2026; for 'brown' in Russian, Italian and French, Bochkarev et al., 2023; D'Achille & Grossmann, 2017; Gréa et al., 2026). Brown is a strongly object-anchored color, a focus of object-and food-derived naming via the metonymy "entity stands for entity's color" (Casson, 1994), and food-related terms are both frequent and prominent in English naming data (Lin et al., 2001; MacDonald & Mylonas, 2010). Material culture may work in the same direction: technological developments in the dyeing industry multiplied the brown shades realized in everyday artefacts, clothing, leather accessories, and upholstery, so that, alongside food, such artefacts could drive the variety and number of brown shades that speakers distinguish and name. We offer this as a suggestive synthesis rather than a causal claim: the qualifier-versussplit mapping is not tidy (French has two "browns" yet is not a classic "two blues" language), and matched cross-linguistic counts are not in hand. One testable prediction does follow, however, and is well suited to diachronic corpora: if brown's lightness-and-purity contrast is the older and more entrenched of the two in everyday use, it should surface earlier and more robustly in historical text than the light-dark refinement of blue.

We keep one distinction explicit throughout. Information-theoretic models describe how an existing lexicon partitions color space efficiently, with elaboration concentrating where communication is least efficient or most needed (Regier et al., 2016; Zaslavsky et al., 2018 Zaslavsky et al., , 2019)); they do not explain why some languages come to have more basic terms than others. Our corpus patterns speak to the first question, the internal differentiation of usage within a lexicon, and not directly to the second. Languages differ in a baseline naming tendency, some leaning on monolexemic terms and others on modified expressions (Alvarado & Jameson, 2002). Which route a language takes may further track language family, environmental salience (Josserand et al., 2021; Twomey et al., 2021), and material culture. Speakers also differ in the perceptual prior these models take to be shared: hue-scaling data show it is neither uniform nor identical across observers (Peterzell, 2026b), and naming data point the same way: Lindsey and Brown (2014) identified two color-naming motifs among American English speakers in the BLUE-GREEN region, one including teal and one without it, which limits how far population-level usage statistics can be read as a single perceptual structure.

4.4. Issues in using corpus linguistics to study perception.

Several caveats temper interpretation.

(a) Corpus frequency reflects communicative need and production, not perceptual categorization per se; convergence with psychophysical evidence should not be assumed.

(b) "Light" and "dark" are primarily lightness modifiers and may index brightness rather than hue subcategories (cf. Bimler, 2025). Corpus evidence from other languages shows that lightness words can foreground lightness alone or, in context, function as full color labels, so their reading is not fixed (Hatakka, 2026), relevant to the "two blues" interpretation, since the goluboj/sinij contrast is itself partly one of lightness (Paramei & Bimler, 2024), and the limits of language effects on blue discrimination remain debated (Martinovic et al., 2020). Indeed, recent EEG evidence indicates that early neural differences once attributed to categorical color perception are driven largely by luminance and chromatic contrast rather than by linguistic categories (Martinovic et al., 2026).

(c) The corrections, while necessary, depend on judgments about chromatic versus nonchromatic use that should be specified and, ideally, validated.

(d) Findings are specific to American English and to COCA's genre mix and time window;

British and other varieties of English need not pattern identically, and indeed already diverge for neighboring categories such as turquoise and lilac (Mylonas & MacDonald, 2016; Mylonas et al., 2017). The genre mix also governs which qualifiers are visible at all. Object-derived brown terms belonging to colloquial speech surface in these data only vestigially: mud brown occurs eight times and shit brown three, frequencies far below any threshold we apply. A corpus of edited and broadcast language will under-represent the registers in which such terms are commonest, so the specifier inventories reported here are conservative for every term, and plausibly more so for brown than for blue.

(e) The drivers of lexical refinement of the BLUE category are multiple and partly contested: environmental salience (sky and sea) and material culture (e.g., dyeing technology) have both been proposed (Josserand et al., 2021; Paramei et al., 2026; Twomey et al., 2021), yet rich, efficient color lexicons can arise without color technology, implicating perceptual prominence and communicative need as sufficient forces (Wnuk et al., 2022) (f) Dense qualification need not indicate lexical restructuring, and the alternatives deserve weighing rather than dismissal. Stylistic convention is one: color qualifiers cluster in descriptive prose, so a term favored by that style will attract them for reasons of genre rather than of category structure. Table 2 bears on this, since chromatic use ranges from about 87% in fiction to between 32% and 36% in news, spoken, web and blog material, and the ordering of terms is nonetheless recovered within the matched subsets of Section 3.3 and again on the eight-register current release of Section 3.7, which weights those styles differently. Lexical productivity is a second: blue is the most derivationally productive English color term (Kerttula, 2002), and a term that readily forms compounds will accumulate collocates for that reason alone; we therefore keep productivity and differentiation apart, and note that the unique-specifier measure counts distinct modifying words rather than derivatives of the color term itself. Domain-specific terminology is a third, and here the point is partly conceded: blue's specifier inventory does include pigment and craft vocabulary (cobalt, ultramarine, Prussian, underglaze), so technical registers contribute to its lead. We do not treat that as a confound to be removed, since material culture is among the proposed drivers of lexical refinement (paragraph e above), but it does mean the lead should not be read as a perceptual signal alone. Cultural preference is a fourth, and we cannot exclude it: the prominence of blue in commercial and design registers is part of what a usage measure is meant to capture, and we treat it as a contributor rather than an artifact. Genre effects are the fifth, addressed by Table 2 and by the register-balanced replication. None of these is eliminated by our data. We accordingly do not claim that the qualifier patterns demonstrate lexical restructuring; they are consistent with gradient differentiation within stable categories, which is the weaker claim this paper makes.

4.5. The vision-language interface.

These results bear on the interface named in this special issue, and it is worth being exact about how. What a corpus records is production: which words speakers chose, how often, and in what company. That is not a measure of perceptual category structure, and Section 4.4 sets out why the two should not be assumed to coincide. What it does supply is a naturalistic record of how a continuous visual domain is carved up in use, gathered without task instructions and from far more speakers than a laboratory study can reach. On that reading the qualifier patterns complement rather than substitute for behavioral, psychophysical and neuroimaging measures, and they sit beside information-theoretic accounts in which color lexicons are shaped jointly by perceptual structure and communicative need (Conway et al., 2020; Gibson et al., 2017; Zaslavsky et al., 2019).

The limit on that reading is itself an active question in vision science: color categorization can dissociate from naming both behaviorally and neurally, and the two appear to draw on distinct functional brain networks (Lindsey & Brown, 2021; Siuda-Krzywicka et al., 2019 , 2021; Witzel & Gegenfurtner, 2018). What we report therefore describes the naming side, and whether the boundary structure it implies is mirrored in perception is a psychophysical question we leave open. Two uses follow. Text-only language models recover perceptual color structure to a degree governed by corpus frequency and adjectival-modifier usage (Abdou et al., 2021; Mohammad, 2011), so qualifier patterns of the kind reported here are the distributional signal from which such models would inherit color-category structure. And the approach yields a cross-linguistic prediction: where a language lacks a basic term for a region of color space, speakers should rely more on modified expressions built from neighboring basic terms, for instance, a language without a basic 'brown' term might lean on 'dark yellow', 'dark red' or 'smoked-colored' (Viti, 2025) where English simply says brown. Whether such compensatory modification in fact arises, or whether an unlexicalized region is instead simply named less often, as communicative-need accounts would allow (Gibson et al., 2017; Zaslavsky et al., 2019), is a question well suited to matched cross-linguistic corpora. Because COCA continues to grow, the same measures can be tracked across successive releases; Section 3.7 takes the first step.

5. Conclusions

In a large, genre-balanced corpus of American English, blue attracts a wider variety of unique chromatic specifiers than any other basic color term, and is among the terms most heavily modified by light and dark. Although frequencies do not establish light blue and dark blue as basic categories proper, the overall pattern indicates that American English speakers differentiate subcategories within BLUE in everyday usage, and that the basic/non-basic color-category distinction may be gradual rather than strictly discrete. Brown emerges as a close parallel, the most densely qualified term of all by common qualifiers, but not qualified in the same way as blue, drawing chiefly on common, largely achromatic modifiers rather than unique chromatic specifiers, consistent with its being a constitutively dark, lightness-defined color whose subcategories are largely lightness and saturation variants. Corpus linguistics thus offers a complementary, naturalistic window onto how a perceptual continuum is lexically carved, a question at the heart of the vision-language interface.
