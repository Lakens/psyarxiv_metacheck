Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity (VILPA) refers to brief and sporadic (e.g., up to 2 min) bouts of vigorous physical activity performed as part of daily living (e.g., stair climbing, very fast walking). VILPA offers several accessibility advantages over structured exercise and confers substantial health benefits. However, there is a paucity of scalable interventions designed to enhance the VILPA experience. The primary aim of the study was to examine the effects of a novel head-mounted augmented reality application on affective and perceptual responses during and following a simulated VILPA bout.

Methods: A counterbalanced within-subjects design was employed and 24 physically inactive adults (Nfemale = 14, Mage = 26.12 years, SDage = 3.56 years) completed 2-min bouts of vigorousintensity incline walking under three experimental augmented reality conditions (i.e., motivational prompts, virtual pet, and gamification) and a no-technology control. Dependent variables included affective valence, arousal, perceived exertion, state attention, enjoyment, remembered pleasure, and heart rate responses. Data were analysed using linear mixed models.

: The virtual pet and gamification experimental conditions elicited more positive affective valence (95% CI [0.41, 1.59], d = 1.13; 95% CI [0.33, 1.50], d = 1.03), greater attentional dissociation (95% CI [8.43, 21.99], d = 1.48; 95% CI [15.09, 28.66], d = 2.13), and higher ratings of enjoyment (95% CI [3.29, 8.62], d = 1.48; 95% CI [3.59, 8.91], d = 1.55) and remembered pleasure (95% CI [10.72, 39.12], d = 1.16; 95% CI [7.38, 35.78], d = 1.01), when compared to control. Motivational prompts did not enhance the VILPA experience relative to control. Conclusion: The findings provide initial evidence that augmented reality technology can enhance affective and perceptual responses to a simulated VILPA bout.

1 Introduction

Physical activity reduces the risk of non-communicable diseases and mental health conditions such as stroke, hypertension, several types of cancers, depression, and all-cause mortality. 1 Despite such well-documented benefits, an estimated 31% of the global adult population (1.8 billion) fail to meet the weekly physical activity levels recommended by the World Health Organization. 2 Hence, there is a pressing need for innovative approaches to promote sustained engagement in physical activity.

One promising avenue concerns rethinking how physical activity is accumulated.

International guidelines previously recommended that adults should accumulate physical activity in bouts lasting ≥ 10 min. 3 However, physical activity of any bout duration is associated with improved health outcomes. 4 Therefore, current physical activity recommendations do not include a minimum bout length, supporting the principle that every move counts. 5 Such amendments have prompted opportunities for health-enhancing time-efficient alternatives to traditional forms of continuous physical activity.

Vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity (VILPA) refers to brief and sporadic (e.g., up to 2 min) bouts of vigorous physical activity performed as part of daily living (e.g., stair climbing, very fast walking). 6 VILPA can be considered an incidental form of physical activity that relies on opportunities presented by daily life. 7 There is emerging evidence from wearable-derived data that VILPA confers substantial health benefits. For example, engaging in three daily bouts of < 2 min VILPA has been associated with a 38-40% reduction in allcause and cancer mortality risk and a 48-49% reduction in cardiovascular disease mortality risk among a sample of 25,241 UK Biobank adults. 8 Incidental physical activity has several accessibility advantages over traditional forms of structured exercise (e.g., circumventing the frequently cited lack of time barrier). 9 Nonetheless, researchers have highlighted the need to develop scalable and acceptable interventions to encourage these behaviours. 10, 11 Unfortunately, interventions designed to promote physical activity have not improved over the past 30 years. 12 One plausible explanation for these findings is that interventions are typically predicated on reflective ("Type 2") processes. Here, it is assumed that providing people with compelling information about how physical activity will benefit their health is sufficient for them to initiate and maintain a physically active lifestyle. 13 Findings from metaanalyses indicate that knowledge-based interventions have negligible effects on health behaviours, 14 and there is growing consensus that physical activity researchers should develop interventions that also target automatic ("Type 1") processes. 15 Affective responses is an umbrella term for a variety of feeling states, typically categorised as core affect (comprising affective valence and arousal), emotion, and mood. 16 Affective responses feature prominently in dual-process models that seek to explain and predict physical activity behaviour. For example, the Physical Activity Adoption and Maintenance Model 2.0 (PAAM) 17 holds that physical activity behaviour is governed by the interaction between explicit and implicit processes. Explicit processes (e.g., intention, executive functions) are theorised to help initiate engagement in physical activity by supporting deliberate and goaldirected actions. Contrastingly, experienced affect (i.e., affective responses during or immediately after physical activity) is an important implicit process supporting habit development and reengagement in physical activity. 17 Virtual realityfoot_0 (VR) technology is often used to enhance physical activity-related experienced affect. 19 For example, a meta-analysis of crossover trials (k = 11) indicated that VR-assisted exercise was associated with large (SMD = 1.45) and moderate (SMD = 0.62) effect sizes on enjoyment and affective valence, respectively, when compared to traditional exercise. 20 A key perceptual mechanism underlying these findings is attentional dissociation, whereby attention is oriented outwardly, away from unpleasant exercise-related interoceptive cues (e.g., elevated respiration rate). 21 However, individual differences in exercise tolerance (i.e., the ability to continue exercising at an imposed intensity even when the activity becomes uncomfortable or unpleasant) can account for a significant portion of variance in affective valence. 22 VR has shown initial promise in enhancing exercise-related experienced affect. 20 However, the technology is unlikely to be employed in real-world settings to accompany incidental physical activity, given that it is not designed to be worn for extended periods while performing activities of daily living. 23 Contrastingly, head-mounted augmented realityfoot_1 (AR) technology can be worn for prolonged periods and holds significant promise for enhancing the VILPA experience in real-time, albeit there is a need to test this possibility empirically. 23 There is also a need to determine the extent to which individuals are willing to use AR applications designed to encourage VILPA. Therefore, we developed a novel AR application designed to accompany VILPA, comprising three core components: motivational prompts, virtual pet, and gamification (see Supplementary Material 1). Each component was designed to provide progressively greater opportunities for attentional dissociation through the use of digital assets.

The primary aim of the study was to examine the effects of the three AR application components on affective and perceptual responses to a simulated VILPA bout. A secondary aim was to explore the feasibility of all-day wearable AR smartglasses to encourage VILPA.

With reference to affective and perceptual responses during VILPA, we hypothesised that the gamification condition would facilitate the most positive affective valence, followed by the virtual pet, motivational prompts, and control conditions, respectively. We predicted that all experimental AR conditions would prompt higher arousal when compared to control. Furthermore, we anticipated that the gamification condition would elicit the greatest attentional dissociation, followed by the virtual pet, motivational prompts, and control conditions, respectively. Regarding affective responses assessed post-VILPA, we hypothesised that the gamification condition would facilitate the greatest enjoyment and remembered pleasure, followed by the virtual pet, motivational prompts, and control conditions, respectively. Finally, to verify comparable VILPA bout intensities across conditions, we predicted that perceived exertion and heart rate responses would be statistically equivalent.

2 Methods

The study was pre-registered (https://osf.io/4jhe6/overview?view_only=95be6272a3934a6f9e4e8e6d3787bd61) and approved by the University College London Research Ethics Committee (0047). Each participant provided informed consent and was compensated with a £20 gift voucher.

2.1 Power and Eligibility

A simulation-based power analysis was conducted in RStudio (v. 2025.05.1+513) in accordance with recommendations for linear mixed models. 25 The extant literature helped inform exercise tolerance scores, as well as estimates for fixed effects, random variance, and residual standard deviation. 26, 27 The simr package 28 was then employed to create an artificial linear mixed model using the formula: valence ~ condition + tolerance_centered + (1 | participant). Thereafter, the mixedpower package was used to explore power across a range of sample sizes. The analysis, predicated on 5000 simulations, indicated that a minimum of 21 participants would be required to obtain .90 power. The associated Quarto markdown file can be located online (https://osf.io/un86y/overview?view_only=6fb20219e7014f0183f19e8b9f037165).

Recruitment was facilitated through word-of-mouth, promotional posters at the university, and social media posts. Eligibility criteria stipulated that participants were: (a) between 18-45 years old, (b) safe to engage in physical activity according to the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire for Everyone, 29 (c) without any visual impairment and/or hearing deficiency, and (d) familiar with treadmill walking. Individuals prone to motion sickness according to the Visually Induced Motion Sickness Susceptibility Questionnaire 30 were excluded from the study, as were individuals classified as "active" (i.e., scores of ≥ 24) on the Godin-Shephard Leisure-Time Physical Activity Questionnaire. 31

2.2 Application Development

We chose incline walking as an exercise modality, as this is a frequently cited form of VILPA that requires little familiarisation. 32 Moreover, vigorous-intensity incline walking can be readily tested in a laboratory environment with precise manipulation of speed and incline.

The AR application development was informed by the compendium of self-enactable techniques to change and self-manage motivation and behaviour (see Supplementary Material 1). 33 A central aim of the application was to enhance experienced affect during vigorousintensity incline walking through AR technology, given the theorised role of experienced affect in shaping anticipated affect and supporting habit development. 17 The AR application was developed by the first author in Unity 6 (v. 6000.0.40f1) and comprised three core components: motivational prompts, virtual pet, and gamification (see Figure 1). The digital assets incorporated within each application component were designed to provide progressively greater opportunities for attentional dissociation. The motivational prompts condition entailed text-based encouragements (e.g., "Let's go!"). The virtual pet condition involved walking alongside a digital dog. Finally, the gamification condition entailed a coin-collecting task. The Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) Checklist, 34 with considerations for health-related extended reality applications, 35 can be located in Supplementary Material 2.

2.3 Experimental Procedures

Participants attended a laboratory testing session lasting ~75 min. Upon arrival, each participant was instructed to attach a heart rate monitor (Polar H9; Kempele, FI), with data being transmitted and recorded on a smartphone (HRV Logger; Amsterdam, NL). Each participant completed a short demographic questionnaire, as well as the Preference for and Tolerability of the Intensity of Exercise Questionnaire. 22 The experimenters (HK and JZ) then measured each participant's height, mass, and resting heart rate. Participants were introduced to each of the measures used during the experimental trials. Furthermore, the experimenter instructed participants how to adjust and fit a VR head-mounted display with AR video seethrough capabilities (Meta Quest 3; Menlo Park, CA), before loading a brief familiarisation scene (see Supplementary Material 2). Participants were then required to complete a modified Balke submaximal treadmill walking test, 36 to ascertain an appropriate incline to meet vigorous threshold during experimental trials (see Supplementary 3).

Participants completed vigorous-intensity treadmill walking under three experimental AR conditions (motivational prompts, virtual pet, and gamification) and a no-technology control, in a fully counterbalanced within-subjects design. Each treadmill walking trial lasted 4 min, consisting of a 90-s warm-up, 2-min VILPA bout, and a 30-s cool-down. The duration of the warm-up was chosen to sufficiently exceed the average time to reach vigorous intensity during incline walking. 32 The duration of the main bout was consistent with definitions of VILPA. 8 The treadmill speed was set to the same value as the modified Balke submaximal treadmill walking test (i.e., 3.5 mph [females], 3.8 mph [males]). The incline was set to the final value recorded during the submaximal test for the warm-up and VILPA bout, and 50% of this value for the cool-down. After completing each trial, the participant commenced a 5-min period of seated rest (see Figure 2).

2.4 Measures

Core affect was measured throughout each trial at baseline, after 2.5 mins, and following the cool-down to capture the dynamic nature of exercise-related affect. 37 However, data obtained at baseline and following the cool-down were not analysed, as these timepoints did not coincide with the experimental AR manipulation. Core affect was conceptualised as a dimensional domain, 38 comprising affective valence as a hedonic quality and arousal as an indication of activation. Affective valence was measured using the Feeling Scale, 39 which is anchored by -5 ([I feel] very bad) and 5 (very good). Arousal was assessed using the Felt Arousal Scale, 40 which is anchored by 1 (low arousal) and 6 (high arousal). Perceptual measures were administered at 2.5 min. Perceived exertion was assessed using the Borg CR10 Scale, 41 which is anchored by 0 (nothing at all) and 10 (extremely strong). State attention was measured using the Attentional Scale, 42 which is anchored by 0 (internal focus [bodily sensations, heart rate, breathing etc.]) and 100 (external focus [daydreaming, external environment, etc.]).

Affective responses were also measured following the completion of each VILPA bout.

Enjoyment was measured using the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale-8, 43 which consists of 8 items attached to 7-point scales (e.g.,1 = I find it pleasurable,7 = I find it unpleasurable [item 1]). Remembered pleasure was assessed using a visual analogue scale attached to the question "How did the physical activity make you feel?" The scale ranges from -100 (very unpleasant) to 100 (very pleasant) in intervals of 1, with scores hidden from participants. 44 Application tolerability was measured using the Simulation Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) following the cessation of each experimental condition. 45 This inventory asks participants to indicate the severity of 16 symptoms (e.g., nausea, blurred vision) on a 4-point scale anchored by 0 (none) and 3 (severe). Upon completion of all four conditions, participants completed a survey into the feasibility (e.g., perceived facilitators/barriers, adoption intentions) of all-day wearable AR smartglasses to encourage VILPA (see Supplementary Material 4 for additional details).

2.5 Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted in Positron (v. 2026.09.1). The data and associated Quarto markdown files can be located online (https://osf.io/un86y/overview?view_only=6fb20219e7014f0183f19e8b9f037165). Robust z scores (± 3.29), predicated on medians and median absolute deviations, were calculated using the performance package 46 to identify the presence of univariate outliers among self-report items. This process indicated the presence of 17 univariate outliers, which were treated by adjusting the raw score by one unit smaller or larger than the next extreme score in the distribution until z scores < ± 3.29. 47 A linear mixed model was fitted to each affective and perceptual variable using the lme4 and lmerTest packages. 48, 49 The models included condition as a fixed effect, centred exercise tolerance scores as a covariate, and participant as a random intercept. Model assumptions (e.g., normality of residuals, linearity) were checked using diagnostic plots. 46 For each model, we report marginal R² for fixed effects and conditional R² for the full model, including random effects. Given our ordered predictions, the overall effect of condition was first assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) on each linear mixed model. Consecutive planned contrasts (i.e., control vs. motivational prompts, motivational prompts vs. virtual pet, virtual pet vs. gamification) were then used to test the pre-registered hypotheses. Two additional exploratory (i.e., non-registered) contrasts assessed differences between control vs. virtual pet, and control vs. gamification conditions. All contrasts were examined using estimated marginal means from the emmeans package 50 and subjected to p value adjustment using the Holm-Bonferroni correction. Thereafter, statistical significance was accepted at p < .05.

Linear mixed models were fitted to intensity verification variables (i.e., perceived exertion and heart rate responses) with condition as a fixed effect, centred exercise tolerance scores as a covariate, and participant as a random intercept. To confirm that similar responses were observed across conditions, two one-sided tests (TOSTs) were then conducted on modelestimated planned contrasts. In this procedure, the results of both t tests are required to reach significance in order for equivalence to be claimed. 51 Equivalence bounds were set at ±1 unit for ratings of perceived exertion and ±5 bpm for mean heart rate, reflecting practically meaningful differences based on the Borg CR10 Scale 41 and heart rate monitoring procedures.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for intervention tolerability scores, as well as closed questions from the feasibility survey. Responses to open-ended questions were analysed using inductive content analysis 52 (see Supplementary Material 4).

Additionally, the virtual pet (Mdiff = 5.96, 95% CI [3.29, 8.62], p < .001, d = 1.48) and gamification (Mdiff = 6.25, 95% CI [3.59, 8.91], p < .001, d = 1.55) conditions prompted significantly higher enjoyment when compared to control (see Figure 4a).

For remembered pleasure, the model yielded a marginal R² of .13 and a conditional R² of .53. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of condition, F(3, 69) = 8.33, p < .001.

The virtual pet vs. motivational prompts planned contrast reached statistical significance (Mdiff = 21.75, 95% CI [6.54, 36.96], p = .002, d = 1.01). Furthermore, the virtual pet (Mdiff = 24.92, 95% CI [10.72, 39.12], p < .001, d = 1.16) and gamification (Mdiff = 21.58, 95% CI [7.38, 35.78], p < .001, d = 1.01) conditions prompted significantly greater remembered pleasure when compared to control (see Figure 4b).

3.4 Physical Activity-Intensity Verification

With reference to perceived exertion, the model yielded a marginal R² of .04 and a conditional R² of .59. The TOST procedure indicated statistical equivalence between control vs. motivational prompts (Mdiff = 0.29, t(69) = -3.12, p = .001), motivational prompts vs. virtual pet (Mdiff = 0.19, t(69) = -3.58, p < .001), and virtual pet vs. gamification contrasts (Mdiff = -0.58, t(69) = -1.84, p = .035; see Supplementary Material 7). For mean heart rate responses, the model yielded a marginal R² of .03 and a conditional R² of .76. The TOST procedure indicated statistical equivalence between control vs. motivational prompts (Mdiff = 0.70, t(69) = -3.77, p < .001), motivational prompts vs. virtual pet (Mdiff = 1.30, t(69) = -3.24, p < .001), and virtual pet vs. gamification contrasts (Mdiff = -2.75, t(69) = -1.98, p = .026; see Supplementary Material 7).

3.5 Application Tolerability

Descriptives statistics indicated acceptable application tolerability across motivational prompts (M = 17.61, SD = 12.84), virtual pet (M = 18.08, SD = 15.47), and gamification conditions (M = 18.08, SD = 13.94).

3.6 Feasibility

Findings indicated that participants were reasonably familiar with the concept of allday wearable AR smartglasses (see Figure 5). Nonetheless, participants revealed somewhat mixed experiences of using such devices, citing positive (e.g., fun, interesting) and negative (e.g. design, poor battery) aspects of the user experience in equal measure (see Table 1).

With reference to perceived facilitators, the majority of participants (n = 19) agreed or strongly agreed that all-day wearable AR smartglasses could prompt them to engage in VILPA (see Figure 5). Participants felt strongly that all-day wearable AR smartglasses could provide useful VILPA-related information (see Figure 5). Open-ended questions revealed that such information spanned real-time data (e.g., heart rate), historical data (e.g., step count), as well as a range of health-supporting information (e.g., hydration, nutrition, sleep; see Table 1).

Closed questions based on the content of the experimental conditions revealed that gamified elements (M = 4.17) and virtual pets (M = 3.96) were more likely to be effective in promoting VILPA when compared to interventions predicated solely on motivational prompts (M = 2.75; see Figure 5).

With reference to perceived barriers, approximately half of the sample agreed or strongly agreed to having privacy concerns about all-day wearable AR glasses (see Figure 5).

Responses to open-ended questions revealed a range of reservations, spanning data processing, location data, video data, and sharing personal data (see Table 1). The appearance of all-day wearable AR glasses was also reported as being a relevant barrier to adoption (see Figure 5), as well as factors pertaining to user safety, maintenance and hygiene, and social acceptance (see Table 1). Over two thirds of participants (n = 17) agreed or strongly agreed that they would adopt an all-day wearable AR smartglasses application to encourage participation in VILPA, with open-ended responses indicating frequent use on a daily basis (see Table 1).

4 Discussion

The primary aim of the study was to examine the effects of a novel AR application on affective and perceptual responses to a simulated bout of VILPA. A secondary aim was to explore the feasibility of all-day wearable AR smartglasses to encourage VILPA. We investigated this among a sample of physically inactive adults, as they are most likely to derive the greatest physical activity health benefits.

4.1 Affective and Perceptual Responses During VILPA

There is a paucity of research examining affective responses to lifestyle physical activity. 53 The present investigation is the first to demonstrate that AR technology can enhance experienced affect during VILPA, with greater affective valences scores reported in the virtual pet and gamification conditions (see Figure 3a). These finding are important as experienced affect has been shown to influence subsequent physical activity behaviour. 54 The findings support a developing narrative that VR can facilitate positive experienced affect during structured exercise. 20, 21 We further this by applying AR technology to VILPA, in response to recent research agendas. 23 The virtual pet and gamification conditions elicited comparable affective valence scores. While each AR application component was underpinned by behaviour change techniques (see Supplementary Material 1), it is noteworthy that the virtual pet condition allowed participants to choose their preferred dog breed to accompany them during the VILPA bout. Greater autonomy over aspects of the exercise experience (e.g., intensity) has been associated with more positive experienced affect. 13 Hence, this additional sense of choice might have contributed towards the positive affective valence scores in the virtual pet condition.

The AR application did not appear to influence arousal scores. These findings oppose related research demonstrating that VR technology can elevate arousal during cycle ergometry when compared to control. 55 This might be because AR technology does not fully occlude the user's physical reality like VR. 56 Consequently, the AR technology may have elicited a weaker sense of presence (i.e., the extent to which an individual feels present in the virtual environment), 57 thereby limiting its capacity to enhance arousal. Alternatively, changes in arousal might be more likely to emerge during prolonged exercise than during the brief bouts of activity typified by VILPA.

Measures of arousal are frequently employed alongside measures of affective valence, as this ensures theoretical coherence when core affect is conceptualised as a dimensional domain. 38 The combination of positive affective valence and high arousal has been shown to be a strong indicator of exercise enjoyment. 58 Therefore, it is important to consider how the AR application might be further developed to enhance arousal during VILPA. The AR application relied exclusively on visual content. Incorporating an auditory stimulus, such as music, may bolster its effectiveness, given the well-established capacity of music to regulate arousal during exercise. 59 Comparable state attention scores were observed in the control and motivational prompts conditions (see Figure 3b), both of which were below the midpoint of the scale, suggesting that participants were associating rather than dissociating in these conditions. The presentation of the motivational prompts (i.e., small text-based cues) may have limited their capacity to capture participants' attention. Attentional dissociation is a key mechanism underlying the effectiveness of audio-visual exercise interventions, as directing attention away from internal fatigue-related cues can attenuate their negative influence on experienced affect. 60 The limited capacity of the motivational prompts to enhance attentional dissociation may also help explain why this condition did not elicit more positive affective valence when compared to control.

There was a clear step change in state attention scores between the control/motivational prompts and virtual pet/gamification conditions. A plausible explanation is that the virtual pet and gamification conditions provided a continuous stream of digital AR content throughout the VILPA bout, whereas the motivational prompts condition presented participants with textbased prompts intermittently. Hence, the virtual pet and gamification conditions likely consumed greater attentional resources than the motivational prompts condition, in accord with parallel processing models of attention. 61 The state attention scores observed in the virtual pet and gamification conditions broadly align with those reported in previous studies of VRassisted exercise, suggesting that sufficiently engaging AR content can elicit attentional responses comparable to those observed in fully immersive virtual environments. 21, 62

4.2 Affective Responses Post-VILPA

The virtual pet and gamification conditions significantly enhanced enjoyment and remembered pleasure when compared to control (see Figure 4). These findings build on previous research demonstrating that VR-assisted exercise can increase enjoyment and remembered pleasure relative to traditional exercise. 20, 21, 63 This is promising when viewed through the lens of the PAAM 2.0 model, 17 as positive experienced affect is proposed to shape more favourable appraisals of anticipated affect, as well as facilitating habit development. The present findings provide preliminary support for the potential of AR technology to encourage sustained engagement in VILPA, albeit with the caveat that this requires further testing over a prolonged period and in more ecologically valid settings. 23

4.3 Physical Activity-Intensity Verification

The TOST procedure indicated equivalence among perceived exertion and mean heart rate responses. The finding that state attention differed across conditions but perceived exertion remained equivalent, is consistent with previous VR-related research. 21, 55 It is possible that state attention and perceived exertion are underpinned by distinct neurocognitive processes when assessed using self-report measures. Hence, there is opportunity to examine these mechanisms using neurophysiological techniques. 19 The comparable heart rate responses also confirmed that participants were working under equivalent physiological loads across conditions, consistent with the intended VILPA protocol.

4.4 Application Tolerability

Application tolerability scores were comparable across experimental AR conditions and well below the threshold proposed to represent a "bad simulator" (i.e., SSQ scores ≥ 40). 64 From an applied perspective, AR technology might be particularly appropriate when promoting time-efficient forms of physical activity, such as VILPA, given that simulation sickness appears to worsen with longer exposure duration. 65

4.5 Feasibility

Encouragingly, most participants (n = 19) indicated that AR could prompt them to engage in VILPA (see Figure 5), demonstrating some initial support for this form of technology. These findings are timely given recent policy initiatives identifying wearable technologies as a cornerstone of future preventative healthcare. 66 Contemporary wearables typically monitor physical activity. However, AR technology has the capacity to actively shape the VILPA experience in real-time. The feasibility results also support the view that AR glasses are a suitable form of technology to encourage VILPA when compared to smartphones and smartwatches (see Figure 5). Successful implementation is likely to depend on technology acceptance, with participants identifying several perceived facilitators and barriers to the adoption of all-day wearable AR smartglasses (see Table 1). For example, participants cited fun, motivation, social support, and creating more VILPA-related opportunities as facilitators. Participants also recognised the large potential for AR technology to gamify the VILPA experience, aligning with previous research that has identified gamification as an important enabler of VILPA. 67 The cost of all-day wearable AR glasses were reported as being a salient barrier to adoption (see Figure 5). Hence, access to this form of technology may initially be too costly for lowerincome users. This underscores the need for affordable hardware to avoid exacerbating existing disparities in physical activity across socioeconomic positions. 68

4.6 Strengths and Limitations

We developed a novel AR application, grounded in theory, and designed to enhance the experience of a simulated VILPA bout. The application was documented using the TIDieR Checklist, 34, 35 enhancing reproducibility, an approach that remains uncommon among incidental physical activity-related intervention research. 69 The sample comprised inactive adults, in line with recent research agendas highlighting the need to evaluate immersive technologies beyond healthy, physically active samples. 23 Exercise tolerance scores were included as a covariate in each linear mixed model given their influence on affective responses. 22 As an initial investigation, the present study examined a convenience sample of relatively young adults. Therefore, the extent to which the findings apply to middle-aged and older populations remains be to established. Extending this work to these groups represents an important next step, given the substantial health benefits associated with VILPA in later adulthood. 8 This research should also examine whether the technical barriers previously identified in relation to smartphone-based VILPA interventions among middle-aged and older adults 70 extend to AR-based approaches.

4.7 Implications for Practice and Future Research

Incidental physical activity offers several accessibility advantages over traditional forms of structured exercise. 9 Nonetheless, interventions designed to promote VILPA remain at an early stage of development and have largely focused on providing VILPA-related information, an approach that is unlikely to produce meaningful behaviour change in isolation. 14 In contrast, we sought to enhance the VILPA experience by modifying the environment through the use of head-mounted AR technology. A clear implication is that AR can be used to facilitate positive experienced affect. This was reflected across affective valence, enjoyment, and remembered pleasure responses, each of which has been implicated in physical activity adoption and maintenance. 17 The study was conducted under laboratory conditions to provide the high degree of experimental control required to administer vigorous-intensity incline walking. However, as all-day wearable AR smartglasses become increasingly accessible, future research should evaluate the effectiveness of AR interventions for supporting VILPA in real-world settings. 23 This would also enable researchers to determine whether the acute effects observed herein translate into habit development and sustained physical activity participation, in accord with relevant theory. 17 Furthermore, the AR application could be optimised by combining the most effective components (i.e., virtual pet with gamification).

4.8 Conclusion

VR technology is increasingly used to enhance the exercise experience. 23 However, many individuals cite a range of barriers that hinder their willingness to engage in leisure-time exercise. 71 VILPA overcomes several of these barriers by enabling vigorous physical activity to be integrated into everyday routines. 70 Nonetheless, there remains a dearth of interventions capable of enhancing the VILPA experience in real-time. 10 The present investigation provides the first evidence that head-mounted AR technology can enhance the VILPA experience.

Virtual pets and gamification elicited more positive affective valence, greater attentional dissociation, and higher ratings of enjoyment and remembered pleasure, when compared to control.
