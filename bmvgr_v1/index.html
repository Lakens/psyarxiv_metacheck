How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Aging is related to changes in the usage of emotion regulation strategies due to shifting motivational goals, life experience, and declines in physiological, cognitive, and social resources. Music supports the facilitation of music mood regulation strategies (i.e., Entertainment, Revival, Strong Sensation, Diversion, Discharge, Mental Work, and Solace), but age-related changes in their usage and underlying psychological functions remain underexplored across adulthood. The current cross-sectional study addresses this gap by having 250 participants (M age = 40.88 years, SD age = 17.78, range = 18 -87) complete the Brief Music in Mood Regulation Scale (Saarikallio, 2012) and experimenter-created items exploring how much the aforementioned strategies are facilitated through serving Bodily, Cognitive, or Social functions. Regression models showed that older participants use less music for mood regulation. This trend was observed in all strategies except Revival, whose use remained stable over time. Linear Mixed-Effect models showed that music resonated in participants' bodies to facilitate Entertainment and Revival, while it helped with cognitive thought and emotion processing for Mental Work. The remaining strategies were underpinned by both Bodily and Cognitive functions. No age differences were observed here. These results contribute to the understanding of music-based mood regulation and promote music as a psychoeducational, health, and wellbeing tool for all ages.

Introduction

Affective states such as emotions and moods shape cognitive and social behaviours in everyday life, contributing more broadly to one's survival and long-term health and wellbeing (Gross, 2015). Emotion-regulatory and socialization processes through which one learns to interpret and react to affective signals in context-appropriate ways are essential for nurturing individuals' adaptive and flourishing behavior (Brackett et al., 2019; Denham, 2019). Though knowledge and preferences for regulation are acquired in daily life, the process may also change across the lifespan (Riediger & Bellingtier, 2022). Using music to regulate one's emotions is often reported by individuals across ages (Saarikalliohttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UDkQ0n 2009 (Saarikallio, , 2011;; Trehub et al., 2019) , but little is known about how its use changes from early to late adulthood. Here, we specifically analyse age-related changes in music mood regulation (MMR) strategy usage and their underlying psychological functions.

General emotion regulation and aging

Emotion regulation is a goal-driven process whereby an individual modulates the experience, intensity, and duration of positive or negative affective states (Gross, 2015).

According to the process model of emotion regulation (Grosshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?qtujvX 1998) , five families of regulatory strategies (i.e., situation selection, situation modification, attention deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation) can be implemented across the timeline of emotion generation to influence the trajectory of how that emotion unfolds and, consequently, how the individual experiences it. The first four families of regulation strategies are antecedent-focused, meaning they are applied prior to fully developed emotions; whereas the last family is response-focused and applied to regulate the output of formed emotions (Gross, 2015). In the extended version of this model, tactics (i.e., how one implements the strategies) are also proposed as a component of the full emotion regulation process (Gross, 2015).

Across the lifespan, emotion regulation processes dynamically evolve, as reflected in one's overall motivation to regulate and the use and efficacy of particular strategies (Riediger & Bellingtier, 2022). Particularly in adulthood, older adults are recognised for their preference for positive stimuli (i.e., positivity effect; Reed & Carstensen, 2012) and for reporting higher levels of wellbeing compared to their younger counterparts despite declines in biological, cognitive, and social domains (Charles & Carstensen, 2010; Livingstone & Isaacowitz, 2021). Several lifespan emotional development theorieshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5npjQo including the socioemotional selectivity theory (Carstensenhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?htsTAa 1993; Carstensen et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zj3oS7 1999) , the selection, optimization, and compensation with emotion regulation theory (Urry & Gross, 2010) , and the strength and vulnerability integration model (Charles & Carstensen, 2010) , respectively ascribe changes in motivation, resource availability, and acquired emotion regulation experience to explain these age-related observations. Though empirical work underpinning these theories shows support for age-related differences in general emotion regulation, some reviews report mixed findings (Allen & Windsor, 2019; Isaacowitz, 2022). Whether age-related differences are exclusively observed in general emotion regulation or if they extend to other regulation tactics also remains unclear.

Music-based regulation

Music listening frequently serves as a tactic to maintain or alter both short-and long-term affective states, supporting the processes of emotion and mood regulation, respectively (Schäfer et al., 2013; van Goethem & Sloboda, 2011). Amongst models on music-based regulation (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0Hh7Ks Baltazar & Saarikalliohttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0Hh7Ks 2019; Eerola et al., 2024) , Saarikallio and Erkkilä (2007) present a prominent framework sharing aspects with general emotion regulation theories. From this perspective, music mood regulation (MMR) is also goal-activated and then carried out through musical engagement supporting distinct behavioral and cognitive strategies: Entertainment (i.e., creating, maintaining, or enhancing positive moods and atmospheres), Revival (i.e., gaining positive energy and relaxing), Strong Sensation (inducing and experiencing powerful, intense emotions), Diversion (removing unwanted feelings and thoughts), Discharge (releasing negative emotions), Mental Work (reappraising and understanding emotions), and Solace (promoting a sense of understanding and acceptance towards troubling emotions). Paralleling the process model, all strategies except Discharge are positively correlated with reappraisal, and are categorised as antecedent-focused (Saarikallio, 2012).

While the use of music as a tactic for mood regulation may benefit individuals of diverse age groupshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zpyoGy including infants (Dou & Cirellihttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TS30o5 2025; Trehub et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?pf91RO 2015) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Thvx1x young children (Saarikallio, 2009) , adolescents (Saarikallio & Erkkilä, 2007) , and adults (Saarikallio, 2011; Siltainsuu & Peltola, 2024) , research on age-related differences associated with the process remains limited. In developing the Music in Mood Regulation Scale (Saarikalliohttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?r3asbY 2008) and the Brief Music in Mood Regulation Scale (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?r3asbY Saarikallio, 2012) , self-report tools measuring individuals' usage of the MMR strategies, the reliance on music for mood regulation was suggested to increase during teenage years, but stabilise during early adulthood. Across later stages of adulthoodhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4CaRzU the repertoire of the MMR strategies was not found to change significantly in different age groups (Ansani et al., 2025; Saarikallio, 2011) , but changes in their usage were not examined. One study by Belfi and colleagues (2022) found that sensitivity to musical reward (i.e., the experience of pleasure and emotions from engagement in musical activities; Mas-Herrero et al., 2013) decreased in older age groups. As this construct is positively correlated with the MMR strategies (Tan & Harrisonhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SxYYdX 2025) , one might expect the same trend in MMR strategy usage, but further empirical investigation is needed to verify this.

The emotional use and relevance of music are linked to the broader psychological functionality of music (Laiho, 2004; Schäfer et al., 2013). For music listening, Schäfer et al. (2013) identify three psychological functions including: arousal and mood regulation (e.g., music for regulating physiological responses), self-awareness (e.g., music helping one think about who they are, how they feel), and social relatedness (e.g., music helping with bonding, affiliation, feeling closeness). Despite being relevant across ages (Schäfer & Riedelhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?qa6WNS 2018) , cultures (Boer & Fisher, 2012), and the subscales of musical reward (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013), the functions have not been mapped onto the seven MMR strategies, leaving limited knowledge on how MMR strategies are facilitated in terms of music's underlying psychological functions.

Given that age-related differences in emotion regulation have often been justified by changes in resources, one might suspect the same to underlie possible age-related differences in MMR. The psychological functions mentioned above capture the bodily, cognitive, and social domains of musical experience, while also reflecting the resources used for general emotional regulation strategies. For example, bodily resources may be required to change the physiological basis or expression of an experienced emotion, while cognitive resources such as attention, memory and executive functioning may be sought to modify one's understanding and management of an emotion-eliciting stimulus (Allen & Windsor, 2019). Social networks and resources may also be relied upon to obtain emotional support and advice to alter the impact of an emotion-eliciting stimulus (Allen & Windsor, 2019). Though general emotion regulation research suggests that strategy implementation is often resource-costly and we generally expect resources to decline with age (Urry & Grosshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?tV9YhQ 2010) , there may also be more detailed differences in terms of which resources preserve better than others in MMR contexts. For instance, Groake and Hogan (2016) identified that younger adults were more likely to listen to music for managing arousal and social connections, while older adults emphasised eudaimonic functions related to gaining feelings of transcendence, positivity, and personal meaning. Understanding how the psychological functions of music underpin the seven MMR strategies might help to explain whether age-related changes in strategy usage are related to the costs of resources for mood regulation in a musical context.

Aims/present study

The present study sought to investigate how aging is associated with the use of music mood regulation strategies identified by Saarikallio and Erkillä (2007). We approached this through measuring strategy usage with the B-MMR scale (Saarikallio, 2012) and modelling the trajectories of their use from young adulthood to old age. Here, we hypothesised that the frequency in the use of music for mood regulation will decrease with age. From developmental emotion perspectives, we expect this as older adults are theorised to have more motivation to engage with positive stimuli and have greater experience in regulation abilities; therefore, reducing the need to rely on music for mood regulation and only harnessing it for situations that are known to lead to optimal results. We also expect this to reflect trends found in changes in sensitivity to musical reward across adulthood.

A secondary aim for this study was to investigate the underlying functions of music in facilitating MMR strategies and how they might change with age. Utilising the psychological functions of music listening from Schäfer et al. (2013) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VkKsbC we questioned whether MMR strategies are facilitated by music serving a Bodily, Cognitive, or Social function. Given that Entertainment, Revival, Strong Sensation, and Discharge are strategies that relate to regulating physiological responses and arousal levels, and feeling or releasing emotions felt in the body, we hypothesise that music resonates in participants' bodies and serves a Bodily function in particular to regulate mood via these strategies. For the strategy of Mental Work which requires self-reflective engagement in one's feelings, we hypothesise that music specifically serves a Cognitive function by supporting the processing of thoughts and emotions. Lastly, we hypothesise that music serves both a Cognitive and Social function (i.e., music helps participants' feel connected to others) for Solace because one may rely on another to feel accepted and understood, but this might also come in the form of self-acceptance. A function-related hypothesis was not generated for Diversion, as the strategy is about directing cognitive attention away from worries but it has also been shown to activate focus on musical elements (e.g. rhythm) rather than a focus on thoughts (Baltazar & Saarikallio, 2019). Age-related changes in the functions were kept exploratory.

Methods

Participants and power analysis

A stratified sampling method was used to recruit a total of 250 participants across different age groups: 18-25 years old (n = 50), 26-39 years old (n = 100), 40-64 years old (n = 50), and 65-99 years old (n = 50). A sensitivity power analysis run on G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) showed that such a sample size allowed us to detect a 10-year age slope of β = 0.09 (i.e., a very small or negligible effect according to Cohen, 1988) with 80% power under a significance level of α = 0.05 (two-sided).

Quota sampling was used to balance participants' sex in each group. Another sensitivity power analysis showed that the study was powered enough (80% power, α = 0.05, two-sided) to detect a minimum age × gender interaction effect corresponding to a difference in slopes of approximately 0.20 standard deviation units per decade. Figure 1 depicts the distribution of our sample grouped by age and gender. All participants of this study were recruited and compensated for their participation via Prolific, an online experimental recruitment platform. Only participants who spoke English as their primary language were recruited across all available countries on Prolific, but priority was given to those who had Canadian or British nationality and resided in these countries to parallel work reported in Tan and Harrison (2025). Informed written consent was provided by all participants.

Procedure

Upon recruitment in Prolific, participants completed the online questionnaire via Qualtrics. First, participants read the study overview and provided informed consent. Then, participants were asked to share demographic information (e.g., age, gender, nationality) before moving on to complete the B-MMR and experimenter-generated items on the underlying music functions of B-MMR strategies. In this paper, we only report results related to these items; however, the full survey also included questions on the musical and technological aspects of MMR across adulthood and the role of parenthood on MMR (hence the larger sample size in the 26-39 years old group). The median completion time of the full survey was 42.87 minutes (SD = 22.20 mins). Data collection occurred between March and April 2025. All procedures of this study were completed in accordance with the University of Toronto's ethics guidelines and approved by the University of Toronto Research Ethics Board (Protocol #: 56440).

Measures

Usage of music mood regulation strategies.

To assess participants' use of different MMR strategies, the Brief Music in Mood Regulation scale (B-MMR; Saarikallio, 2012) was used. The scale comprises 21 items ranked on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from "1 -Strongly Disagree" to "5 -Strongly Agree". The average score of each subscale was calculated. Moreoverhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?EKh0xA as the B-MMR scale exhibits a second-order factor structure (Ansani et al., 2025; Saarikallio, 2008 Saarikallio, , 2012)) , an overall scale score was also computed. Cronbach's alpha for the total score was .93 and ranged from .72 to .85 for the subscales, demonstrating acceptable internal consistency.

Functions.

To better understand what psychological functions music serves to facilitate each MMR strategy, we developed a question asking "In this situation, how much does music regulate your mood through facilitating the following?" Each situation mapped onto a MMR strategy in the context of music listening, and participants responded on a Likert scale running from "1 -Not at all" to "7 -Very much" to items responding to Bodily, Cognitive, and Social functions. The items respectively were "Music resonates in my body", "Music helps me process my thoughts and feelings", and "Music makes me feel connected to others".

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses and data visualisations for this study were completed using R Studio (version 2024.04.0+735). To address the relationship between age and the usage of music mood regulation strategies, we conducted linear regressions on scaled (z-scored) B-MMR values. For each strategy and the overall B-MMR score, three model instances were used and compared (Rodgers, 2010) to check the effects of gender (contrast coded, see Mayhew & Simonoff, 2015) and possible gender × age interactions. In Model 1, age was the only predictor, while in Model 2, gender was added as a control variable. Participants who did not identify as either Man or Woman were excluded from model comparison, but were included for all other analyses. Finally, in Model 3, we allowed for the effect of age to vary between genders (i.e., interaction model). The three models were subsequently compared based on Likelihood Ratio Tests (LRTs), R-squared change, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayes Factors derived via BIC approximation (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mUJwqb Wagenmakers, 2007). The best-performing model was used to identify the effect of age on MMR strategy use. We report the regression coefficient (β) as a change in the outcome variable's standard deviation for a 10-year increase in age.

Regarding the functions of music underlying MMR strategies, a Linear Mixed-Effects model was fitted on z-scored function ratings using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). The variables of age, function, and strategy, as well as their interactions, were inserted in the fixed part of the model. To account for repeated measures, participant was included as a random intercept. Results

Model comparisons

LRTs (Appendix 1) confirmed that Model 1 was the best performing for all MMR strategies except Entertainment. This indicates that gender does not seem to influence the prediction of the use of music for mood regulation strategies when age is known. For the Entertainment strategy, the results were mixed as LRT preferred Model 2 (p = .028), whereas the BF showed just anecdotal evidence for Model 1 (BF 1-2 = 1.35). AIC comparison was inconclusive as Δ = -2.91, where a value less than -10 is considered to show no support for the more complex model (Burnham & Anderson, 2010, p. 71). Regarding the Diversion strategy, the LRT approached significance (p = .075) for Model 2, whereas the BF showed moderate evidence for Model 1 (BF 1-2 = 3.15). The difference in ΔAIC values was -1.21. For the remaining strategies, the LRT p-values were above .154 and the ΔAIC were between 0.12 and 1.97. The BFs favoring Model 1 were above 5.62, suggesting moderate evidence for the exclusion of the gender covariate.

Music mood regulation strategy usage and age differences

Within our full sample, music was frequently used for mood regulation as represented by the high overall B-MMR score (M = 3.98, SD = 0.65, on a 1-5 scale) and the scores of each strategy, listed in order of most to least used: Entertainment (M = 4.27, SD = 0.91), Strong Sensation (M = 4.17, SD = 0.72), Solace (M = 4.13, SD = 0.82), Mental Work (M = 4.06, SD = 0.83), Diversion (M = 4.03, SD = 0.80), Revival (M = 3.79, SD = 0.88), and Discharge (M = 3.39, SD = 1.06).

At the level of individual strategies, a negative effect of age was also observed at a statistically significant level for Entertainment (β = -.14), Mental Work (β = -.14), Diversion (β = -.13), Discharge (β = -.13), and Solace (β = -.13, ps < .001), and Strong Sensation (β = -.07, p = .046). Underlying psychological functions of music mood regulation strategies

For each MMR strategy, participants rated how much music listening regulated their mood through facilitating Bodily, Cognitive, and Social functions. While on average, all three functions were reported to contribute to the facilitation of B-MMR strategies, some participants reported not relying on particular functions (i.e., responded with "1 -Not at all") for specific strategies. For example, some participants reported not relying on music to serve Social functions for Entertainment, and others reported not relying on music to serve Cognitive functions for Revival. Interestingly, across all B-MMR strategies, evidence that some participants reported not relying on Social functions to facilitate them was found.

To determine whether a particular function was most relied on to facilitate the B-MMR strategies, Linear Mixed-Effect models were used. Marginal contrasts (Table 1, Figure 3) revealed that Social ratings were consistently lower than Bodily and Cognitive ratings for all B-MMR strategies. Specifically, the difference between Social and Bodily ratings for all strategies ranged from Δ = -0.33 to -0.92 (all ps < .001). For Social and Cognitive ratings, the difference ranged from Δ = -0.39 to -1.03 (ps < .001) for all strategies except Entertainment (Δ = -0.06, 95% CI = [-0.21, 0.09], p = .434).

Regarding comparisons between Bodily and Cognitive ratings, differences varied between strategies. Bodily ratings were higher than Cognitive ratings for Entertainment (Δ = 0.27, 95% CI = [0.14, 0.39], p < .001) and Revival (Δ = 0.34, 95% CI = [0.21, 0.46], p < .001), while the reverse was found for Mental Work (Δ = 0.20, 95% CI = [0.07, 0.32], p < .01). This latter trend approached significance for Solace (Δ = 0.13, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.25], p = .056). In the remaining strategies (i.e., Strong Sensation, Diversion, and Discharge), no significant differences were found between Bodily and Cognitive ratings (ps > .05). Age differences in underlying functions of music mood regulation strategies

In contrast to the usage of B-MMR strategies, age was not a significant predictor of whether a particular function was relied on to facilitate B-MMR strategies (Appendix 2, Figure 4). The slopes for most functions underlying the B-MMR strategies were negative, but none reached statistical significance after FDR corrections (all adjusted ps > .05). For the Revival strategy, the slope was 0.00 for Bodily functions and 0.03 for Cognitive functions. Here, neither reached statistical significance after FDR corrections either (all adjusted ps > .05). These results suggest that the function of music underlying the facilitation of the B-MMR strategies remains relatively stable across age.

Discussion

Using age as a predictor, we questioned if the usage of MMR strategies and their underlying functions changed across adulthood in this study. From B-MMR scores and experimenter-generated items, we found that while strategy usage declined with age, age differences were not present in the underlying psychological functions of MMR strategies.

Overall, MMR strategies were mainly reported to be facilitated by music serving Bodily or Cognitive functions, whereas Social functions were least relied on.

Our results show that music remains a prominent tactic for mood regulation despite slight decreases in usage with age. Averaged across the full sample, high scores sitting above the scale midpoint were observed for all seven MMR strategies. With age, negative trends were observed at a small, but statistically significant level for overall MMR and the strategies of Entertainment, Mental Work, Diversion, Discharge, Solace, and Strong Sensation. The use of Revival did not reach significance, although numerically it also moved in a negative direction.

The declines we observed might be explained by how affect regulation abilities progressively develop across adulthood. Previous research has characterised adolescence and early adulthood as periods of greater emotional intensity and instability (Bailen et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?g0AlO8 2019) , therefore emphasising more reliance on music to regulate mood. By middle adulthood, individuals seem to have gained more skills and thus regulate negative emotions more adaptively than emerging adults (Zimmermann & Iwanski, 2014) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wDrux2 older adults have been suggested to have greater abilities in optimising daily life, attention, and appraisal towards positive stimuli and emotional outcomes than younger individuals (Carstensen, 1993; Charles, 2010; Reed & Carstensen, 2012).

Through acquired emotion regulation experiences then, adults may gradually become less reliant on an external tactic such as music to regulate mood. This corresponds with how older adults have previously reported lower levels of musical engagement as reflected in declines in musical reward-specifically, in the musical seeking subscale (Belfi et al., 2022)-and in general musical consumption (Skeffington et al., 2026). Though fewer engagements in music listening might prompt less opportunities to reflect on how the activity influences regulation, one might question other factors contributing to this trend. For example, music accessibility and the digitalisation of music listening practices may also explain age-related reductions in music consumption for general use and mood regulation with age (Krause & North, 2016). While younger adults may be more readily adapted to digitalised distribution of music in today's technological society, older adults may be impacted by generational restrictions. The full survey from which this paper was derived includes data on the technological side of MMR, as such, we plan to address this in future research.

Notably, however, only small coefficients were observed for declines in MMR strategy use. This aligns with past findings that young adults and the elderly use a similar range of MMR strategies and that music remains as an important tactic for regulatory purposes also in old age (Saarikallio, 2011; Saarikallio & Erkkilä, 2007; Siltainsuu & Peltola, 2024). The insignificant result for Revival might be explained by the socio-emotional selectivity theory (Carstensen, 1993) whereby older adults' motivation shifts from future-oriented and knowledge-based goals to those relating to their immediate emotional wellbeing due to their perceptions of future time being limited. From this perspective, the frequency of using strategies maximising positive experiences, such as that of Revival, would be suggested to increase with age, paralleling the positivity effect (Reed & Carstensen, 2012) and gained life experience (Charles & Carstensen, 2010) observed in older adults. Though Entertainment shares the regulatory goal of increasing positive affect as Revival, it could be expected to exhibit the same age-related trends but this was not the case in our results. This difference may relate to Entertainment referring to music as a background, which is highly typical for the young generation who have been identified to use music as a digital, cinematic soundtrack for life (Kourilehto et al., 2026). Further, investigation involving audio feature analysis of the music used for facilitating MMR strategies (e.g., Tan & Harrison, 2025) might reveal insight into whether more positive stimuli were used to support MMR in older adults as compared to younger adults.

To identify how music brings about emotional changes for mood regulation, we investigated the underlying psychological functions of each MMR strategy. As hypothesised, we found that Entertainment and Revival were mainly reported to be facilitated by Bodily functions in terms of music resonating in individuals' bodies and Mental Work was mainly facilitated by Cognitive functions as music helped individuals process their thoughts and feelings. The expectation regarding a primary relevance of Bodily function for Strong Sensation and Discharge was not supported. Rather, for these strategies, as well as Discharge and Solace, music served Bodily and Cognitive functions equally to facilitate them.

Indeed, past research has demonstrated that individuals can experience a combination of both physical and mental sensations when listening to music, including feelings of chills, the heart skipping a beat, and being touched, absorbed, or in awe (Peck, 2022; Silvia & Nusbaum, 2011; Sloboda, 1991). It would be likely, then, for these experiences to also be felt during music listening for mood regulation. The dominance of Bodily functions underpinning Entertainment and Revival might be explained by how pleasure and reward derived from music listening, affective states closely associated with the outcomes of the aforementioned strategies, are often felt or expressed through bodily reactions such as chills (Blood & Zatorre, 2001) or sensory-motor movement (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013). With regards to Cognitive functions underpinning Mental Work, the items of the subscale innately suggest cognitive work on understanding, working through, and clarifying thoughts and feelings (Saarikallio, 2012). Furthermore, in the creation of the subscale, it was found to have a positive correlation with reappraisal, which more broadly encompasses the cognitive reconstruction of thoughts and feelings (Saarikallio, 2012).

Across all strategies, social functions were least relied on-individuals did not depend much on music connecting them with others to regulate mood. This was not the case even for Solace, although we expected the contents of Solace, such as feeling understood, accepted, and comforted, to address a social experience. Such finding reflects Schäfer and colleagues (2013), who also found Social functions to be least significant out of the three psychological functions of music listening. It may be that the experience of music listening is considered a private one-something that takes place in one's body and mind-even if the content of those thoughts and feelings would be about social aspects. Overall, the comparatively low prevalence of Social functions underlying MMR strategies may relate to how our study focused on music listening contexts as opposed to music-making. While music listening has been highlighted to occur in both individual and social settings (Ansani et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4Plmd5 2026) , higher Social functions for particular MMR strategies may be found if other forms of musical engagement for mood regulation were addressed. A future direction for research could then be to implement the study of age-related differences in MMR strategy usage and their underlying functions into diverse musical contexts such as group singing (Good & Russo, 2021) or attendance in concerts (Clements-Cortés, 2017; Swarbrick et al., 2024) of which have been found to have effects on bonding and positive emotional outcomes.

In contrast to the age-related differences found in the usage of MMR strategies, no statistically significant findings were identified for the underlying psychological functions of each MMR strategy. Firstly, this indicates that the B-MMR strategies may have relatively stable profiles in terms of how each of them relies on different types of psychological resources. Secondly, this refers to lack of age differences that reflects previous findings that aesthetic experiences to music, as displayed by chills and the feelings of strong emotions, did not decrease across young, middle, and late adulthood (Belfi et al., 2022).

As opposed to resource declines driving age-related differences in general emotion regulation strategy usage, music listening and its functions for mood regulation processes can instead be argued to be a low-effort activity. The enjoyment of music listening in general has long been suggested to result from the activation of psychological processes involving the interpretation of musical structure and its contributions to creating and releasing musical tension (Kohut & Levarie, 1950). Although complexhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?sGcFNn the processes often occur with limited awareness and consciousness (Kohut & Levarie, 1950) , resulting in an experience of low perceived effort that at times, may even be resource-generating and counteracting age-related resource losses.

The emotional and resource benefits of music may explain why the medium serves several health and wellbeing applications beyond mood regulation for diverse populations (Dingle et al., 2021). This can be manifestedhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?OadppW for examplehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UGYMWW as support across domains for those with autism spectrum disorder (Geretsegger et al., 2022) , neurodegenerative disorders (Machado Sotomayor et al., 2021; Matziorinis & Koelsch, 2022) , or mental health disorders (Chan et al., 2011; Pridy et al., 2021). Moreover, a recent study also found that the duration of sports performance increased without costs in energy and bodily resources when individuals listened to music (Danso et al., 2026). Music listening and other forms of musical engagement have also often been referenced to compensate for losses in social resources within aging populations (Ayyildiz et al., 2025; Groarke et al., 2022; Siltainsuu & Peltola, 2024).

The current study addressed age-related changes in usage of MMR strategies and their underlying functions; however, it has some limitations. The study participants were drawn from a cross-sectional sample, so the results could have been derived from cohort differences rather than as a result of age. To truly address developmental differences in MMR, future research could involve following longitudinal changes in individuals' MMR strategy usage across their lifespans. Another important consideration is that this study was dependent on self-report measures which are innately prone to recall bias. The use of experience sampling methodology (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?n5DFaA Randall & Rickard, 2013) might provide a more representative understanding on the frequency and efficacy of MMR strategies within the unfolding situations of everyday life amongst individuals of diverse ages. Though the B-MMR is a validated tool for measuring usage of MMR strategies, our items for measuring psychological functions were experimenter-generated. While it was a deliberate choice in the current study to keep the set of items for the psychological functions as short as possible to avoid participant exhaustion of repeatedly rating multiple items on each function for the seven B-MMR strategies, this limits the comprehensiveness of addressing the psychological functions as psychological constructs. The involvement of mixed-methods would provide alternative measures, and qualitative approaches would allow more in-depth identification of the psychological functions underlying different MMR strategies. To assess the Bodily and Social functions in a more comprehensive manner, one could involve physiological measures, motion-capture, and measures of aesthetic experiences to music, synchrony, and bonding. Participant interviews and performance on cognitive tasks could be used to reveal Cognitive functions. Together, these measures might also give insight into how "costly" MMR is in terms of its resources.

Compared to literature on general emotion regulation, research involving developmental perspectives and age-related differences are minimal in the context of music for mood regulation. This study has offered some preliminary insight into this gap, showing that with age, usage of MMR strategies decreases, but that their underlying psychological functions remain stable. The findings of this study have implications for a range of psychoeducational and health-promotional interventions addressing lifetime development and advocate that music should continue to be made available as a wellbeing tool in everyday life and clinical settings for individuals across the lifespan.
