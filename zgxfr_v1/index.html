This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Everyone experiences emotions, but people vary in their orientations to and beliefs about emotions. Research has linked global beliefs about the controllability and usefulness of emotions with psychological functioning and well-being, but the mechanisms through which those beliefs shape people's well-being remain unclear. Ford and Gross (2019) proposed emotion regulation as a candidate mechanism, such that emotion beliefs influence whether people initiate emotion regulation (identification), which strategies people use to regulate their emotions (selection), and how successfully people are able to implement their selected emotion-regulation strategies (implementation). We tested predictions based on Ford and Gross's ( 2019) framework in everyday life in a seven-day experience sampling study (Australian community sample recruited in 2024; N = 326; 80% female; MAge=26.11, SDAge=8.52). At the identification stage, neither controllability nor usefulness beliefs were associated with initiating emotion regulation. In terms of strategy selection, stronger beliefs about the usefulness of negative emotions were associated with greater use of situation selection and lesser use of expressive suppression, as predicted. Finally, at the implementation stage, controllability beliefs did not moderate the hedonic effects of any regulation strategy, whereas usefulness beliefs about negative emotions predicted a weaker positive association between cognitive reappraisal and negative affect, and believing positive emotions to be more useful predicted a stronger inverse association between rumination and positive affect. Our findings suggest that individual differences in global emotion beliefs may play a smaller role than theorised in daily emotion regulation processes.

Do Emotion Beliefs Influence Emotion Regulation in Everyday Life?

Everyone feels emotions, and yet people differ substantially in how they view their emotional experiences. For instance, some people regard emotions as sources of insight and guidance, whereas others see emotions as irrelevant, useless, overwhelming or beyond their control. Over the last decade, convergent evidence has linked individual differences in emotion beliefs with various markers of psychological functioning and well-being (Becerra et al., 2024; Kneeland et al., 2016). However, the mechanisms through which individual differences in these beliefs influence short-and long-term wellbeing and emotional outcomes remain unclear. Ford and Gross (2019) proposed that emotion beliefs may shape well-being via their influence on emotion regulation-the processes through which people influence which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they experience or express these emotions (Gross, 2015). According to this theory, beliefs about the usefulness and controllability of emotions may influence whether people attempt to modify their emotional experiences in everyday life, how they try to do so, and to what effect.

We sought to test Ford and Gross's (2019) proposal by investigating how individual differences in beliefs about positive and negative emotions were associated with the identification, selection and implementation stages of emotion regulation in daily life in a 7-day experience sampling study (NID=326, Nobs=16, 347). This study provides, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive test of how emotion beliefs shape everyday emotion regulation.

Emotion Beliefs and Well-Being

Over the course of development, people acquire beliefs about the nature of emotions through their own direct experience, parental socialisation (Halberstadt et al., 2013), and through exposure to cultural norms (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Mesquita et al., 2017). For instance, in Western cultures, experiencing happiness and other positive emotions is often valued over experiencing negative emotions, such as sadness (Bastian et al., 2012; De Vaus et al., 2018). Thus, through cumulative personal experience and exposure to prevailing social and cultural values, research suggests that by adulthood people have developed relatively stable (albeit modifiable) beliefs about emotions (Chow & Berenbaum, 2012; Veilleux et al., 2021; Willroth et al., 2023).

GLOBAL EMOTION BELIEFS AND REGULATION IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Over the past decade, researchers have documented reliable links between emotion beliefs and well-being. Evidence suggests that believing emotions are uncontrollable and/or useless is associated with symptoms of depression and anxiety (Johnston et al., 2024; Kneeland et al., 2016). Conversely, experimentally inducing people to believe that emotions are helpful was found to increase well-being (Karnaze & Levine, 2020). Although research has traditionally focused on beliefs about emotions in general, recent work has begun to recognize the importance of differentiating between beliefs about positive versus negative emotions (Ford & Gross, 2019; Peter et al., 2026). For instance, the Emotion Beliefs Questionnaire (EBQ; Becerra et al., 2020) explicitly separates beliefs about positive and negative emotions into distinct subscales, making it possible to compare their unique associations with well-being. Research using the EBQ and other valence-specific measures of emotion beliefs indicates that emotion beliefs about the controllability and usefulness of both positive and negative emotions matter for psychological health (Becerra et al., 2020; Johnston et al., 2024; Kneeland & Dovidio, 2020; Larionow et al., 2024; Rogier et al., 2023; Willroth et al., 2023).

Emotion Beliefs and Emotion Regulation

While the existing literature has established links between emotion beliefs and psychological health, the mechanisms that underpin these relationships remain underspecified. One plausible means through which emotion beliefs may link with well-being is their influence on emotion regulation processes (Ford & Gross, 2019). Emotion regulation refers to intentional (but not necessarily conscious) attempts to influence the trajectory of emotions (Gross, 2015). According to Gross's (2015) extended process model, emotion regulation involves three main stages: (a) identification, where people evaluate their current emotions and decide whether to initiate regulation; (b) selection, in which people determine the most appropriate strategy with which to regulate their emotions; and (c) implementation, where people deploy their chosen regulation strategy (Gross, 2015). 1 Ford and Gross (2019) theorise that individual differences in beliefs about the usefulness and controllability of emotions may impact each stage of emotion regulation in distinct but predictable ways.

Emotion Beliefs and Identification

People may decide to regulate their emotions if they identify a gap between how they currently feel and how they want to feel (Gross, 2015; Tamir et al., 2021). Understanding how, when, and why people decide to regulate their emotions is important, as maladaptive decision-making in emotion regulation initiation has been linked with both under-and over-regulation of emotions (Daniel et al., 2024), as well as with a range of psychopathologies, including eating disorders, schizophrenia, and anxiety (Gross et al., 2019; Raugh & Strauss, 2022). Theoretically, Ford and Gross (2019) propose that (a) more strongly believing that emotions are controllable should predict more frequent emotionregulation attempts, whereas (b) more strongly believing that emotions are useful (or "good") should be associated with a decreased likelihood of deciding to regulate emotions.

To our knowledge, only two studies have investigated how emotion beliefs are related to emotion-regulation initiation. In line with Ford & Gross's hypothesis, Wittkamp et al. (2022) found that when people evaluated emotions as less harmful, they were less likely to initiate emotion regulation, even after controlling for momentary emotion valence and arousal. Also in line with Ford and Gross's (2019) predictions, Petrova and colleagues (2024) found that momentary increases in usefulness beliefs were negatively associated with regulation attempts, whereas increases in controllability beliefs were associated with increased attempts to regulate. However, these effects were no longer present after controlling for emotion intensity. Petrova et al.'s (2024) findings suggest that transient changes in emotion beliefs may relate to regulation initiation, although these associations may be partly driven by changes in the intensity of emotional experience that co-occur with fluctuations in emotion beliefs. However, Petrova et al. (2024) used self-reported effort as a proxy for initiating emotion regulation attempts, which may reflect regulatory engagement after the decision to regulate has already been made rather than the earlier appraisal of whether to regulate or not (Gross, 2015). Therefore, direct empirical testing of whether beliefs about both the usefulness and controllability of emotions influence the decision to regulate is still needed. Further, both Wittkamp et al. (2022) and Petrova et al. (2024) assessed momentary rather than global beliefs about emotions. Although momentary beliefs may be relevant to emotion regulation, most work so far has focused on the associations between global emotion beliefs and well-being (Kisley et al., 2024; Veilleux et al., 2021). Investigating how individual differences in global emotion beliefs are related to the identification stage of emotion regulation matters, because global emotion beliefs may function as a lens through which people typically engage with, and respond to, their emotions (Dweck et al., 1995; Suri et al., 2015).

Emotion Beliefs and Strategy Selection

Once people have decided to regulate their emotions, they must decide which strategies to use to achieve their regulation goal (Gross, 2015). According to Ford and Gross's (2019) framework, individuals should be more likely to select regulation strategies that are generally effective in downregulating emotions they believe to be useless, and strategies that are effective in up-regulating emotions they believe to be useful. For instance, someone who strongly believes positive emotions to be useful should select strategies that maximize positive emotions. Certain strategies (e.g., distraction, cognitive reappraisal) are generally effective in increasing positive and decreasing negative emotions, whereas other strategies (e.g., rumination, expressive suppression) tend to have contra-hedonic effects (Boemo et al., 2022; Webb et al., 2012). Thus, to the extent that people are aware of the hedonic outcomes of different strategies (Sheppes et al., 2011), their beliefs about the usefulness of emotions should guide their strategy selection.

Further, if people believe their emotions are uncontrollable, they may perceive successful regulation as less attainable, and therefore invest less in regulating. Indeed, some models of emotion regulation conceptualize strategy selection as involving a cost-benefit analysis, in which people consider the expected attainability and benefits of different regulatory options (Tamir, 2016).

Accordingly, Ford and Gross (2019) hypothesise that stronger beliefs that emotions are uncontrollable may lead people to consider fewer regulation strategies and, consequently, reduce the likelihood that they select an effective strategy.

Most empirical work linking emotion beliefs with emotion regulation has focused on their associations with strategy selection and use. In line with Ford and Gross's (2019) predictions, individuals who more strongly endorse beliefs that emotions are uncontrollable (Hong & Kangas, 2022; Kim et al., 2024) or useless (Hong & Kangas, 2022) tend to report less frequent use of regulation strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal, acceptance) associated with pro-hedonic outcomes and greater use of strategies (e.g., avoidance, suppression, rumination) typically associated with contra-hedonic outcomes. Consistent with Ford and Gross's (2019) theoretical framework, Petrova et al. (2024) found that stronger beliefs that negative emotions were helpful and controllable were associated with greater use of cognitive reappraisal, whereas stronger beliefs that negative emotions were less helpful were associated with greater use of distraction. Similarly, Wittkamp et al. (2022) found that greater perceived harmfulness of emotions was associated with greater use of rumination, avoidance, and experiential suppression (i.e., regulation strategies associated with contra-hedonic outcomes). Together, these findings suggest that beliefs about emotions may shape the strategies individuals select to regulate them.

Emotion Beliefs and Implementation

In the third stage of emotion regulation, people implement their chosen strategy (Gross, 2015). This stage is especially critical because it is the moment when regulation strategies are translated into concrete actions to influence emotions. Accordingly, measuring only which strategies people tend to select says little about how strategies influence emotions (Bonanno & Burton, 2013). For instance, a chosen strategy may be poorly implemented, resulting in the individual feeling worse, when trying to feel better. A growing body of work has examined contextual moderators of strategy implementation outcomes (e.g., Pauw et al., 2022; Wenzel et al., 2020). By contrast, less is known about how stable individual differences, including emotion beliefs, influence the efficacy of implementing regulation strategies, especially in real-world settings (Doré et al., 2016; Hughes et al., 2020). Ford and Gross (2019) propose that emotion beliefs matter for how effectively people are able to implement their chosen regulation strategies. Specifically, people who believe emotions are more useful should translate their selected strategies into a regulatory action that helps them attain or maintain those emotions, whereas people who believe emotions are less useful will be more likely to implement strategies that help them avoid or reduce those emotions. Thus, because people often hold pro-hedonic goals in everyday life (Bianchi et al., 2026; Kalokerinos et al., 2017), beliefs about the usefulness of emotions may shape regulation according to the hedonic goal of increasing or maintaining versus decreasing or avoiding a given emotional state. Further, Ford and Gross (2019) hypothesize that individuals who believe emotions are more controllable will regulate emotions more effectively, whereas those who believe emotions are less controllable will regulate emotions less effectively, because they are less likely to learn and successfully deploy strategies effectively.

Research exploring associations between emotion beliefs and individual differences in the effects of implementing strategies have relied on self-reported regulation difficulties (e.g., Johnston et al., 2024; Preece et al., 2022). This literature indicates that stronger beliefs in emotion uncontrollability are indeed associated with greater difficulties in regulating affect, and that beliefs that emotions are useless are likewise linked to poorer regulatory outcomes (Becerra et al., 2024; Hong & Kangas, 2022; Johnston et al., 2024). While informative, these approaches do not necessarily indicate how global emotion beliefs relate to individual differences in the momentary outcomes of implementing strategies in daily life (Kobylińska & Kusev, 2019). Testing this important question requires finer-grained, temporally sensitive methods (e.g., experience sampling) that can test whether and how valence-specific, dispositional emotion beliefs predict implementation success in everyday contexts.

Procedure

The study comprised two parts: a baseline session (Part 1), followed by a seven-day ESM phase (Part 2), commencing the day after Part 1. Codebooks for both parts are available at https://osf.io/vhsau.

Part 1: Baseline Survey

Participants first attended an in-person baseline session, which lasted 45-60 minutes, with researchers available to support participants with any questions. After providing informed consent, participants completed self-report questionnaires assessing their global beliefs about emotions, demographics, and several other measures not relevant to the current study. Three attention checks embedded in the baseline survey were used to screen out inattentive participants. Upon completion of the baseline survey, participants were instructed to download the SEMA3 app (O'Brien et al., 2024) on their smartphones, they were introduced to the ESM protocol, and they completed a practice survey using the SEMA3 app to ensure their comprehension.

Part 2: ESM Surveys

The ESM part began the day after the baseline survey and continued for seven consecutive days.

Participants received ten prompts per day, delivered through the SEMA3 app on a stratified pseudorandom schedule spanning a 14-hour period each day. ESM surveys were scheduled at a random time within ten daily 30-minute survey windows (e.g., 8:30-9:00, 10:00-10:30) with a 60-minute break between successive survey windows (e.g., no survey scheduled between 9:00 and 10:00). Participants selected one of three pre-defined ESM sampling schedules (7:30am-9:30pm; 8:30am-10:30pm; or 9:30am-11:30pm) to best match their daily routine. Each ESM questionnaire contained a total of 27 questions with conditional branching balanced to ensure that each ESM survey contained an equal number of items. ESM surveys remained open for completion for up to 30 minutes, after which they expired and could no longer be completed. Researchers emailed participants on days 3 and 7 with content based on participants' compliance (i.e., encouragement and offering assistance to participants with less that 70% compliance; praising those above 70%).

Analytic Strategy

We conducted multilevel models for each research question, with momentary surveys (Level 1) nested within persons (Level 2). We used a maximal inclusivity approach in all our models, including a random intercept and random slopes for all within-person predictors (Barr et al., 2013). Model specifications including modifications to address convergence issues are reported in Supplementary Materials S1.

We addressed each research question through a 2-step modelling approach. At Step 1, we modelled each of the four emotion beliefs (positive controllability, positive usefulness, negative controllability, negative usefulness) as separate predictors of emotion regulation. At Step 2, we ran combined models including all four emotion beliefs for research questions 1 and 2, and including the two beliefs that were congruent in valence with affect for research question 3 (i.e., beliefs about positive emotion when considering the impact of regulation on positive affect; and beliefs about negative emotion when considering the impact of regulation on negative). Given that individual differences in affect intensity are known to correlate with emotion-regulation processes (e.g., Blanke et al., 2022), we controlled for mean affect by including person-means of positive and negative affect as Level-2 covariates in all models. Given the number of tests included in our analyses, we pre-registered adjusting the threshold for significance using Bonferroni correction, and cautiously interpreting results with p values between .05 and .001. Discussion

Ford and Gross (2019) recently proposed that beliefs about the controllability and usefulness of emotions may be related to emotion regulation by (a) shaping how often people initiate regulation, (b) influencing which strategies they select to regulate their emotions; and/or (c) moderating how effectively people can implement regulation strategies. Using ESM data from an Australian community sample, this study is the first to comprehensively test Ford and Gross's (2019) framework by investigating how global beliefs about positive and negative emotions were related to operationalizations of these three stages of regulation in daily life.

Global Emotion Beliefs Play a Limited Role in Shaping Emotion Regulation Processes.

We present a summary of our key findings in Table 7. At the identification stage, contrary to our predictions, derived from Ford and Gross's (2019) framework, global beliefs about the controllability and usefulness of emotions were not associated with people's decision to regulate emotions in daily life.

Our findings suggest that people who believe emotions to be uncontrollable or useless are equally likely to initiate emotion-regulation attempts as those who believe emotions to be controllable or useful.

Our findings contrast with previous research providing evidence for associations between momentary emotion beliefs and initiation of emotion regulation (Petrova et al., 2024; Wittkamp et al., 2022). Importantly, our work examined global beliefs about emotions, whereas previous studies focused on evaluations of the specific emotion being experienced at a given moment. This distinction may help explain why we did not observe the predicted associations at the identification stage. Indeed, emotion beliefs can vary in their level of abstraction, ranging from superordinate beliefs about emotions in general to more contextualized beliefs about specific emotions, situations, or targets (Ford & Gross, 2019; Becerra et al., 2020). It is therefore possible that beliefs about a particular emotional experience are more proximal to the decision to regulate than broad, superordinate beliefs about emotions in general.

Accordingly, global emotion beliefs may reflect how people generally interpret and understand their emotions in abstract terms, but they may not be sufficiently specific to influence whether regulation is initiated at any given moment in daily life.

At the selection stage, we found limited evidence for associations between global emotion beliefs and strategy use. However, in some cases, these findings were not consistent across model specifications (see Table 7 and Suppl. Two hypothesized effects, which held across model specifications, were that individuals endorsing stronger negative-usefulness beliefs tended to report greater use of situation selection and lower use of expressive suppression. Finally, moving to the implementation stage, we begin by interpreting the interaction effects central to our research questions. Overall, we found limited evidence that emotion beliefs were systematically associated with individual differences in the affective consequences of implementing regulation strategies. Indeed, contrary to our hypothesis and the conceptual framework by Ford and Gross (2019), controllability beliefs did not moderate the hedonic effects of any regulation strategy.

In contrast, we found that using rumination was associated with greater reductions in momentary positive affect among individuals who reported stronger beliefs about the usefulness of positive emotions. Overall, however, the small number of significant interactions suggests that emotion beliefs may exert only a limited influence at the implementation stage of emotion regulation.

Turning to the main effects of strategies on hedonic outcomes, within-person associations between regulation strategy use and affect were broadly consistent with established patterns in the emotion regulation literature. For instance, increases in rumination and suppression were associated with higher negative affect and lower positive affect (e.g., Brans et al., 2013; Brockman et al., 2017).

Conversely, situation selection was associated with lower negative affect and higher positive affect, consistent with theoretical models positioning situation selection as an antecedent-focused strategy that regulates emotion by shaping exposure to emotional situations (Gross, 1998 (Gross, , 2015)).

We also observed some seemingly paradoxical effects of regulation strategies on momentary affect (e.g., cognitive reappraisal and distraction were, on average, associated with increases in negative affect). Such patterns are relatively common in daily life studies (e.g., Kalokerinos et al., 2019; Koval et al., 2023) and may reflect the tendency for increases in negative affect to precede, rather than follow, strategy use. Although these findings may partly reflect the temporal ordering of affect and strategy use, the temporal design of our study limits the conclusions that can be drawn about the direction of these associations (Boemo et al., 2022).

An additional observation from the implementation analyses is that only usefulness beliefs were uniquely associated with individuals' average emotional experiences, with stronger beliefs that negative emotions are useful predicting lower mean levels of negative affect and stronger beliefs that positive emotions are useful predicting higher mean levels of positive affect. In contrast, neither positive nor negative emotion controllability beliefs showed unique associations with corresponding affective outcomes. These findings are noteworthy, given that previous research has focused more extensively on the correlates of controllability beliefs. For example, Becerra et al. (2024) and Johnston et al. (2024) reported associations between controllability beliefs and symptoms of depression and anxiety, whereas usefulness beliefs were not similarly associated with these outcomes. However, other evidence suggests that usefulness beliefs are also related to emotional experiences (Willroth et al., 2022). Thus, our work extends upon existing evidence by suggesting that global beliefs about the usefulness of emotions may account for unique variance in everyday emotions, whereas controllability beliefs may not. This distinction may be particularly relevant for understanding everyday affective experience, given that much of the existing evidence has focused on clinical symptoms and other global well-being measures.

Do Emotion Beliefs Not Matter, Then? Evaluating Significance and Robustness of the Findings.

The limited evidence provided by our findings for the role of emotion beliefs across the three stages of emotion regulation could be interpreted in two ways: it may indicate that these beliefs play a relatively modest role in everyday emotion regulation, or it may reflect limitations in our study design or data-analytic approach. Several features of the present study make the latter explanation unlikely.

We conducted a large-scale intensive longitudinal study, including over 300 participants who completed, on average, 50 ESM reports over a week. Hence, our data provide a strong basis for detecting meaningful associations and examining dynamic emotion regulation processes as they unfold in naturalistic contexts (Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013). Indeed, across the three stages of emotion regulation, we observed robust associations between mean levels of positive and negative affect and regulatory processes. Similarly, autoregressive effects of emotion, included as covariates to account for temporal dependencies in affect, were consistently significant and positive, reflecting the wellestablished phenomenon of affective inertia (Koval et al., 2021). Further, at the implementation stage, we observed significant associations between several regulation strategies and affective outcomes that were broadly consistent with existing theoretical and empirical work, including links between rumination, suppression, situation selection, and daily emotional experiences (Brans et al., 2013; Brockman et al., 2017; Webb et al., 2012). Together, these findings corroborate the robustness of our study design and analytical strategy for identifying meaningful associations. While we propose that our findings are not merely driven by weaknesses in our study design and analytic approach, it may nevertheless be premature to conclude that emotion beliefs play little or no role in emotion regulation. In particular, several limitations on the generality of our findings (discussed below) point to specific contexts in which emotion beliefs may have a more substantial impact on emotion regulation.

Limitations and Future Directions

Given that our conclusions are based on a single intensive longitudinal study, replication in independent samples would be an essential next step (Nosek et al., 2022). Establishing whether the associations that held across our robustness analyses also replicate across different samples, emotion belief measures, and methodological approaches would be critical for determining which findings may reflect sample-specific variability (Brandt et al, 2014). These considerations also point to some constraints on generality, some limitations of the present work and avenues for future research.

Constraints on Generality.

Our sample was predominantly female, and comprised largely Asian participants living in Australia. The present findings may therefore reflect processes stemming from the intersection of cultural background and the Australian sociocultural context, rather than beliefs about emotions that are universal across cultural settings. Further, these sample characteristics may shape the extent to which the observed associations generalise to other populations. For example, gender differences have been documented in aspects of emotional experience and regulation (Nolen-Hoeksema, 2012; McRae et al., 2008). Similarly, cultural background may influence beliefs about emotions, including the perceived value, controllability, and social consequences of emotional experiences, as well as the goals individuals pursue when regulating emotions (Matsumoto et al., 2008; Ford & Gross, 2019).

Future research should therefore examine whether the present findings generalise across samples with greater variation in gender, cultural background, and sociocultural context. In particular, cross-cultural investigations may be valuable for determining whether the limited and selective associations between emotion beliefs and regulatory processes observed here reflect general features of emotion regulation or are shaped by culturally specific beliefs about the functions and management of emotions.

Another potentially relevant characteristic of our community sample was that participants generally endorsed relatively high levels of both emotion controllability and usefulness beliefs.

Although the observed distributions of emotion beliefs still reflected meaningful individual differences, greater heterogeneity in emotion beliefs may provide a stronger test of the conditions under which these beliefs influence emotion regulation processes. Future research could examine these questions in populations expected to exhibit greater heterogeneity in emotion beliefs, such as clinical samples, where beliefs about emotions are often more extreme and therefore more strongly implicated in emotional functioning (Hong & Kangas, 2022; Peter et al., 2026; Somerville et al, 2024).

Examining whether emotion beliefs exert stronger or more pervasive effects in such populations would help clarify the GLOBAL EMOTION BELIEFS AND REGULATION IN EVERYDAY LIFE conditions under which these beliefs are most consequential. Methodological Considerations. On a methodological level, we assessed global emotion beliefs using the Emotion Beliefs Questionnaire (EBQ), which measures beliefs about emotions in general, rather than beliefs about one's own emotions. Although these broader beliefs are theoretically important, it is possible that self-referential beliefs-for example, beliefs about the controllability or usefulness of my emotions-are more proximally related to everyday regulatory behaviour (Caprara et al., 2008; Gratz & Roemer, 2004). Future work should compare the predictive utility of general versus self-referential emotion beliefs to determine the roles of each in daily emotion regulation processes.

A related consideration concerns the level at which emotion beliefs were assessed. The EBQ captures beliefs as a relatively stable individual difference, whereas beliefs about emotions may also fluctuate within persons across situations (Petrova et al., 2024; Wittkamp et al., 2022). It is possible that momentary beliefs (e.g., whether a particular emotion is seen as useful right now), are more proximally related to everyday emotion regulation processes. This may explain why previous studies reported reliable within-person associations among momentary beliefs with identification and strategy selection.

Future work assessing global and momentary beliefs within the same design could shed light on whether beliefs are more strongly associated with daily emotion regulation processes at both levels.

As another consequence of our methodological approach, the present findings primarily reflect emotion regulation in everyday contexts (Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013). In community samples, this method primarily captures relatively modest fluctuations in affect (Dejonckheere & Mestdagh, 2021).

However, the role of emotion beliefs may depend on the intensity and significance of the emotional events being regulated. Although our analyses accounted for between-person differences in mean affect intensity, our study likely did not capture people's feelings highly significant or personally important contexts. It is possible that beliefs about the controllability or usefulness of emotions become more influential when individuals encounter personally meaningful, or highly consequential situations, where regulatory decisions carry greater psychological significance (Gross, 2015; Sheppes et al., 2015). Future research should therefore examine whether the influence of emotion beliefs varies according to the intensity and personal significance of affective experiences.

Finally, in line with previous research, we assume that people generally engage in pro-hedonic regulation (i.e., seeking to decrease negative and increase positive feelings) in everyday life (Riediger et al., 2009). However, people may pursue pro-hedonic goals in the service of various emotion-regulation motives (i.e., the higher-order end states that emotion regulation serves; Tamir, 2016). Growing evidence shows that people regulate emotions for a wider variety of reasons besides feeling good or bad (Bianchi et al., 2026; Kalokerinos et al., 2017). Distinguishing emotion regulation based on motives may be particularly important for understanding the role of emotion beliefs (Tamir, 2016; Tamir & Ford, 2012). For instance, beliefs about the usefulness of emotions may exert relatively little influence when individuals regulate emotions simply to feel better, but may become substantially more important when regulating emotions to facilitate attainment of other goals. Hence, future studies examining emotion regulation motives alongside emotion beliefs may help clarify the conditions under which beliefs shape regulatory processes.

Conclusion

We conducted the first comprehensive investigation of global controllability and usefulness beliefs for both positive and negative emotions in shaping emotion regulation processes at the identification, selection and implementation stages. Leveraging data from a large and diverse Australian community sample, and drawing on the strengths of experience sampling methods, this study captured people's regulation attempts, their use of six emotion regulation strategies, and their momentary affective experiences with fine-grained resolution across naturalistic contexts.

GLOBAL EMOTION BELIEFS AND REGULATION IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Our findings suggest that global beliefs may play only a modest role across the three stages of emotion regulation in everyday life. We found no evidence that global controllability or usefulness beliefs link to people's decision to regulate. Regarding strategy selection, contrasting with some previous evidence, our findings suggest that usefulness (more so than controllability) beliefs may promote greater use of situation selection and less expressive suppression. This provides some limited support for the hypothesis that holding more adaptive beliefs about emotions may be linked with selection of hedonically beneficial strategies. Finally, our analyses suggest that emotion beliefs may hold only a limited influence at the implementation stage of emotion regulation. Specifically, controllability beliefs did not moderate the hedonic effects of any regulation strategy. Usefulness beliefs associations with strategy-related changes in affect were limited to two effects: stronger beliefs that negative emotions are useful were associated with a weaker positive association between cognitive reappraisal and negative affect; and stronger beliefs that positive emotions are useful were associated with a stronger negative association between rumination and positive affect.

Taken together, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that emotion beliefs simply do not matter. Rather, they suggest that their influence may be more nuanced and context-dependent than previously assumed. Future research can therefore build on our work's insights, by examining the value of momentary, self-oriented (rather than general) beliefs, as well as extending this work to populations with greater emotion-regulation difficulties (e.g., clinical samples) and investigating contexts that place stronger demands on emotion regulation than routine everyday settings.
