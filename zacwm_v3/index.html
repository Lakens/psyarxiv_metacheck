Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

ps ≤ .04 Others’ Expectations items, exhibited significant S-χ² statistics (all ps ≤ .04).
ps ≥ .18 The remaining items showed no evidence of statistically significant misfit (ps ≥ .18) and likewise exhibited small RMSEA values.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
ps ≥ .18 The remaining items showed no evidence of statistically significant misfit (ps ≥ .18) and likewise exhibited small RMSEA values.

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. 1. Introduction and Theoretical Background

People-pleasing is generally understood as a tendency to prioritize others' needs, wishes, and approval over one's own preferences and interests. Although such behaviors may facilitate interpersonal harmony, excessive people-pleasing has been associated with difficulties in assertiveness, heightened approval seeking, interpersonal dependency, and psychological distress. Recent theoretical work has further suggested that people-pleasing represents a distinct interpersonal disposition characterized by a strong motivation to avoid interpersonal conflict and maintain social acceptance, even at personal cost. Consequently, valid assessment of individual differences in people-pleasing is important for both research and applied contexts (Blötner, 2026; Kuang et al., 2025).

To facilitate empirical investigation of this construct, the People Pleasing Scale was recently developed and validated. Initial validation studies provided evidence for the threefactor structure (Responsibility, Others' Expectations, Neglect of Own Needs), sufficiently high estimates of reliability, and construct validity using classical test theory (CTT) approaches. These findings established the People Pleasing Scale as a psychometrically promising instrument for assessing people-pleasing tendencies in adults.

Although the original validation of the People Pleasing Scale yielded promising evidence regarding reliability and construct validity, several important psychometric questions remain unanswered. In contrast to CTT, item response theory (IRT) enables the evaluation of item discrimination, category thresholds, conditional reliability, and local dependence at the item level. Therein, item discrimination parameters indicate how effectively an item differentiates between respondents with similar trait levels, whereas threshold parameters identify the levels of the latent trait required to endorse increasingly higher response categories. In addition, IRT affords the identification of specific regions of the trait continuum at which a measure provides the greatest precision. These analyses bear implications for score interpretation and refinement of the measure as they can identify particularly informative items and highlight potential redundancies, among others (Embretson & Reise, 2000).

Importantly, an IRT investigation is not simply a replication of previous validation efforts using different statistical techniques. Rather, IRT provides information that is unavailable within conventional CTT frameworks. For the People Pleasing Scale, such analyses can clarify whether the scale adequately captures the full spectrum of peoplepleasing tendencies, whether some items contribute disproportionately to measurement precision, and whether all response options contribute meaningful information or whether certain categories are redundant or underutilized. These issues are directly relevant to the interpretation and application of test scores and therefore constitute an important extension of the original validation research (Embretson & Reise, 2000).

Conceptually, people-pleasing may be understood as a broad interpersonal disposition or as a constellation of interrelated but distinguishable facets. The People Pleasing Scale was developed to capture three theoretically derived dimensions, namely Responsibility, Others' Expectations, and Neglect of Own Needs (Blötner, 2026). Nevertheless, these facets are expected to share substantial common variance because they reflect different manifestations of a broader tendency to prioritize others' needs and interpersonal harmony. Consequently, both unidimensional and multidimensional representations warrant empirical evaluation.

Accordingly, we conducted an IRT-based evaluation of the People Pleasing Scale.

Using a Graded Response Modeling approach (Samejima, 1997), we examined the discrimination and threshold parameters of the items, evaluated model fit and responsecategory functioning, and investigated the measurement precision with which the measure assesses people-pleasing across the latent trait continuum. In doing so, the present study aims to provide psychometric information that complements previous validation findings (Blötner, 2026) and advances the understanding of the People Pleasing Scale.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Sample

The present study partially re-analyzed data from the original scale-development project (Blötner, 2026). The contribution therefore lies not in providing an independent validation but in generating information unavailable within the CTT framework, such as measurement precision across the latent continuum, category functioning, and potential itemlevel issues. More specifically, our present research sample is composed of both commercial panel data, specifically the two German samples from Blötner's (2026) studies (Nafter data exclusions = 1,894), as well as an additional data collection conducted at German universities, Ntotal = 2,538. As such, our final sample size exceeds the recommended minimum sample size requirements for IRT analyses (n > 500; Jiang et al., 2016). Our extended data collection involved only the ten-item People Pleasing Scale and questions about demographics. Across samples, the corresponding items were mandatory to respond to, such that complete data existed for all questions.

From the total sample, 1,918 (76%) participants self-identified as female, 580 (23%) as male, and 40 (1%) self-identified as nonbinary or did not respond. The mean age was 32.9 years (SD = 12.3, range from 18 to 81). Most participants (n = 2,328; 92%) reported a high school-equivalent or university degree as the highest obtained qualification. Moreover, most indicated currently being enrolled as a university student (n = 2,203; 87%). The comparatively high average age despite the large proportion of university students reflects substantial participation from mature and part-time students as well as the partial inclusion of participants from a commercial panel.

2.2 Measures

The People Pleasing Scale (Blötner, 2026) assesses Responsibility, Neglect of Own Needs, and Others' Expectations with three, four, and three items, respectively. Responses were collected on a five-point scale,1 = Does not apply at all,2 = Does not apply,3 = Neutral,4 = Applies,5 = Completely applies. Blötner (2026) provided initial evidence in favor of the three-factor structure, criterion validity (behavioral economic games), construct validity (concerning benevolent, aversive, and emotion-related personality features), and residual measurement invariance across genders (suggesting that manifest test scores of the People Pleasing Scale can be meaningfully compared between men and women).

2.3 Analytical Strategy

In a set of preliminary analyses, we computed response frequencies, means, standard deviations, and skewness parameters for all items of the People Pleasing Scale. Item response distributions were inspected to identify potential floor or ceiling effects and to evaluate utilization of the five response categories.

All subsequent analyses were conducted in R using the package mirt (Version 1.46.1;

Chalmers, 2012) with expectation-maximization estimation. We employed the Graded Response Model (Samejima, 1997), which is appropriate for ordered polytomous item responses and estimates both item discrimination and category threshold parameters.

The latent structure of the People Pleasing Scale was evaluated using confirmatory item factor analytic models estimated within the mirt framework. Besides the three-factor structure reported in the original scale-development study, we also fit a single-factor model.

Model fit was evaluated using the fit statistic M2, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI). These indices were used jointly to determine the adequacy of the proposed latent structure. Moreover, we computed Sample-Size Adjusted Bayesian Information Criteria (SABIC) for both model alternatives, with lower absolute values indicating better fit.

To test the assumption of local independence within the preferred model alternative, we computed Yen's Q3 statistics, that is, item residuals after fitting the model. Because no universally accepted cutoff exists, we considered both the traditional criterion (|Q3| > .20; Chen & Thissen, 1997) and adjusted criteria that account for characteristics of the instrument and sample (Christensen et al., 2017). More specifically, Christensen et al. (2017) argued that fixed cutoffs are often inappropriate and recommended evaluating whether the particular Q3s differ by more than .20 from the average Q3. Reporting multiple criteria affords a more comprehensive assessment of the extent to which local independence may have been violated.

Item-level model fit was evaluated using the S-χ 2 statistic (Kang & Chen, 2008; Orlando & Thissen, 2000) as well as RMSEA derived from the same model. Given the large sample size, S-χ 2 values were interpreted cautiously because even trivial deviations from model expectations may achieve significance. Therefore, conclusions regarding item fit were based primarily on RMSEA values, with S-χ 2 statistics reported for completeness and comparability with previous research.

For each item, discrimination (a) and category threshold (b) parameters were estimated. Discrimination parameters indicate the degree to which an item differentiates between individuals with different levels of the latent trait, whereas threshold parameters indicate the latent trait levels at which adjacent response categories are equally probable (Embretson & Reise, 2000). Following Baker (2001), we deemed discrimination parameters to be low, moderate, high, and very high when they exceed 0.35, 0.65, 1.35, and 1.70, respectively. Furthermore, we applied the following classifications of θ: Well below average (θ < -2), below average (-2 < θ < -1), average (-1 < θ < 1), above average (1 < θ < 2), and well above average (θ > 2).

The functioning of the five response categories was evaluated through inspection of category response curves derived from the graded response models fitted for each subscale because the curves from the unidimensional model are easier to interpret than those from the multidimensional model. Particular attention was paid to the ordering of threshold parameters and to whether each response category displayed a distinct region of peak probability.

Reliability was evaluated using conditional standard errors of measurement. Item information functions were additionally inspected to determine the contribution of individual items to measurement precision. These analyses were used to identify which items contributed most strongly to the reliable measurement of people-pleasing tendencies.

The three-factor model representing the dimensions Others' Expectations, Neglect of Own Needs, and Responsibility proposed by Blötner (2026), however, demonstrated even better fit to the data, M2(2) = 19.79, p < .001, CFI = .99, RMSEA = .06, SABIC = 59,052.63. Correlations among the factors ranged from .74 to .78 (see our supplement for details and for additional fit indices). These correlations indicate substantial overlap while remaining sufficiently below unity and thus support the empirical distinguishability between the three facets. Therefore, our subsequent analyses rested on the three-factor, multidimensional graded response model.

Although 10 item pairs exceeded the conventional threshold of |Q3| > .20 (Chen & Thissen, 1997), only four surpassed the more conservative adjusted criterion proposed in the literature (Christensen et al., 2017). More specifically, the average residual correlation was Q3, mean = -.10. The largest residual associations were observed among items within the same subscales, suggesting that the detected dependence primarily reflects content overlap or shared wording rather than a substantial violation of the underlying latent structure (Chen & Thissen, 1997; Noventa et al., 2026). Two of these involved the second Responsibility item in relation to the remaining Responsibility items, whereas one involved the residual correlation between the second and third Others' Expectations items. Importantly, the observed residual correlations were modest in magnitude and did not indicate pervasive local dependence across the scale. Thus, the magnitude of the present effects appears insufficient to materially affect substantive conclusions (Chen & Thissen, 1997; Noventa et al., 2026).

4. Discussion

This study investigated the People Pleasing Scale (Blötner, 2026) from an item response-theoretical perspective. We did so to expand on the psychometric information about the measure provided by the seminal work that built on evaluations as per the Classical Test Theory. Most prominently, the original, CTT-based validation study suggested all items functioned similarly, which can be tested by means of applying IRT. Moreover, the original study treated the scale as uniformly reliable, whereas IRT shows where on the latent trait continuum reliability varies. Last, unlike IRT, the original validation could not determine which trait levels were required to display specific levels of item endorsement. Accordingly, we tested whether the People Pleasing Scale accurately discriminates between individuals with similar trait levels (a), which trait levels are required to obtain low, moderate, or high scores on the scale (b1 to b4), and at which regions of the latent trait continua the items and subscale scores provide ample information (i.e., item information and reliability, respectively). The findings largely support the utility of the People Pleasing Scale, even though certain aspects stand out.

4.1 Dimensionality of the People Pleasing Scale

Consistent with previous CTT-based research on the People Pleasing Scale (Blötner, 2026), the three-factor IRT model outperformed a unidimensional alternative. At the same time, the strong factor correlations suggest that the dimensions share considerable common variance and may reasonably be conceptualized as facets of a broader people-pleasing disposition. Thus, Responsibility, Others' Expectations, and Neglect of Own Needs constitute distinguishable but closely related manifestations of an overarching construct. This interpretation is further supported by previous evidence indicating differential relationships within the nomological network with cognitive empathy and extraversion, for instance, despite substantial overlap among the dimensions (Blötner, 2026).

4.2 Item-Level Issues of the People Pleasing Scale

While the results provided some evidence of local dependence among items, the observed residual associations were weak and limited in scope. Local dependence often arises from content overlap or common stimulus features (e.g., when items stem from the same subscale). Such minor violations are not unusual in practice and do not necessarily compromise model validity. As the magnitude of local dependence was generally small, the assumption of local independence can be regarded as sufficiently met for the present analyses.

Nevertheless, the clustering of elevated residuals among conceptually similar items suggests some degree of item redundancy and may cause slight inflation of discrimination estimates for affected items (Chen & Thissen, 1997; Noventa et al., 2026).

Although several items displayed statistically significant S-χ 2 values, the magnitude of model misfit was negligible as evidenced by consistently small RMSEA values. Given the large sample size, these findings likely reflect minor departures from model expectations rather than meaningful deficiencies in item functioning.

Besides ostensible local dependence in some cases, some items performed poorly in the remaining multidimensional IRT analyses. More specifically, the third items tapping into

Others' Expectations and Neglect of Own Needs revealed overlapping response categories in the category probability plots, and the third items tapping into Others' Expectations and

Responsibility provided comparatively little measurement information across the continuum.

Instead of limiting the utility of the measure at large, however, these findings suggest promising candidates for future item reduction studies. Decisions regarding item removal should, however, additionally consider content validity and require independent validation.

The findings also provide an empirical answer to the question posed in the title. It can be answered by means of the upper threshold parameters b4, which were comparatively low in magnitude (b4, max = 1.63), suggesting that endorsement of the highest response categories does not require extremely high levels of the latent trait. Thus, endorsement of the highest response categories can occur already at moderately above-average levels of the latent trait.

4.3 Limitations and Future Directions

Despite the size of the utilized sample and the empirical contribution of this study to the assessment of people-pleasing, the present research was not without limitations, especially with respect to the sample. More specifically, it entailed comparatively young, highly educated, and mostly female university students, primarily enrolled in an undergraduate psychology course. Thus, the sample may have been somewhat preselected because females and individuals studying social sciences usually report higher scores on benevolent personality traits than males and those studying law, business, and economics (Barbee et al., 1993; Vedel, 2016).

Although analyses of differential item functioning of these features may provide valuable information regarding group-specific differences, they were not essential for addressing the central aims of the present study, namely evaluating item-level functioning and measurement precision of the scale as a whole. Questions concerning differential item functioning across demographic groups represent an important but conceptually separate line of inquiry that should be addressed in dedicated follow-up studies.

Another limitation of the present study is that recruitment took place exclusively in Germany, a relatively individualistic country in which personal goals are often prioritized over those of salient ingroups (Hofstede et al., 2010). Consequently, the extent to which the present findings generalize to more collectivistic cultural contexts remains unclear. One might expect individuals in collectivistic cultures to exhibit stronger people-pleasing tendencies, as greater value is placed on social harmony, interpersonal obligations, and attending to the needs of others (Hofstede et al., 2010). In light of these limitations, replications of the present findings in culturally more diverse samples, along with examinations of differential item functioning across cultural groups, are needed to evaluate the generalizability of our findings.

4.4 Conclusion

The findings support the People Pleasing Scale as a psychometrically sound instrument, but refinements are necessary. Beyond confirming favorable discrimination and reliability, the present study demonstrates that the most extreme response categories can be endorsed at only moderately elevated trait levels and identifies specific targets for future revisions. These results illustrate the value of item response-theoretical approaches for advancing the measurement of people-pleasing beyond the perspective of classical test theory.
