Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Full text

Abstract:

Non-ordinary states of consciousness (NOSCs), whether entered via psychedelics, breathwork, or other means, represent profound alterations in perception, emotion, and cognition. While research has largely focused on the cortical dynamics underpinning such shifts in consciousness, empirical evidence on the role of the autonomic nervous system (ANS) in NOSCs has remained observational. As a result, the potential contribution of the ANS to the phenomenological dimensions of NOSCs remains under-explored. Beyond simply regulating bodily functions, the ANS mediates processes directly linked to the core effects of many NOSCs, from emotional regulation to interoception and cognitive function.

Here, we propose a mechanistic framework by which the ANS may shape NOSCs. Integrating historical perspectives on neurophenomenology with current empirical evidence, this framework posits that bidirectional feedback loops between central and autonomic pathways enable deviations from autonomic homeostasis to spark altered information processing and adaptive neural reorganization. We explore two variations of this scenario based on differing levels of empirical support: (1) autonomic state functions as a contextual variable shaping the phenomenological quality of NOSCs, based on more extensive empirical evidence, and (2) autonomic dynamics causally contribute to the emergence of NOSCs by destabilizing entrenched predictive models, a more speculative claim. In either case, we propose the ANS as a participant in, not merely an effector of, the mechanisms generating NOSCs, with coordinated sympathovagal co-activation serving as a hallmark of peak NOSCs.

We offer this framework as a hypothesis-generating complement to the strong cortical focus that has dominated NOSCs research in the past decades.

Non-Ordinary States of Consciousness

Non-Ordinary States of Consciousness (NOSCs) are defined as temporally finite mental states in which perception, emotion, cognition, and one's overall sense of self deviate noticeably from everyday experience, or 'normal waking consciousness' (Ludwig, 1966). As such, NOSCs typically cause marked shifts in both the content and the structure of subjective experience (Timmermann et al., 2023). This definition includes a broad spectrum of phenomena from dreams and lucid dreaming to psychedelic states, breathwork, hypnosis and trance as well as meditative states (Fincham et al., 2026; Flor-Henry et al., 2017; Ludwig, 1966; Vaitl et al., 2005). Particularly NOSCs that are entered deliberately, e.g. via psychedelics, breathwork or trance, are increasingly recognized as potentially powerful tools to boost mental health by loosening maladaptive beliefs (Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019; Ko et al., 2022; Yaden and Griffiths, 2021; Zeifman et al., 2023). These effects are thought to act by allowing experiential confrontation and breakthrough in the face of challenging life experiences, and by fostering feelings of awe and interpersonal connection (Erdmann et al., 2026; Peill et al., 2022; Roseman et al., 2019; Zeifman et al., 2023).

Research into the physiological basis of NOSCs has so far largely focused on the role of the central nervous system (CNS), with the strongest evidence base accumulated in the field of psychedelics. For instancehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MghQg0 studies have uncovered several consistent neural signatures of psychedelic states: (1) reorganization of large-scale cortical networkshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zbUD82 characterised by increased between-network connectivity and more selective within-network changes (Girn et al., 2026https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?raJaZP Preller et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2024) , which has been interpreted as enhanced integration across the cortical hierarchy (see Girn et al., 2026), (2) decreased activity and connectivity of the Default Mode Network (DMN), likely linked to a dissolution of self-referential processing and metacognitive awareness (Lebedev et al., 2015; Muthukumaraswamy et al., 2013) ; and (3) increased neural signal diversity (Carhart-Harris et al., 2014; Schartner et al., 2017) , possibly indicating a broader repertoire of processing states and a deviation from ingrained 'trains of thought' (Lyon, 2026). These neuronal signatures appear to at least partially overlap with those of other NOSCs. For instancehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Jq2AKY meditative and hypnotic states also suppress DMN activity (Brewer et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1izqFl 2011; McGeown et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HayWUm 2009) , shamanic trance states reconfigure DMN network connectivity (Hove et al., 2016) , and ASCs in high-ventilation breathwork (HVB) have been reported to correlate with decreased DMN functional connectivity (Schmitz et al., 2026 , Porter et al., 2026) , as well as increased neural signal complexity and flattened cortical processing hierarchy (Lewis-Healey et al., 2024; Porter et al., 2026).

Computationally, these cortical dynamics are thought to disrupt learned models of perception by relaxing top-down priors and enhancing bottom-up information flow, leading to a mode of information processing that is shaped by real-time perceptual experience rather than predictive expectation (Carhart-Harris et al., 2014; Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019). Phenomenologically, these alterations correspond well to the subjective experiences that emerge during NOSCs, characterized for instance by ego dissolution, in which the usual boundaries between self and world dissolve, giving rise either to feelings of unity, bliss, and mystical insight, or anxiety, dread and a sense of self-fragmentation (Dittrich, 1998; Griffiths et al., 2011; Grof, 1980). Other typical phenomena include the sudden emergence of new insights as well as experiences of emotional breakthrough (Peill et al., 2022; Roseman et al., 2018).

The quality and phenomenology of such subjective experiences has been shown to predict subsequent long-term changes at the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral level across a range of NOSCs including psychedelics and HVB (Havenith et al., 2025; Ko et al., 2022; Porter et al., 2026; Roseman et al., 2018; Yaden and Griffiths, 2021). Specifically, experiences of transcendence, awe and interpersonal connection appear to open a window of opportunity to revise maladaptive processing patterns (Brouwer and Carhart-Harris, 2021; Yaden and Griffiths, 2021). In the case of psychedelics, such long-term 'revisions' are hypothesized to be supported at least in part by heightened neuroplasticity, including neuromodulatory and metabolic signaling (De Vos et al., 2021; Ly et al., 2018).

Subjective experiences are significantly shaped by interoception of ANS functions such as heart beat, breathing rate and gut function, both in terms of their intensity, valence and cognitive appraisal (Azzalini et al., 2019; Critchley and Garfinkel, 2017). For instance, at the sensory level, the cardiac and respiratory phase at which a stimulus arrives alters how it is perceived and how strongly it is felt (Allen et al., 2022; Garfinkel and Critchley, 2016; Garfinkel and Eccleston, 2025). Rather than merely reporting information about bodily state, interoceptive processes are thus thought to contribute to an active inference process in which ascending visceral information and descending autonomic outflow jointly modulate perceptual and affective aspects of subjective experience (Barrett and Simmons, 2015; Seth and Friston, 2016).

Given this evidencehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?N99AmW and the shared experiential alterations featured in many NOSCs, we suggest that ANS alterations deserve investigation for their role in shaping NOSCs, either as features of an overall 'bodyset' (Uthaug et al., 2025) , i.e. a conjunction of physiological processes that together modulate subjective NOSCs qualities, or as causal mediators of NOSCs onset. During NOSCs, autonomic activity may operate beyond its typical range, thereby potentially opening the door to the profound alterations in perception, emotion, and self-awareness characteristic of these states. Understanding such autonomic dynamics provides a crucial puzzle piece to the question how NOSCs emerge, and may help guide the safe and effective therapeutic use of interventions such as psychedelic therapy or HVB. This review aims to outline ways in which ANS dynamics may contribute to or drive NOSCs, focusing mainly on high-arousal NOSCs like psychedelics, HVB, and trance. However, this does not imply that physiological activation is a necessary condition for self-transcendent experiences, as exemplified by low-arousal NOSCs like meditation, hypnosis, or floatation-REST (Bonnelle et al., 2025; Brewer et al., 2011; Hruby et al., 2024; McGeown et al., 2009; Parola et al., 2026). In the following sections, we will first give a basic outline of ANS function, and then discuss the pathways by which autonomic dynamics can support NOSCs, specifically (1) as a contextual variable shaping the phenomenological quality of NOSCs, or (2) as a potential causal mediator of NOSCs via destabilization and reconsolidation of information processing (via the Pivotal Mental States framework).

The Autonomic Nervous System

The ANS is the part of the peripheral nervous system that regulates survival-related bodily functions, such as cardiac activity, digestion, breathing, pupillary dilation, urination, and sexual arousal. Autonomic output is generated in the brainstem and hypothalamic circuits, and modulated by a cortical-subcortical central autonomic network (Beissner et al., 2013). The ANS consists of two main complementary branches: the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the parasympathetic nervous system (PNS). The SNS and the PNS contain both afferent (signalling from body to brain) and efferent (from brain to body) connections to the CNS. The SNS is often referred to as the "fight or flight" system, supporting metabolic deployment and preparing the body for action in response to a disruption of homeostasis or a stressor; while the PNS is often considered as the "rest and digest" or "feed and breed" system, supporting downregulation, stress recovery, and return to homeostatic baseline via energy conservation.

Autonomic communication between brain and body is predominantly ascending, as approximately 80-85% of vagal fibres are afferent sensory pathways conveying visceral information to the brain, while only 15-20% are efferent motor fibres (Berthoud and Neuhuber, 2000). These afferents terminate in the nucleus tractus solitarius, from which visceral information is relayed via the parabrachial nucleus and thalamus to the insular cortex, the principal cortical site of interoceptive representation (Craig, 2002; Critchley et al., 2004). Efferent descending regulation operates through a partially overlapping set of structures, often termed the central autonomic network, comprising insula, anterior cingulate, amygdala, hypothalamus and periaqueductal grey, which together modulate autonomic outflow in anticipation of and in response to changing demands (Beissner et al., 2013).

While the cortical control of descending autonomic outflow will be detailed belowhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?tPlix4 the functional consequences of ascending signalling are increasingly well documented; cardiac-derived signals influence large-scale brain dynamics and conscious perception (Azzalini et al., 2019; Candia-Rivera et al., 2022) , and cardiac-evoked cortical responses have been observed to predict meditative depth in experienced practitioners (Nath et al., 2025, BioRxiv). As suchhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Ug2o2R autonomic efferents and afferents are therefore recursively coupled in active inference (Seth and Friston, 2016) , rather than arranged in a fixed hierarchy, and this architecture has consequences for how the ANS should be positioned in accounts of consciousness. Throughout this review we therefore treat the ANS as a participant in inference, rather than as merely an effector of central commands.

One of the main functions of the SNS is as the driver of arousal and stress responses. Note that throughout this reviewhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VUusRj we use the terms "stress" and "stress response" purely in their physiological sense, to denote the coordinated nervous, endocrine and immune response elicited by a real or anticipated challenge to homeostasis (Chrousos, 2009) , and "arousal" to denote the resulting state of physiological activation. This response involves the coordinated activation of the sympathetic-adreno-medullar (SAM) axis, the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and the immune system (Chu et al., 2024). Specifically, a fast sympathetic stress response is initiated involving the SAM axis, releasing adrenaline and noradrenaline directly into the blood, priming the body for action via a number of adjustments such as contraction of smooth and cardiac muscles, and increased cardiac output (Godoy et al., 2018). A second, slower component of the stress response is mediated by activation of the HPA axis, in which hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), often in concert with vasopressin, stimulates adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) release from the anterior pituitary, leading to the secretion of glucocorticoids, including cortisol, which promotes glucose mobilization and modulates immune and inflammatory responses (Sapolsky et al., 2000).

If this initial stress response successfully resolves the homeostatic disruption, the PNS then activates, promoting metabolic conservation for the restoration of homeostasis. Anatomically, parasympathetic fibres arise from both a cranial outflow, via cranial nerves III, VII, IX, and X, and a sacral outflow (S2-S4) innervating the distal colon, bladder, and reproductive organs. Most regulatory functions of the PNS are largely mediated through the 10th cranial nerve (X), called the vagus nerve, which branches to widespread regions of the body, innervating most organs including the gastrointestinal and cardiovascular systems. At rest, the vagus nerve exerts a continuous inhibitory influence on the heart, slowing its rate through acetylcholine release. This vagal regulation of the heart functions as a dynamic brake, which is transiently withdrawn during acute arousal to allow for increased physiological activation and cardiac output. Once the source of arousal is removed, a 'vagal rebound' of PNS activity engages, rapidly decelerates the heart rate, promoting a restoration of homeostasis following disruption. Notably, sympathetic activity can remain transiently elevated during this recovery phase, creating a period of co-activation between SNS and PNS (Mezzacappa et al., 2001; Weissman and Mendes, 2021).

The vagal rebound phase has also been associated with the release of oxytocin and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), both of which are linked to accelerated autonomic recovery and reduced cortisol output (Engert et al., 2016; Linz et al., 2019). What's more, through its role in neuronal growth, maintenance, and synaptic plasticity, BDNF may facilitate adaptive reorganization of neural and autonomic networks during stress recovery (Linz et al., 2019). These recovery dynamics suggest that vagal rebound and transient autonomic co-activation may be particularly relevant for understanding the plasticity-promoting and integrative effects reported during NOSCs, as will be explored in later sections.

Importantly, the way that autonomic arousal is subjectively experienced depends on appraisal of contextual factors (Schachter and Singer, 1962). Sympathetic activation can be experienced as a threat or a challenge (Blascovich and Tomaka, 1996; Seery, 2011), or as excitement and joy (Russell, 1980; Shiota et al., 2011), depending on contextual interpretation (Schachter and Singer, 1962). In the Circumplex Model of Affect, distress corresponds to high arousal with negative valence, and excitement or elation to high arousal with positive valence (Russell, 1980).

The success of the stress response is ultimately measured by the organism's ability to not only return to homeostasis, but to develop anticipatory adaptive regulation through allostasis, a process of anticipating demands and adjusting physiology in advance, rather than merely correcting deviations after they occur (Sterling, 2012). When the autonomic response fails to restore homeostatic balance or establish effective allostatic strategies, repeated stress may become chronic, and parasympathetic recovery may become blunted or delayed, resulting in impaired autonomic flexibility (Figure 1b). Persistent or dysregulated engagement of the stress response has been implicated in stress-related psychopathology (Kozlowska et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?RWD1kw 2015; Lanius et al., 2012) , and a chronically dysregulated baseline autonomic state may influence the experiential quality of an NOSC, as will be discussed in a later section. Conversely, effective restoration of autonomic balance following acute activation of the stress response, mediated by vagal rebound and sympathovagal co-activation, supports a healthy adaptive autonomic response. We propose that this transient restorative co-activated phase may serve as a core mechanism through which NOSCs exert therapeutic effects, as will also be explored in later sections.

Autonomic Space

If earlier theories proposed that the SNS and PNS functioned strictly in opposition (Langleyhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?inkIqY 1921) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?F2MxqM contemporary research suggests that the two branches can operate largely independently to mediate complex physiological adaptations (Berntson et al., 1991; Weissman and Mendes, 2021) , and pharmacological blockade studies indicate that considerable inter-individual variability exists in how they interact (Berntson et al., 1991 (Berntson et al., , 1994)). As such, the ANS is better understood as a two-dimensional 'autonomic space', shaped by SNS and PNS activation as complementary and largely independent factors, rather than by a single axis of (mutually exclusive) activity balance between SNS and PNS (Berntson et al., 1993).

The acute stress response demonstrates stereotyped autonomic functions in which the SNS and PNS largely act in a mutually exclusive manner. Reciprocally organized patterns have been observed across a range of cognitive and affective challenges, including social stress, mental arithmetic, and reaction-time tasks (Berntson et al., 1994; Weissman and Mendes, 2021). Howeverhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?NUEv7n consistent with the two-dimensional model of autonomic regulation (Berntson et al., 1993 (Berntson et al., , 1991)) , reciprocal SNS-PNS control represents only one sub-region of a broader autonomic space (Figure 2). However, co-activated ANS states have received comparatively little attention to date. Specifically, a smaller body of evidence has demonstrated that autonomic co-activation occurs during recovery from social stress, consistent with vagal rebound, in exercise, and in aversive classical conditioning in human and animal models (Berntson et al., 1993; Obrist et al., 1965; Weippert et al., 2013; Weissman and Mendes, 2021). Co-activation has also been associated with increased emotional contagion alongside reduced intensity of both positive and negative affect, possibly reflecting a self-regulated yet flexible and responsive emotional state (Sinai et al., 2023).

Evidence for autonomic co-inhibition, in which both branches are simultaneously withdrawn, remains particularly limited. Static apneahttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?vIlpVN or breath holding, offers one candidate case, where heart rate has been observed to decrease without a corresponding rise in beat-to-beat variability (Asmussen and Kristiansson, 1968; Kiviniemi et al., 2012) , a dissociation difficult to reconcile with straightforward vagal dominance. Notably, psychedelic-like experiences have been reported during HVB combined with extended apnea, with longer and more intense breath holds associated with greater likelihood of intense NOSCs (Lewis-Healey et al., 2024). Whether this reflects genuine co-inhibition, and whether it bears on NOSCs phenomenology, remains untested.

The two-dimensional framework of autonomic regulation illustrated in Figure 2 highlights how distinct patterns of sympathovagal engagement can give rise to qualitatively different physiological states, extending beyond simple arousal-relaxation dichotomies. We propose that such distinct patterns of ANS activation are also instrumental in giving rise to qualitatively different experiential states. Although direct evidence linking the phenomenology of NOSCs to specific autonomic configurations remains limited in contemporary neuroscience, this perspective has a long-standing history in neurophenomenological and ethnographic traditions worth examining before expounding modern empirical evidence.

The History of Autonomic Neurophenomenology

Conceptualizing NOSCs as a spectrum from active (aroused) to passive (relaxed) has a standing history in both psychophysiological and ethnographic traditions. In the 1960s, Ernst Gellhorn established a foundational framework conceptualizing the nervous system as organized around two complementary physiological systems: the ergotropic system (akin to SNS) and the trophotropic system (akin to PNS) (Gellhorn, 1967 (Gellhorn, , 1970)). Critically, Gellhorn's framework demonstrated that these systems, while typically operating in reciprocal opposition, can respond with a degree of independence to physiological challenges, tracking with Berntson's conceptualization of the autonomic space model.

Building on Gellhorn's foundation, Roland Fischer made the conceptual leap of mapping these autonomic configurations onto altered states of consciousness. In his "Cartography of the Ecstatic and Meditative States" (1971), Fischer proposes a hallucination-meditation continuum between ergotropic (i.e. sympathetic) arousal and trophotropic (i.e. parasympathetic) activation. Fischer describes how increases in ergotropic arousal track with an increase in 'hallucinatory' states, enhancing the speed of information processing, desynchronizing EEG signals, and intensifying sensory experience; while trophotropic activation supports 'meditative' states, with perceptual stability and synchronization of neural activity (Fischer, 1971). Critically, Fischer observed in this NOSCs continuum, constancy of self and corporeal awareness are linked to "levels of arousal characteristic of our daily survival routines", describing ordinary autonomic homeostasis as the basis for effective top-down regulation. As arousal shifts towards the extreme in either direction along this axis, Fischer describes the cortex as increasingly dependent on subcortical processing streams, experiencing what Fischer terms a "loss of freedom" in cortical modulation of subcortical activity. Thus, at extreme sympathetic or parasympathetic ends of the continuum, cortical and subcortical activity become "indistinguishably integrated," giving rise to experiences of unity and the dissolution of self-boundaries, similar to contemporary neurophenomenological observations pinpointing the reduction of top-down predictive processing as a hallmark of peak NOSCs (Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019; Fischer, 1971).

The observation that extreme activation of either ANS branch could precipitate a counterforce of the other, leading to a simultaneous 'eruption' of both systems was first characterized by Kiely and Gellhorn (1972). This dynamic was further developed by Laughlin and colleagues, who proposed that ritualistic "drivers", such as drumming, chanting, or flicker light stimulation, may intentionally recruit autonomic extremes to generate co-activated ANS states (Laughlin et al., 1992). These configurations are described as "hyperergotropic with trophotropic eruption", wherein enhanced sympathetic drive erupts into a sense of flow; or "hypertrophotropic with ergotropic eruption", wherein deep parasympathetic states are intensified by a release of sympathetic energy. These terms capture the idea that extreme autonomic states featuring SNS activation, PNS activation, or both, can be deliberately induced through ritual practice. The corresponding NOSCs may represent phenomenological expressions of ANS dynamics that exceed homeostatic bounds, particularly under conditions in which top-down cortical control is reduced and bottom-up interoceptive and affective signals become dominant. Further, Kiely and Gellhorn hypothesize that retuning, or rebalancing of ergotropic-trophotropic systems through ritual practice is fundamental to many traditional healing techniques, with such states serving fundamental roles in healing and transformation across cultures (Brouwer et al., 2025; Laughlin et al., 1992).

These historical frameworks linking autonomic and experiential dynamics were developed largely in the absence of the physiological measurements now available and have therefore rarely been subjected to direct empirical testing. In the next chapter, we therefore turn to contemporary empirical work on ANS dynamics during NOSCs, beginning with classical psychedelics, which offer the most systematically characterized case of autonomic engagement during NOSCs.

Psychedelics and the ANS

A substantial body of evidence indicates that classical psychedelics reliably engage the ANS, particularly during the onset and peak phases of the experience. Physiological markers of sympathetic activation, including increased heart rate and systolic blood pressure, pupil dilation, muscle tremors, and elevations in circulating adrenaline and cortisol, have been consistently reported following administration of compounds such as LSD, psilocybin, and DMT (Nichols, 2016; Passie et al., 2008 Passie et al., , 2002)). Despite the robustness of these findings, relatively few studies have directly examined how autonomic changes relate to the subjective phenomenology of psychedelic states.

One of the first modern investigations to explicitly link autonomic dynamics to psychedelic experience demonstrated that LSD administration produces a marked increase in sympathetic activity accompanied by parasympathetic withdrawal, indexed by elevated heart rate and reduced high-frequency heart rate variability (HRV; Olbrich et al., 2021). Importantly, the magnitude of sympathetic activation was positively associated with the intensity of subjective psychedelic effects. Notably, pretreatment with ketanserin, which is commonly used to block psychedelic effects via antagonism of the serotonin 2A (5-HT2A) receptor, was associated with increased parasympathetic activity. This finding suggests that ketanserin's attenuation of psychedelic effects may not be solely attributable to central 5-HT2A blockade, but may also involve peripheral parasympathetic stimulation that dampens sympathetic arousal during psychedelic onset (Olbrich et al., 2021).

Recent work has characterized autonomic dynamics of psychedelic states at higher temporal resolution, studying the fast-acting compound N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT). Following intravenous administration of DMT, a pronounced increase in sympathetic activity closely mirrored the time course of subjective intensity ratings, indicating a tight coupling between autonomic arousal and experience depth (Bonnelle et al., 2024). Further work has demonstrated that during DMT administration, sympathetic arousal indices correlate not only with subjective emotional intensity, but also predict a shift towards negative valence, including increased unpleasantness and challenging/difficult content (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?2ibgQW Amelio et al., 2026, BioRxiv). Notably, at high doses, this pattern of increased arousal and negative affective tone coexists with enhanced states of bliss, reflecting the mixed phenomenology characteristic of intense psychedelic experiences. Interestingly, the sympathetic activation following DMT administration has also been observed to be accompanied by a concurrent increase in high-frequency HRV, a marker of parasympathetic modulation (Rosas et al., 2023). Together with elevated heart rate, this pattern is consistent with sympathovagal co-activation rather than simple sympathetic dominance, which may provide an explanation for the mixed phenomenological reports.

Consistent with this, indices of sympathovagal coupling during the DMT experience correlate with subjective characteristics of mystical experiences, such as insightfulness and spiritual significance (Bonnelle et al., 2024). In parallel, DMT administration has been associated with increased heart rate entropy, reflecting greater complexity in cardiac dynamics, possibly arising from the simultaneous influence of sympathetic and parasympathetic inputs (Rosas et al., 2023, BioRxiv). Notably, increases in cardiac entropy were correlated with increases in brain entropy, a well-established neural signature of psychedelic states associated with an expanded repertoire of brain activity patterns (Atasoy et al., 2017; Tagliazucchi et al., 2014).

Given the bidirectional architecture of the ANS (see above), the coupling of cardiac and neural entropy is equally consistent with both of the roles we propose for the ANS. Cardiac complexity may simply register the central dynamics driving autonomic outflow, in which case the autonomic state accompanies the altered state and may colour how it is experienced without contributing to its emergence. Alternatively, given the predominance of ascending vagal signalling, the interoceptive stream may itself contribute to the cortical dynamics in question.

Finally, although data on post-acute autonomic effects of psychedelics remain sparse, a single case report examining autonomic function following DMT administration reported that the acute sympathetic surge was followed by a pronounced increase in HRV (as indexed by RMSSD), a marker of parasympathetic activity, on the day after the experience (Lawrence, 2022). This delayed parasympathetic rebound may reflect a stress-recovery process and offers a plausible physiological substrate for the commonly reported psychedelic "afterglow," characterized by enhanced well-being and emotional openness (Evens et al., 2023).

In parallel with autonomic effects, classical psychedelics also produce marked neuroendocrine changes consistent with an acute stress response. Early work demonstrated that LSD induces a characteristic biphasic pattern of adrenaline release, with an initial rise peaking approximately 30 minutes post-administration, followed by a transient suppression below baseline and a secondary elevation several hours later, while blood glucose levels remain unaffected (Liddell and Weil-Malherbe, 1953). More recent studies have confirmed that LSD significantly elevates plasma adrenaline, cortisol, prolactin, and oxytocin, indicating coordinated sympathoadrenal, HPA and hypothalamic neuroendocrine activation (Schmid et al., 2015). Plasma concentrations of LSD closely correlate with cortisol and corticosterone levels as well as with subjective psychotropic effects, suggesting a possible link between neuroendocrine activation and experiential intensity (Strajhar et al., 2016). Similar HPA axis activation has been observed with other psychedelics. Psilocybin acutely increases cortisol concentrationshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TGzWSp peaking approximately 80 minutes after administration (Mason et al., 2023) , while ayahuasca and 5-MeO-DMT elevate salivary cortisol levels (Galvão et al., 2018; Uthaug et al., 2020). Together, these findings indicate that psychedelic-induced NOSCs are accompanied by a robust but transient endocrine stress response.

In parallel, several studies in healthy volunteers report increased plasma concentrations of oxytocin following psychedelic administration (Holze et al., 2021; Schmid et al., 2015). Oxytocin is closely linked to parasympathetic activity, and is known to promote relaxation, social bonding, empathy, and emotional openness (Uvnäs Moberg et al., 2019). These properties have led to the hypothesis that oxytocin contributes to the enduring therapeutic effects of psychedelics by facilitating post-stress recovery and social-affective plasticity (Schindler et al., 2018). More recent theoretical work proposes that oxytocin release following an initial sympathetic surge during psychedelic experiences may support a hormetic stress response, whereby a transient physiological challenge enhances adaptive neural and behavioral pathways through improved stress recovery (Carter, 2025). The fact that psychedelics elevate both cortisol and oxytocin is well established; whether this constitutes a coordinated recovery process that contributes to long-term outcomes remains to be tested.

At a mechanistic level, the 5-HT2A receptor, the canonical receptor targeted by classical psychedelics including LSD, psilocybin, and DMT, appears central to these effects. As the primary excitatory serotonin receptor in the cortex, 5-HT2A activation recapitulates multiple features of physiological stress and may function as a stressor in its own right (Murnane, 2019). 5-HT2A receptors are expressed both centrally and peripherally, including in the gastrointestinal tract. Given that vagal afferents convey gastrointestinal signals centrally, peripheral receptor activation may contribute indirectly to interoceptive awareness and emotional salience, alongside the effects of peripheral vasoconstriction (Reed and Foldi, 2024). However, the initial sympathetic peak is likely mediated centrally, as animal studies demonstrate that 5-HT2A receptors expressed on neuroendocrine cells in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus mediate the release of stress-related hormones, including oxytocin, prolactin, ACTH, and corticosterone, in response to the 5-HT2A agonist DOI (Zhang et al., 2002). These findings provide a plausible neurobiological link between psychedelic pharmacology, autonomic activation, and coordinated neuroendocrine responses.

Taken together, the evidence reviewed here is consistent with the idea that classical psychedelics acutely engage the SNS and HPA axis; and there is preliminary evidence that this activation is accompanied, both concurrently and long-term, by PNS signalling. Such a biphasic and partly overlapping profile is consistent with the ergotropic-trophotropic dynamics proposed by Fischer, Kiely and Gellhorn (Kiely and Gellhorn, 1972; Fischer, 1971). The correlational findings reviewed here support our hypothesis that autonomic state shapes how these experiences unfold; however our proposal that autonomic engagement contributes causally to their emergence, has so far not been tested directly (see below).

High-Ventilation Breathwork

High-Ventilation Breathwork (HVB) comprises a range of practices featuring accelerated, deep and continuous breathing (i.e. a form of volitional hyperventilation) over a prolonged time (Fincham et al., 2023a). Popular modern HVB practices include Holotropic Breathwork (Grof and Grofhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?S6nyJU 2023) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TMGwtx Sudarshan Kriya Yoga (Zope and Zopehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?yzfX9c 2013) , Rebirthing Breathwork, Conscious Connected Breathwork (CCB; Havenith & Nemri, 2025) , and the Wim Hof Method® (Kox et al., 2014). Crucially, HVB has been reported to reliably produce transient alterations in perception, emotion, and cognition that strongly resemble classical psychedelic states (Fincham et al., 2026; Grof, 1988; Havenith et al., 2025; Lewis-Healey et al., 2024; Nichols, 2016; Puente., 2013; Porter et al., 2026). For instancehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4RHWQP approximately 36% of participants in a single HVB session met criteria for a "complete mystical experience" (Porter et al., 2026) , comparable to rates observed with a moderate dose of psilocybin (Griffiths et al., 2011). Similarly, scores in two classical surveys of psychedelic experiences, the 11-Dimensional Altered States of Consciousness Questionnaire (11D-ASC) and the Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ), were statistically indistinguishable between HVB and psilocybin across 11 of 15 sub-scales, with the remaining sub-scales mainly referring to alterations in visual perception (Havenith et al., 2025). Qualitative reports of HVB experiences consistently describe vivid imagery, autobiographical insight, somatic-emotional release, and experiences of unity or dissolution of self-boundaries, mirroring domains commonly observed in psychedelic states (Canello et al., 2026; Porter et al., 2026; Uthaug et al., 2022). In other words, HVB produces intense NOSCs, but achieves this purely through self-directed shifts in breathing rhythm.

In line with these findings, neurophysiological studies also highlight overlap with psychedelic brain states. HVB has been reported to increase neural entropy, signal complexity, flatten cross-cortical hierarchy, and decrease oscillatory alpha power, consistent with one of the most consistent neural signatures of classical psychedelics (Carhart-Harris et al., 2014; Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019; Godfrey et al., 2025; Lewis-Healey et al., 2024; Porter et al., 2026).

At a physiological level, the accelerated, deep breathing utilized by HVB quickly expels carbon dioxide (CO₂), reducing plasma CO₂ concentration (hypocapnia), and increasing blood and cerebrospinal fluid pH (respiratory alkalosis) (Kety and Schmidt, 1948; Schüttler et al., 2020; West, 2012). This releases noradrenaline and increases neuronal excitability, while simultaneously reducing cerebral blood flow through CO₂-dependent vasoconstriction (Bednarczyk et al., 1990; Schüttler et al., 2020). Paradoxicallyhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?q1uHEs despite increased blood oxygenation (Kox et al., 2014) , oxygen delivery to brain tissue declines due to vasoconstriction, and because CO₂ depletion impairs the release of oxygen into tissues, a phenomenon governed by the Bohr effect (West, 2012). The importance of this physiological cascade is further supported by recent findings demonstrating that reductions in end-tidal CO₂ concentration predict NOSCs depth in CCB and Holotropic Breathwork, consistent with hypocapnia as a physiological gate for the induction of NOSCs (Havenith et al., 2025). While HVB reduced cerebral blood flow by an average of 45.5%, this reduction was not associated with altered state intensity, suggesting local rather than global hypoperfusion plays a role in its phenomenological effects (Schmitz et al., 2026). This result is also consistent with results reported by Kartar et al. (2025), who found that specific reductions in CBF e.g. in insular cortex did predict NOSCs depth.

During HVB, reduced cerebral blood flow in core interoceptive regions, including the insula and anterior cingulate cortex, correlates with stronger phenomenological reports of Oceanic Boundlessness (OBN), a measure reflecting attenuated self-other boundaries (Kartar et al., 2025). The reductions in cerebral oxygenation elicited by HVB therefore trigger measurable shifts in interoceptive processing, suggesting that they may disrupt the fidelity of interoceptive signaling. Within an active-inference framework, these perturbations could be interpreted as increasing interoceptive prediction error, thereby weakening the typical weighting of bodily signals that normally constrain self-related inferences (Barrett, 2016; Barrett and Simmons, 2015). Notably, the insula and anterior cingulate are not only targets of ascending interoceptive input but also core nodes regulating autonomic outflow. Hypoperfusion here therefore affects two central hubs of bidirectional integration rather than a one-way relay, illustrating the non-hierarchical relationship between afferent and efferent autonomic signalling proposed above.

High-Ventilation Breathwork and the ANS

Given its sole reliance on conscious breathing, HVB is one of the most accessible ways to modulate one's autonomic state, since respiration is one of the only autonomic functions that can be modulated with motor control. In addition, breathing continuously impacts autonomic state: inhalation transiently withdraws the vagal parasympathetic brake while enhancing sympathetic drive, whereas exhalation promotes parasympathetic signalling; this coupling underlies respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), the cyclical increase and decrease of heart rate across the respiratory cycle (Eckberg, 2003; Fincham et al., 2023b).

In line with this, HVB appears to trigger dynamic changes in ANS functioning that share features with those evoked by psychedelics. While evidence so far remains sparse, a recent study suggests that like psychedelics, HVB may elicit a biphasic autonomic pattern, beginning with sympathetic activation and followed by a vagal rebound phase (Havenith et al., 2025). This dynamic is reflected in changing autonomic biomarkers post-session, whereby the inflammatory marker interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) increases post-session, suggesting activation of a transient inflammatory response associated with acute SNS activation. At the same time, alpha-amylase (α-amylase), a marker of ongoing SNS activity, decreased post-session. This pattern is consistent with a transition from initial sympathetic activation during HVB toward autonomic recovery following the breathing period, which may contribute to the emotional resolution, calm, and integration commonly reported after HVB sessions (Havenith et al., 2025). Interestingly, post-session increases in IL-1β were negatively correlated with three 11D-ASC subscales, possibly reflecting that greater experiential depth during HVB may have been associated with a comparatively stronger vagal rebound and hence reduced inflammatory signalling post-session (Havenith et al., 2025). On the opposite phase of this sequence, Kartar et al., (2025) showed that individuals reporting stronger experiences of Oceanic Boundlessness (OBN scores > 50%) also exhibited greater parasympathetic withdrawal at breathing onset, indexed by reduced heart-rate variability (HRV) and increased heart rate, further supporting a link between autonomic arousal and NOSCs (Figure 3a). Together, these findings are consistent with our hypothesis that robust deviations from autonomic homeostasis contribute to HVB-induced NOSCs; however the question whether this relation is causal or merely correlative has so far not been addressed.

This interpretation is further supported by insights into the cardiac dynamics during HVB. During the active breathing period of HVB, heart-rate variability (HRV) becomes highly volatile, possibly reflecting quick alterations in the balance between SNS and PNS via RSA (Canello et al., 2026 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Z8vuOa Figure 3b). We speculate that this volatility may reflect transient sympathovagal co-activation as the system moves between states, contributing to the increased cardiac complexity we suggest characterises NOSCs. Post-session, HRV increases relative to pre-session levels, a pattern consistent with PNS-driven recovery following acute physiological perturbation (Figure 3c; Canello et al., 2026). Notably, post-session HRV gains were most pronounced in individuals who reported a wider range of emotional experiences during the session, whereas peri-session HRV did not predict post-session HRV, suggesting that post-HVB autonomic effects, including rebound, may vary with the quality of the experience (Canello et al., 2026). Together, current evidence suggests that HVB induces a distinctive physiological and neurocognitive state characterized by early autonomic shifts toward sympathetic dominance followed by parasympathetic recovery, hypocapnia-driven neurometabolic shifts, attenuated interoceptive precision, dynamic cardiac modulation, and increased neural complexity. How these interacting processes contribute to profound alterations in perception, affect, and self-experience remains to be established, though the parallels with classical psychedelic phenomenology are striking. Notably, in HVB, the initial driver is respiration itself (Havenith et al., 2025), an autonomically regulated function also under voluntary control, which has a profound effect on many other autonomic functions like heart rate, blood pressure etc. (Eckberg, 2003). As such, the practice itself acts on the ANS directly, plausibly upstream of any ensuing central neurochemical and phenomenological effects. Given this, we propose HVB represents a model case of autonomically induced NOSC. Whether repeated HVB practice leads to more enduring alterations in autonomic regulation, beyond the transient effects summarized here, remains an open question.

Other forms of non-pharmacological trance

Beyond HVBhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UAoGtU various other non-pharmacological trance states, such as hypnotic and shamanic trance, have been documented, all of which share substantial phenomenological and psychological overlap with other high-arousal NOSCs (Oswald et al., 2023) , such as alterations in spatiotemporal perception, increased experiential absorption, modification in body sensation, change in the sense of self-agency and ownership, complex visual imagery, intense emotions, out-of-body experiences, as well as mystical experiences including feelings of unity and ego dissolution (Flor-Henry et al., 2017; Gosseries et al., 2024; Grégoire et al., 2024).

Auto-induced cognitive trance (AICT) is a modern adaptation of traditional Mongolian shamanic trance, stripped of ritual and spiritual elements (Grégoire et al., 2024). Similar to shamanic trance, AICT features a clear yet narrowed perception of external surroundings, an intense flow experience, enhanced inner imagery, altered somatosensory processing, shifts in self-awareness, subjective changes in time and space perception, modified body awareness, and altered cognitive and emotional states, along with sensory disengagement (Oswald et al., 2023). This trance is learned through standardized training, initially using specific sound loops and later personalized movements or vocalizations. AICT is associated with reduced parasympathetic and increased sympathetic activity (Oswald et al., 2023). Crucially, the observation that individuals with the deepest trance states showed less parasympathetic withdrawal, is in line with our hypothesis that a dual engagement of SNS and PNS may contribute to the phenomenology of NOSCs (Oswald et al., 2025).

With respect to religious worship experiences, higher sympathetic activity has also been observed in relation to the felt presence of God (Walter and Altorfer, 2025). Interestingly, depth of religious experience correlated negatively with the Baevsky Stress Index, an index of sympathetic dominance. This pattern again implies co-occurring parasympathetic engagement, consistent with our proposal that sympathovagal co-activation is recruited during peak NOSCs.

Pivotal Mental States

A useful general framework for situating NOSCs within stress physiology is the concept of Pivotal Mental States (PiMS). PiMS are defined as transient, hyperplastic states that enable rapid learning and psychological transformation, typically emerging during periods of heightened uncertainty or challenge (Faye et al., 2018; Brouwer and Carhart-Harris, 2021). Within this framework, transformative experiences are proposed to arise when entrenched predictive models are destabilized, allowing for increased sensitivity to new information and subsequent adaptive updating. Importantly, PiMS are not specific to pharmacological interventions, but can be induced via diverse pathways, including serotonergic agonism, intense emotional experiences, or self-generated physiological stressors (Brouwer and Carhart-Harris, 2021). What unifies these states is the coordinated recruitment of sympathetic arousal-related neuromodulatory and metabolic systems that temporarily shift the brain into a flexible, learning-optimized regime. In this sense, we propose that PiMS provide a mechanistic bridge between the phenomenology of NOSCs and their underlying autonomic substrates, framing transformation as a process of destabilization followed by reconsolidation rather than as a singular or static alteration of consciousness. This perspective motivates closer examination of how autonomic and stress-related processes interact with neural dynamics during NOSCs, which we address in the following section, focusing on psychedelic states.

ANS contributions to the neural signatures of psychedelic states

Based on the PiMS framework, we can hypothesize how ANS dynamics may contribute to the destabilization and reconsolidation processes at the heart of PiMS. At the neural level, one of the most robust signatures of psychedelic states is a reduction in alpha-frequency (8-12 Hz) oscillatory activity (Carhart-Harris et al., 2016; Muthukumaraswamy et al., 2013). Alpha rhythms, generated through recurrent thalamo-cortical and cortico-cortical loops, function as a dynamic sensory gate, rhythmically inhibiting cortical excitability and supporting top-down predictive control, attentional allocation, and the stabilization of internal models (Jensen and Mazaheri, 2010; Klimesch et al., 2007; Mayer et al., 2016). Elevated alpha power correlates positively with default mode network (DMN) integrity and internally oriented, self-referential cognition (Knyazev et al., 2011; Mantini et al., 2007).

Acute stress responses triggered by salient, unexpected, novel, or noxious events, have been observed to induce a transient reorganization of information flow within the brain, characterized by a shift from top-down predictive processing toward bottom-up sensory sampling (Bouret and Sara, 2005; Hermans et al., 2014 Hermans et al., , 2011)). This shift is thought to be mediated by increased engagement of the salience network and enhanced locus coeruleus-noradrenergic signaling, which desynchronizes alpha oscillations and increases neural gain (Ehrhart et al., 2000; Sridharan et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2019). Within predictive-processing frameworks, such states reflect elevated uncertainty requiring rapid model updating, consistent with both the Free Energy Principle and the REBUS model of psychedelic network reorganization, wherein reduced precision-weighting of high-level priors permits bottom-up signals to exert greater influence over perception and cognition (Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019; Peters et al., 2017). This sequence also corresponds to Fischer's account of arousal-driven desynchronization and loss of cortical independence from subcortical processing, now described in terms of precision-weighting rather than cortical "freedom."

Alpha suppression can also be mediated by acetylcholine, which selectively enhances the precision of sensory representations rather than broadly amplifying uncertainty sensitivity as noradrenaline does (Aston-Jones et al., 1999; Bauer et al., 2012; Moran et al., 2013; Osipova et al., 2003). PNS stimulation has been shown to reduce and desynchronize alpha activity (Lewine et al., 2019), and parasympathetic rebound has been associated with cholinergic dominance (Collins et al., 2021; Martin et al., 2024), though the relationship between HRV and alpha power is context-dependent (Attar et al., 2021). We propose a dynamic sequence in which an initial noradrenergic phase destabilizes existing predictive models by lowering alpha-mediated sensory gating, followed by a parasympathetically biased, cholinergic phase that facilitates perceptual stabilization, learning, and consolidation. On this account, autonomic co-activation could support both the expansion of conscious content and the integration of newly acquired information into enduring cognitive and emotional models.

Beyond neuromodulation, sympathetic arousal also induces marked shifts in cerebral energy metabolism. Adrenergic signaling promotes a transition toward aerobic glycolysis, particularly in astrocytes, supporting rapid ATP production for high-demand neural excitability, high-entropy neural states, and neuroplasticity (Dienel and Cruz, 2016; Luppi et al., 2024).

By recruiting the sympathetic nervous system and its associated neuromodulatory and metabolic cascades, psychedelic-induced NOSCs and other PiMS may thus force the brain into a high-energy, high-entropy regime. This regime, characterized by reduced top-down constraint, increased bottom-up influence, and altered weighting of priors, may underlie the frequently reported sense that psychedelic experiences feel unusually vivid or "more real than real," reflecting a temporary reorganization of how information is selected, weighted, and integrated into conscious experience.

Importantly, PiMS are outcome-agnostic: the destabilization of entrenched predictive models can resolve toward either wellbeing or pathology, depending on contextual factors and on the capacity to consolidate and integrate the experience within an existing interpretive framework (Brouwer and Carhart-Harris, 2021). This context-dependence is reflected in ethnographic accounts of ritual traditions, in which NOSCs are deliberately induced through both directions of homeostatic extremity, whether via sensory and physiological amplification, as in drumming, chanting, or high-intensity movement, or via deprivation, as in fasting, darkness, isolation, or sleep restriction (Laughlin et al., 1992; Ludwig, 1966). The bidirectionality of these induction methods is consistent with Fischer's autonomic framework, wherein departures from homeostatic range in either the ergotropic or trophotropic direction may support the emergence of NOSCs. Such traditions typically embed the destabilizing event within structured practices of preparation and integration, suggesting that the contextual scaffolding surrounding a PiMS may be as consequential for its outcome as the physiological perturbation itself. This may help explain why not every activation of the stress response reliably induces NOSCs. Acute physiological challenges such as cold-water immersion or laboratory social stress produce comparable sympathetic and HPA activation followed by parasympathetic rebound, yet have not been observed to occasion NOSCs (Schwabe and Schachinger, 2008; Weissman and Mendes, 2021). Such stress engages stereotyped defensive responses, oriented toward removing a threat and restoring homeostasis (see Kozlowska et al., 2015). We propose that ritual and pharmacological induction, by contrast, produce autonomic activation in the absence of an external threat, offering no defensive action to take and instead directing attention inward toward the destabilization itself. This may permit autonomic engagement to unfold outside the hierarchical constraints of stereotyped behavioral responses to stress, occupying configurations that would otherwise be transient or defensively organized. Similarly, exercise produces substantial sympathetic and endocrine activation and does not commonly induce NOSCs, but this arousal is largely bound to serving an ongoing metabolic demand and restoring homeostasis. Interestingly howeverhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4JjE3H practices involving extreme endurance exercise appear among techniques historically used to induce NOSCs (Rhodes, 1987) , consistent with Fischer's proposal that extreme arousal beyond the bounds of ordinary homeostatic function destabilizes the constancies through which experience is normally organized, irrespective of what generated it. These effects may be primarily contextual, or may reflect differential engagement of the serotonergic system, proposed to gate PiMS entry.

The role of 5-HT2A in PiMS

Central to the PiMS framework is the proposal that these states are mediated by a specific molecular gateway: increased signalling at the 5-HT2A receptor (Brouwer and Carhart-Harris, 2021). Criticallyhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?jzeviy this gateway is held to be accessible without exogenous agonism, as chronic stress upregulates 5-HT2A binding and expression (Brouwer and Carhart-Harris, 2021; Murnane, 2019; Takao et al., 1995) , priming the system, while acute stress triggers the 5-HT release capable of activating it (Amat et al., 1998). The relationship between stress and 5-HT2A signalling is proposed to be non-linear: below a threshold of severity or chronicity, stress engages ordinary regulatory responses, whereas beyond it the system shifts abruptly into the hyperplastic mode characteristic of a PiMS. Physiological challenges implicated in this upregulation include hypoxia, fasting, sleep deprivation, inflammation, and pain, alongside the cognitive and social stressors more commonly studied (Brouwer and Carhart-Harris, 2021). Of particular relevance to breathwork-induced NOSCshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?iHtY1U brainstem and midbrain serotonergic neurons have been proposed to act as chemoreceptors sensitive to extracellular pH (Richerson, 2004; Severson et al., 2003) , offering a candidate route by which the respiratory alkalosis and hypocapnia characteristic of HVB might engage this system directly. On this account, the diverse induction methods reviewed here, whether pharmacological, respiratory, or ritual, may converge on a common serotonergic mechanism, with intentional autonomic engagement representing one route by which that mechanism can be reached.

The relationship between stress and 5-HT2A signalling appears bidirectional. Just as stressors of sufficient intensity engage the receptor system, 5-HT2A agonism itself recapitulates multiple features of physiological stress and may function as a stressor in its own right (Murnane, 2019). On this view, the serotonin receptor system is not merely responsive to challenge but constitutes part of a stress-response architecture serving adaptability. Consistent with this, a bipartite model of serotonergic modulation proposes that 5-HT2A mediates active coping strategies, engaging adaptive neuroplasticity to proactively address a source of stress, while 5-HT1A, whose signalling is enhanced by SSRIs, mediates passive coping, or tolerance of a stressor that cannot be resolved (Carhart-Harris and Nutt, 2017). Whether autonomic engagement contributes causally to this process, or shapes the quality of the resulting experience, remains an open question.

Centering the ANS as a therapeutic mediator

Can the understanding of psychedelic experiences as an expression of the stress response help predict readiness to undergo such experiences? Sport and exercise science offers a partial analogy: optimal physiological states, indexed through measures of SNS-PNS balance, predict enhanced athletic performance and recovery (Shafeek and Tk, 2024). We hypothesize that a similar logic may apply to NOSCs, such that entering an altered state from a position of autonomic balance promotes both an effective deployment of the sympathetic response and subsequent parasympathetic recovery (Figure 4).

In keeping with this notionhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?vuojh4 it has been proposed that lower baseline vagal tone and high SNS activity may index greater preparedness to engage a sympathetic response (Matsumura et al., 2021) , whereas higher baseline vagal tone may reflect a propensity to evaluate safety in the environment (Thayer et al., 2009). On the other hand, high sympathetic activity has been associated with vulnerability to stress and impaired emotional regulation, reflecting less effective PNS-mediated post-stress recovery mechanisms (Luecken and Appelhans, 2006; Pinna and Edwards, 2020). Individuals with a strong baseline vagal tone may therefore experience less intense psychedelic journeys, whereas those with a high sympathetic tone, while more likely to undergo intense experiences, might experience less parasympathetic recovery, and may therefore fail to reach the beneficial state of SNS-PNS co-activation previously found to be associated with spiritual/insightful experiences (Bonnelle et al., 2024). This may be particularly relevant for individuals whose baseline autonomic state reflects chronic stress or dysregulated recovery, a consideration with implications for screening and preparation in clinical settings.

Although more work is needed to confirm this hypothesis, one study found that participants with greater SNS-PNS balance at baseline reported higher spiritual experience ratings after being administered DMT, and that this relationship was mediated by higher SNS-PNS co-activation during the DMT experience (Bonnelle et al., 2024). SNS-PNS balance may therefore provide a physiological marker of 'readiness' to undergo a deeper and more transformative psychedelic experience, characterized by strong SNS activation and subsequent vagal rebound. Cultivating such balance before initiating a NOSChttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?qIWwOT for instance via a contemplative practice (Crosswell et al., 2024) , may therefore contribute to enhancing the subjective quality of the NOSCs, and increase the likelihood of a so-called 'mystical experience', a notion that is in line with suggestions that a cultivation of optimal psychological and physiological states can improve safety and efficacy of NOSCs (Timmermann et al., 2023). These observations warrant further research into the potential to develop markers of 'readiness for acute alterations of consciousness' based on the state of the ANS (as well as other psychological factors), which could be used as predictors of peak experiences, and thereby of positive outcomes in psychedelic-assisted therapy and other NOSCs-based therapies (Fincham et al., 2023a; McGeown et al., 2009; Roseman et al., 2018; Yaden et al., 2021). Should these associations be confirmed, ECG-derived measures of cardiac autonomic dynamics may offer accessible biomarkers to inform the preparation and facilitation of therapeutic NOSCs, and ultimately to improve their efficacy.

Distinguishing the means from the end

Although the focus of this review is on high-arousal NOSCs, high arousal is not a necessary condition for transcendent experiences. Instead extreme deviations from homeostatic norms in the sympathetic (ergotropic) or parasympathetic (trophotropic) dimensions can be seen as mediators of expanded consciousness. Therefore it may be possible to broaden the framework for high-arousal NOSCs introduced here to encompass other forms of self-transcendent experiences that do not involve equally drastic sympathetic arousal, such as those cultivated through contemplative practices. Some forms of 'samadhi' experiences, hypnotic or dream-like trance states are associated with autonomic quiescence, general reduction of brain activity, decreased heart rate, parasympathetic dominance, and slow-wave EEG activity (Gingras et al., 2014; Winkelman, 1986). This is consistent with our proposal that NOSCs can occur in multiple sub-regions of autonomic space, whether through ergotropic or trophotropic extremes, or the co-activated autonomic states at their boundaries.

Recent evidence indicates that sympatho-vagal engagementhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BQSuMM captured by a metric of HRV amplitude proposed to index the dynamic interplay between PNS and SNS activity, can track the degree of ego reduction reported by participants during a highly immersive VR-guided meditation (Bonnelle et al., 2024) , previously shown to elicit self-transcendent experiences (Bonnelle et al., 2024; Glowacki et al., 2022; Parola et al., 2026; Vidal et al., 2024). Notably, this relationship was absent during standard guided meditation (non-VR), which yielded lower ego-reduction scores. This suggests that the correlation between autonomic dynamics and ego reduction may only emerge beyond a certain threshold of self-transcendence or absorption, potentially reflecting a state of heightened alignment between autonomic and central nervous systems.

Throughout this review, we have proposed two claims regarding the role of the ANS in NOSCs. Our first claim, more directly supported by current empirical evidence, is that autonomic state functions as a contextual variable shaping the phenomenological and affective quality of NOSCs. Classical work by Schachter and Singer (1962), demonstrated that sympathetic arousal induced via epinephrine injection was interpreted very differently depending on environmental and social context, echoing work on the sensitivity to extra-pharmacological variables, the 'set and setting', in the phenomenological and affective quality of NOSCs experiences (Hartogsohn, 2016 (Hartogsohn, , 2017)). As such, during NOSCs, the state of the ANS may function as a "body set", a somatic substrate through which consciousness is experienced, filtered, and emotionally integrated. A sympathetically dominant state may amplify intensity and vividness; a co-activated state may facilitate regulated arousal or blissfulness. Recent work emphasizes the importance of body set for psychedelic experience and beyond (Uthaug et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?uukCVJ 2025) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?OsqaSa and empirical evidence supports this mechanism: sympathetic arousal during psychedelic experiences correlates with experiential intensity and valence (D'Amelio et al., 2026) , and sympathovagal co-activation predicts the depth of spiritual/mystical experiences (Bonnelle et al., 2024).

The second, more speculative claim is that the ANS may play a causal role in NOSCs emergence and deepening. Building on the PiMS framework, we propose one plausible mechanism: sympathetic activation may destabilize entrenched predictive models, while the subsequent, partly overlapping parasympathetic engagement may support the reconsolidation processes that enable psychological transformation and adaptive reorganization of neural activity. This mechanism appears to be relevant to conditions of parasympathetic rebound following sympathetic activation, which can operate across diverse induction pathways, pharmacological 5-HT2A agonism, acute stress, and potentially direct autonomic modulation through intensive HVB. However, direct mechanistic evidence for ANS-driven NOSCs induction is largely limited to HVB, where respiratory manipulation engages the autonomic system directly. While the phenomenological similarities between HVB-induced and psychedelic-induced NOSCs are suggestive, considerably more research is needed to establish whether autonomic stimulation itself serves as a causal mechanism for NOSCs induction in other cases.

A third possibility warrants consideration: autonomic changes may merely reflect downstream effects of CNS-driven processes rather than contributors to them. If 5-HT2A agonism constitutes the molecular gateway to PiMS, and if certain forms of stress can engage that gateway endogenously, then the autonomic activation accompanying NOSCs may be a parallel consequence of the same process rather than a contributor to it. Under this interpretation, the correlations reviewed here would be expected even if autonomic activity were entirely epiphenomenal. The evidence currently available does not distinguish between these accounts; blocking 5-HT2A receptors prior to LSD attenuates SNS engagement and enhances PNS tone, consistent with autonomic involvement, but equally consistent with common upstream causation (Olbrich et al., 2021).

To further disentangle these effects, pharmacological blockade studies could selectively attenuate autonomic signalling while leaving central 5-HT2A transmission intact. Such designs would replicate the logic of the classical blockade work underpinning the autonomic space modelhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?sYJikQ in which muscarinic and β-adrenergic antagonists were used to isolate the independent contributions of each branch (Berntson et al., 1991; Jose and Taylor, 1969) , but with subjective experience as the outcome measure. Peripheral β-adrenergic blockade would test whether attenuating sympathetic signalling alters the intensity or valence of the experience, while peripherally restricted muscarinic blockade with glycopyrrolate, would test the contribution of vagal afferent signalling without introducing central anticholinergic confounds. No such study has yet been conducted, and we therefore offer this framework as hypothesis-generating rather than established.

We argue, however, that framing this question as a choice between cause and consequence may itself be misleading. As outlined at the outset, the decisive predominance of ascending vagal fibres implies that the ANS is not primarily an effector enacting central commands, but a major source of the interoceptive signalling that shapes cortical dynamics, which then in turn regulate autonomic outflow. In such a mutually coupled system, cause and effect are not cleanly separable. Asking whether the ANS drives or reflects NOSCs may therefore be less productive than asking how autonomic and central dynamics co-evolve across the course of different NOSCs. This review is offered in that spirit: not as a claim that consciousness is autonomically determined, but as an argument that the ANS belongs in accounts of consciousness as an active contributor rather than as physiological background.

Conclusion

In this review, we propose a unifying framework in which NOSCs, whether induced via classical psychedelics or nonpharmacological practices, leverage the autonomic arousal cascade to support a transient expansion of consciousness and promote adaptive neurophysiological and psychological long-term outcomes. In this framework, high-arousal NOSCs can be seen as a form of controlled autonomic activation, generating a surge of metabolic energy that allows consciousness to move beyond the constraints of its default mode. Central to this process is an initial sympathetic response followed by parasympathetic rebound, enabling a surge of neuroendocrine signalling to support adaptive plasticity. In addition, the global physiological disruption caused by high-arousal NOSCs is proposed to serve as an 'autonomic workout', providing a beneficial challenge that enhances resilience to future emotional, cognitive, and biological stressors (hormesis, see Faye et al., 2018).

Finally, resting baseline autonomic tone may serve as a measurable biomarker of readiness for NOSCs, offering predictive insight into the intensity, quality, and adaptive potential of these transformative experiences. As such, real-time tracking of autonomic dynamics via ECG may allow facilitators to guide participants towards optimal autonomic balance before and during therapeutic NOSCs. By integrating autonomic dynamics and stress physiology, this framework provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how NOSCs can catalyze psychological flexibility, emotional regulation, and long-term resilience, complementing the CNS-focused perspectives that have so far often dominated research into NOSC.
