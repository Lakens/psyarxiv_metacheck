Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

This randomized experimental study evaluated a web-based debiasing intervention designed to improve clinical judgment in the assessment of adult major depressive disorder (MDD) in Brazil. The intervention, adapted from prior work on pediatric bipolar disorder, taught Bayesian probability estimates and other debiasing strategies. Brazilian psychologists (N = 535) were assigned to either the intervention or an active control condition featuring a brief educational video, and were assessed post-intervention (n = 299). Outcomes were decisionmaking errors and diagnostic accuracy in response to vignettes, which depicted diagnostic challenges that could elicit cognitive biases. Assignment to intervention was associated with significantly fewer decision-making errors and greater diagnostic accuracy. Intervention effects were stronger for the vignette involving diagnostic risk estimation, as supported by intervention-by-vignette interaction effects. Intervention effects across vignettes were not statistically significant when only categorical diagnostic tasks were considered. Years of clinical practice and postgraduate training were largely unrelated to outcomes, and decisionmaking errors were not associated with treatment decisions. Overall, findings provide preliminary evidence of the transferability of this brief, web-based debiasing intervention to a distinct clinical and cultural context, although effect sizes were substantially attenuated compared with those observed in the original intervention.

Effects of a Web-Based Debiasing Intervention on Brazilian Psychologists' Accuracy on

Major Depressive Disorder Diagnostic Tasks: A Randomized Training Intervention

Accurate diagnosis is key for mental healthcare, as it directly shapes treatment decision-making, professional communication, and resource allocation within systems of care (Clark et al., 2017; Hunsley and Mash, 2020). However, diagnostic classification relies heavily on clinical judgment, which is susceptible to errors and biases (Garb et al., 2025), contributing to poor diagnostic reliability. For major depressive disorder (MDD), DSM-5 field trials reported a test-retest reliability of k = .25, classified as "questionable" (Regier et al., 2013). Although estimated reliabilities were substantially higher in ICD-11 trials (Reed et al., 2018; k = .64-.74), the use of a joint-rater design limits comparability. Joint-rater design likely overestimates real-world interrater agreement because ratings are not fully independent: clinicians observe each other's questioning during the interview, which reduces natural variability in diagnostic decision-making. In vignette-based studies (using ICD-11 guidelines), about 20% of cases with MDD failed to be diagnosed as such by clinicians (Kogan et al., 2021; Hualparuca-Olivera et al., 2025). Improving the accuracy of MDD diagnosis is therefore a global mental health priority, as depression is among the leading causes of years lived with disability worldwide (GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2022).

One possible source of diagnostic unreliability is the fallible nature of human judgment in general. Cognitive biases that affect judgment in numerous fields are also common among clinicians, and affect diagnostic thought processes in predictable ways (Croskerry, 2003; Garb et al., 2025). Representativeness bias is a common one, where raters focus on perceived similarity of a particular case to the subjective representation of a category (Kahneman and Frederick, 2002). This often happens in diagnostic reasoning, leading clinicians to judge the probability of someone having a disorder based on how well they fit the prototype of a patient with that disorder (Garb, 1996). In depressed patients, the limitation of this heuristic is evident: an intuitive prototype about major depressive disorder may be that a patient with that condition looks depressed, but patients with bipolar disorder (among others) will often look depressed too, despite warranting a different diagnosis (Angst, 2011). Race biases also may affect clinicians' prototypes (Moskowitz et al., 2012), potentially leading to differences in assessment, even when the same clinical picture is present. For example, in clinical pictures with mixed mood and psychotic symptoms, some evidence suggests that schizophrenia may be overdiagnosed in Black patients, while affective disorders are underdiagnosed (Gara et al., 2019). Cultural forms of symptom expression may also play a role, as clinicians may be less likely to recognize presentations associated with a specific group (e.g., irritability as a symptom of depression for Black people in the US; Payne, 2012). In anchoring bias, information previously presented may also affect people's judgment, whether or not that information is relevant (Chapman and Johnson, 2002). As a result, clinicians may overemphasize the importance of the information that is first presented about a patient, giving less attention to subsequent (and possibly more relevant) information (Friedlander and Stockman, 1983). Other kinds of biases may lead clinicians to prematurely terminate diagnostic assessment. Those include search satisficing bias, in which people tend to stop the search for alternative solutions once a "good enough" one is found (Howard, 2019), and overconfidence bias, in which clinicians simply overestimate the accuracy of their own judgments (Berthet, 2022).

Based on this body of research, interventions to counter cognitive biases and improve decision-making can be developed. These debiasing interventions have shown promising results with health care professionals (Lambe et al., 2016; Ludolph and Schulz, 2018). For example, Myung et al. (2013) developed an intelligent tutoring system in which pathology and dermatology residents can have their diagnostic thought process compared to an "expert model." The use of this system has led to an improvement in the accuracy of resident's diagnostic reasoning, which is attributed to the opportunity of reflecting upon their own thought processes by comparing them to the expert model. In another study, medical students allocated in a debiasing condition (awareness training and use of a checklist) performed better in diagnostic and case management decisions (assessed through clinical vignettes), when compared to others who received training focused on task content (Chew et al., 2016).

Although these results are promising, little research has been done on debiasing interventions with mental health professionals. One exception, in the context of pediatric bipolar disorder, is a web-based video debiasing intervention developed by (Jenkins and Youngstrom, 2016), which was shown to improve mental health professionals' accuracy in diagnostic assessments. It combined several components to improve clinicians' decision making process, including (a) developing insight and awareness about cognitive biases, (b) providing information regarding mood disorders and their diagnostic criteria to decrease reliance on memory, (c) actively considering alternatives and other cognitive forcing strategies, (d) using Bayesian probability estimates based on likelihood ratios and (e) providing opportunities for metacognition and simulation of decision processes. When tested among clinicians working in the US and Canada, the intervention successfully improved accuracy in assessing clinical vignettes, both overall and in each of the presented clinical scenarios (Jenkins and Youngstrom, 2016).

The present study extends previous work by Jenkins and Youngstrom (2016) by testing the transferability of that intervention to new contexts: from pediatric bipolar disorder to the related diagnostic domain of adult MDD, and from United States and Canada to the new cultural and linguistic context of Brazil (see Supplemental Material for details on the adapted intervention). The adapted intervention retained the core components of the original intervention (except for decreasing reliance on memory), and incorporated examples and applications tailored to adult MDD. The present study aimed to test the efficacy of this adapted intervention in improving clinical judgment. Outcomes were measured using case vignettes constructed to be susceptible to specific cognitive biases, and included clinicians' diagnostic accuracy and decision-making errors across vignettes (primary outcomes) and for each individual vignette (secondary outcomes). Additionally, as in the original study, we investigated whether decision-making errors were associated with differences in clinicians' preferred next clinical action for the hypothetical cases (other secondary outcomes). Our hypotheses were as follows:

Hypothesis 1: Participants in the intervention condition would demonstrate higher diagnostic accuracy and fewer decision-making errors across vignettes (primary outcomes).

Hypothesis 2: Participants in the intervention condition would demonstrate higher diagnostic accuracy and fewer decision-making errors within each individual vignette (secondary outcomes).

Hypothesis 3: Decision-making errors would be associated with differences in clinicians' suggested next clinical action within each individual vignette (secondary outcomes).

Materials and Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited through announcements on social media targeting Brazilian psychologists between April and September 2024. A total of 1,096 individuals accessed the study and reported having an active professional practice in psychotherapy or psychological assessment during the data collection period, meeting eligibility criteria. Among these, 299 provided outcome data to be included in the main analyses

Case Vignettes

Five case vignettes modeled diagnostic decision-making tasks in this study. The vignettes were based on published case studies, selected to cover different cognitive biases associated with diagnostic decision-making. Vignettes were previously rated by three experts to confirm that case descriptions were clear, resembled real-world patients and were congruent with the proposed main diagnostic hypotheses. Experts were all licensed psychologists in Brazil, with at least 5 years of clinical practice and a Master's or Doctoral degree in psychology. Vignettes were accompanied by images of the respective hypothetical patients, borrowed either from the 10k US Adult Faces Database (in the case of vignette 0; Bainbridge et al., 2013) or faces with emotionally neutral expressions from the Brazilian Facial Expressions Database (for the remaining vignettes; Rodrigues, 2015). Supplemental Table 1 presents a sample vignette (Vignette 1, which is also briefly described in the corresponding section of this manuscript).

Vignette 0 (practice exercise).

Based on Friedman (2014), this vignette was used for the practice exercise. It described a depressed patient whose episode was prompted by the loss of a first-degree family member who was currently depressed. Participants were also given a PHQ-9 test score for the patient, indicating a positive screening result for MDE/MDD, and asked to estimate the probability that such a patient had MDD. After recording their estimate, participants were given feedback regarding their accuracy when compared to a Bayesian estimate that took into account prevalence, family history of MDD and PHQ-9 results (as detailed for Vignette 2).

Vignette 1 (considering depression with psychotic features).

This vignette was based on Carter et al. (2022) and Rothschild (2014). It depicted a patient with a guilt-related delusion that started inside a depressive episode. Participants were also given information pointing to the absence of previous psychotic symptoms or recent substance use. For this vignette, fully accurate responses were to consider both MDD (or other specified depressive disorder [OSDD]) and bipolar disorder (I, II or other specified) among their main diagnostic hypotheses. Responses that included only one of these diagnoses were considered to be partially-accurate (because the available information was not sufficient to have decided between these possibilities). Psychotic symptoms are presented first in the vignette's text, theoretically making the assessors particularly prone to anchoring bias (by being prompted to anchor in a schizophrenia-spectrum diagnostic hypothesis).

Vignette 2 (risk estimates). Vignette 2 (based on case 43 in Prendergast and

Jackman, 2019) presented a young woman whose depressive symptoms started after a particularly stressful period in her job. Participants were told that she has had positive test results in the PHQ-9, and no family history of any mental disorder. Then they were asked to estimate the risk that Camila was suffering from major depressive disorder, considering the hypothetical setting had a 20% MDD prevalence. As in the practice exercise, accuracy of participants' estimates was assessed in comparison to a model-based Bayesian estimate, using likelihood ratios, 20% prevalence, negative family risk and positive PHQ-9 results (in this case, 23%, see the Supplemental Material). A prevalence of 20% is consistent with that reported in outpatient clinical settings in Brazil (e.g., Paiva et al., 2019; Camargos et al., 2024). PHQ-9 likelihood ratios were estimated from extant data on the PHQ-9 diagnostic accuracy in clinical settings (Inoue et al., 2012; Shin et al., 2019) and observational studies on family risk for MDD (Smith and Bielecky, 2012; Prokofyeva et al., 2013; Saha et al., 2013). Bayesian post-test probabilities were calculated according to the standard procedure employed in Evidence-Based Medicine (Straus et al., 2019) and Evidence Based Assessment (Youngstrom et al., 2018). According to these calculations (presented in the Supplemental Material), responses of 23% were considered accurate, and those that fell within a ± 5% interval from that estimate were considered partially accurate (Jenkins and Youngstrom, 2016). Because the patient has had positive results in the PHQ-9, we theorized that assessments of this vignette were vulnerable to overconfidence bias.

Vignette 3 (detecting comorbid conditions).

Based on Brem et al. (2020), vignette 3 presented a woman experiencing significant anxiety (worry, rumination and somatic symptoms) and depressive symptoms (hopelessness, suicidal ideation). Responses that included Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and MDD (or OSDD) as diagnostic hypotheses were considered fully accurate, and those that considered only one of those were considered partially-accurate. Because GAD is commonly comorbid with MDD, but GAD symptoms were more clearly present, we conceptualized that search-satisficing bias could lead participants to ignore MDD as a diagnostic hypothesis in this case.

Vignette 4 (considering history of mania). This vignette, adapted from (Oquendo, 2014), described a male higher education student presenting with severe depressive symptoms (anhedonia, suicidal ideation), alongside a prior period characterized by increased productivity and reduced need for sleep. Responses were coded as accurate when they considered both MDD (or OSDD) and bipolar disorder (I, II or other specified). Because the clinical presentation primarily reflected a depressive episode, the vignette was conceptualized to elicit reliance on the representativeness heuristic, thereby increasing the likelihood that participants would overlook a bipolar disorder hypothesis.

Measurement

Demographic and background information. Clinicians provided information on demographic variables including their gender, race, age and educational degree. They were also asked about their professional background, including theoretical orientation, caseload, and experience as a clinician.

Diagnostic accuracy.

For vignettes 1, 3 and 4, participants selected their most probable diagnostic hypotheses for the case at hand from a list of 19 possible diagnoses (plus an "other, please specify" option). The question explicitly asked participants to also include those diagnoses they would consider as comorbid, or that would need further assessment.

Participants could choose between one and three hypotheses for each vignette. Correctness of diagnostic hypotheses was coded according to the previously mentioned criteria (see Case Vignettes section), as1 = "accurate",2 = "partially-accurate", or3 = "fully accurate".

Risk estimate accuracy.

For vignette 2, clinicians gave an estimate of the probability that the patient had MDD (0 to 100%), given the settings' clinical prevalence (20%), her PHQ-9 and family history information. As previously mentioned, participants' responses were coded as an ordinal variable defining the accuracy of their estimate when compared to a ±5% interval around a model-based Bayesian probability estimate, using likelihood ratios calculated from published research papers and assuming a 20% clinical prevalence of MDD.

We also calculated a continuous variable corresponding to clinicians' distance from that Bayesian estimate. Our original model-based Bayesian estimate used likelihood ratios calculated from studies that assessed the accuracy of the PHQ-9 in specialized care settings (see "Risk Estimates Calculation", in Supplemental Material), reflecting the usual level of care for psychologists in Brazil. Alternatively, we also calculated likelihood ratios from metaanalytic data including studies across different settings (Negeri et al., 2021), and employed them in sensitivity analyses.

Decision-making error status. For diagnostic hypotheses, "inaccurate" and "somewhat accurate" diagnoses were coded as committing a decision-making error. For risk estimates, as in the original intervention (Jenkins and Youngstrom, 2016), only responses that fell outside the ±5% interval around the Bayesian risk estimate were counted as decisionmaking errors. In sensitivity analyses, we also explored the robustness of results when such an interval was broadened to ±15%.

Overall diagnostic accuracy and decision-making errors.

To represent participants' overall ability to identify MDD-relevant diagnostic possibilities under uncertainty, consistently with the ordinal intervention study (Jenkins & Youngstrom, 2016), composite scores for diagnostic accuracy were computed by summing diagnostic accuracy and risk estimate accuracy across vignettes for each participant. Overall decision-making errors were computed in the same way for the respective variable. To examine robustness across types of tasks, an alternative computation of the composites, considering only categorical hypothesis selection (vignettes 1, 3 and 4) and excluding risk estimation (vignette 2), was also calculated and reported in sensitivity analyses.

Next clinical actions.

For each vignette, participants chose one or two next clinical actions they would prioritize in the clinical care for the hypothetical patient depicted in it.

Options included "refer to psychiatric consultation", "extend and/or deepen assessment" and "start or refer to psychotherapy". Participants were also allowed to include different options through an "other, please specify" alternative.

Video evaluation and self-evaluation. After completing the vignettes, participants were asked to give their opinion regarding different aspects of the video they watched, which was different based on whether they were randomized to intervention or the control condition. Participants used a 5-point likert scale (1 = "Totally disagree" to5 = "Totally agree") to indicate if they considered the video to be interesting, useful, easy to understand and pleasant to watch. Participants were also invited to self-evaluate their understanding of specific content areas covered by the videos (e.g., mood disorders and MDD; see other contents described below).

Procedure

The data collection for this study was based on SurveyMonkey (http://surveymonkey.com), with the intervention videos hosted on Vimeo (https://vimeo.com/). Participants could enter the study through social media posts or directed ads. After digitally signing informed consent forms, participants started with the baseline survey, and then SurveyMonkey's built-in randomization feature assigned them to the control or intervention condition, with an intended 1:1 allocation ratio. The intervention condition consisted of a 27 minute video, which first reviewed the main aspects of mood disorders, with special focus diagnostic criteria for MDD (Part 1) and then presented biases and heuristics that are likely to occur in the context of diagnosing MDD, as well as debiasing strategies to counter them (Part 2). Debiasing strategies included opportunities for metacognition and simulation of decision making processes, developing insight and awareness about cognitive biases and heuristics, considering alternatives, cognitive forcing strategies and the use of an actuarial prediction rule, combining likelihood ratios of measures and predictors to update risk estimates. This intervention was adapted from previous work by Jenkins and Youngstrom (2016), also taking in consideration the derived version developed by Le (2018). The control condition included only the first part of the intervention (review of mood disorders/MDD), lasting 12 minutes.

After completing the video, participants were directed to the clinical vignettes. The first vignette (0) served as a practice exercise with corrective feedback (in the experimental condition) or a familiarization exercise (in the control condition). Participants then proceeded to the remaining vignettes, randomized to standard order (vignette 1, 2, 3, 4) or inverse order (vignette 4, 3, 2, 1). For each vignette, participants either selected their main diagnostic hypotheses (vignettes 1, 3 and 4) or gave risk estimates (vignette 2), and selected one or two clinical actions they would prioritize in managing the hypothetical case described in the vignette. Finally, participants were invited to evaluate the video corresponding to their assigned condition (treatment or control) and rate their understanding of its contents.

Analyses

To analyze the accuracy of participants' diagnostic hypotheses when the "other, please specify" alternative was chosen, we manually checked participants' responses to define if they chose an accurate diagnostic hypothesis using nonstandard terminology. We coded responses that could reasonably be interpreted as coherent with an MDD hypothesis (e.g., "psychotic depression", "major depressive episode", "melancholia") as accurate depressive disorder hypotheses. The same procedure was used to code bipolar hypotheses.

Our main analyses were linear regressions using cases with complete outcome data, and tested intervention effects on (a) overall diagnostic accuracy, (b) overall decision-making errors. Linear regressions were also used to analyse the continuous secondary outcome, i.e., decision accuracy in the vignette involving risk estimation. For other secondary outcomes, binary logistic regression models tested intervention effects on decision-making error status on each of the vignettes. Ordinal (cumulative link) regression models tested intervention effects on diagnostic accuracy for vignettes involving diagnosis selection (1, 3 and 4). We used proportional odds models with flexible thresholds, which assume that the effects of the predictors are constant across all levels of the outcome variable, but allow the thresholds between these levels to vary (Christensen, 2019). This is equivalent to taking into account the differences in relative "difficulty" between the levels of our response variable (e.g., it takes more to go from one to two accurate diagnoses than it takes to go from zero to one). This decision was consistent with our outcome variable, considering that different cognitive biases make it much easier for clinicians to come up with one plausible diagnosis (e.g., MDD) than to consider all the relevant hypotheses (Howard, 2019). To assess potential violations of the proportional odds assumption, likelihood-ratio tests were performed investigating the improvement in model fit when scale and nominal effects were included for each variable (Christensen, 2019). When the corresponding improvement in fit approached statistical significance (p < .10), the effects were considered to merit investigation in sensitivity analyses. For our other secondary outcome included in Hypothesis 3, chi-squared tests investigated associations between decision-making error status and next clinical action.

Participants missing one or more individual vignette's data were excluded listwise from that vignette's analysis only, and kept in others for which they provided data.

For our vignette-specific decision-making error and diagnostic accuracy outcomes (secondary outcomes), we also performed sensitivity analyses including scale effects for variables whose likelihood-ratio tests suggested potential violations of the proportional odds assumption. As previously described, likelihood-ratio tests of nominal and scale effects were used to test for violations of the proportional odds assumption. However, scale effects were preferred in sensitivity analyses because they allow the same variable to be included simultaneously as a scale effect and predictor in the model (Negeri et al., 2021), thereby permitting the desired comparison with the main analyses.

Specifically for Vignette 2, involving risk estimates, sensitivity analyses examined the robustness of intervention effects on (a) decision-making errors using a wider tolerance range around the Bayesian estimate (±15% rather than the original ±5%), and (b) risk estimate accuracy using a model-based estimate recalculated from likelihood ratios for the PHQ-9 across different settings.

In sensitivity analyses for all decision-making error and diagnostic accuracy outcomes, vignette order effects and their interaction with intervention were included in the models. For our main outcomes -overall decision-making errors and accuracy -we also examined the relative robustness of our results in different sensitivity analyses: (a) exclusion of the risk estimation task (vignette 2) from composite outcomes, thus considering only categorical decision-making tasks; (b) switching the modeling strategies to mixed logistic (decision-making errors) and mixed ordinal regression (decision accuracy), modeling fixed effects for vignettes (deviation-coded) and their interaction with intervention; (c) intention-totreat analyses including all randomized subjects through fully conditional specification (FCS) multiple imputation (Dong and Mitani, 2023); and (d) a second intention-to-treat analysis excluding the risk estimation task (vignette 2) from the outcomes, i.e., a combination of (a) and (c). FCS multiple imputation used 10,000 iterations and 20 imputed datasets and employed all available auxiliary variables (Mainzer et al., 2024). Results across imputed datasets were combined using Rubin's rules. The vignette intervention interaction effects in the mixed models described (b-d) were also interpreted as formal tests for differences in intervention effects across vignettes identified in our secondary, vignette-specific analyses.

Intervention effects were dummy coded. Sensitivity power analyses indicated that our minimum detectable effect size (considering α = .05, β = .2) was OR ≥ 1.95 at the level of individual vignettes (simulation-based, k = 10,000; Shotwell, 2015) and d0.34 ≥ across vignettes (analytical power analysis, Champely, 2020). Discussion

This study investigated the effects of a short, web-based debiasing video intervention on clinicians' accuracy on adult MDD diagnostic tasks using case vignettes. The intervention built upon previous work in pediatric bipolar disorder (Jenkins and Youngstrom, 2016), and was adapted to the Brazilian context and to a closely related diagnostic domain -namely, assessment of adult MDD. Participants were randomized to receive either the full intervention, which included information of common biases affecting diagnostic decisionmaking and strategies to counter them, or a control condition consisting only of the first part of the intervention: a brief review of mood disorders focusing on MDD and its diagnostic criteria. Outcomes were defined consistently with the original study and were assessed using case vignettes validated by local experts, capturing (a) clinicians' diagnostic accuracy and (b) clinicians' decision-making errors. Statistical analyses followed the approach established by the prior studies, complemented with a set of sensitivity analyses.

Findings supported higher overall accuracy in diagnostic decision-making tasks and fewer overall decision-making errors across vignettes in the intervention group, as predicted by Hypothesis 1. Higher accuracy and fewer decision-making errors were also observed for half of the vignettes (risk estimation and detection of possible comorbidity), partially supporting Hypothesis 2. The direction of observed effects converges with that of the original intervention study (Jenkins and Youngstrom, 2016), underscoring the potential utility of the employed debiasing strategies for the new diagnostic and cultural/linguistic context.

However, the estimated effect sizes (d = -0.41 for decision-making errors, d = 0.33 for diagnostic accuracy) were much smaller than those reported in the original intervention (d = -1.67 and d = 1.08, respectively). In addition, two of the individual decision tasks did not yield statistically significant differences between conditions in the present study, and all sensitivity analyses excluding risk estimates from main outcomes led to confidence intervals that included null effect. This may indicate some limits to the generalizability of the intervention, which could stem from a number of reasons. The mismatch could be related to differences in unknown attributes of diagnostic tasks, such as task difficulty, or the proportion of decision-making errors that is attributable to the cognitive biases targeted by the intervention. They could also be attributed to limits in cultural transferability of some debiasing mechanisms, although probably not in general, as simple debiasing interventions have shown benefit in non-WEIRD reasoners (Boissin et al., 2024). More specifically, there could be limitations to the generalizability of teaching strategy employed in the intervention (web-based, video instruction with a single practice exercise). Participant selection could also be a source of variation in results, since this study recruited participants from social media, while the original one recruited through listservs, fliers and word of mouth.

Somewhat surprisingly, both vignettes for which no statistically significant effect was observed involved differential diagnosis between MDD and bipolar disorder -the diagnostic distinction most closely aligned with the context of the original intervention. One possible explanation is that the control condition itself exerted partial "debiasing" effects for this specific differential diagnosis, attenuating the difference between conditions. Because it included explicit comparisons between MDD and bipolar presentations and reviewed all MDD criteria, including the need to rule-out bipolar disorder, the control condition may have "made the task easier", which has been listed as a type of debiasing by some authors (Croskerry, 2003) but not others (Soll et al., 2015). In contrast, when addressing search satisficing bias, the intervention addressed common comorbidities (e.g., GAD) that were not covered in the control condition, which may explain the significant effects observed in the corresponding diagnostic task (vignette 3). The same is also true for the vignette evolving risk estimates, since Bayesian risk estimation was a very specific content of the intervention condition.

Our results indicate that the intervention was associated with improvements in participants' risk estimation as measured through different standards. Absolute differences from model-based Bayesian estimates were smaller in the intervention group, both when PHQ-9 likelihood ratios were extracted from studies in specialized mental health settings (Inoue et al., 2012; Shin et al., 2019), and when they were extracted from a meta-analysis considering multiple settings (Negeri et al., 2021). Effect sizes were smaller when the general meta-analytic estimate was considered, which is expected, given that the intervention intentionally used more conservative likelihood ratios from studies in mental health settings.

This aligns with Evidence-Based Medicine's principle of considering test accuracy in the context it will be effectively used (Whiting, 2011; Straus et al., 2019).

Allocation to intervention was also associated with less decision-making errors in risk estimates, whether the thresholds for congruence were set around a ±5% or a ±15% range around the model-based point estimate. However, most participants' responses stood outside both of these ranges. Therefore, our results indicate the intervention may contribute to improve participants' relative calibration, while also making it clear that it didn't produce absolute calibration. Broader training interventions including the direct implementation of structured decision-support tools (e.g., probability nomograms; Garb et al., 2025) may be needed to bring calibration closer to model-based estimates.

With the exception of one vignette, we did not find significant associations between years of practice or postgraduate clinical training and diagnostic accuracy. This suggests that any putative effect of experience (if present) is likely small, consistent with findings from the clinical judgment literature. In the meta-analysis conducted by Spengler et al. (2009), the contribution of experience (either educational or clinical) to accuracy was generally modest (e.g., d = 0.12). One exception observed in the present study was the vignette conceptualized to elicit anchoring bias (patient with guilt-related delusions), for which participants who had undergone postgraduate clinical training produced significantly more accurate diagnostic hypotheses. Previous research has shown that clinicians with more years of practice are less likely to adhere to diagnostic hypotheses presented in a referral letter (Spaanjaars et al., 2015). On the other hand, specialists may be more prone to anchoring on diagnostic hypotheses involving severe conditions raised by patients themselves (Ordóñez, 2025). Taken together, these findings suggest that the association between experience and susceptibility to anchoring bias is moderated by the nature of the diagnostic task.

The presence of a decision-making error was generally not significantly associated with next clinical action, contradicting Hypothesis 3. One exception was vignette 1, conceptualized to elicit anchoring effects by presenting psychotic symptoms before the depressive ones. In this vignette, clinicians that considered both MDD and bipolar disorder hypotheses more often chose to refer the hypothetical patient to a psychiatrist. It is possible that the broad level of decision-making employed in our questions was not sensitive enough for the other vignettes, given their characteristics. For example, while 65% of clinicians selected "further assessment" as one of the next clinical actions in vignette 4, the focus of that assessment would likely be different for clinicians that (possibly adhering to a representativeness heuristic) ignored the possibility of a previous manic or hypomanic episode (First, 2014).

Similarly, although 80% of clinicians endorsed psychotherapy as one of their next clinical actions for vignette 3, the therapeutic focus would likely differ between those considering comorbid GAD (or MDD; Barlow, 2021) versus those ignoring comorbidity, which could be caused by search satisficing. In sum, some decision-making errors may influence how clinicians implement an action, but not which broad action they choose. Therefore, future studies could add more fine-grained options to clinical actions, especially when omitted diagnoses could lead to similar high-level treatment decisions.

Participants' evaluations of the intervention were generally positive, although less favorable than those of the control condition on some of the aspects assessed across conditions (understandability, pleasantness). These differences may reflect the longer duration of the intervention video (27 minutes, versus 12 minutes in the control), as well as the more unfamiliar nature of its content. However, even for the part of the intervention where participants' had the lowest self-rated understanding (risk estimation using likelihood ratios), most responses remained positive.

Limitations

This study relied on short-term, vignette-based assessment of outcomes. Therefore, it remains to be investigated whether the observed effects may generalize to everyday clinical practice, or to what extent they may endure in the long term. These have been ubiquitous limitations in diagnostic debiasing research (Lambe et al., 2016), although exceptions exist (Graber et al., 2014). Our use of an online data collection strategy also generates important limitations to our findings. Less than one-third of those entering the study completed at least one vignette, and thus 73% potential participants were excluded from the study. However, comparisons between participants versus the rest of the eligible pool (from the available data, n = 601), did not reveal any statistically significant differences (considering variables such as years of practice, workload, proportion of patients with MDD, theoretical orientation and demographic variables). Online data collection allowed us to reach a large number of practicing clinicians in Brazil, which would not be possible through in-person data collection, given available resources. A more intensive data collection strategy would also likely limit participation, which could diminish the precision of our estimates, especially for individual vignette analyses, which relied on ordinal regression.

The intervention condition had additional 15 minutes when compared to the control condition, and included a practice vignette with relevant content and corrective feedback.

Because these elements were constitutive of the intervention condition, our research design was not able to distinguish effects stemming specifically from debiasing components in the intervention from those related to the extra instruction and practice in the intervention condition. Furthermore, because the practice vignette corrective feedback closely resembles the risk estimate outcome measure, this confounding is even more relevant for that specific task.

An important difference in attrition was observed between intervention and control groups and, although multiple imputation-based intention-to-treat sensitivity analyses were performed as an attempt to mitigate bias, these analyses cannot eliminate biases due to data potentially not missing at random (NMAR). Although demographic characteristics were not significantly different between intervention and control groups and potential predictors of outcome were controlled for in accuracy analyses, other unmeasured factors contributing to adherence may also be associated with better outcome (e.g., motivation). Because these factors may create between-group differences in outcomes that are not explained by the intervention (Molero-Calafell et al., 2024), the results from this study should be considered preliminary. At the same time, they are in the hypothesized direction and consistent with the prior randomized trial (Jenkins and Youngstrom, 2016).

Effect sizes observed in this study were smaller than those observed in the original intervention (Jenkins and Youngstrom, 2016). Because more than one feature of that intervention was changed in building the version investigated in this study (including the focus condition, age group, target population and language) many competing hypotheses may explain the difference in effect estimates between studies. Nonetheless, shifting the focus to adult MDD helpfully portrays the potential of debiasing interventions to a greater audience, considering the much larger prevalence of that condition (GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2022). Conducting the study in a Latin-American country contributes to the generalization of research on debiasing interventions, which has been predominantly confined to high-income countries (Boissin et al., 2024).

The sample of the current study included psychologists from Brazil, most of whom were women (76%) and White (67%), with the majority having completed clinical certifications beyond those strictly required for clinical practice in this context (72%). A considerable proportion of participants identified as Black (including both Black and Brown, 30%), which closely reflects the general profile of Brazilian psychologists (Conselho Federal de Psicologia, 2022). However, the generalizability of our findings to professionals in other countries or continents, as well as to specific subsamples of psychologists in Brazil (e.g., Indigenous professionals) are likely limited. Additionally, the current version of the video intervention does not include audio description of visual content, which restricts accessibility for people who are blind or have low vision. It should also be noted that this study investigated the effects of a debiasing intervention on diagnostic accuracy in assessing hypothetical cases. Therefore, vignettes used for outcome assessment were designed such that commonly occurring biases would likely affect diagnostic accuracy -providing an opportunity to observe intervention effects. Although vignettes' content was validated by clinical experts, this process did not include experimentally testing whether the vignettes actually elicited the intended biases. Rather, it focused on generating vignettes that depicted presentations likely to occur in clinical practice.

Accordingly, our results should be interpreted as reflecting potential effects of the debiasing intervention on diagnostic accuracy, while the mechanisms underlying improvements in accuracy remain to be investigated in future research.

Future Directions

This study used a randomized experimental design to investigate the efficacy of a brief debiasing intervention in improving accuracy on MDD diagnostic tasks. To our knowledge, it is the first study to test the efficacy of a debiasing intervention specifically aimed at improving MDD diagnosis. Overall, the results provide preliminary evidence of the transferability across a different diagnostic and cultural context of the intervention developed by Jenkins and Youngstrom (2016), when appropriately adapted. However, effect sizes appear to vary depending on context and comparator conditions.

The vignettes employed in this study were designed to mirror real diagnostic challenges and were constructed to be susceptible to specific kinds of cognitive biases. Future research could use think aloud and other forms of process tracing (Baron, 2007) to investigate how different cognitive processes are actually recruited during those diagnostic tasks.

Another important direction would be to evaluate the intervention's effects in real clinical settings -for example, by examining charts or assessment reports produced by clinicians who received the intervention (Graber et al., 2014).
