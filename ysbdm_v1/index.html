How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate). This validation was made with an earlier, looser pattern; the pattern now requires “significant” or “significance” to follow the trigger word closely (#695).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. Abstract

One of the most fundamental questions that we face across all facets of our social lives is that of how resources ought to be distributed. Recent experimental research suggests there is a high degree of interindividual heterogeneity which determines how we make decisions when faced with these dilemmas. However, the extent to which this heterogeneity may manifest in other facets of social interactions is not yet known. Across two behavioral experiments, we explore three questions using a modified Dictator Game, wherein deciders have access to information necessary to assess both Need and Equity. In Experiment 1 (N = 51), we find that judgments of others' choices were explained by 'egocentric perspective-taking': individuated preferences elicited via behavior explain how people judge others' decisions. In Experiment 2 (N = 251), we find that -despite not being explained by participants' stated values -task-elicited distributive preferences corresponded to differences in policy views and political orientation, though not meritocratic perceptions. These findings point to the importance of characterizing individual differences for developing an understanding of distributive justice.

Introduction

One of the most fundamental yet contentious questions faced by society is that of how resources ought to be distributed. Should all members of society chip in to support those who need help, or do those that earn the most deserve to keep their extra share? Should managers give bonuses to their most productive employees, or reward themselves instead? Should estates be divided equally, or should some beneficiaries receive larger shares? These considerations permeate many important societal debates such as taxation policies and the provision of social benefits, and indeed even choices as mundane as "Should I share this cookie with my friend or keep it all for myself" evoke questions of social justice: most specifically, attention to what is commonly termed distributive justice (Tyler 2015).

Conventional theories of distributive justice propose that people use multiple, qualitatively distinct, moral principles to evaluate their prospective decisions.

Moreover, these theories have converged on the central claim that specific contextual factors determine which principle is appropriate in a given scenario. One particularly influential example is Deutsch's theory of distributive justice, which posits that context-specific goals underlie the selection of a particular distributive principle, with each respective principle leading to the realization of different goals (Deutsch, 1975). He proposed three such goals -those of incentivizing productivity, fostering harmony, and promoting welfare -and, as a result, three corresponding principles -those of Equity, Equality, and Need. Equity proposes that resources should be distributed in proportion to an individual's contributions (Adams, 1963; Adams, 1965), Equality contends that resources should be divided equally, while Need suggests that resources should be distributed according to individual need.

Another influential factor which is not included in this theory is that of Self-Interest: allocating resources in a way which is personally beneficial. This context-dependent view of distributive justice has received substantial empirical support, both experimentally and correlationally (i.e. Deutsch, 1985; Capellan et al., 2007; Reeskens & Van Oorschot, 2013; Alcaniz-Colomer, Valor-Segura, & Moya, 2023; Vavra, Galván, & Sanfey, 2024). However, despite the ubiquity of context-dependence in existing theories of distributive justice, individual differences are frequently shown to be a substantially larger source of variation in distributive behavior (Engel, 2011; Galván & Sanfey, 2026) though comparatively fewer studies have explored the implications of this individual heterogeneity in distributive justice. More pointedly, existing theoretical frameworks which have been employed to explain distributive justice do not unpack this individual heterogeneity. The current work seeks to extend these frameworks by exploring important implications for individual heterogeneity in distributive justice. These are, respectively, assessing how these individual differences explain our judgments of others' behavior, exploring how they might shape our political opinions, and identifying from where they might originate.

One important methodological consideration is how preferences for distributive justice are elicited, that is, how do we establish whether individual heterogeneity exists in the first place? Here we will explore distributive justice in the context of an incentive-compatible variant of the Dictator Game (Kahneman, Knetsch, & Thaler, 1986), a well-studied socioeconomic task (Engel, 2011). In our variant, we seek to explore how considerations of Equity and Need factor into distributive choice -in addition to those of Equality and Self-Interest. To this end, participants are presented with certain information about themselves and their partner -namely, their financial need and their performance on an effortful task -and then choose how to allocate tokens between themselves and the partner, allocations which have real monetary consequences. The key advantage of eliciting these preferences in the context of an incentive-compatible task is that behaviorally observed preferences carry a reduced risk of socially desirable responding or observer effects (Camerer & Hogarth, 1999; Levitt & List, 2007; Kamenica, 2012) concerns which are particularly relevant for morally salient topics such as distributive justice. To characterize participants' preferences for allocations based on the four principles of Equity, Equality, Need, and Self-Interest, we will utilize a computational modeling approach to address three research aims.

The first aim of this study (Experiment 1) is to assess whether individual heterogeneity in distributive preferences has direct implications for how we judge the decisions of others? People utilize different principles when faced with the same situation, but does that mean that we take this into account when observing others' behavior? If so, the standard of a 'reasonable person' would be give others the benefit of the doubt: we should judge others' behavior whichever principle best justifies that choice. If people do not consider others' behavior valid however, they judge others' behavior according to their own personal standards. This would equate to a kind of 'egocentric perspective-taking', wherein we judge others' behavior based on what we would have done in the same situation: there is some evidence in the literature to suggest that 'egocentric perspective-taking' may indeed describe such judgments (Goodwin & Darley, 2008; Meindl, Iyer, & Graham, 2019). An further consideration herein is that of moral hypocrisy: that we hold others to higher standards than we live up to ourselves (Batson et al., 1999; Lammers et al, 2015). We will explore whether the extent of this moral hypocrisy varies systematically as a function of the particular distributive preferences that individuals hold. While some preliminary evidence exists to support this notion (Graham et al., 2015), as yet no research has connected individual differences in moral hypocrisy to a larger framework of distributive preferences.

The second aim (Experiment 2) speaks to an often unaddressed critique of experimental research, namely that findings in the laboratory may not actually generalize to the respective real-world phenomena of interest. This is a particularly potent critique for studies of distributive justice, as economic inequality is a highly salient political issue which is becoming even more influential in voters' decisions and political orientation (Hawkins et al., 2018; Zivkovic, 2021; Wike, 2025). From a theoretical perspective, preferences of distributive justice should play a substantive role in motivating individual political attitudes and behavior, in particular with reference to policies which apply to economic inequality. A substantial body of prior work has tied behaviorally-elicited distributive justice preferences to political orientation (Bechtel, Liesch, & Scheve, 2018; Epper et al., 2024; Galván & Sanfey, 2026), but we extend this here to explore the potential implications of individual heterogeneity in distributive justice for understanding political orientation, policy opinions, and meritocratic perceptions.

The third and final aim of this study (also Experiment 2) is to identify the characteristics which might underlie individual heterogeneity in distributive justice.

Previous studies have aimed to characterize the institutional and cultural factors which might underlie such preferences (Sampson, 1980; Leung & Bond, 1984; Kim, Park, & Suzuki, 1990; Bechtel, Liesch, & Scheve, 2018; Almas et al., 2025), while additional experiments have described the demographic characteristics (Andreoni & Vesterland, 2001; Croson & Gneezy, 2009; Korndorfer, Egloff, & Schmukle, 2015) and personality inventories (Ben-Ner & Kramer, 2011; Zhao, Ferguson, & Simillie, 2016; Hilbig & Thielmann, 2025) associated with distributive justice preferences.

However, this work has only weakly tied such trait measures with actual preferences for distribution, with others suggesting that the lack of conceptual alignment between these measures and those of distributive justice may underlie these small effects (Becker et al., 2012). The current work will explore a promising candidate framework , namely that of the recently-expanded Moral Foundations Theory (MFT; Graham et al., 2013; Graham & Yudkin, 2022; Atari et al., 2023). MFT posits that multiple moral concerns underlie moral judgments, and that the subjective importance of these concerns is relatively stable within a person (Graham et al., 2013). MFT is operationalized via questionnaire and contains six scales (i.e. foundations), of which three in particular are relevant to distributive justice, namely those of Proportionality, Equality, and Care, which largely correspond to the principles of Equity, Equality, and Need respectively. This experiment primarily aims to address which moral traits underlie individual heterogeneity in distributive justice, but will also examine whether stated moral concerns in general are an accurate representation of behaviorally observed preferences for distributive justice.

Thus, the present study will characterize computational preferences for distributive justice elicited in an incentive-compatible decision-making task. Based on these behaviorally-derived preferences, this study will assess how individual differences might extend beyond our own behavior, and where they might originate. Herein, three aims will be addressed: how heterogeneity shapes our judgments of others' behavior, whether it manifests in our political opinions, and whether it originates in known psychological traits.

Experiment 1

Experiment 1 sought to assess whether distributive justice preferences -elicited via an incentive-compatible Dictator Game -explain how people judged the fairness of others' choices, in particular if they do this in reference to their own preferences or, instead, if people identify that other preferences may be valid and thus utilize a 'reasonable person' standard when judging others. Participants played in the role of the Dictator in Session 1, allocating resources between themselves and another player. In in Session 2 participants were in the Receiver role, viewing dictator decisions purportedly made by other players, which they then judged in terms of fairness.

Session 1. Upon clicking on the hyperlink to join the experiment, participants were redirected from Prolific to a plain webpage. Here participants were informed of the terms of their participation, directed to read the participant information brochure, and allowed to either cancel their participation or to begin the experiment. Median completion time for this experiment was approximately 40 minutes.

Financial Need and Performance Scores.

Both Financial Need and Performance scores ranged from 0 to 1 in increments of 1/6. Financial Need and Performance were fully crossed, resulting in a 7x7 design. All possible combinations of Financial Need and Performance were shown twice, with equal Financial Need and Performance scores shown four times, resulting in 100 trials in total.

Thus, selected trials had weak collinearity between these principles (i.e. r < 0.2) and consistently put these principles into conflict with each other -enabling parameters to be estimated via the computational model. A priori simulations indicated that mixture weights were reliably estimated (i.e. r > 0.9) for this set of trials.

On each trial, participants played with a new partner -as indicated by that player's screen name. Results

Individual Heterogeneity in Distributive Choice

Group-Level Behavior. A linear mixed effects model was estimated to assess whether the provided cues had an effect on decision-making, that is whether the Performance and Financial Need scores influenced the proportion of the endowment that participants kept for themselves. This model contained fixed effects for the main effects of Performance and Need as well as the interaction. In line with a maximal modeling approach (Barr et al., 2013), the model also contained random slopes for both main effects and the interaction as well as a random intercept. A Type III ANOVA was conducted with F values derived from the Kenward-Rogers approach for estimating degrees of freedom. This analysis revealed that distributive choices were influenced by Performance (F(1, 250) = 213.536, p < 0.001), Need (F(1,250) = 237.729, p < 0.001), and their interaction (F(1, 250) = 8.706, p = 0.003): participants kept more for themselves when they had higher Performance or Need scores, though when they had both high Performance and Need scores they kept relatively less than the combined main effects indicated. This model explained some variance in mean choice behavior though most of its explanatory power lay in interindividual differences (Marginal R² = 0.265, Conditional R² = 0.763).

Computational Modeling. In order to directly assess the interindividual differences in allocation behavior, we employed a computational modeling approach. Here, four principles were considered as potential rules which guided Dictators' choices: those of Equity, Equality, Need, and Self-Interest (Equation 1). A given choice's adherence to these rules (i.e. U(i) p ) determines how much utility (U(i) m ) a given person with distributive preferences (m) can receive from the choice (i). The particular weighting of these rules (W p m ) in the utility computation varies from person to person, as captured by a set of parameters. These terms are elaborated upon in the Supplementary Materials (pg. 4). To directly assess whether all four principles are necessary to describe distributive choice, we compared this 4-principle model to simplifications which only comprised 1, 2 or 3 principles (Supplementary Materials, pg. 5). The 4-principle model was compared to these alternative models by assessing BIC, which estimates model accuracy, penalizing model complexity. This comparison revealed that the 4-principle model statistically outperformed all alternative models (p < 0.001) (see Supplementary Materials (pg. 6).

Judgments Reflect the Same Heterogenous Preferences as Decisions

To examine whether judgments of others' choices and one's own decisions do, or do not, use the same underlying principles, we first assessed how the fairness judgments themselves were impacted by the manipulations of interest, namely the Amount Received (i.e. the partner's Dictator Game allocation), the Financial Need score, and the Performance score. All main effects and interactions were considered as fixed effects, and all main effects and an intercept were considered as random effects (no random interaction effects could be estimated). This model was found to weakly explain fairness judgments (Marginal R² = 0.116, Conditional R² = 0.224). A Type II ANOVA -reported in Table 2 -revealed that fairness judgments were largely shaped by how the Amount Received reflected Financial Need (Estimate = 0.067, SE = 0.003) and Performance (Estimate = 0.056, SE = 0003).

These trends are visualized in Figure 2. Note. Average fairness ratings are shown as a function of Amount Received, with different lines representing various levels of Financial Need and Performance scores. As 15 tokens reflects a 50-50 split of resources, Fairness Judgments largely appear symmetrical irrespective of Self-Interest though violations of Need appear to elicit harsher fairness judgments compared to violations of Equity.

The key question we aim to answer in this experiment is how people judge others' choices: do they give others the 'benefit of the doubt', cynically assume the worst of others' intentions, or judge others' behavior according to their own standards.

To examine this question, three linear mixed effects models were specified according to the Maximal Model (Barr et al, 2013). In the 'reasonable person' model, Fairness Judgments were predicted solely by the non-selfish principle which the Amount Received best followed. In the cynicism model, Fairness Judgments were solely predicted by the non-selfish principle which the Amount Received followed worst. In the 'egocentric perspective-taking' model, Fairness Judgments were predicted by Subjective Utility -derived from participants' choice parameters from Session 1. This Subjective Utility was computed from the perspective of the decider, reflecting the idea that participants judge others according to their own distributive preferences (i.e. 'egocentric perspective-taking'). As Figure 4 shows, 'egocentric perspective-taking' best explained how people judge the fairness of others' choices. Additionally, when compared to the group-level model (i.e. Figure 3), 'egocentric perspective-taking' was found to better explain fairness judgments (Δ Log Likelihood = -377, Marginal R² = 0.214, Conditional R² = 0.366), despite being less complex (Δ Parameters = -13). Thus, Fairness Judgments were best described by the idiosyncratic preferences observed in behavior.

A second question which we sought to address is whether moral hypocrisy factors into these judgments. Here, we expanded the 'egocentric perspective-taking' model to identify whether certain individuals' fairness judgments systematically deviated from 'egocentric perspective-taking' as a function of certain distributive preferences (i.e. Deviation: U(i) p × W p m ). We first established that Deviations did exist (χ 2 (22) = 1309, p < 0.001) and that these Deviations were present for both Self-Interest (Estimate = -0.988, SE = 0.183, χ 2 (1) = 29.115, p < 0.001) and Equality (Estimate = -0.881, SE = 0.214, χ 2 (1) = 17.018, p < 0.001), but not Need (χ 2 (1) = 0.667, p = 0.414) nor Equity (χ 2 (1) = 0.011, p = 0.916). We further expanded this model to assess whether Deviations might be accompanied with Cynicism: this expansion lead to significant improvement (χ 2 (10) = 71.788, p < 0.001). Crucially, the positive relationship between Cynicism and Fairness Judgments at the sample level was nonsignificant when controlling for 'egocentric perspective-taking' (p = 0.924). However, it was positively associated with fairness judgments among those who were more Self-Interested (Estimate = 0.727, SE = 0.221) and Equality-seeking (Estimate = 0.485, SE = 0.265), though the latter was marginally non-significant.

Discussion

This first experiment addressed an often overlooked issue in distributive justice, that of how people judge the fairness of allocations chosen by others. To do this, we employed a computational modeling approach, which revealed that allocation decisions seem to be undergirded by four principles of distribution, namely Equity, Equality, Need, and Self-Interest. Using these individuated parameters, we show that idiosyncratic distributive preferences better characterize how people judge others' distributive choices when compared to a 'reasonable person' standard. That is, rather than giving others 'the benefit of the doubt', we find that participants' judgments are consistent with 'egocentric perspective-taking': our data shows that people judge others' behavior based on what we would have done in the same situation.

A secondary finding of this experiment is that some individuals' judgments were also subject to a certain degree of moral hypocrisy. Although participants generally judged others' decisions based on their own distributive preferences, some decision preferences were associated with hypocritical shifts in how these individuals judged others. While individuals who made context-dependent distribution decisions (i.e. utilizing Equity or Need) were morally consistent, context-invariant distributors (i.e. utilizing Equality or Self-Interest) seemed to shift away from 'egocentric perspective-taking' and towards moral hypocrisy. Namely, their judgments of others' decisions seem to involve selectively judging others' behavior based on the prosocial principle which was most severely violated -in line with the idea that these individuals may be 'assuming the worst' of their partner's intentions.

Experiment 2

Experiment 2 seeks to address two additional research aims: firstly, to assess whether individual heterogeneity in distributive justice preferences may explain differences in political views between people, and secondly to explore whether this individual heterogeneity may originate in traits proposed by Moral Foundations Theory. Using the same measures in Experiment 1, we collected a larger sample in Experiment 2 in order to validly test these hypotheses. We also adapted the task to reduce the completion time of this experiment: the modified Dictator Game was changed from a binary response to a free choice variant in order to decrease the number of trials required to fit the computational model. This reduced total completion time by approximately 15 minutes.

Results

Individual Heterogeneity Defines Distributive Justice

Group-Level Behavior. A linear mixed effects model was estimated to assess whether Performance and Financial Need scores influenced the proportion of the endowment that participants kept for themselves. This model contained fixed effects for the main effects of Performance and Need as well as an interaction. In line with a maximal modeling approach (Barr et al., 2013), the model also contained random slopes for both main effects and the interaction as well as a random intercept. A Type III ANOVA was conducted with F values derived from the Kenward-Rogers approach for estimating degrees of freedom. This analysis replicated the results of Experiment 1: distributive choices were influenced by Performance (F(1,250) = 213.536, p < 0.001), Financial Need (F(1,250) = 237.729, p < 0.001), and their interaction (F(1,250) = 8.706, p = 0.003): participants transferred less when they had larger Performance and Financial Need scores, and gave slightly less when they scored higher on both. This model explained some variance in mean choice behavior though most of its explanatory power lied in interindividual differences (Marginal R² = 0.265, Conditional R² = 0.763).

Computational Modeling. In order to directly assess the interindividual differences in choice behavior, we employed a computational modeling approach.

Here, four principles were considered as potential rules which guided Dictators' choices, those of Self-Interest, Equality, Equity, and Need (Equation 1). In order to directly assess whether all four principles describe distributive choice, we compared this 4-principle model to simplifications which only comprise 3, 2, or 1 principle. The 4-principle model was compared to these alternative models by assessing BIC, which rewards model accuracy and penalizes model complexity. This comparison revealed that the 4-principle model statistically outperformed all alternative models (p < 0.028): these results are reported in the Supplementary Materials (pg. 8). The distributive preferences (W p m ) observed in our sample are plotted in Figure 2.

Distributive Justice Preferences are Related to Political Opinions

We explored the relationship between distributive preferences and various political opinions. Specifically, we considered whether these distributive preferences as assessed empirically -Equality, Equity, and Need -might correspond to political views around tax policy, government spending, social security, political orientation, and meritocratic perceptions. We also consider how participant income might act as a potential mediator of this relationship. To this end, multiple regression analyses were conducted to assess the role of these distributive preferences, income, and their interaction upon various political opinions. Task-elicited distributive preferences were predictive of all political opinions, with the exception of meritocratic perceptions. However, only views on government spending and meritocratic perceptions were predicted by the interaction with income. Distributive Justice is Not Captured by Moral Foundations

To identify the psychological substrates which underlie distributive preferences, we assessed the extent to which self-reported moral foundations predicted taskobserved preferences for Equality, Equity, Need, and Self-Interest. Moral concerns for Equality, Proportionality, and Care were considered as predictors of each distributive preference in four multiple regression models: one per principle. Type II ANOVAs revealed that moral concerns for Proportionality did not significantly predict preferences for Equity (F(1) = 2.155, p = 0.143), concerns for Equality were not significantly predictive of preferences for Equality (F(1) = 0.328, p = 0.567), while concerns for Care were significantly predictive of Need in the opposite direction than hypothesized (Estimate = -0.035, SE = 0.014, F(1) = 6.670, p = 0.010). Thus, no evidence for the hypothesized relationships between stated moral concerns and observed distributive behavior were not found in this data. The use of Self-Interest in allocation choices was predicted by moral concerns for Care (Estimate = 0.073, SE = 0.022, F(1) = 10.587, p = 0.001), indicating that people who reported themselves as caring more about others' well-being in actuality tended to actively ignore information about others' financial need and, instead, allocated more money for themselves. Discussion

This experiment directly addressed two important questions. The first was whether distributive preferences could explain political opinions, assessing five important views about public policy. The first three concerned opinions on various sets of redistributive policies: namely Taxation, Government Spending, and Social Security.

Here, we found that certain task-elicited preferences (i.e. Equity and Need) were associated with opposition to these policies while others (i.e. Equality and Self-Interest) were associated with support for such redistributive policies. For the fourth measure, that of Political Orientation, context-specific preferences were associated with conservative views while context-invariant preferences were associated with progressive views. Interestingly, however, none of these distributive preferences were associated with the fifth measure, that of meritocratic perception. This suggests that distributive preferences may influence political opinions directly, rather than via some indirect pathway: for example, by changing how people perceive economic inequality. Instead, our results suggests that distributive preferences reflect normative views of fairness which shape political opinions in addition to interpersonal choices. The current study offers evidence supporting the claim that interindividual heterogeneity in distributive choice has potential policy and political ramifications.

The second question we addressed was whether this individual heterogeneity is related to certain psychological traits. Given the tenuous links between distributive preferences and individual factors established by prior research, here we assessed Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) as a framework of personality-like traits due to its strong conceptual overlap with distributive justice. To do this, we explored how moral concerns for the MFT factors of Proportionality, Equality, and Care could directly predict behavioral preferences for principles of Equity, Equality, and Need respectively, with Self-Interest being predicted by low concern across all three moral foundations. However, no such relationships emerged in the data: with a sample size of 250 participants, Experiment 2 is a valid test of this hypothesis as it was well-powered to detect even weak correlations. These findings affirm the importance of utilizing behavioral, incentive-compatible, elicitation procedures for morally salient preferences, as stated values often do not overlap with actual behavior when moral considerations clash with material self-interest (Camerer & Hogarth, 1999; Levitt & List, 2007). One particularly interesting detail was the finding that moral concerns for Care were positively predictive of Self-Interest: despite self-identifying as being concerned with others' well-being, these people chose to largely ignore people who were in financial need in order to keep more money for themselves.

A limitation to the specificity of these findings lies in the collinearity of distributive preferences. Although distributive preferences were found to predict differences in political opinions, it remains unclear which particular preference(s) actually drove the effects observed in the present study. An additional, important, qualification is that the observed effects are consistently small: for example, most pseudocorrelation coefficients do not exceed 0.2 in the political data. Although weak effects are the norm, rather than the exception in such research (Kivikangas et al., 2020; Arcenaux et al., 2024), it is important not to overstate the magnitude of these findings.

General Discussion

In this study, we directly addressed three questions, which explore how in distributive justice. We first sought to verify this in our data, assessing how people decide to distribute resources as a function of task performance and financial need. Using a modified Dictator Game, and in line with previous findings (i.e. Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Bolton & Ockenfels, 2000; Cappelen et al., 2007), behavior across both experimental studies was found to be significantly related to a plurality of moral principles. These data thus demonstrate that different people have different preferences, and confirming that interindividual heterogeneity characterizes distributive behavior. By employing a computational modeling approach (Galván and Sanfey, 2025) we were able to derive preferences for distributive justice, which allowed us to subsequently address three questions of interest: "do our own distributive justice preferences shape how we view others' behavior", "what are the correlates of these distributive preferences in political opinions?", and "what psychological traits might underlie these differences?". Data across both studies provides clear answers to these questions. In Experiment 1, we found that judgments of others' behavior is explained by individuals' own behaviorally-elicited preferences. Thus, individual heterogeneity in distributive justice directly determines how we judge others' behavior, as opposed to referencing some universal "reasonable person" standard. Moreover, there were additional individual differences in the extent that people's judgments were found to deviate from their own internal standards: among people whose behavior reflected context-invariant distributive norms, we found morally hypocritical judgments which were explained by the least charitable assumption. This least charitable assumption is the opposite of "giving the benefit of the doubt" and, as such, participants selectively judged their game partners' choices based on whichever norm was most severely violated.

In Experiment 2, we explored several implications of this heterogeneity of social preferences in terms of the political opinions that people hold, and found that these distributive preferences corresponded to policy views and political orientation though, crucially, not meritocratic perceptions. This finding is particularly important because it suggests that those preferences for distributive justice which dictate behavior in interpersonal settings may also dictate policy preferences, without altering underlying perceptions about how fair society is or is not. This dissociation is a meaningful one, as it evidences the idea that the same social values which are elicited in interpersonal games may directly impact political opinions, rather than some less direct, less intuitive, relationship. The remaining question of interest is which psychological traits might underlie these preferences.

As prior studies have only shown tenuous links between trait measures and behavioral preferences, we considered a trait framework which has clear conceptual ties to the notion of distributive justice: Moral Foundations Theory.

Despite this link however, we found no evidence of a relationship between these trait-like moral foundations and distributive justice preferences in Experiment 2.

Taken together, these findings indicate that individual differences in distributive justice can shape our behavior, our judgments of others' behavior, and our attitudes towards various societal issues -all while remaining inaccessible via selfreport measures, at least as assessed here. These individual differences matter, because distributive justice manifests in our behavior, our judgments, and our opinions on society. However, as yet there is no clear evidence as to what mechanisms or traits may underlie these individual differences-posing an interesting challenge for future work.

Although the participant sample examined here is certainly more representative than convenience samples of university students, an important caveat of these findings are the sample characteristics. Here, we recruited participants from a WEIRD country via an online participation platform, which disproportionately comprises highly educated, young, digitally engaged adults. While this does limit the generalizability of the present work to broader populations, we believe that these findings point to the importance of individual differences in preferences for justice and fairness, and that this is a promising direction for future research to explore in more diverse populations. Together, these findings indicate that a comprehensive account of distributive justice must be defined at the level of the individual, incorporating trait-like differences as well as state-dependent influences.
