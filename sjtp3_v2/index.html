How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

To assess psychological tests and scales for violations of intergroup comparability -termed Measurement Non-Invariance (MNI) in factor analysis and Differential Item Functioning (DIF) in item response theory -statistical testing has emerged as the dominant approach, but inherently fails to differentiate between major and negligible violations. In response to this, upwards of 37 effect size measures for both MNI and DIF have been proposed over the last decades, using vastly different conventions in naming and notation, with no clear consensus on interpretation. This arguably constitutes a severe hindrance for the much-needed popularization of non-dichotomous assessments of intergroup comparability.

We address this by systematically deriving a unified framework for a large class of measures from the theoretical definition of first-order measurement invariance. This yields a rich and interpretable taxonomic framework derived from just four fundamental types:

Expected Difference (ED), Expected Absolute Difference (EAD), Total Expected Difference (TED) and Total Expected Absolute Difference (TEAD), which are universally applicable, irrespective of the underlying measurement model. Seen as instances of these four measures, this framework includes the 37 identified measures, uncovering both a notable 'Jangle' and vast taxonomic gaps in the literature. Finally, we show the relations between measures of this framework and provide a step-by-step guide for practical applications, which we illustrate using models of both factor analysis and item response theory on real-world data.

Effect-Based Assessments of Intergroup Comparability -The Practical Impact of Measurement Non-Invariance & Differential Item Functioning on Psychological Tests and Scales

Psychological research commonly revolves around the quantitative measurement of unobservable constructs such as personality traits or psychopathologies. Conceptualized as continuous latent variables, they are measured indirectly through indicators -observable variables assumed to be correlated with the latent traits of interest -usually items on questionnaires and tests. A major caveat of the latent variable paradigm is that valid intergroup comparability is not guaranteed by default. For instance, there is evidence that after-treatment scores on depression scales overestimate depressive symptomatology, introducing longitudinal bias that may confound evaluation of treatment efficacy (Fokkema et al., 2013). In cross-cultural research, acquiescence has been shown to be considerably higher in countries with stronger social norms of deference (Lechner et al., 2019). Age and gender differences (Sharp et al., 2014) are also typical sources of such biases. A priori, group-specific differences in response behavior may be present in any relevant grouping.

Without establishing that indicators measure invariantly across groups and time, observed group and longitudinal differences cannot be reliably traced back to 'true' latent differences, prompting the need to detect violations of intergroup comparability methodically.

Statistical Evaluations of Intergroup Comparability

For quantitative evaluations of intergroup comparability, the key insight is the observation that such violations manifest in distinct unobservable-observable relationships in the respective groups. Hence, the statistical foundation is formed by group-specific latent measurement models, which separately estimate this relationship for each relevant group.

As such, investigations of intergroup comparability can be operationalized as determining differences of group-specific measurement models across groups; a lack of meaningful differences is then seen as evidence of intergroup comparability. The most prominent latent measurement models in psychological research are derived from Factor Analysis (FA) & Item Response Theory (IRT), respectively postulating a linear & logistic or probit link between unobservable and observable variables. Within each of these measurement paradigms, different terms are used for this statistical concept of intergroup comparability.

The presence of violations thereof is declared Measurement Non-Invariance (MNI) in FA and Differential Item/Test Functioning (DIF/DTF) in IRT. These notions can be viewed as twin concepts (Thissen, 2025), derived from a shared latent-trait-theoretic definition (Meredith, 1993) unspecific to any measurement model type, although it should be noted that the frameworks of MNI and DIF/DTF are not fully interchangeable; Measurement Invariance (MI), the lack of MNI, is typically tested for in stages -starting from configural MI, then weak MI, followed by strong MI and finally strict MI -an approach with no equivalent in IRT. By contrast, item-level DIF testing is a routine practice, whereas MI analyses rarely focus on differences at the item level. In essence, group-specific model comparisons commonly operate on the parameter level, with parameters having fundamentally different interpretations in IRT and FA models, hence this differentiating terminology is justified to some degree. Nonetheless, the core idea of comparing a more constrained (MI) model to a less constrained (MNI) model is indeed shared across model paradigms.

Effect-Based Assessments of Intergroup Comparability

Dating back to as early as Dorans and Kulick (1983), there has been an ongoing trend to develop effect size measures for amounts of MNI or DIF/DTF. Such effect-based assessments of intergroup comparability aim to capture the severity and practical impact of differences in measurement across groups, rather than relying solely on null-hypothesis significance testing, which -by design -can only ever provide evidence for the mere presence of differences. Reviewing the literature reveals a surprisingly high number of proposed effect size measures, both for MNI and for DIF/DTF, that have accumulated over the last decades. Throughout publications, some common themes can be identified. First, proposals are rarely -if ever -based on theoretical definitions that specify the actual quantity being measured. Second, specific 'design choices' in the construction of measures are rarely motivated. Third, there seems to be no consensus on notation, nor on interpretation, despite many similarities across proposals. Navigating this vast array of different measures is challenging, even for experts.

In this paper, we aim to systematically resolve these issues for a broad range of measures. Based on precise mathematical definitions, we conceptually distill a large, model-agnostic class of measures theoretically derived from the concept of first-order measurement invariance (Bauer, 2017). This formulation leads to a rich taxonomic framework, encompassing 112 conceptually distinct measures, cf. 2, which are viewed as instances of just four basal measures in essence. We show that this framework encompasses at least 37 of the measures that have been proposed throughout the last decades, revealing a notable case of the 'Jangle Fallacy' (Kelley, 1927), as well as vast taxonomic gaps in the literature. Furthermore, this unified formulation uncovers the explicit relations between measures of this class.

The Gaps and the Jangle in the Current State of Research

For investigations of both MNI and DIF, by far the most common approach is using statistical tests to decide whether group-specific models fit significantly better than a single, shared one. Here, the convention is for the null hypothesis to represent intergroup comparability, i.e., the desired presence of MI. On a conceptual level, this has the effect of understanding intergroup comparability as a binary quality factor that, for any given indicator, must either be established or discarded. Direct intergroup comparisons are thus either deemed justified or baseless, with a complete inability to distinguish between mild and severe violations of invariance. This predominantly used all-or-nothing approach is particularly problematic given that higher sample size alone will increase statistical power and therefore the likelihood of non-invariance verdicts, even for potentially minuscule violations thereof. Unbalanced data have also been shown to impact MNI test results (P.-Y. Chen & Chao, 2026). Using fit index cutoffs instead of statistical tests comes with similar problems (F. F. Chen, 2007) and neither allows for any distinction between degrees of violation severity. Some researchers have even gone so far as to argue "seemingly arbitrary benchmarks for assessing MI" to "effectively prohibit meaningful research" (Funder & Gardiner, 2024), reflecting the make-or-break nature of this binary view. It is indeed not always clear how to proceed when a negative result (MNI or DIF) is obtained. This may incentivize researchers to only report results supporting comparability, meanwhile ignoring evidence for the contrary. In summary, as a standalone tool for the investigation of intergroup comparability, it can be argued that statistical testing is insufficient in many regards.

A Conceptual Shift to MNI/DIF as an Amount to be Quantified

These problems can be resolved by a fundamental reconceptualization: Instead of framing MNI and DIF as a yes-or-no question, we can instead determine its magnitude, allowing practical relevance to be quantitatively integrated in the discussion. Recall the well-established fact that statistically significant mean differences can correspond to a practically meaningless effect in actuality -which only becomes apparent through the use of effect size measures such as Cohen's d. Inspired by this, effect size measures for amounts of DIF were formulated as early as 1983 for IRT models (Dorans & Kulick, 1983).

Following this, a multitude of similar measures have been proposed over the last decades (Raju et al., 1995; Flowers et al., 1999; Stark et al., 2004; Meade, 2010; Nye & Drasgow, 2011; Chalmers et al., 2016; Nye et al., 2019; Gunn et al., 2020; M. H. Lai et al., 2025; Schuhbeck et al., 2025). Introducing a Model-Agnostic Taxonomy

In this paper, we aim to reunite IRT and FA in effect-based assessments of intergroup comparability by introducing a novel notational framework from the ground up, which is... ... Model-Agnostic. All measures introduced within the framework are designed to be applicable to any latent measurement model that allows for model-implied expected responses to be calculated. This includes the entirety of FA and IRT models.

This work is organized into eight parts. First, we review the theoretical concept of '1 st -order MI', on which the entire taxonomic framework is based. Second, we define the concept of an 'expected response function' which we subsequently illustrate for commonly used latent measurement models. Third, based on the general, model-unspecific notion of expected response functions, we formally construct the framework by introducing ED & EAD for a given group's amount of 1 st -order MNI and TED & TEAD for the total, i.e., across-group amount of 1 st -order MNI when accounting for a given grouping. Fourth, we use the framework to derive formulas for amounts of 'MNI-induced bias' in commonly used (standardized) mean difference effects. Fifth, we summarize the core measures of our framework and show how they relate to one another. In particular, we derive and illustrate when and why measures agree or disagree. Sixth, we provide a step-by-step guide for the practical application and interpretation of any such effect size measures of MNI. Seventh, we illustrate the use on one empirical example twice, using both an FA and an IRT measurement model. Finally, in Appendix A, we provide a detailed taxonomic classification for all 37 identified measures of 1 st -order MNI in terms of our proposed framework.

Recall that investigating 1 st -order MI for a given group g operationalizes to investigating equality of estimated expected responses, i.e., where R g models expected responses estimated specifically for group g, whereas R is designed to have optimal fit with regard to the entire population.

In Summary -Relating the Measures to One Another

This framework generalizes the idea of comparing group-specific response behavior to a baseline response behavior. This idea is operationalized by comparing the expected response function R g derived from a 'non-invariant' (i.e., group-specific) measurement model, to a baseline expected response function R derived from an 'invariant' (i.e., global/reference) measurement model. Given adequate model fit of the non-invariant model, the average difference R g ´R can be interpreted as a measure of the amount of 1 st -order non-invariance (2) relative to the 'invariant' model. Our framework captures precisely such average expected response differences, yielding a unified class of effect size measures for the average amount of 1 st -order non-invariance. As we have shown, this class can be described entirely by a few basal measures of group and across-group amounts of MNI.

• ED is the group average (signed) difference of expected responses. It is especially useful to estimate how non-invariance will impact observed means, relative to the 'invariant' baseline.

• EAD is the group average absolute difference, describing the average error when using the 'invariant' model to describe the response behavior of the group. Using squared errors instead yields the numerically simpler approximation RESD.

• TED & TEAD are the across-group average signed & absolute difference, respectively, capturing the corresponding total effect of MNI accounting for proportional group sizes.

These basic measures are directly related by the following inequalities, which are true on both the group and across-group level.

ED ď EAD pď RESDq , TED ď TEAD pď RTESDq.

Similar to Cohen's d, the interpretability and comparability of values can be increased by standardizing the measures via division by a suitable standard deviation estimate. This more abstract formulation in terms of expected response functions can be realized for item-responses and sum-score-responses. A Step-by-Step Guide for Effect-Based Analyses of MNI

In practice, applying the measures presented in this paper in real-world analyses of measurement non-invariance involves several steps. To make applications more accessible, we outline this workflow in the following step-by-step guide, highlighting what needs to be considered at each stage of the analysis.

Step 1) Choose the Scope of Non-Invariance.

First, choose the groups in which possible non-invariances should be accounted for. Such a partition into groups is typically determined by distinct values on one or more covariates, e.g., g " 'Germans' as one group determined by the covariate country or g " 'German Women' as one group determined by the covariates country and gender. Different measurement occasions can also be used to determine a partition into distinct groups for which non-invariance should be accounted for. The sample size n g of each group should be deemed sufficiently large for meaningful measurement model estimation.

Step 2) Determine a Suitable Baseline.

The effects of non-invariance described in this paper are always relative to a baseline response behavior, thus determining a suitable baseline is crucial for the interpretability of such effects. This may be guided by the underlying null hypothesis. For instance, a reference group r may be an appropriate baseline in more confirmatory settings. An example of this is comparing translations of a scale to its original. In this case, response behavior on the original scale can be chosen as a meaningful baseline. In more exploratory settings, when no group constitutes a meaningful default, the globally fitted response behavior usually constitutes a less artificial baseline.

Note that since such global baselines effectively pool all distinct response behaviors into a single compromise, the largest group will usually appear the most invariant.

Step 3) Identify the Scale.

As with any analyses of MNI, the scales of the latent factors need to be identified to ensure comparability of all relevant models (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). In practice, this can be realized by defining an anchor item. Since -by definition -such an item will appear perfectly invariant across all groups, special care should be taken in choosing a suitable anchor item, for instance using cluster-based anchor item identification and selection (Pohl et al., 2021).

Step 4) Estimate Measurement Models.

Given the choices of Steps 1-3, a corresponding 'non-invariant' (i.e., less restricted) multi-group model needs to be estimated for the group-specific response behavior. The fit of this model should be deemed sufficient; if the model is substantially misspecified, the resulting effects will lose any meaningful interpretation.

Furthermore, the baseline model needs to be specified. For a reference group baseline, the reference group may either be included in the multi-group model, or a separate model can be used. For a globally fitted baseline, a 'single-group' (i.e., fully restricted) model can be estimated. Since such a model depends on the mixture of groups and their relative proportions, so will the implied expected response function. Naturally, the model estimation procedure itself may also impact resulting effect values. All models need to be specified using the same anchor item. Step 5a) Calculate Suitable SDs for Standardization. For standardized effects of non-invariance, similar to Cohen's d, an estimate s for the standard deviation of observed responses X needs to be computed; s i for the item-level effects and s T for sum-score-level effects. A straightforward choice is the standard deviation of the entire sample; this ensures that the resulting effects are all on the same scale and thus directly comparable. Depending on the specific setting, other choices may be more suitable. Step 6) Calculate Effects of Non-Invariance. Based on Step 5, multiple effects of non-invariance can be calculated. In general, SEAD -i.e., EADpgq{s -can be used as the default measure of non-invariance: Reporting the table of all group's SEAD effects yields a fine-grained evaluation of MNI in the strict sense. For a summary effect of non-invariance, accounting for differences in this group partition, the effect STEAD can be reported for the average non-invariance, along with the range of SEAD -cf. Equation (21b) -denoting the extreme cases of non-invariance. Note that for methodological research that requires numerical simplicity, the measures SRESD & SRTESD constitute beneficial approximations for SEAD & STEAD that can be used and interpreted analogously. Finally, a supplementary direction of non-invariance can be calculated for the group and across-group level. Step 6a) Calculate Impacts of Non-Invariance on Observed Means. In specific cases where the impact of non-invariance on observed means is of interest, the values of the measure SED -i.e., EDpgq{s -can be reported in a table. If desired, a summary effect STED 3 and the range of SED can be reported as well. Furthermore, the impact of non-invariance on observed effects, such as Cohen's d, can be calculated -cf. Equation (17). Step 7) Interpret Results. The calculated effects provide a nuanced evaluation of non-invariance by quantifying its severity. Arguably, interpretation of measures is the most straightforward when effects are compared between groups, not evaluated as single values in isolation. If a categorical verdict based on the calculated effects is desired, thresholds can be employed, cf. Nye et al. (2019) for instance, differentiating between small, medium and large effects, although one-size-fits-all definitions of these categories should always be taken with a grain of salt. It should be clearly stated that all such evaluations of non-invariance are relative to the chosen baseline; this fact should be reflected in any interpretation. Researchers should also be aware that many measurement models assume normally distributed latent traits. Ignoring non-normality in the calculation may substantially skew the resulting effects of non-invariance. For valid results in such cases, approaches such as skew factor analysis (Hashemi et al., 2020) may be employed. All things considered, the overarching goal is almost always to answer the question 'Is the impact of non-invariance practically relevant in this case?'. This renders a final, binary verdict ultimately unavoidable, purely by the nature of decision-making. However, in contrast to the usual statistical test results which by themselves effectively determine interpretation, reporting effects of non-invariance paints a much richer and more informative picture that -a priori -makes no claim as to what constitutes a meaningful violation. Any dichotomy arises only in the subsequent, qualitative discussion of statistical results and is arguably much more transparent. For instance, while it may be possible to deem a non-invariance effect of around .2 to be negligible given large mean difference effects bigger than .8, this decision could be critically challenged based on the reported statistical values alone. Similarly, significant non-invariances may also be deemed acceptable in the discussion if the effect cancels out across items. In that sense, effect sizes provide a more nuanced view of measurement invariance that goes beyond the usual all-or-nothing view of statistical testing. Of course, what constitutes an acceptable range of non-invariance effect sizes is ambiguous and should always be questioned critically by both authors and readers.

Discussion

In this paper, we introduced a model-agnostic taxonomic framework of effect size measures for MNI and DIF, capturing and extending more than 40 years of research on such effects. Starting from the formal notion of 1 st -order MI, we introduced expected response functions, which are used to operationalize evaluations of 1 st -order non-invariance.

Based on this, we defined four basal measures quantifying such amounts of non-invariancewith respect to the selected baseline -directly in the metric of the indicators: ED & EAD for the amount of 1 st -order MNI within a group, and TED & TEAD for the total amount of 1 st -order MNI across a partition of groups, both with corresponding effect directions.

From the definition of ED and TED, we derived explicit formulas for the amount of MNI-induced bias in commonly used (standardized) mean difference effects, with respect to the chosen baseline expected response function. Classifying the 37 effect size measures identified in the literature within this framework showed that all of them conceptually reduce to these four basal measures, 34 of them to just ED and EAD. This pronounced conceptual overlap -i.e., "Jangle" (Kelley, 1927) -stands alongside vast taxonomic gaps in the literature, which the 112 conceptually distinct measures of our framework fill.

Our dual illustration on the same data demonstrated that the framework can be applied irrespective of the type of measurement model used, highlighting that the resulting effect size values depend on the choices made. This emphasizes that choices about the baseline and the quantity of interest shape the conclusions, and that effect-based assessments make these choices explicit. The taxonomy and the accompanying step-by-step guide are meant to support researchers in making such choices deliberately. In our illustration, we discussed and illustrated how different scenarios and research goals call for different effect size measures. Specifically, researchers need to decide a) whether the amount of MNI or its practical impact should be quantified, b) whether it makes sense to assume a reference category, c) whether to calculate effects using signed or unsigned differences, d) whether the effect should be standardized, e) whether a (normal) distribution of the latent variable is assumed (inferential vs. descriptive), f) whether itemor scale-level responses are to be investigated, and g) whether to focus on evaluating MNI within each group separately or in total across a partition of groups.

Some of these decisions can be informed directly by the research question or study design, e.g., assuming a reference category may make sense if a scale was developed in one context and is now transferred, for instance translated, to a new context. Others remain relatively arbitrary.

Nonetheless, researchers should critically consider which choices can be justified and select an appropriate effect size measure accordingly, instead of relying solely on "standard" measures such as d MACS that may not suit their research goals.

Limitations and Future Research

The measures in our proposed taxonomy inherit most limitations of the effect sizes for MNI/DIF that it contains. Most notably, the accuracy is inherently tied to the fit of the respective expected response functions. When working with model-implied expected responses of models with insufficient model fit, estimates for MNI should be interpreted with caution. This, of course, is a problem with latent variable modeling in general. For inferential estimates, robustness could be increased by formulating confidence intervals.

Note that bootstrap-based confidence intervals exist for d MACS (M. Lai, 2024), which could be similarly implemented for ED and EAD in general. There is also the general problem of effect sizes possibly overestimating null effects, as a result of sampling variability. Different regularization methods exist (e.g., based on spike and slab priors) to counteract this (Van den Bergh et al., 2021), which can be applied for MNI effects as well.

Perhaps the most notable limitation is the ambiguity with respect to what constitutes a 'large' MNI effect. There are simulation studies for d MACS , aiming to establish benchmarks (Nye et al., 2019), which should be taken with a grain of salt. Instead of using simulation studies or empirical benchmarks to determine threshold values for what constitutes small, medium, or large effects, researchers need to define tolerable amounts of non-invariance -comparable to approaches such as approximate MI (Van de Schoot et al., 2013) -for each application or research context themselves, although further methodological research on ED and EAD may provide guidelines and meaningful defaults that researchers can use as a starting point for their own research.

Other Approaches to Investigating MNI/DIF

As mentioned before, for dichotomous IRT models, DIF effect size measures on parameter level have been suggested (Hasselblad & Hedges, 1995; Camilli & Penfield, 1997; Doebler, 2019), making use of the log odds ratio from the Mantel-Haenszel test. However, because they quantify model parameter discrepancies, they cannot be understood as measures of 1 st -order MNI, making them substantially different and inherently tied to dichotomous IRT models. Similarly distinct effect size approaches based on classification accuracy exist as well for common factor models (Ozcan & Lai, 2025 , 2026). Furthermore, the so-called Partial Gamma Coefficient is also sometimes used for DIF effects in polytomous models (Henninger et al., 2025), formulated using an ability level matching criterion. Bayesian approaches to MNI investigations have been proposed, for instance the Region of Measurement Equivalence (ROME) (Zhang et al., 2023), determining the range of latent trait levels for which MNI is "practically equivalent to zero".

Conclusion

It should be stated clearly that this effect size approach to MNI is not supposed to be understood as a replacement for statistical tests, but rather as an essential tool to be used in conjunction with them. This work and the presented taxonomy can guide researchers in selecting suitable effect size measures depending on their research question and overall analysis goal. Accordingly, we hope to contribute to paving the way for MNI effect size measures becoming routine in evaluations of intergroup comparability. In this way, we can move beyond the rather unproductive dichotomy of MI testing and toward informing group comparisons and comparability research more broadly.
