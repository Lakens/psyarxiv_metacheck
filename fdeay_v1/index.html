How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Introduction: Machine learning models for psychotherapy outcome prediction are typically evaluated using aggregate performance metrics, with prediction errors treated as unsystematic noise. This study examined whether conditional prediction difficulty is systematically associated with patient characteristics rather than with the choice of algorithm, and whether patients who are systematically harder to predict can be identified using baseline information.

Methods: A secondary analysis was conducted on a naturalistic sample of 1,858 outpatients.

Fifteen machine-learning algorithms were trained to predict end-of-treatment symptom severity. Inter-model residual correlations, k-means clustering of residual profiles, and LASSO logistic regression were used to examine the consistency, structure, and predictors of prediction error. Results: Residuals were highly consistent across all 15 algorithms (r = .89), indicating a shared source of predictive difficulty. Clustering identified a well-predicted majority (n = 1,479) and a hard-to-predict subgroup (n = 379). The latter was characterized at baseline by somatic distress, emotional inhibition, and interpersonal anxiety, while marital stability and depressive symptoms were associated with better predictability. Conclusion: Prediction difficulty in psychotherapy outcome research is systematically associated with patient characteristics rather than with the choice of algorithm, representing implications for the design and communication of clinical decision-support tools.

Clinical and Methodological Significance of this Article

This study reframes prediction error in psychotherapy outcome modeling from an incidental nuisance into a substantive object of inquiry, demonstrating that predictive difficulty is systematically associated with patients rather than with model-specific noise.

Methodologically, by showing that residuals are highly consistent across fifteen structurally diverse machine-learning algorithms, it establishes that efforts to improve architecture or feature engineering alone cannot resolve poor predictions for certain individuals, and it introduces cross-algorithm residual analysis as a practical strategy for distinguishing systematic from random error. Clinically, the identification of a hard-to-predict subgroup (roughly one-fifth of patients, characterized at baseline by somatic distress, emotional inhibition, and interpersonal anxiety) suggests that these baseline features may mark patients whose outcomes depend on factors not represented in standard intake assessment. Because this subgroup is identifiable from intake data, the findings support augmenting algorithmic decision-support tools with individual-level uncertainty estimates and brief predictability screeners, so that clinicians can flag cases where automated predictions are least reliable and where enhanced assessment, progress monitoring, or consultation are most warranted.

Predicting the Unpredictable: Identifying Patient-Level Drivers of Predictive Error Across Psychotherapy Outcome Algorithms

Despite the life-changing benefits of psychotherapy for many, a significant minority of patients fail to respond to first-line treatments or even experience symptomatic deterioration (Barkham et al., 2023). Addressing this heterogeneity in treatment response has become a central goal in psychotherapy research, highlighting the need for more precise and individualized approaches to mental health care delivery. This has fueled growing interest in precision mental health care, which aim to optimize outcomes through data-driven strategies that allow tailoring interventions and clinical decisions to each individual's unique characteristics and needs (Bickman, 2020; Moggia et al., 2024; Lutz et al., 2019). These goals have been increasingly pursued in the last decades through the application of machine learning methods capable of integrating high-dimensional sociodemographic and clinical data to predict treatment outcomes at the individual level. The application of these methods in psychotherapy research has expanded significantly over the last decade (Aafjes-van Doorn et al., 2021), demonstrating their utility in predicting a wide array of clinically significant outcomes, such as dropout (Bennemann et al., 2022 , Giesemann et al., 2023), or treatment non-response (Gómez Penedo et al., 2024; Hammelrath et al., 2024), among others. Such approaches aim to support clinical decision-making by identifying patients at risk of poor outcomes, thereby facilitating more adaptive and personalized care (Lutz et al., 2022; Webb & Cohen, 2021).

Current predictive models are typically evaluated using aggregate metrics (e.g., accuracy, RMSE, AUC), which implicitly conceptualize residuals (the gap between predicted and observed outcomes) as either irreducible noise or model misspecification. In this sense, residuals are generally treated as unsystematic noise and are rarely examined as meaningful outcomes in their own right. However, from the perspective of precision mental health, this assumption warrants scrutiny. A high average predictive performance does not ensure that the predictions are equally accurate for all patients. It remains possible that a subset of individuals is consistently difficult to predict, irrespective of the modeling technique used, due to factors such as clinical complexity, symptom instability, measurement limitations, and unobserved contextual influences. From this perspective, prediction error may contain meaningful information about the limits of predictability at the individual level.

This possibility points to an underexplored source of heterogeneity in psychotherapy outcome prediction, namely variability in patient-level predictability (understood as predictability conditional on the information routinely available at intake rather than in any absolute sense). If multiple algorithms with distinct inductive biases and functional forms produce large prediction errors for the same patients, this suggests that prediction difficulty reflects systematic patient-level characteristics rather than model-specific shortcomings. Conversely, if prediction errors were largely model-specific, efforts to improve the model architecture or feature engineering may be sufficient to address poor predictions. Neither outcome can be assumed in advance: research on predictive multiplicity has shown that models with nearly identical aggregate performance, trained on the same data, routinely assign conflicting predictions to individual cases (Marx et al., 2020; Black et al., 2022).

Shared training information constrains average accuracy, but does not by itself determine

agreement at the level of individual residuals. Distinguishing between these alternatives is essential for understanding the limits of predictive modeling and for determining how algorithmic predictions should be interpreted and applied in clinical settings.

To date, and to the best of our knowledge, only one study in psychotherapy has explicitly examined prediction errors as a stable, patient-level phenomenon (Leon et al., 1999). Most methodological efforts have focused on increasing model complexity, expanding the predictor sets, or optimizing hyperparameters to improve global accuracy, rather than examining the structure of prediction error itself. Although these strategies may yield incremental improvements, they do not directly address whether certain patients remain systematically difficult to predict across models. An additional unresolved question is whether patients who are difficult to predict form meaningful subgroups and whether such subgroups can be characterized using baseline sociodemographic or clinical variables.

Identifying systematic differences between well-and poorly predicted patients has important implications. Conceptually, this would indicate that the degree to which a patient's outcome can be anticipated from intake data is itself patterned rather than random, raising the question of which clinical features underlie this pattern. Practically, this could inform the development of decision-support tools that signal when predictions are likely to be less reliable and when additional assessment or clinical judgment may be particularly important. Throughout, we use the term conditional prediction difficulty to refer to the magnitude of prediction error that is shared across structurally diverse models trained on the same baseline information. It should be distinguished from two adjacent quantities. Model error refers to the discrepancy between predicted and observed outcomes for a single model, and includes estimation error, model misspecification, and measurement error in the outcome; conditional prediction difficulty is the component of that error which persists across models and is therefore attributable to the patient rather than to the algorithm. Clinical complexity, in turn, is a construct that may account for conditional prediction difficulty but is not equivalent to it, and which we treat here as an explanatory hypothesis rather than as part of the definition.

Conditional prediction difficulty is also distinct from the uncertainty captured by confidence intervals: while confidence intervals reflect statistical precision at the aggregate level, conditional prediction difficulty refers to the degree to which a given individual's outcome can be anticipated from baseline characteristics. A model may produce narrow confidence intervals while still generating systematically large errors for specific patients whose relevant sources of variance remain unobserved. Finally, the construct is by definition relative to a given predictor set: evidence that a patient is difficult to predict from baseline clinical information does not imply that their outcome is unpredictable in principle.

In this modeling context, a large residual indicates that a patient's outcome departs from the group-level regularity linking baseline clinical characteristics to outcome. When such departures are idiosyncratic across models, they are best understood as noise. When they are shared across structurally diverse models and predictable from intake data, as we hypothesize here, they indicate that the mapping from baseline clinical status to outcome is weaker for some patients than for others. Residual magnitude is therefore clinically informative precisely because it identifies, at intake, the patients for whom intake information is least sufficient.

Building on the premise that prediction errors may be systematically patterned rather than purely stochastic, the present study shifts the focus from optimizing predictive accuracy to analyzing the nature of predictive failures. A key conceptual distinction is relevant here: whereas random error refers to chance-based difference between a predicted value and the observed, systematic error implies that outcomes are consistently mis-estimated for particular patients to a relevant degree across modeling approaches. If the magnitude of prediction error can itself be anticipated from baseline patient characteristics, then what appears as "error" at the aggregate level may in fact reflect systematic limitations of the available predictor set for specific individuals, rather than stochastic noise.

By examining the residuals generated by multiple ML algorithms, this study aims to determine whether conditional prediction difficulty is systematically associated with patient characteristics rather than with the choice of algorithm. Specifically, this study seeks to identify whether individuals with certain characteristics are consistently difficult to model regardless of the algorithm used and to uncover the baseline clinical and sociodemographic features that might distinguish these patients.

Based on the above rationale, the following hypotheses were tested. First, given evidence that structurally different algorithms trained on the same data frequently disagree at the individual level, we hypothesized that individual-level prediction errors would correlate positively across different machine learning algorithms, indicating that some patients are consistently more difficult to predict from baseline information regardless of the model type (H1). Second, we expected that patients could be partitioned into distinct subgroups based on their residual profiles, with these groups showing significant differences in the overall prediction error magnitude (H2). Third, we hypothesized that baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics would differ significantly between the identified groups (H3). Finally, we anticipated that a sparse multivariate model would identify a non-zero subset of baseline variables capable of discriminating between these groups, with selected predictors showing interpretable associations with the likelihood of better or worse prediction (H4).

Methods

Patients and Treatment

The present study is a secondary analysis of a naturalistic sample of outpatients (N = 1,858) treated at Osnabrück University between 2009 and 2023, of whom 65.93% were female. The mean age of the patients was 38.24 years (SD = 13.11), and they attended an average of 46.69 treatment sessions (SD = 25.31). Patients were treated by 220 therapists in a naturalistic clinical setting. While treatments remained consistent with Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) principles, therapists were not restricted to a single intervention manual; instead, they were encouraged to draw from diverse clinical sources or disorder-specific protocols in consultation with their supervisors. Clinical supervision was systematically provided every four sessions to ensure treatment integrity and support clinical decision-making. Diagnostic classifications were established through semi-structured clinical interviews (First et al., 1995) in accordance with the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD-10; World Health Organization, 2016). The most prevalent primary diagnoses were mood (affective) disorders (F30-F39, 46.99%), and neurotic, stressrelated, and somatoform disorders (F40-F49, 39.29%), while the remaining 13.72% presented with other primary conditions.

Comorbidity was reported in 49.95% of the patients.

Comprehensive patient characteristics are detailed in the Supplemental Material 1. Study eligibility required the completion of a baseline assessment battery and formal commencement of treatment following the diagnostic phase. Individuals presenting with acute mania, active suicidality, or psychotic symptoms during the intake interview were excluded from the analysis.

The study protocol was conducted in strict adherence to the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and all procedures complied with national and institutional ethical standards for research involving human subjects.

Measures

Outcome Questionnaire-30 (OQ-30)

To monitor therapeutic change, we used the OQ-30, a 30-item abbreviated version of the OQ-45 (Ellsworth et al., 2006). This self-report instrument assesses three primary domains: symptomatic distress, interpersonal functioning, and social role performance. Higher cumulative scores indicate greater functional impairment. In the present study, the OQ-30 demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .91).

Questionnaire for the Evaluation of Psychotherapeutic Progress (FEP-2)

Therapeutic progress and clinical outcomes were assessed using the FEP-2 (Lutz, Tholen, et al., 2006). This 40-item scale employs a 5-point Likert system (ranging from "never" to "always") to evaluate well-being, interpersonal relationships, symptomatology, and goalrelated incongruence. The internal consistency of the FEP-2 in this sample was good (α = 0.73).

Inventory of Interpersonal Problems-32 (IIP-32)

Interpersonal difficulties were measured using the German short form of the IIP-32 (IIP-32; Barkham, Hardy & Startup, 1996). Participants rated 32 items on a 5-point scale (0 = not at all to4 = extremely), identifying specific interpersonal excesses or inhibitions. The scale's eight subscales (domineering, vindictive, cold, socially inhibited, non-assertive, overly accommodating, self-sacrificing, and intrusive) correspond to the interpersonal circumplex model. Reliability in this sample was high (α = 0.87).

Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)

The Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI; Derogatis, 1993) was used as a concise measure of psychological and physical distress. The BSI assesses nine primary dimensions: somatization, obsessive-compulsive disorder, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychoticism. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to4 = extremely) based on the distress experienced over the past week. The Global Severity Index (GSI) was used as the primary outcome metric. The BSI items demonstrated excellent internal reliability (α = 0.97).

Questionnaire on Emotional Well-being (EMO)

Emotional states over the preceding weeks were evaluated using the EMO questionnaire (Questionnaire for Emotional Well-being, EMO; Bartram & Rogner, 1994). This 30-item questionnaire is structured into six dimensions, capturing three negative affective states (anxious-depressive, deactivation, and anxious-excited) and three positive states (anxietyfree-relaxed, activation, and euphoric). Responses were recorded on a 4-point frequency scale (0 = not at all to3 = often). The instrument showed robust internal consistency in the current sample (α = 0.93).

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke, Spitzer & Williams, 2001) is a 9-item self-report measure assessing the DSM-IV criteria for major depressive disorder. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), yielding total scores between 0 and 27. Scores between 5 and 9 indicate mild depression, while scores between 10 and 14 indicate moderate depression. The instrument demonstrated adequate internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .86-.89).

Data Preprocessing

Analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.1; R Core Team, 2025). Model training and validation were performed using the caret package (v7.0.1; Kuhn, 2008). To ensure unbiased estimation, we employed a nested cross-validation approach with 5 outer folds (for model evaluation) and 5 inner folds (for hyperparameter tuning). Prior to modeling, data were preprocessed within each training fold using k-Nearest Neighbors (k = 5) imputation, centering, and scaling to prevent data leakage. To capture diverse inductive biases, we trained 15 different machine learning algorithms under identical CV conditions. The modeling series included linear and regularized models, such as Generalized Linear Models (GLM), Bayesian Generalized Linear Models (bayesglm), and Regularized Generalized Linear Models

Model-Level Correlation and Systematic Predictability (H1)

To test whether prediction difficulty is consistent across algorithms, we constructed a correlation matrix of the out-of-fold residuals for all 15 algorithms. To ensure the reliability of the correlation estimates, patients were included in this analysis only if they provided nonmissing residuals for at least two of the models. Consistent with standard psychometric and statistical conventions (Cohen, 1988), we interpreted inter-model correlations above r = .70 as indicating strong systematic agreement, suggesting that a substantial proportion of prediction error is shared across modeling approaches.

To ensure the robustness of these findings, we employed a bootstrapping procedure with 2,000 iterations. For each resample, we calculated the average off-diagonal correlation (representing the mean shared error between the different algorithms) to derive a 95% confidence interval (CI). This approach allowed us to determine the stability of the observed systematic predictability and to confirm that the shared error patterns were not driven by specific outliers or sampling bias. Furthermore, we conducted a permutation test (2,000 iterations) to compare the observed average correlation against the distribution of mean intermodel correlations expected under the assumption of no systematic patient-level effect (that is, a scenario in which prediction errors are unrelated to patient identity across algorithms). In each iteration, the residuals of each algorithm were independently shuffled across patients, and the mean off-diagonal correlation was recomputed. The proportion of permuted statistics equal to or exceeding the observed value yielded a one-sided p-value. Finally, we performed hierarchical clustering (using 1 -r distances) and the average linkage method to visualize families of models with similar error patterns using a heatmap.

Patient Clustering by Residual Profiles (H2)

To operationalize individual predictability, patients were partitioned into distinct subgroups based on their residual profiles across all models. We applied k-means clustering to the standardized residual matrix using Euclidean distance, where the optimal number of clusters (k) was determined by the Silhouette index (evaluated for k = 2-15) and confirmed through bootstrap stability (B = 2000, k range = 2-15). To validate the integrity of the cluster solution, we planned a one-way ANOVA to compare the average Mean Absolute Residual (MAR) across the identified groups. This operationalization is based on distance rather than direction: patients were grouped according to how far their observed outcomes fell from model predictions, irrespective of whether those outcomes were over-or underestimated. The clusters therefore represent differences in the magnitude of prediction error, not in any systematic tendency to mis-estimate outcomes in a particular direction. Groups were labeled based on their average mean absolute residual, distinguishing "well-predicted" from "hard-topredict" patients.

Characterizing the "Hard-to-Predict" Subgroup (H3 & H4)

To investigate the clinical and sociodemographic markers of prediction difficulty (H3), we compared baseline characteristics between the clusters. Univariate comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and x 2 or Fisher's exact tests for categorical data. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR). To identify the most robust predictors of predictive failure (H4), we developed a multivariate LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection

Operator) logistic regression model with the glmnet package (v4.1.10; Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani, 2010), using cluster membership as the outcome variable. Given that the number of clusters was determined empirically, a binomial logistic regression was planned in the case of a two-cluster solution, and a multinomial logistic regression in the case of three or more clusters. This approach allowed us to identify a sparse subset of baseline variables that best discriminates between patient clusters. The model used a penalty parameter (λ) to perform simultaneous variable selection and regularization, effectively shrinking non-informative coefficients to zero. The optimal penalty was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, specifically selecting λ min , which is the value that minimizes the out-of-sample deviance (i.e., the point where prediction error was lowest when testing the model on unseen folds). Unlike the nested cross-validation employed for the predictive models, a single cross-validation loop was considered sufficient here, given that the purpose of the LASSO was variable selection rather than unbiased performance estimation. Because LASSO coefficients are subject to shrinkage bias, which complicates traditional inference, we adopted a post-selection estimation approach. The variables retained by the LASSO at λ min were re-entered into a standard logistic regression (GLM). This allowed us to calculate unshrunken Odds Ratios (OR) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI), providing a more reliable estimation of effect sizes and statistical significance for the dimensions that signal a higher likelihood of predictive failure.

An OR >1 indicated that higher baseline scores in a given dimension increased the likelihood of a patient being harder to predict, whereas an OR < 1 indicated a reduced likelihood of a patient being less predictable. To quantify the overall explanatory capacity of the post-selection model, pseudo-R² statistics (McFadden and Nagelkerke) were computed using the DescTools package. As no true R² exists for logistic regression, these statistics approximate the proportion of variance in cluster membership explained by the model:

McFadden's R² reflects the proportional reduction in the log-likelihood relative to a null model containing no predictors, while Nagelkerke's R² rescales this estimate to range between 0 and 1, allowing for a more intuitive interpretation analogous to the R² in linear regression.

Transparency

The study utilizes de-identified secondary data. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the study design, hypotheses, and analytic plan were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF), where all analytic scripts are publicly available: https://osf.io/yqd25/overview?view_only=6e8f575b330d414ea396950ae88b88fc.

Results

Prior to examining inter-model consistency, we report the overall predictive performance of the 15 algorithms across the nested cross-validation folds. All models predicted end-oftreatment BSI-GSI scores using the full set of baseline sociodemographic and clinical variables. Model performance varied moderately across algorithms (see Table 1), with RMSE ranging from 0.447 to 0.548 and R² ranging from .09 to .26. The best-performing model was random forest (RMSE = 0.447, MAE = 0.330, R² = .26), while the monotone multilayer perceptron showed the weakest performance (RMSE = 0.548, MAE = 0.403, R² = .09). These values are consistent with the typical predictive ceiling observed in psychotherapy outcome research when using baseline information alone, and provide the necessary context for interpreting the residual-based analyses that follow.

Systematic Predictability and Inter-Model Consistency (H1)

To test our first hypothesis (H1), we examined whether prediction errors were consistent across the 15 machine learning algorithms. The analysis revealed a high observed mean offdiagonal correlation among the residuals (r = .89). The bootstrapping procedure (B = 2,000) demonstrated that this estimate was highly stable, yielding a narrow 95% confidence interval [0.884, 0.903].

To further validate these findings, we performed a permutation test (2,000 iterations) to compare the observed correlation against a null distribution in which no systematic patient-level effect exists. The permutation test yielded p < .001, indicating that inter-model agreement of this magnitude was not observed in any of the 2,000 permuted datasets. Furthermore, the hierarchical clustering of model residuals (Figure 1) confirmed that error patterns were shared across all families of algorithms (except for the multi-layer perceptron neural network), regardless of their inductive biases (e.g., tree-based vs. linear models). These results support our first hypothesis, indicating that prediction difficulty is not an artifact of specific modeling techniques but is systematically associated with the patients themselves, given the baseline information available. Patient Subgroups and Prediction Difficulty (H2)

The clustering analysis supported the existence of systematic patient-level patterns in prediction difficulty (H2). Although bootstrap stability suggested a three-cluster solution (k = 3), the Silhouette index identified k = 2 as the most robust and parsimonious fit for the data.

Consequently, a two-cluster solution was adopted. This resulted in two clearly differentiated groups: a "Higher residual" cluster (HR; n = 379) and a "Lower residual" cluster (LR; n = 1,479). One-way ANOVA confirmed that these clusters represented statistically distinct levels of prediction difficulty (F(1, 1856) = 999.0, p < .001). As expected, the HR group exhibited a significantly larger mean error (MAR = 0.69) than the LR group (MAR = 0.26).

This substantial gap in error magnitude across 15 different algorithms indicates that the clusters captured a consistent dimension of prediction difficulty, concentrating predictive failure within a specific 20.4% of the sample.

Clinical Markers of Prediction Difficulty(H3 & H4)

Univariate comparisons revealed widespread baseline differences between the clusters. After FDR adjustment, 167 of the 227 variables (74.01%) significantly distinguished the two groups (p adj < .05) (See Supplementary Material 1). Multivariate LASSO logistic regression further refined this profile by shrinking the initial pool of 167 variables to 23 key predictors.

To account for the shrinkage bias inherent in LASSO and to provide traditional statistical inference, these 23 predictors were re-entered into a post-selection logistic regression (GLM) model. This two-step approach allowed for the calculation of un-shrunken Odds Ratios (OR) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI), identifying which baseline dimensions significantly signaled a higher (OR > 1.0) or lower (OR < 1.0) likelihood of being harder to predict. These results confirm H3 and H4. While patients with higher social support and more typical depressive symptoms follow more predictable treatment trajectories, those characterized by somatic distress, emotional inhibition, and interpersonal fears represent a specific challenge for standard algorithmic modeling. This distinction suggests that prediction difficulty is systematically associated with baseline clinical characteristics rather than reflecting random noise within the sample.

Discussion

The present study examined whether prediction difficulty in psychotherapy outcome research is systematically associated with patients rather than being a byproduct of model misspecification or stochastic noise. By analyzing the residual profiles generated by 15 machine learning algorithms applied to a large naturalistic outpatient sample, we sought to determine whether certain individuals are systematically harder to predict regardless of the modeling approach employed, and whether such individuals can be characterized using baseline sociodemographic and clinical variables.

The first hypothesis posited that prediction errors would correlate positively across different machine learning algorithms, indicating a shared, patient-level source of predictive difficulty. This hypothesis was strongly supported by the results. The observed mean offdiagonal inter-model correlation was r = .89 (95% CI [.884, .903]), which substantially exceeded the threshold of r = .70 conventionally used to indicate strong systematic agreement (Cohen, 1988). The permutation test confirmed that this level of inter-model consistency could not be attributed to chance (p < .001), and hierarchical clustering of residuals revealed that shared error patterns held across algorithmically distinct model families (including linear, tree-based, and neural network architectures) with the partial exception of the monotone multi-layer perceptron. These results have methodological implications for evaluating and reporting predictive performance in psychotherapy research. The high degree of inter-model agreement suggests that a substantial portion of prediction error is not attributable to model configuration, but is rather specific to certain patients. This reframes the optimization of predictive accuracy as a necessary but insufficient goal. Prediction from baseline information was difficult across the sample, as reflected in the modest R² values obtained, but the resulting errors were not evenly distributed: they were substantially larger for a subgroup of patients whose clinical presentations exceed the explanatory capacity of the available predictors. The partial exception of the monotone multilayer perceptron may suggest that certain nonlinear architectures capture the dimensions of clinical variation that other models do not, a possibility that warrants targeted investigation in future studies.

The second hypothesis proposed that patients could be partitioned into distinct subgroups based on their cross-algorithm residual profiles, with these groups differing significantly in overall prediction error magnitude. This hypothesis was confirmed. Although bootstrap stability analyses favored a three-cluster solution, the Silhouette index identified a two-cluster solution as the most parsimonious and structurally robust partition. The resulting groups (a "Lower Residual" (LR) cluster (n = 1,479) and a "Higher Residual" (HR) cluster (n = 379)) differed markedly in their mean absolute residuals (MAR = 0.26 vs. MAR = 0.69, respectively), with this difference reaching statistical significance in the one-way ANOVA (F(1, 1856) = 999.0, p < .001). The stability of this partition across 2,000 bootstrap resamples supports the interpretation that it reflects a genuine structural feature of the data rather than sampling variability.

The third and fourth hypotheses predicted that the HR cluster would differ from the LR cluster in baseline characteristics (H3), and that a sparse multivariate model would identify a subset of those variables as robust discriminators (H4). Both hypotheses were supported. Univariate comparisons revealed widespread baseline differentiation, with 167 of the 227 tested variables (74.01%) significantly distinguishing the two groups after FDR correction. LASSO regularization subsequently reduced this set to 23 key predictors, which were then entered into a post-selection logistic regression to obtain unshrunken effect size estimates.

Of the 23 variables retained by the LASSO, several can be grouped into three broad domains. We note that this grouping was not specified in advance and constitutes an interpretive summary rather than a tested structure. Somatic and body-related distress, indexed by hot-cold sensations (OR = 1.23) and breathing difficulty (OR = 1.19), showed the strongest associations with membership in the higher-residual cluster. Emotional inhibition, specifically difficulty in emotional expression (OR = 1.20), and interpersonal anxiety, indexed by fear of social rejection (OR = 1.19), showed associations of similar magnitude. In the opposite direction, marital status (OR = 0.57) showed the strongest association with lower likelihood of higher-residual membership, followed by core depressive symptoms (OR = 0.78). These associations are open to several interpretations, none of which can be adjudicated with the present data. One possibility is that items of this kind index clinical features whose course depends more heavily on circumstances arising during treatment than on baseline clinical status, which would be consistent with the shared-residual pattern observed across algorithms. On this reading, the comparatively lower residuals among married patients and among those presenting with prototypical depressive profiles would reflect greater correspondence between intake information and subsequent course, rather than any protective property of these characteristics as such. Testing this interpretation would require process and contextual data collected during treatment, and we advance it as a hypothesis for future work rather than as an account of the present findings. Individual itemlevel associations should not be given substantive weight in isolation, particularly as these are associations with membership in a cluster derived from the same dataset.

Importantly, and consistent with a precision mental health perspective, the identifiability of this profile at baseline suggests that the degree to which a patient's outcome can be anticipated from intake data may be a clinically meaningful quantity, rather than merely a statistical byproduct of measurement error or model misspecification.

Clinical Implications

The findings of the present study have several practical implications, especially for deploying machine learning tools in psychotherapy settings. First, they suggest that aggregate predictive performance metrics, while necessary, may be insufficient on their own for clinical translation. Aggregate metrics describe the average level of predictive error but not its distribution across patients, and a model with a given overall performance may be considerably less accurate for a clinically meaningful subset of cases. This does not imply that overall performance is uninformative, but rather, it highlights the value of complementing aggregate evaluation with individual-level uncertainty estimates, particularly in clinical contexts where predictions are applied to one patient at a time. Therefore, decision support systems in psychotherapy contexts should complement point predictions with individual-level uncertainty estimates. Second, the identifiability of the hard-to-predict subgroup using baseline characteristics opens the possibility of developing a predictability screener: a brief set of baseline indicators that could flag, at intake, those patients for whom algorithmic predictions are likely to be less reliable. Such a screener could prompt clinicians to supplement algorithmic outputs with enhanced clinical assessments, continuous progress monitoring, or case consultations, particularly when prediction difficulty co-occurs with a prediction of poor treatment outcome, as this combination may represent the highest-priority cases for clinical attention. Third, the high stability of predictive failure across models suggests that a small, diverse ensemble may be sufficient to identify hard-to-predict patients, without requiring large model libraries. Future work should investigate the minimum number of models needed to approximate these estimates. Finally, and more broadly, the results underscore the value of studying predictive difficulty not merely as a methodological problem but as a clinically meaningful signal.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First, future research should focus on replicating these findings by extending the predictor space to incorporate dynamic treatment-related variables (such as early symptom change, therapeutic alliance trajectories, and other within-treatment processes), allowing a better characterization of the sources of prediction difficulty.Second, the study was conducted within a single university outpatient clinic primarily using CBT-oriented treatments. While the naturalistic design enhances ecological validity, generalizability to other treatment modalities, healthcare systems, cultural contexts, or clinical populations remains uncertain. Replication across independent samples and treatment settings will be essential. Third, the residual-based clustering approach inevitably depends on analytic choices regarding preprocessing, clustering algorithms, and residual operationalization. More fundamentally, partitioning patients into discrete groups implies that predictability is a categorical phenomenon, whereas it is more plausibly a continuous dimension. The two-cluster solution adopted here, while statistically justified and clinically interpretable, necessarily obscures variation within groups and may oversimplify the underlying structure. Future studies employing diverse methodological approaches may help refine the taxonomy of patient-level predictability. Fourth, the LASSO model was restricted to main effects and did not explore interaction terms between predictors. It is therefore possible that combinations of baseline characteristics, rather than individual variables, may carry additional information about predictive failure that the current model was unable to detect. Future work should consider interaction regularization approaches to address this possibility. Finally, the present study focused on overall treatment outcome prediction and did not examine temporal fluctuations in predictability throughout therapy. It is possible that some patients become more or less predictable at different treatment stages.

Longitudinal analyses examining dynamic changes in predictive reliability may therefore provide important additional insights.

Conclusion

The present study provides empirical evidence that prediction difficulty in psychotherapy outcome research is systematically associated with patients rather than with the choice of algorithm. Across 15 machine learning algorithms representing diverse architectural families, residual correlations were high and statistically consistent, and patients could be reliably partitioned into well-predicted and hard-to-predict subgroups. The hard-to-predict group, comprising approximately one-fifth of the sample, was characterized at baseline by somatic distress, emotional inhibition, and interpersonal problems. These findings suggest that the degree to which a patient's outcome can be anticipated from intake data is itself clinically patterned, with direct implications for the design, evaluation, and communication of algorithmic decision support tools.
