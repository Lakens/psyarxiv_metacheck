Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Depression is one of the most prevalent and disabling mental health conditions worldwide, with an estimated 5.7% of adults currently experiencing depression, around 332 million people globally. Rumination, a repetitive pattern of thinking that involves comparing one's circumstances with how one would ideally like them to be without engaging in problem-solving, is the cognitive vulnerability most strongly associated with depression. Despite the evidence, there are no studies that have employed causal discovery and inference to investigate whether rumination has a causal effect on depressive mood using N-of-1 psychological time series data. To address this gap, this study conducted a secondary analysis of publicly available data on an American Latino Female diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) who reported on her feelings 4 times per day over 39 days. To conduct causal discovery, the Vector Autoregressive Linear Non-Gaussian Acyclic Model (VAR-LiNGAM) algorithm was used. To conduct causal inference, the g-formula, implemented via a Monte Carlo simulation, was used to estimate the Contemporaneous Effect, the 1-lag Structural Direct Effect and the 1-lag Total Effect of rumination on depressed mood. The findings indicated that high rumination was associated with a 15.6-point increase in depressed mood contemporaneously and a 1.7-point increase in depressed mood approximately 4 hours later. This evidence further substantiates the importance of interventions designed to reduce ruminative thinking and, consequently, improve depressive symptoms.

1 Introduction

Depression is one of the most prevalent and disabling mental health conditions worldwide, with an estimated 5.7% of adults currently experiencing depression, around 332 million people globally (World Health Organization, 2025), and an estimated lifetime prevalence of 10.8% (Lim et al., 2018). Depression is characterised by persistent low mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure in everyday activities, often accompanied by feelings of hopelessness or pessimism about the future, impaired concentration, changes in sleep and appetite, and psychomotor disturbances, such as restlessness or feeling "on edge" (Liu et al., 2015; Schrijvers et al., 2008). Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) carries one of the highest suicide risks of any mental disorder. A meta-analysis of 26 population-based registry studies indicated that individuals with MDD were 8.6 (95% CI: [5.7, 13.0]) times more likely to die by suicide compared to the general population (Arnone et al., 2024), and a meta-analysis of 27 studies involving 3,275 suicidal deaths indicated that mood disorders were present in 43.2% of cases (Arsenault-Lapierre et al., 2004). Furthermore, the burden is disproportionate for certain groups. For instance, a population-based survey of nearly 393,000 American adults showed a higher lifetime depression prevalence among women (24%) than men (13%) and among White (22%) compared to Hispanic (15%) adults (Lee et al., 2023).

Rumination, a repetitive pattern of thinking focused on one's distress, its causes, and its consequences (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991), is the cognitive vulnerability with the strongest research evidence supporting its effect on depression (Visted et al., 2018). Rumination contributes to the onset and maintenance of depression, particularly through brooding. Brooding involves comparing one's circumstances with how one would ideally like them to be but without engaging in problem-solving, instead of reflective pondering, which involves deliberate thinking efforts to gain insight into the nature of the problem and develop solutions (Treynor et al., 2003). A meta-analysis of 34 studies including 2,311 participants found that individuals with current MDD reported substantially greater rumination than healthy controls (Hedges' g = 2.10, 95% CI [1.90, 2.30]), the largest difference compared to other emotional regulation strategies, such as avoidance (Hedges' g = 1.29, 95% CI [0.93, 1.65]) and suppression (Hedges' g = 1.12, 95% CI [0.64, 1.59]) (Visted et al., 2018). Furthermore, a meta-analysis of 36 randomised controlled trials involving 3,307 participants indicated that reductions in repetitive negative thinking were strongly associated with reductions in depressive symptoms (β = 0.72, 95% CI [0.46, 0.99]). In addition, Rumination-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), a specialised form of CBT that teaches individuals how to change abstract ruminative thinking ("Why does this always happen to me?") into problem-focused thinking ("What can I do next?"), showed the strongest effect in reducing repetitive negative thinking (Hedges' g = 0.76, 95% CI [0.40, 1.12]) when compared to traditional CBT (Hedges' g = 0.63, 95% CI [0.42, 0.85]) (Spinhoven et al., 2018). Despite its maladaptive role, depressed individuals report perceiving rumination as useful, believing that repeatedly thinking about their emotions can help them find the causes and triggers of their depression, understand previous mistakes and prevent future ones, and make sense of past experiences (E. Watkins & Moulds, 2005).

Rumination is theorised to cause depressive symptoms (Nolen-Hoeksema, 2000) through causal mechanisms that operate over minutes or hours (Moberly & Watkins, 2008; Nolen-Hoeksema & Morrow, 1993). As such, recent studies have started using ecological momentary assessment (EMA), where participants use a smartphone to report on behaviours, cognitions and emotions several times a day, to understand the effects of rumination on depression as they unfold in everyday life. In a study among 93 English adults, in which participants reported on their thoughts and feelings 8 times per day over 7 days, rumination showed a concurrent (within-person, omitted hereafter for simplicity) association with negative affect (β = 0.50, SE = 0.03) and a temporal association with negative affect around one and a half hours later (β = 0.11, SE = 0.03) (Moberly & Watkins, 2008). In a subsequent study among 200 Belgian university students, assessed 10 times per day over 7 days, rumination was temporally associated with negative affect (β = 0.04, 95% CI [0.02, 0.06]) and negative affect was temporally associated with rumination (β = 0.12, 95% CI [0.10, 0.14]) around one hour later (Blanke et al., 2022). Overall, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 37 EMA studies provided robust evidence that rumination was temporally associated with negative affect (β = 0.16, 95% CI [0.07, 0.26]) over the subsequent hours (Boemo et al., 2022).

The network theory of psychopathology (Borsboom, 2017), developed over the past two decades, provides an important theoretical framework to conceptualise the causal effect of rumination on depressive symptoms (Hoebeke et al., 2024). According to the network theory, cognitions, behaviours and emotions can establish mutually reinforcing causal effects, becoming self-sustaining and constituting psychopathology (Borsboom, 2017). For example, sad mood and helplessness may trigger rumination, which in turn intensifies sad mood, leading to further increases in rumination and thereby contributing to the persistent nature of depressive symptoms (Ebrahimi et al., 2021). Despite the strong overlap with cognitive behavioural theories, which traditionally postulated causal effects between symptoms, such as that thoughts cause emotions and behaviours (Robinaugh et al., 2020), one fundamental contribution of network theory (which also incorporates concepts from network science, such as symptom clustering, to describe psychopathology) was to propose that the (network containing all these) effects could be estimated from psychological data (Borsboom, 2017). The network theory led to the development of statistical models (and the implementation of statistical methods used in other fields) that have enabled researchers to estimate a person-specific network for each participant of an EMA study (Bringmann et al., 2013), and also aggregate these multiple person-specific networks into a group-level network to understand which effects between symptoms are experienced by several participants (Vitanza et al., 2026). Other researchers have conducted studies focusing specifically on evaluating and interpreting the person-specific network of a single participant (i.e., N-of-1) of an EMA study, without conducting group aggregation (Bak et al., 2016; Santiago et al., 2026).

Given the causal nature of network theory (Borsboom, 2017), researchers are increasingly adopting causal discovery and causal inference methods to investigate psychological networks (K. Park et al., 2026; Ribeiro Santiago et al., 2025; Santiago et al., 2026; Waldorp et al., 2026), particularly being explicit about the causal (instead of descriptive or predictive; Hernán et al. (2019)) intent of the analysis (with the use of causal language; Hernán (2018)) and incorporating confounding considerations (Huang et al., 2023). While established statistical methods enabled the estimation of the association between symptoms in a personspecific network, recent methodological developments have enabled the discovery, identification and estimation of causal effects between symptoms in a person-specific network, using intensive longitudinal data (i.e., time series data) from a single participant (N-of-1) of an EMA study (Cai et al., 2026; Hyvärinen et al., 2010). Despite these advances, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies that have used causal discovery and inference methods to specifically investigate whether rumination has a causal effect on (instead of predicting; Moberly & Watkins (2008)) depressive symptoms using N-of-1 time series data. To address this research gap, we conducted a secondary analysis of time series data of a single individual diagnosed with MDD who answered questions related to mood 4 times a day over approximately 30 days (Fisher et al., 2017). The purpose of the analysis is to estimate the causal effect(s) of rumination on depressive symptoms. The analysis followed the subsequent analytical steps: (1) descriptive analysis; (2) causal discovery; and (3) causal inference.

2 Methods

2.1 Study design and participants

The study used publicly available time series data from a study by Fisher et al. (2017), which evaluated mood and anxiety symptoms in 40 individuals clinically diagnosed with MDD and/or generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) prior to initiating cognitive-behavioural therapy. The participants used their smartphones to report on their behaviours, cognitions, and emotions 4 times per day for approximately 30 days. The assessments (prompted by smartphone notifications, referred to as "beeps") were scheduled quasi-randomly within 4 equal assessment windows across the 12 hours following each participant's self-reported wake-up time and separated by at least 30 minutes (for further information, see Fisher et al., 2017; Fisher & Bosley, 2020). Given the current study aim to evaluate rumination in a single individual, which requires a substantial number of measurements for causal discovery and estimation (Kummerfeld et al., 2024; Stevenson et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025), we selected the participant who met the criteria of the largest number of measurement occasions and at least 90% complete data on the study variables (Jones et al., 2019). The participant initially selected ("P007") was an American Black female aged between 25 and 30 years (range provided to maintain participant anonymity) with a primary clinical diagnosis of MDD and multiple comorbidities (e.g., GAD, agoraphobia). However, visual and statistical inspection of the time series (Supplementary Figure 1) revealed, starting around day 26, several sequences of consecutive responses with the value of 50 (i.e., the midpoint of the visual analogue scale) across all study variables, strongly suggesting a decline in measurement quality (Vogelsmeier et al., 2026). For this reason, the second participant who met the criteria was selected ("P001"), as no comparable evidence of non-optimal responding was observed (Figure 1). The participant was an American Latino female aged between 18 and 24 years with a primary clinical diagnosis of MDD and with GAD and panic disorder as comorbidities. During the study, the participant received 152 beeps, with an average of 3.9 beeps per day (SD=0.4), over 39 days. The average time between assessments was 4.0 hours (SD=0.4) during the daytime, and 12.2 hours (SD=1.1) overnight.

The original study was approved by the University of California Institutional Review Board, Committee for Protection of Human Subjects (Protocol No. 2014-03-6138; Personalized Interventions for Anxiety and Depression). The current study is a secondary analysis of de-identified, publicly available data from this study, obtained through the openESM database (Siepe & Kloft, 2026), with the original data publicly available on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/5ybxt/), so ethical review and approval were not required in accordance with local legislation and Adelaide University requirements.

2.2 Measures

For the purposes of the current study, the items included in the daily assessment measured rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991) and the two core symptoms of MDD, which are depressed mood (measured as feeling down) and anhedonia (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Considering the evidence that rumination increases hopelessness (Sarin et al., 2005) and physiological arousal (Ottaviani et al., 2016), and both are constituents of depression (Kroenke et al., 2001), items measuring hopelessness and restlessness were also included. Beginning with the item stem "To what degree have you. . . ", the five items assessed were: (1) down ("felt down or depressed?") ; (2) anhedonia ("experienced loss of interest or pleasure?") ; rumination ("dwelled on the past?") ; hopeless ("felt hopeless?") ; and restless ("felt restless?"). Importantly, while rumination was evaluated through dwelling on the past (Fisher et al., 2017), single-item measures can be limited in measuring complex psychological constructs, so this item may not capture all processes implicated in rumination (e.g., negative thinking) (Song et al., 2023). The items were rated on a 0-100 scale, anchored from 0 ("none at all") to 100 ("as much as possible"), with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity.

2.3 Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted with R software version 4.5.3, using R packages mtsdi (Junger & De Leon, 2015), kcpRS (Cabrieto et al., 2019) and KFAS (Helske, 2017), and Python software, using the Python package lingam (Ikeuchi et al., 2023). Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools (ChatGPT, OpenAI, and Claude, Anthropic) were used to assist with programming the analysis code. All code was reviewed and verified by the authors, who take full responsibility for the analyses and results. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the R script (including Python code) for this study can be found in the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/7q8u4). This study was not pre-registered. Across the time series, wave missingness, occurring when all items have missing information at a measurement occasion (likely due to the participant receiving the beep but skipping the assessment) (Burger et al., 2022). Despite the high assessment completion rate by this participant (>90.0%), the proportion of missingness (9.9%) can still introduce biases in parameter estimation (>5%, Graham, 2009). As such, we conducted single imputation using a modified Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm with smoothing splines, a method which showed good accuracy under distinct patterns of missingness (and even under missing not at random) (Junger & De Leon, 2015), resulting in an imputed sample with 152 observations.

2.3.1 Descriptive analysis

In the descriptive analysis, to examine the contemporaneous and temporal associations between symptoms, we conducted a cross-correlation function analysis. More specifically, we used Pearson's correlation coefficient to examine contemporaneous associations at lag-0, where lag is defined as the number of intervals between two measurement occasions, and temporal associations from lag-1 to lag-6 (pragmatically chosen as the maximum lag since, for psychological processes, temporal associations are frequently strongest with the symptoms measured at the preceding assessment and tend to weaken rapidly as the lag increases; Siepe et al. (2025)).

2.3.2 Causal discovery

The second stage of the analysis was causal discovery, which uses an algorithm to identify the causal structure, including contemporaneous and temporal causal effects, from the data. More specifically, we employed the Vector Autoregressive (VAR) Linear Non-Gaussian Acyclic Model (VAR-LiNGAM) algorithm (Hyvärinen et al., 2010), considered one of the best-performing causal discovery algorithms for time series with small sample sizes (Niu et al., 2024). Given a set of assumptions (such as linearity, independent non-Gaussian errors and causal sufficiency, presumed to hold hereafter), the VAR-LiNGAM algorithm can identify a (time-unrolled) Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), where nodes represent symptoms and directed edges represent direct causal effects between symptoms (Hyvärinen et al., 2010). To discover the causal structure, the VAR-LiNGAM algorithm implements the VAR model to estimate preliminary lagged associations, with optimal lag order (in this study, up to the arbitrarily chosen maximum of 4) selected according to the minimum BIC, then exploits residual asymmetries to identify the direction of contemporaneous effects (the intuition is that, when two variables are considered and residuals are non-Gaussian, the residual from regressing the effect on its cause is independent of the cause, but the residual from regressing the cause on its effect is not independent of the effect), and then finally combines these estimates to recover both contemporaneous and lagged causal effects (Hyvärinen et al., 2010). The assumption that residuals are non-Gaussian is plausible for psychological data (Shi et al., 2025), since around 3 out of 4 psychological outcomes are non-Gaussian (Cain et al., 2017), requiring (according to Cramer's Decomposition Theorem) at least the exposure or the residual to be non-Gaussian (Glymour et al., 2019).

Considering the large sample requirements for causal discovery of N-of-1 time series, researchers have argued that conducting power analysis to investigate whether the available sample size (i.e., number of measurement occasions) is adequate should be considered the "bare minimum" (Kummerfeld et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). As such, in our study, we conducted a simulation-based (post-hoc) power analysis to evaluate whether the VAR-LiNGAM was sufficiently powered to discover the causal structure. More specifically, for each sample size (50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300) commonly observed in EMA studies, we simulated 500 time series generated according to distinct DAGs with 5 variables, contemporaneous and lagged causal effects with moderate standardised effect size (0.30) and plausible edge density (0.40, Constantin et al., 2026), and Laplacian (i.e., non-Gaussian) errors. The VAR-LiNGAM was then fitted to each simulated dataset, and we evaluated its sensitivity ("power"), precision, and specificity to detect lagged effects, contemporaneous adjacency (i.e., the presence of a contemporaneous effect) and contemporaneous direction (i.e., the direction of a contemporaneous effect) (Kummerfeld et al., 2024), with values equal to or higher than .80 indicating adequate performance (Zhang et al., 2025). The power analysis informed whether the current N-of-1 study sample size provided adequate power for VAR-LiNGAM to recover the underlying causal structure. As a means of sensitivity analysis, we also investigate distinct standardised effect sizes (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5) and robustness to violations of the VAR-LiNGAM assumptions (i.e., Gaussian errors).

If the current N-of-1 study was adequately powered, before the application of the VAR-LiNGAM algorithm, the next step was regime identification. The reason is that the VAR-LiNGAM algorithm requires the assumption of causal stationarity, occurring when the causal structure remains stable (i.e., the same causal effects are present) within a period (Runge, 2018), and these periods can be referred to as regimes (Saggioro et al., 2020; Santiago et al., 2026). In psychological processes, such regimes may correspond to periods where behaviours and the effects established between them are stable, such as depressive and manic states in bipolar disorder, recovery and relapse in substance use disorders, and binge-eating and restrictive-eating in eating disorders, among others (Hamaker et al., 2010; Santiago et al., 2026).

Importantly, when an exposure (e.g., rumination at time t-1) has a temporal causal effect on two outcomes (e.g., rumination and depressed mood at time t), changing one causal effect changes the contemporaneous correlation of the outcomes and, consequently, the contemporaneous correlation matrix (Wright, 1934). When several exposures and outcomes are considered, changes in multiple contemporaneous and temporal causal effects (i.e., changes in the causal structure) will generally change the contemporaneous correlation matrix, and the preservation of the contemporaneous correlation matrix due to changes in effects cancelling each other "will rarely occur" in practice (Pearl, 2009, p. 63; Wang et al., 2018) Therefore, considering that changes in the contemporaneous correlation matrix generally indicate changes in the causal structure (a rationale that underlies the use of the contemporaneous covariance matrix to identify regimes with distinct causal structures, Ahmad et al., 2024), we explore this possibility with the use of multivariate Kernel Change Point detection on Running Statistics (KCP-RS). More specifically, we used KCP-RS to identify change points that separated the time series into regimes where there were differences in the (running) contemporaneous correlations (Cabrieto et al., 2019), potentially indicating a distinct underlying causal structure (this interpretation, however, should be made with caution, since the performance of the KCP-RS or other methods to detect changes in the underlying causal structure in psychological data is unknown and constitutes an important future avenue of methodological research). In the current study, the KCP-RS algorithm evaluated a maximum of 3 regimes, with a sliding window of 30 observations (accounting for 123 windows) and 1,000 permutations with an alpha level of 5% and Bonferroni correction. In case more than one regime was detected, the VAR-LiNGAM algorithm was applied separately to each regime. In each regime, the VAR-LiNGAM identified the causal structure, represented as a DAG.

2.3.3 Causal inference

Once the causal structure was discovered, the next step was causal inference to estimate the magnitude of the causal effects of interest. For the causal inference analysis, the rumination variable was dichotomised at the mean (M=57.1), which was close to the neutral value (50) of the visual analogue scale, and interpreted as low rumination (0; ≤ mean) and high rumination (1; > mean). In the current study, the causal estimands of interest were: (1) the Contemporaneous Effect of rumination on depressed mood (CE t = Y at=1 t -Y at=0 t ), defined as the contrast between the potential (outcome of) depressed mood at time t under high compared to low rumination at time t; (2) the 1-Lag structural Direct Effect (LDE ), defined as the contrast in potential depressed mood at time t under high compared to low rumination at time t -1, while holding other variables h at time t -1 and t (such as a mediator or exposure at time t) constant; and (3) the 1-Lag total Effect of rumination on depressed mood (LE (1) t (a t ) = Y at-1=1,at t -Y at-1=0,at t ), defined as the contrast between the potential depressed mood at time t under high compared to low rumination at time t -1, while keeping the exposure (i.e., rumination) at time t constant (Cai et al., 2026).

While the main outcome was depressed mood, we also investigated the effects of rumination on other depressive symptoms (e.g., hopelessness) as complementary outcomes. One important advantage of a within-person (i.e., N-of-1) study is that there is no confounding from time-invariant confounders, such as sex, age, socioeconomic position and ethnicity, since these characteristics (in most cases) stay constant during the study period (e.g., one month) (Mittleman & Mostofsky, 2014). However, the causal effects can still be biased due to time-varying confounding, including due to exposure-confounder feedback, which occurs when a time-varying confounder is caused by a previous exposure (Cai et al., 2026; Hernán & Robins, 2020). For example, if rumination at time t -1 causes hopelessness at time t, and hopelessness at time t causes rumination and feeling down at time t, there is exposure-confounder feedback. In this case, hopelessness is a mediator of the effect of rumination at time t -1 and a confounder of the effect of rumination at time t on feeling down at time t, so traditional methods for confounding adjustment (e.g., outcome regression) are inappropriate, and the g-formula is employed for identification of the causal effect of interest (e.g., 1-lag total effect) (Cai et al., 2026; Hernán & Robins, 2020).

In the current study, to conduct causal inference with the g-formula, even after evaluating causal stationarity, which is whether the presence of causal effects is constant over a period of time, it's also necessary to evaluate and take into account (deviations of) effect stationarity, which is whether the magnitude of the causal effects is constant over a period of time (Cai et al., 2026). As such, following Cai et al. (2026), the causal effects were estimated with state-space models, which allow (the magnitude of) causal effects to be time-varying, thereby accommodating departures from effect stationarity. More specifically, for the outcome (e.g., feeling down), covariates (e.g., hopeless) and exposure (i.e., rumination) models, we evaluated (all possibilities, through a grid search) whether enabling one or more causal effects (and intercepts) to be time-varying (instead of constant) would improve model fit according to the AIC and BIC (Bringmann et al., 2018; J. M. Haslbeck et al., 2021). Then, for the causal effects identified as time-varying, we implemented the Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) change point detection algorithm on the coefficients to identify periods where the effects were periodic stable (i.e., constant within a certain period). For parsimony, in case there were no periods of stability, the effects were considered constant (instead of time-varying at every time point). After the change point detection, the resulting outcome, covariate, and exposure models, with constant and (with or without) periodic stable time-varying causal effects, were re-estimated and considered as the final models (Cai et al., 2026). For visualisation purposes, the DAG was represented as a longitudinal network, which also included the magnitude of the estimated unstandardised effects (from the final outcome, covariates and exposure models), where nodes represent symptoms and the directed edges represent direct causal effects (Santiago et al., 2026).

Once the outcome and covariate models were established, the causal effects of interest (e.g., 1-lag total effect) could then be estimated using the g-formula (notably, the exposure model was not required for the g-formula, since the exposure was specified at a given value, such as low or high rumination). More specifically, according to the g-formula, a Monte Carlo simulation was conducted, where the exposure was specified (e.g., low or high rumination at time t) and its effects on the mediators (e.g., hopeless) and the outcome (e.g., feeling down) were sequentially simulated by repeatedly drawing (state-space model) coefficients from their sampling distributions. The causal effect was calculated as the difference between the (average) simulated outcomes under the two specified exposures, with uncertainty quantified using 95% confidence intervals (Cai et al., 2026). Finally, to evaluate model fit (i.e., whether the g-formula was correctly specified), we compared the observed with the natural course trajectories. The natural course trajectories are the predicted outcome, covariates and exposure trajectories under the g-formula, without manipulating the exposure. To obtain the natural course trajectories, the outcome, covariates and exposure were recursively simulated using the g-formula, starting from the observed values at the first time point (t=1). In the case that the g-formula is correctly specified, the natural trajectories should approximate the observed trajectories (J. M. B. Haslbeck et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2025).

3 Results

3.1 Descriptive statistics

The line plots displaying the measurements of the five symptoms, rumination, down, anhedonia, hopeless, and restless, are shown in Figure 1. The visual inspection of the cross-correlation function plots (Supplementary Figure 2) indicated that symptoms were correlated both with themselves and with other symptoms, with correlations generally persisting up to lag 2 (approximately 8 hours later) and decreasing thereafter. For example, rumination was contemporaneously correlated with feeling down (r=0.35), as well as temporally correlated with feeling down (r=0.12) and other symptoms such as hopeless (r=0.20) at lag-1 (approximately 4 hours later).

3.2 Causal discovery

The next step of the analysis was the power analysis, regime identification and causal discovery. Firstly, the simulation-based power analysis (Supplementary Figure 3) demonstrated that, with 150 measurement occasions, the VAR-LiNGAM achieved adequate performance (≥ .80) in recovering the causal structure in terms of sensitivity, precision and specificity to detect lagged edges (Sens=94.1%; Prec=89.9%; Spec=93.0%), contemporaneous adjacency (Sens=95.4%; Prec=89.2%; Spec=92.3%) and contemporaneous direction (Sens=86.6%; Prec=81.0%; Spec=94.9%) for medium standardised effect sizes (0.30). The sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figure 4) showed, however, that performance was mostly inadequate to detect lagged edges (Sen=13.4%; Prec=78.6%; Spec=97.6%), contemporaneous adjacency (Sen=13.5%; Prec=79.9%; Spec=97.7%) and contemporaneous direction (Sen=10.0%; Prec=58.9%; Spec=98.2%) for small standardized effect sizes (0.10). Furthermore, according to theoretical expectations, the VAR-LiNGAM algorithm had an inadequate performance (<.80) to detect the contemporaneous direction of medium standardised effect sizes, irrespective of sample size, when errors were Gaussian (Supplementary Figure 5).

Given that the study was adequately powered for VAR-LiNGAM to recover causal structures characterised by medium standardised effect sizes, we proceeded with the regime identification and causal discovery. In the regime identification, the KCP-RS algorithm identified no change points in the correlation structure (K=0, p=0.186), indicating a single regime across the 39 days. In this single regime, the VAR-LiNGAM discovered the DAG displayed in Figure 2. In terms of the lag order, the VAR-LiNGAM identified that the lag order of 1 (BIC=23.12), where symptoms have contemporaneous effects and the maximum lag-1 effects, was the most compatible with the data, compared to lag orders of 2 (BIC=23.18), 3 (BIC=23.20) and 4 (BIC=23.28). In Figure 2, it's possible to see that rumination had a contemporaneous effect on feeling down and feeling hopeless, and a temporal effect on feeling hopeless (approximately 4 hours later). Furthermore, other effects were discovered, such as the contemporaneous effect of restless on rumination and the temporal effect of hopeless on rumination. Furthermore, multiple confounding (backdoor) pathways were identified. For example, when considering the 1-Lag structural Direct Effect of rumination at time t on feeling down at time t + 1, restless at time t was a confounder, since it caused rumination at time t and feeling down at time t + 1 (restless at time t -> hopeless at time t -> anhedonia at time t -> down at time t + 1).

3.3 Causal inference

Once the causal structure was discovered, the final step of the analysis was to estimate the magnitude of the causal effects. Firstly, to investigate whether causal effects were time-varying or constant, we evaluated all possible state-space models, considering one or more effects as time-varying and comparing with the other models (according to the AIC and BIC). The evaluation indicated that constraining effects to be constant was more compatible with the data, with the exception of the contemporaneous effect of feeling down on hopeless (and the intercept of the rumination model), which were time-varying (Supplementary Table 1). The visual inspection of coefficient plots from state-space models where all effects are time-varying (Figure 3) is consistent with this evidence, since the coefficient plots show that differences over time in effect estimates were very small in terms of magnitude. For the only time-varying effect, the effect of feeling down on hopeless, the PELT algorithm identified no periods where the coefficient was periodic stable, so this effect was also treated as constant for parsimony (instead of time-varying at each time point). The coefficients of the final outcome, covariate and exposure models are displayed in Supplementary Table 2 and in a longitudinal network representation in Figure 4. Once the g-formula was implemented, the three causal effects were then estimated. The Contemporaneous Effect indicated that high rumination increased depressed mood by 15.6 points (out of 100 points) at the same measurement occasion compared to low rumination (CE t = 15.6, 95%CI [8.5, 22.7]). The 1-Lag Structural Direct Effect indicated that high rumination had no direct effect on depressed mood at the next measurement occasion (LDE (1) t = 0), approximately 4 hours later. The 1-lag Total Effect indicated that high rumination increased depressed mood by 1.7 points (out of 100 points) at the next measurement occasion compared to low rumination (LE (1) t = 1.7, 95%CI [0.6, 3.5]). Finally, in the evaluation of model fit, Figure 5 indicates that natural course trajectories approximated the observed trajectory across the five depressive symptoms, providing support for the adequate specification of the g-formula.

4 Discussion

The current study investigated the causal effects of rumination on depressed mood in an American female adult with a clinical diagnosis of MDD across 39 days. The findings indicated that engaging in (high) rumination increased depressed mood contemporaneously, and rumination had a small temporal effect of increasing depressed mood around 4 hours later. Importantly, this temporal effect of rumination on depressed mood was entirely mediated by hopelessness, given that rumination increased hopelessness contemporaneously, and hopelessness increased rumination around 4 hours later. We discuss these findings and implications for future depression research and treatment.

In the current study, initially, the descriptive analysis indicated that rumination was meaningfully correlated with the other depressive symptoms, such as feeling down and hopelessness, up to approximately 8 hours later. To determine whether the causal effects between symptoms (responsible for producing the observed correlations) could be identified, the power analysis indicated that, with 150 measurement occasions, the VAR-LiNGAM algorithm adequately identified causal structures characterised by medium effect sizes. These findings are consistent with recent simulation studies indicating that, for person-specific psychological networks with a small number of symptoms (e.g., six symptoms), the strongest effect sizes can be reasonably identified with a number of measurement occasions (e.g., 100 measurements) that is feasible in both research and clinical practice (Mansueto et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). Importantly, even if small effects are not detected, researchers and clinical psychologists are often interested in detecting "only the strongest and most relevant edges" of a person-specific psychological network, since these effects represent the most effective intervention targets (Mansueto et al., 2023 (Mansueto et al., , p. 1063)). Overall, the development and evaluation of causal discovery algorithms specifically for psychological time series, which contain a small number of measurement occasions, seems to be an urgent area for future research.

Prior to the causal discovery, the study identified that the observed time series was characterised by a single regime, suggesting that the causal structure (i.e., which causal effects were present) was the same across the 39 days. In terms of psychological processes, this evidence suggests that both the symptoms and the (presence of the) effects between them were relatively stable across this 39-day period. This evidence is consistent with network theory, which postulates that psychopathology constitutes stable periods where the symptoms establish strong effects among themselves (Borsboom, 2017), and with the study participant's clinical presentation, who was diagnosed with and experienced MDD throughout the study (39-day) period (only later receiving cognitive behavioural therapy; Fisher et al. (2017)). The correspondence between regimes and psychopathological states has been observed in a recent study of a Dutch male who transitioned into depression over 239 days, where the causal discovery analysis identified two distinct regimes, corresponding to non-depressive and depressive periods, respectively (Santiago et al., 2026).

Subsequently, in this regime, the causal discovery with the VAR-LiNGAM algorithm identified that the symptoms established contemporaneous effects (lag-0) and temporal effects over (the maximum of) one measurement occasion (lag-1), which was around 4 hours later. This evidence is congruent with arguments that lag-1 effects are the most plausible to describe psychological processes (Lüdtke & Robitzsch, 2022). For instance, in terms of the effect of rumination on hopelessness, the existence of a lag-2 effect would require that rumination had an effect on hopelessness over two measurement occasions, around eight hours later, after accounting for the effect that rumination already had on hopelessness in the next measurement occasion, around four hours later, and the effect of subsequent rumination. As such, these longer lagged effects, such as over two (lag-2) or three (lag-3) measurement occasions, are considered harder to conceptualise as plausible psychological processes and may require stronger theoretical justification (Ehm et al., 2019).

Once the causal structure was discovered, the next step was evaluation of the magnitude of the causal effects, and whether these were constant or time-varying. This study provided evidence indicating that, in most cases, the magnitude of the effect remained constant across the study period, since the observed variations in the magnitude of the effects over the 39 days were not meaningful. This evidence contrasts with other recent studies. For example, a study of 20 German adults with a diagnosis of recurrent depressive disorder, who answered daily mobile assessments for an average of 274 days, investigated whether effects, such as the effect of rumination at time t -1 on feeling down at time t, were better characterised as constant or time-varying. The findings indicated that, for some participants, the magnitude of the effects varied within a period of weeks, while the effects "stayed stable over months for others" (Siepe et al., 2024, p. 1). As another example, a study among 105 American adults with a current diagnosis of MDD, who answered mobile assessments 3 times per day over 90 days, indicated that effects were time-varying across the three months, and for 85% of the participants, the symptom with the strongest (combined) effect on the other symptoms changed at least once (Nemesure et al., 2024). Overall, there is growing evidence that effect stationarity, the assumption that effects are constant, should not be expected to hold in psychological time series data, and the presence of time-varying effects should be investigated and modelled instead (Cai et al., 2026).

In the next step, causal inference was used to estimate the effects, and their magnitudes were consistent with psychological theory. Firstly, rumination had a contemporaneous direct effect on depressed mood. In a series of five experiments amongst American adults randomised to rumination, distraction and gratitude conditions, ruminating for eight minutes increased depressed mood compared to distraction (r sp = -.08, 95% CI [-.13, -.02]) and gratitude (r sp = -.06, 95% CI [-.09, -.02]), indicating that rumination lowers mood within minutes (Layous et al., 2023). Following the original study by Nolen-Hoeksema et al. (1993), these experimental induction studies, such as Layous et al. (2023), in which participants are experimentally induced to ruminate and which show that the effects of rumination on mood occur within minutes, have been replicated multiple times (Donaldson & Lam, 2004; R. J. Park et al., 2004).

Secondly, rumination had a small temporal effect on depressed mood over the next measurement occasion, around 4 hours later. Studies have also provided evidence that the effects of rumination can last for a few hours (Blanke et al., 2022; Marian & Sava, 2023). A study conducted among 111 Romanian undergraduate students, who completed mobile assessments 3 times per day over 21 days, reported that negative thoughts, such as "I'm no good" (β = .09), increased depressive mood 4 hours later. The researchers discussed that, while the effects of negative thoughts on emotions are instantaneous, ruminating on these negative thoughts can prolong low mood for hours (Marian & Sava, 2023). Furthermore, in our study, the temporal (total) effect of rumination on depressed mood was mediated by hopelessness (which rumination had a contemporaneous direct effect on), which is also consistent with evidence (Rosario-Williams & Miranda, 2023; Sarin et al., 2005). For instance, in a study conducted among 87 university students in Canada, over the course of four days, mediation analysis showed that the effect of rumination on subsequent depressive symptoms was mediated by hopelessness, since rumination increased hopelessness (pr = .344, p = .001), and hopelessness increased depressive symptoms (pr = .435, p < .001) (Sarin et al., 2005). Finally, the current study findings indicated that rumination had a contemporaneous indirect effect on anhedonia, which was mediated by depressed mood. Recently, Rutherford et al. (2023) postulated, based on previous studies, that rumination taxes and leads to deficits in working memory, reducing the ability to disengage from negative thoughts that contribute to low mood, as well as to perceive which choices and experiences generate pleasurable outcomes, contributing to anhedonia. The researchers note that, despite anhedonia constituting one of the two core symptoms of depression, there is surprisingly limited research on the effects of rumination on anhedonia (Rutherford et al., 2023), including among ecological momentary assessment studies (Webb et al., 2024).

Overall, one of the main findings of the study is that, since the effect of rumination on depressive symptoms is potentially causal, reducing rumination constitutes an important intervention target to reduce depression. In terms of intervention, Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) has one of the most robust evidence bases for reducing rumination, since it combines mindfulness practices, where individuals learn to accept and decenter from their negative thoughts, and cognitive-behavioural techniques, which teach them how to identify and challenge maladaptive negative thoughts. A meta-analysis of 29 Randomised Clinical Trials (RCTs) including 2,535 participants indicated that MBCT significantly reduced rumination (SMD = -0.51, 95% CI [-0.64, -0.39]). There are also interventions focused on rumination itself, such as Rumination-Focused Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (RFCBT), which teaches individuals how to shift abstract ruminative thinking ("Why do I always feel this way?") to problem-solving thinking ('What can I do to solve this problem?') (Spinhoven et al., 2018; E. R. Watkins, 2009).

The current study has several contributions. One fundamental contribution is the application of newly developed causal inference methods (Cai et al., 2026) to investigate the causal effect of rumination on depressed symptoms using psychological time series data. To the best of our knowledge, while causal discovery has been increasingly applied to time series to identify psychological networks (Santiago et al., 2026; Waldorp et al., 2026), including using publicly available data from Fisher et al. (2017; Vitanza et al., 2026), this is the first study to estimate causal effects through formally defined causal estimands (Cai et al., 2026) in person-specific networks. The use of rigorously defined causal estimands makes transparent the assumptions required to estimate causal effects from (observational) data and the potential sources of confounding (Chatton & Rohrer, 2024), supporting a causal interpretation of the estimated effects that corresponds to the causal processes postulated by network theory (Huang et al., 2023). By capturing the effects of rumination on depressed symptoms as they naturally occur throughout the days, the current study complements and expands on the causal evidence from experimental induction studies, typically conducted in controlled laboratory conditions (Donaldson & Lam, 2004; Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 1993; R. J. Park et al., 2004). In this context, the use of causal inference for N-of-1 seems to be required, given the mounting evidence on effect heterogeneity in psychological networks, indicating that population-level estimates, such as the Average Treatment Effect (ATE), can provide a poor approximation of the causal effect for any given person (Fisher et al., 2017 (Fisher et al., , 2018;; Nemesure et al., 2024). Furthermore, a growing body of research indicates that the estimation of person-specific networks can support case formulation, symptom monitoring, and the identification of individualised treatment targets in psychotherapy (Klipstein et al., 2020). For instance, a recent study randomised 62 participants with depressive symptoms to CBT interventions based on person-specific networks and to a standardised CBT intervention. The findings indicated that those who received personalised CBT interventions based on person-specific networks (d=1.24) showed a slightly larger reduction in depression compared to those who received a standardised CBT intervention (d=1.15) (Qu et al., 2026). However, to the best of our knowledge, none of these studies estimated person-specific networks using formal causal methods (e.g., causal discovery), which may be better suited to identifying potential intervention targets that causally influence, rather than are merely associated with, other symptoms.

The current study also has several limitations. Firstly, the causal discovery and inference methods implemented require very strong assumptions, such as causal sufficiency (i.e., no unmeasured confounders), which are unlikely to hold in psychological data. As such, the estimated effects are better interpreted as potential causal effects (Ribeiro Santiago et al., 2025; Santiago et al., 2026). Importantly, while within-person (i.e., N-of-1) studies constitute powerful study designs due to the absence of time-invariant confounders (e.g., ethnicity), the estimated causal effects are still subject to (unmeasured) time-varying confounders (e.g., social isolation) (Wysocki et al., 2022). Secondly, we used discrete-time methods that assume equally spaced measurement occasions, an assumption violated by the study design, which involved quasi-random sampling within 3-hour intervals and longer overnight periods (Fisher et al., 2017). In this case, the estimated effects can be a weighted average of estimates from different time intervals, and result in misleading conclusions regarding their magnitude and/or sign (Kuiper & Ryan, 2018; Ryan et al., 2026). Continuous-time models constitute an important methodological advance that account for unequally spaced measurement occasions, but research in causal identification remains mostly underdeveloped relative to decades of progress in discretetime models (Albert et al., 2019). Fourthly, we assumed that contemporaneous effects, which may arise when psychological processes occur at a faster timescale (e.g., minutes) than the measurement occasions (e.g., 4 hours), were acyclic. The assumption of acyclicity for contemporaneous effects is considered implausible for many psychological processes, since symptoms establish reciprocal (i.e., cyclic) causal effects, and acyclicity prohibits these reciprocal causal effects from being identified contemporaneously, within the same measurement occasion (although, importantly, acyclicity does not prohibit reciprocal temporal causal effects; Ribeiro Santiago et al. (2025)). To address this shortcoming, recently developed causal discovery algorithms can discover cyclic contemporaneous causal effects and acyclic temporal causal effects (Jin et al., 2025), although implementations for N-of-1 psychological time series have yet to be developed. Fifthly, we assumed the causal effects among symptoms were linear, while the effects may be non-linear (VanderWeele, 2012). Sixthly, we conducted causal discovery and inference on the same time series (i.e., "double dipping"), which can lead to overly narrow confidence intervals. However, given the small number of measurement occasions in psychological time series, splitting the time series into training and test samples seems to be impractical (Gradu et al., 2025).

5 Conclusion

The current study investigated the causal effect of rumination on depressed symptoms in an American female adult with a clinical diagnosis of MDD across 39 days. The findings indicated that rumination increased depressed mood contemporaneously and had a small effect on depressed mood around 4 hours later, which was mediated through increases in hopelessness. By capturing the effects of rumination on depressed symptoms as they naturally occur throughout the day, the current study provides ecologically valid evidence on causality that complements controlled laboratory studies. This evidence further substantiates the importance of interventions designed to reduce ruminative thinking and, consequently, improve depressive symptoms.
