How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Approach anxiety (AA) describes a fear or intense nervousness about approaching or initiating social and especially dating interactions, where approach and avoidance goals collide. We review literature on AA in the context of human dating behavior to (a) evaluate the scientific status of AA as a distinct psychological concept and construct and (b) identify key associations with and causes of AA. Our scoping review identified zero studies of AA specifically, despite the widespread recognition of AA in popular media and self-help platforms, although we identified 83 studies of psychological dynamics during the initial phase of social interaction. Academic literature seems to ignore AA despite the many young men who say AA is a major problem. We provide a AA construct clarification and evidence integration of studies of youth social interaction dynamics which describe social or dating anxiety, fear of rejection, or heterosocial competence.

AA describes a complex interplay of sex differences, attachment styles, cultural expectations, and individual psychological factors. We present a Multilevel Courtship Inhibition Framework (MCIF) of AA that connects social skill deficits and psychological challenges with potential evolutionary, sociocultural, and psychological underpinnings, to help catalyze academic AA research.

Introduction "Dating" is a social activity where two humans spend time together to assess their mutual compatibility ("go out"), typically with the expectation or hope to develop a romantic (or potentially sexual) relationship, with dating as an active verb that seeks an outcome. Modern dating patterns emerged in America and expanded relationship possibilities beyond traditional social circles and courtship norms and marriage (Waller, 1937; Fass, 1977). Dating remains a key focus in youth despite social evolution and the effects of technology (Kwok and Wescott, 2020; eHarmony, 2023). Some dating dynamics largely withstood change over the past decades, and markedly, the cultural expectation that men initiate, plan, and finance first dates (with women), which can be psychologically challenging (Mcnamara and Grossman, 1991), and elicit conflicting emotions (Power & Dalgleish, 2009). The papers reviewed below suggest that 30-60% of young men experience significant anxiety or shyness when they try to approach (desired) women, in keeping with the ~40% population prevalence of mild-to-moderate anxiety in these men in general (Paiva et al., 2025).

Such "Approach anxiety" (AA) has been conceptualized in mainstream media as a collusion of approach processes (e.g., optimism, attraction, interest) and avoidance processes (e.g., stress or discomfort to fear, nervousness, shyness, worry, and worthlessness; see La Ruina, 2007; Savoy, 2003). 1 This approach-avoidance competition has been described in field theory and classic motivational conflicts theory (Lewin, 1931; Elliot, 2008), and studies showed avoidance gradients were typically steeper than approach gradients (Miller, 1944), hence, once a potential date was identified, the fear and desire to avoid rejection would typically increase faster than one's desire to pursue the date, which then leads to "vacillation" or hesitation just before the contact was established (the "clash point"). 2 Similarly, the approach-inhibition theory of (social) power explains how high power leads to approach tendencies and focus on reward and freedom, while low(er) power activates inhibition tendencies, thus behavioral constraint and perceptions of social threat and rejection (e.g., Cho & Keltner, 2020).

The lack of a formal scientific definition for AA is compensated by the popular and self-help literatures in which AA is defined as feeling fear or intense nervousness about approaching or initiating an interaction with another person, often a stranger or potential romantic interest (Bachaud et al., 2025); such sources explain AA in terms of fight-or-flight system (and amygdala) activation (e.g., Fisher, 2024; Waisman, 2024), as evident in sweaty palms, increased heart rate, shortness of breath, and a lump in the throat (Strauss, 2011). Dating advice and self-help books and websites describe AA often as an "irrational fear that can stop you from engaging in conversation with strangers. AA isn't just a challenge for introverted guys -no matter how extroverted they are, men rarely love sticking their necks out with strangers" (Anderson, 2024). Relationship Initiation: A Historical Perspective

Pre-industrial humans understand relationships through a lens of family, religion and community networks, as their romantic relationships are often arranged by families (Raffield, Price, & Collard, 2017a,b; Bode and Kushnick, 2021). Couples connect on factors like social and economic status (i.e., income, wealth, occupation, prestige, and education) and family alliances, which was and is common practice across Eurasia, even in modern societies (Allendorf & Pandian 2016; Tahir, 2021). When societies transition from agrarian to industrial economies, they often show a gradual shift towards more individualized approaches to relationship initiation (Luo et al., 2020). Traditional customs and norms around dating often remain influential, and many courtship rituals with specific scripts like formal introductions and chaperoned dates and symbolic meanings persisted as expected and accepted social behavior (e.g., expensive clothes, artefacts, foods, and locations; see Degen et al., 2025; Frost, 2015). Dating emerged early in the 20th century as a distinct and popular social activity that allowed youth in individualized Western societies to meet and explore more and different potential partners (Degen et al., 2025; Waller et al., 1937). Globally most men and women expect men to initiate, plan, and finance these dates early in the relationship, and these societal expectations shape modern dating dynamics, in which men hold the door open, pay, and walk her home at the end of the date (Bailey, 1988; Cate & Lloyd, 1992; Mongeau et al., 2004 , Noel et al., 2016 , Odom, 2012). After the second world war and the second wave of feminism, which challenged sex differences and gender roles, women were higher educated and used to take initiative at work (Camussi & Leccardi, 2005), and increasingly initiated relationships (Jackson, 1993; Lindeman, 2023), while their higher incomes reduced traditionally hypergamous unions (Erát, 2021). Most men (~80-90% across studies) and women (~60%) say they have been on a date initiated by the woman (Lottes, 1993; Mongeau et al., 1993; Kelley et al., 1981), and ~74% of women have asked a man for a date at least once (Lottes, 1993). Hence, most women initiate dates, and can be reluctant to passively wait for male initiative (Grossman & McNamara, 1989). College men remain more active and successful in initiating dates (McNamara & Grossman, 1991; Larsen, 2025), however, and traditional masculine norms as pursuer in dating prevail cross-culturally (Barhana et al., 2015), thus expectations of an active man and a reactive woman (e.g., Bartoli & Clark, 2006; Ronen, 2010).

Traditional courtship norms collide with more egalitarian and progressive ideals in heterosexual relationships. One qualitative study of college-educated women described the entrenchment of courtship scripts in which an assertive, breadwinning male ("provider") woes a responsive, dependent female ("caretaker"), even among progressive and economically independent women committed to equality (Lamont, 2014). Studies of dating show that women favor cues of male willingness and capacity to invest in offspring (e.g., social status, generosity, industriousness, see Mogilski, 2020; Larsen et al., 2025).

Women apparently prefer men who initiate romantic gestures, such as asking for dates or proposing marriage, and perceive these courtship rituals not as traditional cultural norms but as essentialist beliefs about the "nature" of men equipped with a "biologically driven" "need to pursue" women who in turn desired to "be chosen" (mating, e.g., Buss & Schmitt, 1993 , 2016 , Buss, 2016 , 2024; Lamont, 2014).

Some dating advice books (published 2005-2010) became bestsellers and reinforced traditional sex differences and stereotypical dating behavior; exactly the "outdated" cultural imperative (gender script) of the independent (assertive) male provider (e.g., Rose & Frieze, 1989; Eaton & Rose, 2011), which encouraged men to act as initiators, planners, and financiers, while women were submissive followers who focused on looking attractive and coy while limiting physical intimacy (cf. sexual gatekeeping, see Mogilski, 2020; Waller et al., 1937; O'Neill, 2015). A cursory Google search of the term 'learn to date' produced almost sixteen million hits early 2025 (Næss & Aarsand, 2025).

The Digital Age: Online Dating and Virtual Relationships

The advent of the internet and the proliferation of online dating platforms revolutionized how people initiate relationships (e.g., Timmermans et al., 2018). Studies of online dating suggest that individuals experiencing AA tend to prefer (online) mediated communication, which they experience as more comfortable and less intimidating than face-to-face communication (Reid & Reid, 2007 , Shalom et al., 2015). A common critique is that the sheer number of potential matches creates a choice overload, trapping users in a revolving door of brief, superficial relationships (Næss & Aarsand, 2025). A 2024 survey of 923 married adults living in the United States showed that approximately half had met their spouse through an online dating platform (Liesel et al, 2024). Online dating has democratized the dating world and allows individuals from both genders to find and connect with potential partners from around the world. Online dating challenges traditional sex differences, if only because women increasingly initiate contact, and pursue relationships online. The anonymity and control offered by online platforms might empower women to be more proactive in contacting and pursuing potential partners (Sprecher et al., 2008).

Younger women become more assertive and secure about their online behavior, but the idea of generational differences in dating behaviour persists, also in contemporary and heterosexual online dating, for example, men are more likely to send the opening message on dating platforms such as Tinder or OKCupid (Finkel et al., 2012 , Rosamond, 2017), and 80-86% of first messages in dating apps come from men (Bruch et al., 2018; Timmermans et al., 2018). Men frequently adopt a "shotgun" approach to contact initiation, to maximize their matching potential (Finkel et al., 2012; Dinh et al., 2022). This online trend aligns with more variable offline dating behavior, which demonstrates that traditional courtship patterns persist in the expanding digital contexts (Rosamond, 2017; Timmermans et al., 2018), and are stable over the past decade (Dinh et al., 2022). Dating dynamics have evolved over the past century but certain traditional gender roles remained persistent, especially men as dating initiators. We explore how the scientific literature examined contemporary dating behavior, and the meaning of these patterns in modern relationships.

Dating-research is more important than ever

Digital dating researchers often claim that "online dating [has] fundamentally altered the dating landscape for the better" (Finkel et al., 2012). Some challenged this optimism because of the growing concerns over loneliness and solitary living, which has complex individual, societal, and cultural ramifications (Swader, 2019; Smith & Victor, 2019; Leclaire, 2026). The rise in sexual inactivity after dating apps emerged, initially affected men mostly, but more recently also women report disillusionment with digitally mediated dating and intimacy, despite this broader access to potential partners (Degen et al., 2025). This global rise in the severity and persistence of loneliness or social isolation coincided with the rise of smartphones (Haidt, 2024; Twenge, 2023; Surkalim et al., 2022). Loneliness is an unpleasant feeling (negative valence) that motivates humans (negative reinforcement) to seek the company of others (i.e. approach behavior) in order to overcome this unpleasant feeling, and likely evolved to protect one's "social body" from harm (Mund et al., 2025).

In affluent urban Northern-European regions >40% of households are single-person (Esteban, 2020; Kersten et al., 2024), who spend less time in social company and with friends, which suggests a decline in social skills (not unequivocally supported by research though, see Downey & Gibbs, 2020). Nonetheless, Western humans also increasingly disengage from mixed-sex friendship and social groups over the past 30 years (e.g., McPherson et al., 2001; Psylla et al., 2017), a context where youth typically develop and refine social skills related to opposite-sex communication and dating initiation (Glick and Rose, 2011; Haidt, 2024; Sigelman et al., 2018), which coincides with shrinking social circles and reduced face-to-face interactions in general (McPherson et al., 2001; Psylla et al., 2017). Young adults draw on friendship when searching for a spouse, and friends-first dating initiation is the most common and preferred sequence of events (Stinson et al., 2022). The decline in offline social and romantic experiences and the rise in adolescent AA seem to coincide, which suggests a decline in romantic competence (La Greca and Mackey, 2007; Reeves, 2022), a cohort-effect that could melt away over midlife (when due to slower maturation, cf. Hülür et al., 2016), but most likely it seems that AA is on the rise, and coincides with a decline in deep friendship (Dunbar, 2021).

Young men worry about being labeled as "creepy" (i.e. sexually threatening) based on what they perceive as opaque social rules (McAndrew & Koehnke, 2016), 4 and ~44% of 1000 surveyed men reported a significantly reduced willingness to initiate contact with women (Evie-Magazine, 2022), especially single men (~53%). This convergence of loneliness, declining companionship, and unclear relational norms underscores the need to examine gendered dynamics in dating. Traditional expectations still cast men as initiators (e.g., Ronen, 2010), yet shifting social scripts have made this role even more psychologically taxing. Investigating barriers such as approach anxiety (AA) can illuminate how modern dating affects mental health and social cohesion. Evidently there is no formal academic definition of "approach anxiety" or associated phenomena, despite being used frequently online, as outlined in this review.

Below we peruse and synthesize the literature on AA in men in social dating contexts to identify the key themes and gaps in AA research and the implications of AA focusing on four questions: namely, what (1) is the current state of research on "approach anxiety" in social dating among men; (2) which psychological factors associated with AA in social dating; (3) how is AA conceptualized/measured in studies of social dating contexts; and (4) what are the gaps in the existing literature on AA in men?

Search Strategy

Our scoping review followed a systematic search strategy to identified articles across eight different electronic databases: Smartcat (university platform), PsycINFO (mainly for psychology related subjects), SociNDEX (social sciences), MEDLINE (medical), Web of Science (science), Scopus (science), PubMed (medical), and Google Scholar. As search terms we combined keywords that revolved around "approach anxiety", "dating anxiety", and "first encounter", and the full list of search terms is summarized in Table 1.

Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the search results, mainly with the search terms "male, men, man, masculinity, boy, boys" and "dating".foot_2

Screening and Selection

We included (a) peer-reviewed empirical studies of (b) the initial process of dating, pre-dating, and/or psychological problems in (c) in adults of 18 years and older and (d) published between 1900 and 2025.

Hence, we excluded (a) non-empirical papers such as opinion pieces, editorials, or reviews, and (b) phd theses and (c) articles with insufficient methodological and statistical setup. We identified 824 articles, and removed 145 duplicates and 197 articles that did not fulfill our inclusion criteria, resulting in 482 articles that were perused on title and abstract and from the 232 articles in full-text (using inclusion/exclusion criteria) we derived 83 articles for inclusion in our review (see Supplementary Table S1). From these selected studies we extracted data on the authors, publication year, sample size, intervention type, study design, outcome measures, main findings, and the date when each source was last searched or consulted (see Table S1). We organized these data over thematic categories that helped us to synthesize the findings and to identify gaps in the approach anxiety (AA) literature. The heterogeneity of the studies, if only in methodology, led us to a narrative (or "bare bone") review of AA, and the methodological strengths and limitations of the reviewed evidence, and areas for future research.

Anxieties in Men: Is Approach Anxiety (AA) a distinct form of anxiety or manifestation of broader psychosocial dynamics?

People who experience difficulties initiating contact in social or romantic settings can feel (extremely) nervous, fearful, insecure and/or in discomfort in social situations, which is called social anxiety and key to many anxiety symptoms and disorders. Many specific anxieties have been distinguished, such as dating anxiety when interacting with potential romantic partners, or heterosocial anxiety in social situations with members of the opposite sex, which may share underlying psychological and biological machinery with AA, such as approach inhibitions. Below we now dissect four of these categories in detail. Subsequently, we discuss the role of rejection in relationship initiation, and how AA relates to attachment.

Social Anxiety and Social Phobia.

Social anxiety that becomes persistent and intense and interferes with daily functioning can develop into a clinical diagnosis of Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD), according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5; APA, 2013) and International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11, WHO, 2019). We review studies of both general social anxiety and SAD (k= 17), which showed that more socially anxious people (vs. low-anxious peers) reported fewer interactions (with both sexes) and friendships, poor(er) social performances, and reduced social satisfaction after interactions (Dodge et al., 1987; Himadi et al., 1980). Social anxiety may lead to safety behaviors such as speaking softly or avoiding eye contact, which impairs dating initiation (Strulov & Aderka, 2025).

Social anxious participants proved less likely to initiate interactions with physically attractive people, but were more likely to engage with people they perceive as less physically attractive (versus controls, see Wenzel, 2009); this suggests that social anxiety comes with a lower self-perceived mate value (or fitness, that is, their [relative] desirability as a reproductive partner, based on factors such as physical attractiveness, resources, etc.), which is also consistent with their self-reported expectation that people were less likely to initiate interactions with them, which influences the partners and relationships they pursue.

Women were more likely than men to report anxiety symptoms and disorders (Bourdon et al., 1988; Costello et al., 2004; Craske, 2003; Dowbiggin, 2009; Farhane-Medina et al., 2022), which was partly driven by developmental and cultural factors (Craske, 2003; McLean et al., 2009 McLean et al., , 2011)). Men with severe anxiety often reported somatic symptoms, including headaches, appetite loss, and trembling, and felt they lost control, whereas anxious women, in contrast, reported more often social and interpersonal distress, crying, and relational disruptions (Auerbach et al., 2012; Bender et al., 2012). Emotional and cognitive vulnerabilities show sex-specific expressions, such as preponderant externalizing problems in men (e.g., hyperactivity, inattention, substance abuse, and aggression) versus more internalizing social distress in women (Caspi et al., 2014; Zahn-Waxler et al., 2008). Qualitative studies reinforce this dichotomy, as men typically describe anxiety as a persistent, externalized force, conceptualized as uncontrollable and enduring (Drioli-Phillips et al., 2020a). These gender-specific manifestations of problems and experiences are reflected in the diversity in anxiety disorders.

Social anxiety disorder (SAD) has often been used interchangeably with (generalized) "social phobia" as both are characterized by intense fear and anxiety in "one or more social or performance situations" (fear of negative evaluation by others; k= 3, see Table S1), just as AA (see DSM-5-TR diagnosis; and Prisbell, 1988; Cox, 2009). The fear of social situations typically leads to avoidance of social interactions which impacts daily life (and although others may endure social situations with distress, this diversity/nuance is lost in the diagnosis; cf. Laposa et al., 2009). No sex-differences in social phobia have been found (Cox, 2009), but the related concept of high communication apprehension is associated with fewer heterosocial skills, more conditioned anxiety, and more heterosocial apprehension (Prisbell, 1988). While social phobia reflects a broad fear of social evaluation and interaction, a more specific manifestation of this anxiety emerges in heterosocial contexts, the interactions with members of the opposite sex.

Heterosocial anxiety

Heterosocial anxiety captures heterosocial skills, competences, and anxiety (k= 18) papers, and can result in fewer social interactions, poor social performance, and lower satisfaction, and more depression, alcoholism, and sexual dysfunction (Grover et al., 2002 (Grover et al., , 2005)). Anxiety hampers heterosocial skill development, also because avoidance of social situations limits opportunities to learn and practice skills, and anxiety can disrupt the effective use of skills that have already been acquired. Heterosocial skill competence overlaps with general social competencies, but has a more narrow scope, and captures skills critical to navigate opposite-sex interactions, particularly in romantic contexts (Grover et al., 2005).

Heterosocial competence is thought to emerge from same-sex peer interactions and social skills that are typically acquired during adolescence via observation, reinforcement, and peer interaction (Connolly et al., 2000; Dunphy, 1963; Grover, 2007 Grover, , 2012;; Nangle & Hansen, 1998). Such skill acquisition can be hampered by anxiety, negative self-perceptions, and/or perceived physical unattractiveness, and thereby adaptation to mixed-sex peer environments and social expectations (Chee & Conger, 1989; Grover, 2007; Rodebaugh, 2009). Adolescents transition from small same-sex cliques to mixed-sex groups that facilitate romantic connections (Dunphy, 1963; Sigelman et al., 2018), and more popular adolescents have larger networks of friends of both sexes (Connolly et al., 2000; Bukowski et al., 1999). Same-sex social skills, popularity, and perceived competence correlate positively with similar traits in opposite-sex interactions (Miller, 1990). Socially competent adolescents thus join larger networks, which offer more opportunities to develop heterosocial skills, and to partake in mixed-sex groups (Nangle and Hansen, 1998).

Higher anxiety in mixed-sex group interactions associated with delayed dating experiences (Boyle and O'Sullivan, 2013), and youth perceive interactions with romantic partners as more difficult than those with close same-sex friends (Buhrmester et al., 1988). A retrospective analysis found that individuals with a history of social anxiety experienced heightened self-consciousness in social situations during junior high school (ages 12-21), and had fewer dating partners compared to a control group (Albano et al., 1995). Dating anxiety was linked to a later age of first dating relationship and fewer sexual experiences (Boyle and O'Sullivan, 2013). Additionally, adolescents who rarely dated often showed diminished social skills, increased social withdrawal, and romantic dysfunction in adulthood, reflecting developmental continuity (Collins & Sroufe, 1999; Nangle & Hansen, 1998). Evidently dating, social, and heterosocial anxieties highlights how these interconnected forms of anxiety impede social and romantic interactions, which may foster feelings of rejection. Understanding how anxiety manifests in early mixed-sex interactions is crucial, as these formative experiences often lay the groundwork for later romantic development. Building on this foundation, dating anxiety represents a more focused expression of social and heterosocial fears, directly shaping individuals' willingness and ability to pursue romantic relationships.

Dating anxiety

The combination of dating anxiety and early initiation of relationships was covered in 16 papers. Many college students (30-60% of both sexes) self-identified as highly anxious or shy when interacting with the opposite sex (Arkowitz et al., et al. 1978; Zimbardo, 1977). Moderate to high anxiety when dating was reported by most (~83%♂/~76%♀) undergraduate psychology students (McNamara and Grossman, 1991), and many men sought help for discomfort in interactions with women (Neumann et al., 1988). Most Ethiopian and Saudi Arabian participants (~71-72%) reported frequent discomfort during mixed-gender interactions (Garcia-Moreno et al., 2006; Sani et al. 2012), and also Americans and Australians of both genders (~53-57%; Wiersma-Mosley et al. 2019 , Stallman et al., 2009)), and around the globe (11-42% elsewhere, e.g., Canada, Malaysia, Kenya, and Ethiopia; see Adlaf et al., 2004 , Rachel et al., 2012 , Dessie et al., 2013 , Shimekaw et al., 2013). Many (~23-26%) participants reported dating-related stress (France, Norway, and Iceland, see Verger et al., 2010 , Nerdrum et al., 2014 , Talbert et al., 2020; Bernhardsdóttir and Vilhjálmsson, 2013).

Evidently discomfort with the opposite sex is common around the globe, which strengthens the need to study male approach behavior. College students (of both sexes) often (30-60%) report being either very anxious or shy with the opposite sex (Rossmann, 1991), and many US college-aged men reported significant anxiety related to dating (~37% in Grover (2002) and up to ~88% of men in Muhwezi et al., 2020), which the authors explained with factors such as acculturation, globalization, and the affective responses to stressors and hormonal fluctuations in young adults.

Women reported more anxiety during dating than men (on the "Active Intentions for Dating" subscale, see Calvert et al., 1987; or Swami et al., 2022), a dynamic that may reinforce traditional sex differences, wherein men are perceived as the primary initiators in dating scenarios. Without male action, such interactions may fail to materialize, due to mutual anxieties (e.g., Ömür et al., 2015). These discrepancies suggest that cultural, environmental, and contextual factors, such as societal values, university settings, and local experiences of stress, may contribute to variations in the reported prevalence of dating-related stress across different regions. While social and cultural expectations influence who initiates romantic contact, such dynamics are further compounded by emotional factors. The anticipation of rejection, in particular, represents a core component of dating anxiety that can fundamentally inhibit relationship initiation.

The Role of Rejection in Relationship Initiation

Rejection is a profoundly painful experience that humans are strongly motivated to avoid (Eisenberger et al., 2003; MacDonald & Leary, 2005). Consequently, the desire for romantic connection often conflicts with the goal of minimizing the emotional pain of rejection (Clark & Beck, 2010; Murray et al., 2006), especially about one's appearance (Swami et al., 2022). The fear of rejection can significantly hinder relationship initiation, and prevented ~76% of undergraduate participants (both sexes) in one study from pursuing a romantic relationship, and blocked 54% from doing so (Vorauer & Ratner, 1996).

Rejection significantly influences dating behavior through its impact on individuals' sense of belonging and self-esteem, creating a complex interplay of approach-avoidance motivations. Social exclusion triggers two conflicting responses: a cautious effort to restore social bonds, and an active avoidance of further rejection (Maner et al., 2007). For individuals with unmet belonging needs, this regulation process can lead to heightened susceptibility to social influence, as they align their attitudes with peers perceived as potential sources of affiliation, resulting in increased aggression (DeWall et al., 2010).

Threats to belongingness (see Baumeister and Leary's (1995) need to belong) cannot be mitigated by unrelated affirmations. Instead, individuals prioritize actions that specifically restore social connections, which may be hampered by fear of negative evaluation, thus rejected individuals may have reduced capacity to engage socially (Maner et al., 2007). Low self-esteem exacerbates these dynamics, as it is tightly linked to chronic feelings of exclusion and heightened sensitivity to rejection. Fear of negative evaluation from dating partners (often tied to lower self-esteem) can inhibit the ability of young men to approach women, due to concerns about being judged unfavorably (McClure et al., 2010; Swami et al., 2022). Sociometer theory suggests that self-esteem functions as a gauge of relational quality, and individuals with low self-esteem experience even mild rejection as profoundly threatening (Collins & Ford, 2010). Such individuals prioritize self-protection, avoiding risks associated with new connections, and exhibit conservative decision-making, even in non-relational contexts. These patterns suggest that belonging regulation interacts with broader regulatory systems, influencing both social and nonsocial behavior. These findings underscore the intricate ways rejection and its associated fears shape dating behaviors, reflecting individual differences in self-esteem, strategies to regulate their need to belong, and attachment style.

Attachment Theory on Approach Anxiety: Exploring the Impact of Early Bonds on Romantic Pursuit

Attachment theory suggests that early relationships with caregivers shape our ability to form relationships later in life ("internal working models" as blueprint for intimacy and social interaction), resulting in secure, anxious, avoidant, and disorganized types (Bowlby, 1969; Sigelman et al., 2018). The present review identified six papers about how attachment styles influence early dating initiation behaviour, especially in men (Toma, 2022). Securely attached individuals, confident in approaching others, unconcerned with being single, do not feel pressured to initiate contact, and thus play a more balanced role in relationship formation (also Guynn et al., 2008). Avoidant attachment is linked to lower partner-specific desire, which makes romantic initiation challenging, and reduces partner attraction (Blumenstock, 2023).

Avoidant individuals may, however, pursue attractive non-partners, seeking less emotionally intimate encounters (Coffey et al., 2022). Their aversion to intimacy often deters commitment, yet online dating platforms, offering more direct communication, may mitigate discomfort compared to offline interactions (Coffey et al., 2022). Individuals with an anxious-preoccupied attachment style crave intimacy but often doubt its reciprocation, and tend to initiate relationships more frequently, likely driven by a fear of loneliness (Guynn et al., 2008). Attachment anxiety is a general fear of abandonment or rejection that is marked by a hyperactive attachment system and a strong motivation to affiliate, which becomes active during relationship initiation, even in those without anxious tendencies (Eastwick & Finkel, 2008; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007). One study found that attachment anxiety had a negative relationship with dating success (number of matches) for men, but not for women, compared to less anxious counterparts (McClure et al., 2010; Buss, 2016). Sex differences exacerbate these challenges. Societal expectations often position men as initiators in dating scenarios, placing additional pressure on them to approach women (Eaton and Rose, 2011). Conversely, women, traditionally viewed as "gatekeepers," are more selective in partner choices (Larsen et al., 2025), which heightens men's apprehension about potential rejection.

Attachment-related anxiety influences men's preferences for female initiation strategies. Men who favor direct approaches by women tend to experience higher relationship anxiety than those who prefer indirect strategies, suggesting that these preferences may serve as a compensatory mechanism to alleviate fears around initiation (Barhana, 2015; Ömür et al., 2015).

In sum, the difficulties men face in approaching women are shaped by a complex interplay of attachment styles, fear of rejection, societal norms, and traditional gender dynamics, all of which impact their confidence and behaviors in romantic pursuits. Understanding how internal anxieties translate into outward avoidance provides key insight into the mechanisms underlying men's difficulties with social initiation-an issue explored in studies on intersocial avoidance.

Intersocial Avoidance

Heterosocial avoidance in college-aged men was studied via initiation behaviors in laboratory and naturalistic settings (k=4, Twentyman, 1981). Evidently adults who were dating (vs. nondaters) were more likely to initiate interactions and were perceived as more socially skilled, despite reporting similar or even higher anxiety levels (Studies I & II). The primary behavioral divergence emerged during phone interactions; daters were more persistent following initial refusals, suggesting a greater willingness to pursue social engagement. Study III found that nondaters held less accurate perceptions of social norms surrounding initiation, although both groups could identify effective strategies. Study IV extended these findings to real-world contexts, showing that nondaters were significantly more avoidant in initiating interactions outside the laboratory. Twentyman (1981) concluded that heterosocial avoidance among nondaters reflects both cognitive misperceptions and behavioral inhibition, underscoring the importance of integrating laboratory assessments with in vivo observations to better understand social functioning. These behavioral findings highlight that avoidance in social and romantic settings often stems from more deepseated dispositional tendencies. Shyness, in particular, represents a stable individual difference that not only reinforces avoidance patterns, but also shapes how men perceive and respond to potential romantic opportunities (Larsen et al., 2025).

The Role of Shyness

Many studies (k=14) examined the link between approach anxiety (AA) and shyness, and highlighted shyness as a significant barrier to male romantic initiation in. Shy individuals often exhibit negative selfbeliefs and a pervasive fear of rejection, inhibiting self-disclosure and social engagement (Bruch, 1995; Larsen et al., 2025). Shy people also tend to adopt a (more) pessimistic attributional style, internalizing negative outcomes and discounting positive ones, which undermines relational confidence (Johnson, 1995).

Societal norms around masculinity further constrain emotional expression-an issue exacerbated in shy men-thereby impeding the development of intimate bonds (Bruch, 1998). Additionally, shyness diminishes interpersonal competence, making fundamental social tasks such as initiating conversation or expressing feelings particularly challenging (Bruch, 1998).

Shyness was negatively correlated with direct relationship initiation, from social gatherings (r= -.49), bars/nightclubs (-.47), work/class (-.31), to Facebook (-.19; see Sprecher et al., 2019). Shyness was more detrimental to dating for men (in all settings except Facebook) than for women, thus shyness was particularly inhibitory in "open-field" environments, such as social gatherings and bars, where the perceived risk of rejection is higher, and direct romantic overtures are more socially ambiguous. Shy men often adopt a protective self-presentation style, prioritizing the avoidance of negative evaluation over the pursuit of social approval. This defensive orientation limits their willingness to initiate interactions or engage in self-disclosure, thereby reducing opportunities for romantic connection (Bruch, 1998; Manning, 1993). Although young men may endorse more permissive sexual attitudes, shy men tend to engage less in relational sexual behaviors, indicating a disconnect between sexual interest and interpersonal expression. As a result, shy men may turn to solitary sexual activities, such as pornography consumption or masturbation, a (poor) substitute for romantic intimacy (Luster, 2013). Valentiner (2011) examined how shyness influenced two dimensions of social anxiety, namely, performance anxiety (a fear of being scrutinized) and interaction anxiety (a fear of social interaction). Youth with a fixed mindset showed stable or increasing performance anxiety, whereas peers with a growth mindset reported a reduction in performance anxiety. However, mindset did not significantly moderate interaction anxiety, suggesting this form of anxiety may be more strongly shaped by dispositional factors such as personality or early experiences.

Research on children highlights shyness as a key mediator of social development, with shy individuals showing reduced peer initiation, limited verbal engagement, and deficits in expressive and pragmatic language skills (Davis & Buss, 2012; Baardstu et al., 2023). These limitations contribute to lower social competence and fewer skills in group contexts.

Collectively, such findings underscore how shyness can hinder relational development in males, reinforcing the need for interventions that target both social and emotional barriers.

Discussion

Approach anxiety (AA) is a concept that gained prominence in dating and coaching circles and features widely across self-help platforms and media. We critically examined the scientific foundation of the AA construct and perused the peer-reviewed academic literature to conclude that the AA concept has been largely absent from the peer-reviewed scholarship. This lack of AA research was striking given the prevalence of the AA concept in popular discourse, in particular around male-initiated romantic interactions. We explored whether AA is an academically unrecognized concept (phenomenon) that is still largely restricted to popular culture, or whether AA was studied as a neighbouring concept with specific terminology (e.g., dating anxiety). Below we discuss whether AA demands different terminology, and synthesize our main findings into a Multilevel Courtship Inhibition Framework (MCIF) of AA and outline how this MCIF-AA model spurs novel hypotheses and research avenues.

Rethinking 'Approach Anxiety': Is the Term Missing the Mark?

AA appears to have originated from the American pickup artist (PUA) community, which centers on strategies for attracting romantic or sexual partners. While the precise origin of the term is difficult to determine, Neil Strauss's The Game (2005) is among the earliest widely recognized texts to describe the fear of approaching women (to denote acute anxiety and behavioral inhibition that arises immediately before initiating an interaction). This suggests that the term AA gained traction during the late 1990s to mid-2000s, a period in which PUA culture entered mainstream discourse (Bachaud et al., 2025).

AA describes anxiety or situational discomfort, a normal emotion, but the term is increasingly used to imply pathology (as therapy or medication is needed)foot_3 , a diagnosis of AA; whereas clinical (social) anxiety involves chronic, pervasive distress, according to the DSM-5 (APA, 2013), and such criteria are rarely met in these contexts. A medicalized framing (AA) may obscure more accurate explanations such as lack of confidence, social inexperience, or fear of rejection (Scott, 2006). Consequently, the term AA may inadvertently stigmatize a common social challenge, and disempower individuals from addressing it. A more precise and constructive terminology would better reflect the nature of the phenomenon (Podsakoff et al., 2016), while avoiding unwarranted clinical implications.

A critical question is whether AA constitutes a valid psychological construct or merely reflects a culturally contingent interpretation of social discomfort within the context of American dating norms (Bachaud et al., 2025). Limited empirical research has examined the extent to which American dating culture, including its terminology, influences native and global perspectives on romantic behavior. It is therefore plausible that the absence of AA in scientific literature stems from its potentially artificial status, shaped by the commercial interests of dating industries, linguistic limitations, or sociocultural dynamics specific to the United States (Bachaud et al., 2025). We reviewed existing interdisciplinary knowledge to critically evaluate the AA construct's conceptual foundations and overlap with heterosocial anxiety, dating anxiety, heterosexual-social anxiety, dating inhibitions, social interaction anxiety, and fear of negative evaluation in dating contexts.

As the results showed, more interaction leads to better skills resulting in better homo-and heterosocial competence (Grover, 2007). Also, low interpersonal competence (low grasp on social cues and norms, see Twentyman, 1981) is associated with dating anxiety (Adamczyk and Segrin, 2016), which underscores that social skills are key to AA, although research needs to confirm this relationship, especially over adolescence (Connolly et al., 2000; Nangle & Hansen, 1998), when heterosocial incompetence seems directly connected to AA. Psychological contributors to AA include anxiety (anticipatory social anxiety, dating anxiety, and specific anxiety), and future work may explore these associations in detail, and fear of rejection and fear of social interaction (or social phobia), often discussed in terms of attachment styles (i.e.

"mechanisms" that [can] exert in/direct effects on AA). The following paragraphs will try to explain the potentially individual contributions to AA.

Anxiety. AA is closely tied to the broader construct of anxiety, as men with AA are thought to exhibit a generalized fear of initiating interactions with women. AA can be distinguished from general dating anxiety because AA is more prevalent, and AA seems to impose specific but substantial social limitations, which partly overlap with the discourse on shyness (e.g., Scott, 2006; also Larsen et al., 2025).

Our review indicates that between 30% and 60% of college students experience significant anxiety or shyness in opposite-sex interactions, with men disproportionately affected, particularly in the context of initiating romantic approaches (McNamara & Grossman, 1991; Arkowitz et al., 1978; Grover, 2002).

Social anxiety research highlights specific difficulties men face in social and romantic contexts and the role of avoidance learning (Hofmann & Hay, 2018). Individuals with high social anxiety tend to avoid other-sex interactions, perform poorly in social situations, and report lower satisfaction in these interactions compared to those with lower anxiety levels (Dodge et al., 1987). For men, this avoidance behavior may stem from a combination of low self-perceived mate value and fear of rejection, leading them to favor interactions with individuals they perceive as less physically attractive (Wenzel, 2009; O'Neill, 2015).

Socially anxious men often believe others are less likely to engage with them, which can reinforce a cycle of avoidance and missed opportunities to approach women. Additionally, socially anxious men often struggle to form same-sex friendships and broader social networks (Barnett et al., 2020; Himadi et al., 1980), depriving them of opportunities to build self-esteem and practice the interpersonal skills necessary for romantic contexts. The compounding effects of low self-perceived mate value, fear of rejection, and limited social competence, suggest that anxiety can be a significant obstacle for men in approaching women, and highlight the importance of addressing social anxiety within the framework of romantic relationship initiation.

Anxiety can become so intense that it physically inhibits the individual. While research consistently finds that women report higher levels of anxiety than men (Bourdon et al., 1988; Costello et al., 2004; Farhane-Medina et al., 2022), the nature of anxiety symptoms differs significantly between the sexes. Men often externalize anxiety through physical symptoms, such as headaches, trembling, and a sensation of losing control (Auerbach et al., 2012; Bender et al., 2012). This externalization can create a barrier to acknowledging or addressing anxiety's impact on interpersonal interactions, including approaching potential romantic partners.

Next to mental and physical origins, cultural and developmental factors tied to sex socialization may exacerbate AA struggles. Men are often socialized to mask emotional vulnerabilities, which can limit their willingness to seek help or openly discuss anxieties related to dating and relationships (Craske, 2003; McLean et al., 2011). Qualitative studies further emphasize that men tend to perceive anxiety as an uncontrollable, externalized force, which could lead to a sense of helplessness in addressing the fear of initiating romantic interactions (Drioli-Phillips et al., 2020). This perception may discourage them from developing the social skills needed to approach women confidently. Returning to the initial question of whether AA is a genuine psychological construct or simply a form of anxiety, only future research can provide clarity and answer this uncertainty.

Fear. AA does not have to manifest itself as severe anxiety, and can be understood as an immediate, situational fear response. Research indicates that fear of rejection significantly impacts dating dynamics, with 76% of undergraduates reporting that it has prevented them from pursuing a romantic relationship at least once, and 54% stating it has completely deterred them (Vorauer & Ratner, 1996). Several theories can explain this relationship (Hoffman & Hay, 2018), such as the theory of belonging regulation, which builds on Baumeister and Leary's (1995) conceptualization of belonging as a fundamental human need, and identifies fear of rejection as a direct threat to social belonging. Our review suggests a potential link between AA and fear of rejection and the need to further explore this relationship. We therefore added a direct relationship between fear and AA to Figure 1, and an indirect path via the lack of social skills.

Phobia. Social phobia might be the extreme form of men struggling to approach women. Closely tied high communication apprehension is linked to lower social skills with the opposite sex, greater anxiety, and increased fear of social interactions. Together, these factors often lead to avoidance in dating situations, especially when approaching women (Cox, 2009; Hoffman & Hay, 2018). Additionally, individuals with high communication apprehension often perceive themselves as less physically attractive, further exacerbating their reluctance to initiate social interactions. The reviewed literature on social phobia and its impact on dating remains limited and no studies specifically addressed AA. Another potential factor to consider for inclusion in the model presented in Figure 1 is the possibility that a certain percentage of men may indeed develop something akin to AA.

Attachment styles.

Attachment theory is a well-established area of research with significant insights into dating dynamics; however, its application to the earliest stages of romantic initiation remains relatively underexplored. Avoidant attachment is linked to lower desire for partners, making it harder for men with this trait to initiate romantic approaches, and leading them to seek less emotionally involved encounters (Blumenstock, 2023; Coffey et al., 2022). Men with higher attachment anxiety experience fewer matches, likely due to lower popularity, while anxiety does not impact women's match success (McClure et al., 2010). Societal norms, which position men as the primary initiators, exacerbate this pressure and anxiety.

Additionally, men who prefer women to take a more direct approach in dating tend to exhibit higher relationship anxiety, reflecting insecurities linked to their attachment style (Barhana, 2015). Contrarily, Guynn et al. (2008) found that securely attached men do not initiate romantic interactions at higher rates, as preoccupied individuals do. Secure individuals, more comfortable being single, tend to refrain from initiating, and allow women to take the lead. Replicating these studies is crucial to further explore these processes and in particularly men's responses when approached by women. Reportedly securely attached individuals are less likely to experience AA (~40-60% of American population, Meng et al., 2015), and if AA proves to be closely tied to attachment styles, this could help researchers better understand AA.

Shyness.

If only a subset of men experience AA the "anxiety" may be a specific response influenced by individual personality traits, such as shyness (Scott, 2006; Larsen et al., 2025). Shyness can either strengthen or limit interpersonal abilities (Davis & Buss, 2012; Baardstu et al., 2023), and function as a mediator of interpersonal performance, or directly affect AA. Shyness has been shown to negatively impact interpersonal competence, complicating efforts to initiate conversations or express emotions effectively (Bruch, 1998; Larsen et al., 2025). These difficulties can escalate anxiety in social contexts, making the act of approaching women more daunting. Shy individuals often hold negative self-beliefs, which foster a fear of rejection and reluctance to engage in self-disclosure (Bruch, 1995). This anticipatory fear of rejection may constitute a significant component of AA, contributing to social anxiety, dating anxiety, and avoidance behaviors (Hoffman & Hay, 2018). The literature on shyness in early-stage dating is limited, but shyness seems to constitute a crucial link in the AA model (Figure 2) that connects deficits in interpersonal communication skills with personality traits. Inborn or Learned? New Hypothetical Approaches to Understand Complex Behaviour.

A central question is whether AA constitutes a valid psychological construct, and if male reluctance to approach women is evolutionarily rooted or socially conditioned. Evolutionary psychologists tend to argue that sex differences in initiation strategies reflect adaptive reproductive pressures (e.g., Buss & Schmitt, 1993 , 2016; Larsen et al., 2025). Men tend to adopt direct approaches to attract mates, with higher social status linked to reproductive success via reduced childlessness (Zhang & Santtila, 2022). Women, by contrast, are thought to favor indirect tactics (e.g., mediated introductions or passive signaling) and emphasize traits like youth and health to appeal to male preferences. Female mate selection is shaped by evolved priorities such as resource acquisition, warmth, and trustworthiness (Simpson & Gangestad, 1991 , 1992; Sprecher et al., 2008). Men may thus experience elevated anxiety when perceiving deficiencies in these valued domains, reinforcing sensitivity to rejection cues.

Many theorists hypothesized (H1) that AA in men is influenced by evolutionary pressures stemming from female mate selectivity, which increases men's uncertainty about the success of their courtship attempts (e.g., especially in lay conversations, see Bachaud et al., 2025). AA presents a paradox when viewed through the lens of strategic pluralism theory (Trivers, 1972; Gangestad & Simpson, 2000), which posits that men are generally inclined to maximize reproductive success through multiple partners, while women's preferences vary by relational context. The inhibitory nature of AA, which reduces men's likelihood of initiating romantic interactions, appears to contradict this evolutionary framework, prompting critical questions about its origins and adaptive function.

One important insight may stem from sex differences in dating goals. While both men and women seek to reduce uncertainty and assess romantic potential, men more frequently report sexual motives that may be less tied to romantic interest (Mongeau, 2004). However, both sexes prioritize physical attractiveness in short-term contexts and pursue long-term partnerships (Li & Kenrick, 2006; Wiederman & Dubois, 1998; Sprecher et al., 2008). These findings highlight that not all individuals align with stereotypical mating goals. AA may thus reflect a learned sense of mismatch between personal intentions and perceived dating norms, contributing to uncertainty and inhibition in romantic initiation. A second hypothesis (H2) may thus hold that AA stems from a perceived mismatch between personal dating goals and societal or partner expectations, leading to uncertainty and fear of rejection.

Men exhibit greater variance in reproductive success than women, with some men fathering disproportionately more offspring, but female reproductive success is more evenly distributed (Buss et al., 2016; Favre & Sornette, 2012; Ross et al., 2023); also genetic studies indicate that fewer men contributed to current human generations (Favre et al., 2012; Karmin et al., 2015; Lippold et al., 2014). This higher variance in male reproductive success is largely driven by sexual selection, wherein women tend to prefer mates with greater status and resources, prompting male-male competition and risk-taking behaviors, often linked to higher male mortality rates (Buss et al., 2016; Larsen et al., 2025). While human monogamy moderates reproductive skew compared to the polygynous patterns typical in other mammals, disparities remain (Ross et al., 2023). A plausible hypothesis is that AA functions as an adaptive regulatory mechanism, fostering caution in contexts with high perceived rejection risk, thereby reducing wasted mating effort and social costs in ancestral environments (Gilbert, 2001). A third hypothesis (H3) is therefore that AA in men evolved as an adaptive response to the high variance in male reproductive success, serving to optimize the timing and context of courtship to increase the likelihood of mate acceptance.

AA may be especially salient during emerging adulthood (ages 18-29), a developmental stage marked by the pursuit of intimacy, romantic exploration, and identity formation (Davila et al., 2020). The emotional significance of relationship choices during this period may heighten sensitivity to rejection. Age and life experience are key moderating factors; as individuals gain relational experience, self-confidence and social competence typically increase, potentially reducing AA. Conversely, limited experience or underdeveloped social skills-common in high-pressure environments like college-may sustain or exacerbate AA during this critical life stage. A fourth hypothesis (H4) is therefore that emerging adults with less relationship experience will exhibit higher levels of AA compared to their more experienced peers. This effect will be more pronounced in high-pressure social environments (H4b), such as college settings, where dating and relationship exploration are more prevalent.

While the tendency to AA might be rooted in evolutionary theories, social learning might also have a strong influence on behaviour and perceptions. Howard et al. (1987) acknowledge the impact of sex role socialisation and power dynamics. Sadalla et al. (1987) also recognise the role of traditional sex difference in heterosexual attraction. This acknowledgement opens the door for social learning to shape anxieties and fears related to social interaction, including approaching women (e.g., Hoffman & Hay, 2018). We could therefore hypothesize (H5) that men who report higher levels of reluctance to approach women will show greater physiological stress responses during social interactions, supporting an innate basis for this behavior. However, some behaviors may have an innate component that is then refined through social learning. Evolutionary agent-based simulations have demonstrated that genetic preparedness (an evolutionary adaptation) and social learning often co-evolve in dangerous environments, providing complementary benefits (Lindström et al., 2015). It is possible that AA arises from a combination of innate predispositions and learned behaviors, requiring an explanation that integrates both aspects.

Avoidance behaviour a potential ingroup-outgroup dynamic

Another potential contributor to the development of AA in men may lie in ingroup-outgroup dynamics, as outlined by Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). This framework posits that individuals classify others into ingroups and outgroups based on salient identity markers such as sex, race, or social roles, often leading to ingroup favoritism and outgroup avoidance or distrust. Men may perceive the ingroup (i.e., other men) as familiar and non-threatening due to shared experiences and social norms, whereas women, as outgroup members, may be viewed as unfamiliar or even threatening due to biological, cultural, or social differences. Evolutionary psychologists suggest that such avoidance of outgroup members may have served an adaptive function in ancestral environments by reducing risks of harm, competition, or social instability (Van Vugt & Schaller, 2008).

Men's avoidance of women may also be rooted in evolutionary strategies linked to male-male competition and coalition maintenance. Dominant individuals-particularly males-have historically been perceived as threats by outgroup members due to risks of aggression or resource loss (Tooby & Cosmides, 2010). To preserve ingroup status and coalitional bonds, men may have evolved to avoid behaviors-such as failed courtship attempts-that could jeopardize their social standing, resulting in caution not toward women per se, but toward actions that risk exclusion. Evolutionary research suggests that men evolved to view outgroup males as competitors, while women developed avoidance strategies toward outgroup males to reduce potential threats (Van Vugt & Park, 2009). Consequently, women may subconsciously perceive unfamiliar men as more threatening than in-group males, which can indirectly reinforce men's hesitation in approaching women. These evolved biases continue to shape modern social dynamics, contributing to avoidance behaviors in cross-group interactions. These arguments underlie the hypothesis (H6) that men are more likely to initiate conversations with women they perceive as part of their ingroup (e.g., same ethnicity, shared cultural background, mutual social circle) due to lower perceived social risk.

Traditional Effects on Men's Approaching Behaviour

In our introduction we provided an alternative account of relationship strategy differences that centered on sex differences. This framework posits that individuals internalize gendered behavioral norms through socialization, shaping distinct initiation strategies for men and women. These culturally prescribed roles may elicit pressure, fear, or anxiety in approaching potential partners. Moreover, individuals may reconstruct their relational narratives to align with socially endorsed sex differences (Veroff et al., 1993).

Although traditional narratives depict men as initiators and women as passive in courtship, empirical evidence reveals greater complexity. Women often nonverbally signal interest prior to male approaches (Perper, 1985), effectively guiding outcomes. However, Grammer et al. (2000) found no significant difference in nonverbal signaling between women with high vs. low interest during initial interactions, suggesting male difficulty in detecting interest early on. This ambiguity, exacerbated by anxiety, may impair cue recognition and promote avoidance. Consequently, both sexes view direct approaches as most effective, even under ego depletion (Clark et al., 1999; Lewandowski et al., 2012). Future research could explore whether "approach anxiety" influences the ability to read facial expressions and behavioral cues be important and makes the process of men approaching harder.

Limitations and Future View

The primary finding of this paper is the notable scarcity of academic literature on AA. This gap may stem from the topic's interdisciplinary complexity-spanning psychological, sociological, and cultural domains-as well as limited scholarly recognition of male-specific barriers in initiating romantic interactions. Additionally, the predominance of female researchers in dating-related studies raises the possibility that research priorities may reflect sex-based differences in perspective, potentially shaping the field's focus.

Much of the literature in this domain spans multiple decades and relies heavily on self-report measures, self-assessments, or controlled laboratory studies. Given individuals' limited introspective accuracy in romantic contexts, the validity of such data is questionable. These methodological limitations underscore the need for more ecologically valid and methodologically rigorous approaches in future research.

Due to the scope of this work, certain topics could only be addressed in a more limited capacity.

However, the role of factors such as self-efficacy and self-esteem appears to be particularly significant and warrants further investigation. Additionally, some studies, such as Sprecher et al. (2008), have explored the concept of "need" in the context of dating, which could also be a valuable area to examine in relation to AA.

Importantly, heterosocial anxiety is not exclusive to men. Grover (2002) found that college women commonly reported fear and anxiety in dating contexts. To assess this, a female-adapted version of the Survey of Heterosexual Interactions (SHI) was developed, showing strong correlations with anxiety and social skills measures; higher SHI scores were associated with greater assertiveness. These findings highlight that women also face substantial challenges in other-sex interactions. Calvert et al. (1987) further criticized the male-centric focus of earlier research, emphasizing that dating anxiety affects both sexes. Sprecher et al. (2008) suggest that modern Western men may shift focus from screening for fit, reproductive partners to perceiving most women as viable mates due to perceived scarcity of 'less healthy' women. This broadened perception may heighten AA by increasing the perceived stakes of each interaction, framing rejection as both a social and reproductive loss. The resulting pressure could exacerbate fear of failure and reinforce avoidance behaviors. Conversely, perceived abundance of potential partners may reduce anxiety by lowering the significance of individual encounters. These competing hypotheses warrant empirical investigation in future research.

Recent changes in dating practices, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, have reduced in-person interactions and increased reliance on online platforms (Jeronimus, 2025). While this shift offers new opportunities, it also presents challenges. Dating anxiety and perceived COVID-19 impact are both linked to elevated depressive symptoms (Dattilo et al., 2022), particularly among college students with reduced social exposure. Individuals with social anxiety often prefer online communication due to its lower perceived social risk (Reid & Reid, 2007). Consistently, higher dating anxiety is associated with greater motivation for online communication, suggesting that digital contexts may feel safer for those experiencing dating-related distress (Sumter & Vandenbosch, 2019).

Another consequence of increased online engagement is the emergence of Fear of Missing Out (FOMO), which appears more pronounced among men in digital contexts (Qutishat, 2020). This may exacerbate AA by limiting opportunities for developing offline social communication skills, thereby amplifying issues related to self-esteem and self-compassion. FOMO also aligns with findings that online daters often postpone commitment due to choice overload and prolonged trust-building, driven by concerns over profile authenticity (Paul, 2014).

FOMO's connection to AA may be less apparent, but evidence suggests higher breakup rates among couples who met online, particularly in non-marital relationships (Paul, 2014). This raises questions about whether online daters may exhibit lower social competence and skills, critical for initiating and maintaining healthy relationships (Raby et al., 2015). Additionally, recent studies indicate that traditional gender roles persist in online dating, with men initiating interactions at a 4:1 ratio (Kraeger et al., 2015), highlighting the need for sex-specific research and further exploration in the growing online dating field.

By addressing the gaps, future studies can contribute to the development of effective interventions that empower individuals to navigate the complex dynamics of modern romantic relationships.

Conclusion

This paper was initially conceived as a comprehensive literature review on the topic of "approach anxiety". However, a significant gap in the existing literature was uncovered. As a result, the thesis pivoted towards providing a broad overview of dating research, while also proposing potential theoretical frameworks for future investigations. Based on the current knowledge summarized in this thesis, there is substantial evidence pointing toward AA as a genuine phenomenon affecting many men. However, only precise and targeted research can uncover the definitive answer to this intriguing question. This question presents substantial research challenges, particularly when considering the influence of various factors such as culture, religion, and societal norms. Despite these challenges, this thesis serves as an important first step in a burgeoning research area with vast potential to contribute to the understanding of men's social and relational behaviors, and ultimately, to the development of effective interventions.

Survey of Heterosexual Interactions

The Survey of Heterosexual Interactions (SHI), developed by Twentyman and McFall in 1975, is designed to evaluate individuals' abilities to navigate challenging situations in heterosexual interactions.

The SHI begins with a general questionnaire, followed by 20 specific items where respondents rate their perceived ability to manage the described scenarios on a 7-point scale. Lower scores indicate a lesser selfassessed capability. The SHI's interitem correlations range from 0.147 to 0.779, with a median correlation of 0.345, all of which are statistically significant (p < 0.001). The scale demonstrates strong reliability, with a split-half reliability coefficient of 0.85 and a test-retest reliability over four months also at 0.85. Additionally, Twentyman and McFall found that the SHI correlates negatively with reported anxiety in heterosocial situations (r = -0.42 to -0.69) and positively with self-reported social behaviors (r = 0.79 to 0.84). These findings suggest that the SHI is an effective tool for identifying individuals who may face challenges in heterosexual social interactions.

Social Interaction Anxiety Scale

The Social Interaction Survey (SIS) was a modification of the SHI, developed by Heimberg and colleagues in 1992, is a widely used instrument designed to assess anxiety in a variety of social settings, including those involving unfamiliar or heterosocial interactions. The SIAS evaluates broader social discomfort, such as fear of embarrassment or rejection, making it particularly useful for studying individuals with social anxiety disorder. Scores on this scale range from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater self-esteem. The SIAS exhibits excellent reliability, with Cronbach's alpha values ranging from 0.88 to 0.94, and a test-retest reliability of approximately 0.86 over several months. Additionally, its strong correlations with other established measures of social anxiety underscore its validity as a diagnostic and research tool.

Heterosocial Skills Inventory for Women

The Heterosocial Skills Inventory for Women (HSIW) was developed to address the need for a sexspecific measure of social skills and anxiety in heterosocial interactions (Kolko, 1985). The HSIW is designed to evaluate a range of behaviors relevant to interactions with the opposite sex, including the ability to initiate conversations, sustain dialogue, and express emotions appropriately. The inventory has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with internal consistency reliability coefficients (Cronbach's alpha) typically exceeding 0.80. Furthermore, test-retest reliability analyses have shown that the HSIW produces stable scores over several weeks, supporting its robustness as a measurement tool in both research and applied settings.

Measure of Heterosocial Competence

The Measure of Heterosocial Competence (MHC) was designed by Grover et al. in 2012 to assess individuals' skills and confidence in interactions with the opposite sex, focusing on behaviors and attitudes that contribute to effective social engagement. The 40-item test evaluates domains such as verbal and nonverbal communication, assertiveness, and the ability to navigate typical social situations. Developed to address heterosocial anxiety and enhance social competence measures, it has demonstrated strong reliability (Cronbach's alpha > 0.80) and validity through correlations with social anxiety and dating behaviors. The MHC is widely used in research and interventions targeting social skill development and perceived receptivity of their partner. Taking together, all 3 theories together might help to develop a fundamental theory to understand AA.

A future theory might propose that men's behavior in initial encounters is driven by adaptive strategies to mitigate perceived risks and maximize relational rewards. Drawing from social penetration theory men likely engage in shallow, low-risk disclosures to test compatibility while avoiding vulnerability. The intimacy process model provides the concept of responsiveness, which highlights the importance of feedback; positive signals from a potential partner can alleviate AA and encourage further interaction, while negative or neutral responses may inhibit engagement. Stimulus-value-role theory contributes to understanding AA by framing how men evaluate potential partners across stages, focusing first on surfacelevel stimuli (e.g., appearance or social cues), then exploring shared values, and only later considering long-term role alignment. This integrated framework could further explore how external influences, such as sex difference and prior experiences, shape men's approach strategies. For instance, societal expectations may pressure men to initiate contact, amplifying anxiety, while responsiveness from a partner may serve as a critical factor in overcoming this barrier. Similarly, cultural and individual factors, such as attachment styles or previous relationship successes, might influence the degree of risk men are willing to take. By combining these theories, a future model could offer a dynamic, context-sensitive understanding of male behavior in romantic initiation. This framework would not only highlight the complexity of earlystage interactions but also suggest interventions-such as social skills training or re-framing societal norms-to support men navigating these pivotal moments in forming connections.

Appendix II What kind of theories of Initiation do we know?

Research on relationship initiation has been informed by several theoretical frameworks, each offering unique insights into how romantic connections are formed (Clark et al., 2099). These theories explore different aspects of attraction, compatibility, and the processes underlying relationship development. However, critics argue that these theories remain incomplete, often failing to fully encapsulate the complexities of relationship initiation.

Social Penetration Theory

Altman and Taylor's (1973) Social Penetration Theory highlights the critical role of self-disclosure in relationship initiation. This theory posits that relationships deepen as individuals gradually share more personal information, both in breadth (variety of topics) and depth (intimacy of topics). Early stages of relationship development involve rapid disclosure, but the rate slows as the relationship matures.

Reciprocity, where both partners equally share personal information, is essential for the progression of intimacy. The theory underscores self-disclosure as a mechanism for building trust and connection in early interactions.

Intimacy Process Model

Reis and Shaver (1988) conceptualized intimacy as a dynamic, reciprocal process involving selfdisclosure and partner responsiveness. Their model suggests that the initiation of intimacy depends on the ability of a partner to listen empathetically, demonstrate understanding, and respond with care and acceptance. Such responses encourage further disclosure, creating a positive feedback loop that fosters intimacy and relationship growth. The model also introduces the concept of "interpretive filters,"

acknowledging that individuals' perceptions of their partner's actions influence the development of intimacy.

Stimulus-Value-Role Theory

Proposed by Murstein (1970), the Stimulus-Value-Role (SVR) theory conceptualizes romantic relationship development as a staged process. In the initial stimulus stage, attraction is largely based on superficial characteristics, such as physical appearance or initial impressions. As the relationship progresses, shared values, beliefs, and attitudes (the value stage) become more influential. Ultimately, long-term compatibility depends on the roles each partner assumes (the role stage), such as those related to career, family, or household dynamics. This theory underscores how relationships evolve from surfacelevel attraction to deeper emotional and functional compatibility. Levinger and Snoek's (1972) ABCDE Model provides a sequential framework for understanding relationship progression. The model outlines five stages:

ABCDE Model

Attraction: Initial impressions, influenced by appearance, behavior, and nonverbal cues.

Building: Exploration of shared personality traits, values, and interests to foster connection.

Continuation: Strengthening the relationship through trust, affection, and commitment.

Deterioration and Ending: While part of the model, these latter stages are not central to the initiation of relationships.

The ABCDE Model emphasizes the early importance of physical and emotional commonalities in building connections, with later stages focusing on relationship maintenance and breakdown.

Investment Theory of Commitment Developed by Rusbult (1980 Rusbult ( , 1983)), the Investment Theory of Commitment examines the factors contributing to relationship commitment, which often emerge during the later stages of relationship initiation.

Satisfaction:

The balance of rewards and costs in the relationship.

Investments: Time, effort, and resources that would be lost if the relationship ended.

Alternatives:

The perceived quality of other potential partners.

Norms: Social and personal expectations about staying in a relationship.

Comparison Level: Standards for evaluating what a relationship should provide.

The theory emphasizes that early relationship development is influenced by the interplay of these factors, shaping decisions to pursue or deepen romantic connections.
