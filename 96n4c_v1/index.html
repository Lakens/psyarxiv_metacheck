Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Introduction

Gender minority stress theory (Hendricks & Testa, 2012) conceptualises the elevated levels of mental health distress observed among trans and gender diverse (TGD) individuals as socially produced rather than inherent. Specifically, the model identifies discrimination, prejudice, and stigma as key drivers of psychological harm. A substantial body of research supports this framework, demonstrating that both distal stressors (e.g., misgendering, exclusion from services) and proximal stressors (e.g., internalised transphobia, expectations of rejection, and identity concealment) are associated with heightened risk of anxiety, depression, and suicidality (e.g., Price et al., 2024; Testa et al., 2017). However, much of this evidence base has prioritised interpersonal forms of trans negativity, often at the expense of examining structural sources of inequality (i.e., inequalities in social, legal, and institutional systems). This emphasis limits our understanding of how broader social and political systems shape TGD people's experiences of trans negativity and consequently constrains the framing of TGD oppression as a systemic rather than solely interpersonal phenomenon. Despite growing political attention to TGD rights around the globe, little is known about how changes in legal frameworks themselves function as psychologically consequential forms of structural discrimination.

Research examining structural stigma demonstrates its consistent association with poorer psychological wellbeing among TGD people, as well as with proximal stress processes such as concealment, vigilance, and avoidance (e.g., Bränström & Pachankis, 2021; Jones et al., 2017a Jones et al., , 2017b)). Within this literature, considerable attention has been directed towards healthcare as a key site in which structural discrimination is enacted. For example, barriers such as long waiting times for gender identity services (e.g., Squires et al., 2025), provision that is not responsive to gender diversity (e.g., Lisy et al., 2023), and limited inclusion of TGD health in medical education (e.g., Pregnall et al., 2021) have been linked to poorer wellbeing outcomes. Importantly, structural discrimination within healthcare does not operate in isolation but is closely tied to broader socio-legal contexts, including the level of national support, affirmation, and rights afforded to TGD people. Falck and Bränström (2023), in their analysis of 27 European countries, found that TGD individuals living in high structural stigma contexts (e.g., Croatia) were significantly less likely to access gender-affirming care compared to those in lower-stigma countries (e.g., Spain and the UK, at the time of analysis). Barriers to care in high-stigma contexts included fear of seeking treatment, concerns about prejudice from healthcare professionals, and a lack of knowledge regarding where to access specialist services.

Structural constraints such as the unavailability of services and lack of insurance coverage further compounded these barriers.

At the time of analysis in Falck and Bränström (2023) study, the UK was classified as a relatively low structural stigma context, reflecting its legal protections and formal recognition of gender diversity. However, there is growing evidence that the UK's structural context for TGD people is becoming increasingly restrictive. Since 2025, the UK has fallen below the European average for LGBTQ+ rights, currently ranking 22nd out of 49 countries (ILGA-Europe, 2026), with particularly weak performance in the domain of legal gender recognition. This decline is consistent with legal changes to equality law. In For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers ([2025] UKSC 16; hereafter the FWS ruling), the UK Supreme Court held that the terms "woman," "man," and "sex" in the Equality Act 2010 refer to biological sex for the purposes of the Act. Subsequently, the Equality and Human Rights Commission (EHRC) published guidance on the implementation of the ruling (Patel, 2025), from which a number of organisations began revising existing policies. For example, NHS England introduced updated guidance stating that the organisation of some single-sex healthcare services and facilities should be based on patients' sex rather than their gender identity, representing a significant structural change affecting TGD people (NHS England, 2026). Beyond its legal implications, the FWS ruling may function as a form of structural discrimination by altering the conditions of recognition and protection available to TGD people, thereby shaping perceptions of safety, belonging, and legitimacy.

The United States, like the UK, has experienced a rise in discriminatory laws and policies targeting or excluding TGD people (Patel, under review). A growing body of research links these structural developments to poorer mental health outcomes (e.g., Restar et al., 2024; Price et al., 2024). Large-scale evidence spanning all 50 US states indicates that structural discrimination (shaped by discriminatory law) is associated with greater psychological distress, including higher odds of past-month distress and increased prevalence of suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts over the past year (Price et al., 2024). Importantly, the psychological impact of structural discrimination is not limited to enacted policies; anticipation and uncertainty surrounding proposed legislation also appear to be harmful. For example, individuals who were uncertain about TGD-related laws, and who believed potential legal changes might remove or restrict their rights, reported significantly greater odds of depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress disorder (Hughto et al., 2022; Restar et al., 2024).

Harms may also operate indirectly through social and informational environments.

Exposure to media coverage of discriminatory legislation has been associated with increased rumination and physical health symptoms, such as sleep disturbance (Dhanani & Totton, 2023).

In addition, when individuals perceive that members of their social networks endorse such policies, this is linked to heightened rumination, depressive symptomatology, poorer physical health, and increased fear of disclosing a TGD identity. Qualitative findings further suggest that these legislative contexts contribute to feelings of hopelessness about the future thereby negatively shaping overall wellbeing (Dhanani & Totton, 2023). These findings suggest that structural discrimination may exert its psychological impact through relational and existential processes, including belonging, social safety, and anticipated rejection.

In support of this, emerging research has begun to demonstrate the relevance of structural discrimination for experiences of loneliness (e.g., Doyle & Barreto, 2023).

Traditionally, loneliness has been framed as an individual deficit in the capability to initiate and maintain meaningful social relationships (e.g., Peplau & Perlman, 1982; Perlman & Peplau, 1981; Cacioppo & Hawkley, 2009). More recently, however, researchers have argued that loneliness is fundamentally connected to individuals' social group memberships and sense of social identity (Haslam et al., 2018) and a growing body of work has reconceptualised loneliness as a social and structural issue, highlighting that individuals from marginalised groups report higher levels of loneliness due to exclusionary social conditions and reduced opportunities for belonging (Barreto et al., 2024).

Structural discrimination may, therefore, contribute to loneliness by undermining individuals' sense of belonging and perceived social safety within their wider environment.

Supporting this, research with sexual minority (gay) men has shown that objective indicators of structural discrimination are associated with increased loneliness, an effect that operates through heightened perceptions of structural stigma and reduced feelings of belonging (Lattanner et al., 2022). Although research directly examining these processes among TGD populations is not currently available, emerging evidence suggests similar mechanisms may be at play. For example, recent work indicates that loneliness mediates the relationship between trans negativity and mental health outcomes among TGD individuals (Jones et al., 2026), with qualitative findings suggesting that experiences of interpersonal trans negativity contribute to diminished belonging and subsequent feelings of isolation.

Alongside mental health, structural stigma may also shape the extent to which TGD people feel their gender is recognised in everyday life. Social gender role recognition refers to the degree to which individuals feel that others perceive and treat them in line with their gender and closely corresponds to the social dimension of gender affirmation, the process through which a person's gender identity is recognised and supported by others (Jones et al., 2019).

Within gender minority stress theory, the absence of such recognition (i.e., non-affirmation of gender identity) is conceptualised as a distal stressor (Hendricks & Testa, 2012). Social gender affirmation has been associated with lower depressive symptoms and higher self-esteem among trans women (Glynn et al., 2016). Recognition is, therefore, a core component of wellbeing for TGD people. Where legal frameworks no longer recognise a person's gender, this may signal to others that it can be disregarded, reducing the extent to which TGD people feel recognised in their day-to-day interactions (Scheim et al., 2025; Wall et al., 2026). Importantly, recognition is also inherently relational, as it is enacted within social relationships and interactions.

Loneliness may reduce the opportunities available for one's gender to be recognised and affirmed by others, suggesting that it may be one route through which structural discrimination undermines social gender role recognition.

While gender minority stress theory emphasises the harms of minority stress, it also recognises that TGD people draw on resilience resources that may protect against these harms (Hendricks & Testa, 2012; Meyer, 2015). Resilience in this context is not solely an individual trait but is often relational, arising from connections with others who affirm one's identity (Meyer, 2015). Puckett et al. (2025) capture this form of resilience as drawing strength from relationships in which one's gender is accepted, respected and protected (i.e., as identityaffirming resilience). Such resources have been found to buffer the association between stigma and psychological distress among TGD people (Bockting et al., 2013), although evidence for buffering effects is less consistent than evidence for their direct benefits (Mezza et al., 2024).

Identity-affirming resilience, by contrast, reflects the presence of specific relationships in which one's gender is affirmed. TGD people may simultaneously feel disconnected from wider society (i.e., with weak ties) while retaining close relationships (i.e., strong ties) that affirm their identity. On this basis, identity-affirming resilience may weaken the association between loneliness and mental health.

Taken together, this evidence highlights loneliness as a potentially key mechanisms through which structural discrimination may impact psychological wellbeing and social gender role recognition, and identity-affirming resilience as a resource that may lessen these effects.

As such, these constructs are important to consider in understanding the broader psychological consequences of legislative changes affecting TGD lives, including the FWS ruling's interpretation of sex, man and women within the Equality Act 2010.

The Current Study

The FWS ruling has introduced significant uncertainty for TGD people across England, Wales, and Scotland, where the Equality Act (2010) applies. These changes risk legitimising discrimination, limiting access to key services (e.g., healthcare) and spaces (e.g., sports facilities), and weakening existing legal protections. Emerging anecdotal evidence suggests substantial implications for wellbeing, including increased suicidality (Samaritans, 2026).

Given the public prominence of related debates and their potentially harmful consequences, it is concerning that TGD individuals' perspectives remain largely absent from discussions interpreting and implementing this policy at a time when rights protections are being actively contested.

Despite growing evidence linking structural discrimination to mental health outcomes, there has been little integration of structural conditions with the psychological processes of belonging and loneliness that may explain how such harms are experienced by TGD individuals.

The present research addresses this gap by examining how the FWS ruling is perceived and psychologically experienced as forms of structural discrimination. To achieve this, a concurrent mixed-methods design was employed, comprising a qualitative interview study (Study 1) and a cross-sectional survey (Study 2). Convergent parallel mixed-methods designs are particularly valuable when researchers seek to develop a more comprehensive understanding of a phenomenon by integrating complementary qualitative and quantitative forms of evidence and comparing their findings (Creswell, 2014). This approach enabled capture of both the depth of lived experience through narrative accounts and the breadth of associations between perceived structural discrimination, belonging, loneliness, and mental health outcomes (namely anxiety, depression and suicidality). Together, these studies provide the first empirical examination of the psychological impact of the FWS ruling and the first to integrate perceived or anticipated structural discrimination with belonging and loneliness as key explanatory mechanisms in a TGD sample. The findings have important implications for policy and practice, supporting the development of evidence-informed guidance for organisations interpreting and implementing the FWS ruling in ways that protect and promote TGD wellbeing. This is particularly important given ongoing debates surrounding Equality and Human Rights Commission (EHRC) guidance (May 2026), which has been criticised for its non-affirmative approach (Patel, under review).

Study Aims and Hypotheses

The qualitative study (Study 1) aimed to explore how the FWS ruling is interpreted and experienced by TGD individuals, with a focus on its implications for (1) Sense of community belonging and social safety, (2) Experiences of gender affirmation, (3) Overall wellbeing and (4) Support needs and access to support.

The quantitative study (Study 2) tested the following hypotheses. All hypotheses referring to perceived and anticipated structural stigma refer to this in the context of the FWS ruling and Equality Act 2010 definition of sex, man and women: H1. Greater perceived or anticipated structural stigma will be associated with poorer mental health, modelled as a latent factor of anxiety and depression (H1a), higher suicidality (H1b), and lower social gender role recognition (H1c).

H2. Greater perceived or anticipated structural stigma will be associated with greater loneliness.

H3. Greater loneliness will be associated with poorer mental health (H3a), higher suicidality (H3b), and lower social gender role recognition (H3c).

H4. Perceived or anticipated structural stigma will have a significant indirect effect, through loneliness, on mental health (H4a), suicidality (H4b), and social gender role recognition (H4c).

H5. Identity-affirming resilience will moderate the association between loneliness and mental health, such that the association is weaker at higher levels of resilience.

H6. The indirect effect of perceived or anticipated structural stigma on mental health via loneliness will be conditional on identity-affirming resilience (index of moderated mediation), and weaker at higher levels of resilience.

Interviews & Procedure

A semi-structured interview schedule was constructed with a lived experience panel of four TGD people residing in England, Wales or Scotland. This was an iterative process in which members of the panel would provide written feedback via email on the content, terminology and meaningfulness of the proposed questions. The schedule covered people's understanding of the FWS ruling and how it affects on daily life, their wellbeing, sense of belonging, social and legal recognition of their gender, social safety, workplace experiences and sport engagement or participation. The interview schedule used can be found on the OSF.

Participants who consented to take part completed an asynchronous interview via Gorilla, reading each question and audio recording their response, with prompts provided to support them. This method was chosen to support anonymous participation, as the topic was perceived to be highly emotive and one in which trust may be gained through anonymity.

Asynchronous interviews may also facilitate participation among those who feel anxious about social interaction, although they may still preclude those who experience significant voice dysphoria. When piloted with the lived experience panel, the method was considered easy to use and accessible to people who tend not to take part in interviews, although panel members said they would personally prefer a live interview to enable dyadic interaction. Weighing this against the funding available, we proceeded with the more cost-effective asynchronous format.

Analytical Procedure

Data were input into NVivo (15.5.0) and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021). The first author familiarised themselves with the transcripts through reading and re-reading, making initial analytical notes, and maintained a reflexive diary throughout to document emerging interpretations, assumptions, and emotional responses to the data. This enabled critical reflection on how their understanding of the socio-legal landscape for TGD people in the UK, and their research experience in LGBTQ+ health and wellbeing, may have shaped their interpretation of participants' accounts, while also serving as an audit trail of analytic decisions.

Coding was both inductive and deductive, grounded in participants' accounts while sensitised by concepts from gender minority stress theory (Hendricks & Testa, 2012). The first author coded all transcripts in NVivo, and the second author independently coded five transcripts (17% of the dataset). The first author then grouped related codes into broader patterns of shared meaning, and preliminary themes were discussed with the wider research team. Consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, these discussions were not intended to achieve consensus, establish coding reliability, or validate the thematic structure, but to facilitate reflexive dialogue, challenge assumptions, and consider alternative interpretations (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Theme development involved moving recursively between coded extracts, the full dataset, relevant theory, and reflexive notes, and the final thematic structure comprised four themes.

Findings Theme 1: Unwelcome Citizens: A Loss of Belonging

Participants' accounts reflected a profound sense of exclusion from wider British society following the FWS ruling on the terms sex, man and women in the Equality Act 2010 referring to biological sex. Many described feeling not only unprotected by the law but also increasingly unrecognised, undervalued, and unwelcome as members of society. Participants frequently interpreted the FWS ruling as a symbolic statement about whose identities were worthy of protection, as Morgan explained: "I feel a lot of distrust in society at this time. I don't feel protected under the law anymore. Feels like discrimination is now... very much being encouraged, not just allowed."( Morgan) Participants, like Morgan, perceived that legislative and policy changes cascaded into public discourse, shaping increasingly negative attitudes towards TGD people. In doing so, they felt such developments legitimised behaviours and expressions of transphobia that would previously have been more readily recognised as discriminatory. Further supporting this, Quinn explained how the changes to equality law "opens up the floodgates for a lot more transphobia."

Participants therefore understood the ruling not simply as a legal decision, but as a cultural signal that validated prejudice and exclusion.

While legal protections remain in place, including protection from discrimination on the grounds of gender reassignment under the Equality Act 2010 and provision for legal gender recognition through the Gender Recognition Act 2004, participants nevertheless experienced the relationship between legal sex and gender identity as increasingly complex, confusing and uncertain. Within this context, many felt that their rights and protections had become less secure, regardless of the formal legal position. Consequently, participants described a diminished sense of safety and trust in institutional protection, perceiving the ruling as evidence that legal recognition of their identities could be challenged or curtailed at any time: "I think in general it has just made the majority of the trans community feel a lot less safe and a lot less accepted by general society... it now is a lot more acceptable to label trans people as gender ideology or dangerous or misguided and it means that generally we feel like we are disrespected and not recognised in the eyes of the law." (Finley) Additionally, and as noted by Finley, a lack of legal affirmation was perceived as being associated with the spread of misinformation about the TGD community. This is concerning given that misinformation can be harmful both among cisgender people, through acceptance of its claims, and among TGD people, as a minority stressor (Cahill et al., under review).

Participants' accounts suggest that the ruling functioned as a distal stressor while simultaneously increasing expectations of future discrimination and vigilance regarding personal safety, amplifying both enacted and anticipated minority stress even in the absence of direct discriminatory events (Hendricks & Testa, 2012).

Heightening this vulnerability further was the perception that gender presentation was being policed more readily since the FWS ruling as Emery explains: "People have a heightened senses regarding trans stuff, like people are always on the lookout now for signs that someone might be trans, which just kind of hurts everyone really."

(Emery)

In this way, gender was no longer perceived by participants as a private issue but increasingly becoming a public concern; one that the cisgender community now had the legal right to take action against. This theme has demonstrated how participants experienced the ruling as extending beyond legal regulation into the realm of social belonging. The psychological consequences of this perceived social exclusion were considerable. Participants described institutional practices of categorisation and lack of recognition as "dehumanising" and, at times, making them feel "inhuman" (Ellis). The loss of social recognition and sense of belonging in broader society heightened concerns around their safety and changed how TGD people interacted with their social worlds, as will be explored in Theme 2.

Theme 2: Conditional Safety Through Conformity and Passing

Against a backdrop of heightened public scrutiny and the increasing regulation of gendered identities, participants described growing concerns about visibility, scrutiny and safety of TGD people within everyday life. For example, Robin noted: "If I go to the barber shop, you could easily now be walking into an anti-transgender chat, whereas before no one was talking about it...". Several reflected that disclosure of a TGD identity now felt more precarious than it had previously, due to uncertainty about how others may have been influenced by contemporary media and political discourse that had arisen as a consequence of the Supreme Court ruling. Consequently, participants felt that expressing their gender authentically had become riskier, resulting in greater self-monitoring, concealment, and pressure to conform to normative gender expectations: "I think it's potentially made me want to present more stereotypically masculine, just in case, particularly with bathrooms... it does kind of create that bit of pressure to be more stereotypical in the way I present masculinely." (Emery) Participants tended to regulate their gendered expression in anticipation of scrutiny, whether or not the scrutiny occurred. This finding aligns with Gender Minority Stress Theory (Hendricks & Testa, 2012), which proposes that minority stress arises not only from enacted stigma and discrimination but also from the expectation that such experiences may occur.

Participants appeared to engage in heightened vigilance and self-monitoring, anticipating negative reactions from others and adjusting their gendered expression accordingly. While such anticipatory strategies (e.g., concealment, avoidance) may serve a protective function by reducing the perceived risk of confrontation, discrimination, or victimisation, minority stress theory suggests that they can themselves become chronic stressors. The constant need to assess risk, monitor one's appearance, and modify self-expression may place a significant psychological burden on individuals, contributing to ongoing stress even in the absence of overt discrimination. Safety therefore becomes conditional upon silence, concealment, or reduced authenticity.

Others highlighted how occupying a position that enabled them to pass 1 as their gender afforded a degree of protection from the immediate consequences of the ruling. Passing was often constructed as a form of privilege, enabling individuals to access gendered spaces, avoid challenge, and remain largely invisible to public scrutiny. For example, Morgan described themselves as a "pretty well passing person" and therefore unlikely to be subject to policing of their identity. However, participants rarely described this privilege as providing complete safety or affirmation. Rather, passing appeared to function as a temporary shield within a broader social climate that was perceived as increasingly hostile towards TGD people. As Rowan explained, although they had "this element of protection and safety" because they passed, they nevertheless worried about "what restrictions the Equality Act and the High Court ruling will have" moving forward. While passing afforded temporary protection from scrutiny and exclusion, this protection was experienced as precarious, dependent upon continued alignment with binary expectations rather than genuine acceptance of gender diversity. Across participant's accounts, neither conformity nor passing provided lasting security. Instead, heightened public scrutiny positioned safety as something achieved through conformity, concealment, or social legibility rather than through recognition and inclusion, at the cost of authentic gender expression.

Theme 3: Democratic wellbeing: "It's definitely changed how I feel about my future"

Participants' concerns extended beyond immediate safety, with many describing feeling less optimistic about their lives and less able to envision a future for themselves within the UK. For example, one participant, Emery, explained how the FWS ruling had prompted them to consider contingency plans should conditions deteriorate further: "It's definitely changed how I feel about my future and it's made me start to think about what I'd do if things got worse, if this country did become more hateful, if I wasn't able to access healthcare, if I wasn't able to kind of be legally defined as male, where I'd go, what I'd do, whether I'd start looking into DIY hormones and things like that.

This country stops becoming an option." (Emery)

Emery, like others in this study, anticipates a series of escalating losses, including reduced access to healthcare, diminished legal recognition, and increasing societal hostility.

The anticipated trajectory of decline prompted practical contingency planning, including considering emigration and accessing DIY hormones, suggesting that participants viewed the ruling as potentially jeopardising fundamental aspects of their wellbeing and identity. From a minority stress perspective, this illustrates how socio-political developments can shape psychological wellbeing through the anticipation of stigma and loss of rights (Hendricks & Testa, 2012). However, participants' accounts suggested that the impact of the ruling extended beyond individual wellbeing to encompass what might be understood as democratic wellbeing. For example, Eden reflected: "When it comes to future employment it makes me very anxious in the sense that like, will they be accepting of me? Will they judge me? Will they discriminate against me because of who I am?... now it just feels like there's obviously no safety net, there's no way to protect yourself from being discriminated against… it makes you feel hopeless and the uncertainty of things, especially when it comes to working and maybe not being able to achieve certain roles." (Eden)

Eden's account highlights how wellbeing is shaped not only by personal relationships and mental health, but also by confidence in the fairness and protective capacity of social institutions. The fear that discrimination may occur without adequate legal recourse suggests an erosion of trust in democratic safeguards and equal citizenship. As a result, the ruling was experienced not simply as a legal decision, but as a threat to participants' ability to participate fully and equitably in social and economic life. For many, it also signalled that the institutions meant to guarantee equal citizenship were instead making democratic participation more conditional, less safe, and less accessible for TGD people which then impacted on the democratic well-being of TGD people (Connolly et al., 2025; Walters, 2024). In addition to concerns about their own wellbeing, this also led to concerns about the community's wellbeing as a collective, as Rowan reflects: "I think it's going to put a lot of pressure on our community. I think the suicide rates are going to go up even more than they have. I think there are going to be people who are too scared to come out of the closet and live as their authentic selves because the world is terrifying and they're worried about their personal safety." (Rowan) This quote highlights the collective dimensions of democratic wellbeing. Rather than focusing solely on personal consequences, the participant expressed concern that the ruling would create a social environment in which TGD people feel less safe, less visible, and less able to participate authentically in society putting "pressure on our community". For many participants a lack of belonging for their community was perceived to be causative (or predicted to be) of suicidality. This resonates with Joiner's (2005) Interpersonal Theory of Suicide, which explains how experiences that may contribute to feelings of thwarted belongingness, whereby individuals feel disconnected from others and unable to achieve meaningful social inclusion, can elevate suicide risk. This theme is not simply characterised by concerns about present-day discrimination.

Rather, participants described a diminished capacity to imagine a safe, stable, and fulfilling future for themselves and for the wider TGD community. In this sense, the loss of belonging extended beyond immediate experiences of exclusion, shaping participants' expectations, aspirations, and visions of what the future might hold for themselves and their community.

Theme 4: "I found my people": Community as Collective Protection

In the context of growing uncertainty and reduced engagement with wider society,

LGBTQ+ communities emerged as an important source of safety, solidarity, and collective resilience. Rather than withdrawing from LGBTQ+ communities during this period of community stress, many participants described actively seeking connection with others who shared similar identities, experiences, and values. Across accounts, and as per the gender minority stress theory (Hendricks & Testa, 2012), community appeared to function as a form of collective protection, providing both emotional safety and reassurance when broader social and political environments felt increasingly uncertain. As Casey's account illustrates, participants often felt safer and more comfortable among other LGBTQ+ people because of a perceived sense of shared experience and understanding. Belonging appeared to be experienced as a form of "safety in numbers", where connections with others who share similar identities reduced uncertainty about prejudice, judgement, or rejection. While Gender Minority Stress Theory proposes that community connectedness can buffer the effects of minority stress (Hendricks & Testa, 2012), the Social Identity Approach suggests that it is not social connection alone that promotes wellbeing, but the extent to which individuals experience a genuine sense of belonging within valued group memberships (Haslam et al., 2018; Sani et al., 2012). Consistent with this proposition, research has shown that belonging within family relationships is associated with better mental health outcomes among TGD people through increased social support and reduced loneliness (Jones, McDermott, et al., 2026), while also facilitating more positive experiences of gender transition (Jones, Wakefield, et al., 2026).

Many participants also perceived the ruling as strengthening community cohesion.

Rather than fracturing communities, community stress was often described as bringing people together. Charlie reflected on how the Supreme Court ruling had led people in the community to invest in these ties more readily: "I think people are more likely to band together now. I think definitely people, it's opened up a new discussion, it's opened up more engagement and I think as a result more people tend to band together, find their people and join forces. I think if anything it's just created more cohesion, is it cohesion the word? It's just created more of a stronger community vibe, people sticking together more." (Charlie) Community stress associated with the FWS ruling appeared to stimulate a collective response, pointing towards a sense of group efficacy, whereby strength was derived not simply from belonging but from the belief that communities could collectively "join forces" (Haslam et al., 2018). This is consistent with the rejection-identification model (Branscombe et al., 1999), which suggests that discrimination can strengthen identification with a stigmatised ingroup, buffering its psychological effects by fostering solidarity, shared meaning, and collective coping (Ramos et al., 2012; Schmitt et al., 2003). Rather than distancing themselves from a devalued identity, participants appeared to embrace it as a source of empowerment and collective mobilisation (Uysal & Acar, 2025).

Participants also discussed community as a form of protection against anticipated future challenges. Study 2 Method

The design, sampling procedure, hypotheses and analysis plan for this study were preregistered (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/25KX7). All accompanying documents for this project, including our open materials, anonymised data and R code, can be found on the OSF: https://osf.io/25kx7/overview.

Participants & Recruitment

Participants were invited via Prolific. In total 200 TGD adults (18+) residing in England, Wales or Scotland where the Equality Act 2010 applies were recruited. Recruitment took place in April-May 2026 shortly after the EHRC guidance was released. Participants were aged between 18 and 58 (Mage = 29.30, SDage = 8.14), and the majority were based in England (n = 165, 82.5%), followed by Scotland (n = 25, 12.5%) and then Wales (n = 10, 5%). Three participants were removed for failing two or more attention checks.

Two a priori power analyses were calculated. A Monte Carlo Power Analysis calculator for indirect effects (Schoemann et al., 2017) concluded that 200 participants were sufficient to detect an indirect effect at medium effect size (standardised beta .23) at .80 power. We also conducted an a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007) for the moderation effect. We specified the smallest effect size of interest of f² = 0.04. This indicated a required sample size of 199 participants at .80 power. Although larger samples are recommended for detecting small indirect effects, this sample size is consistent with established practice in research with TGD populations, which are relatively small and hard to reach. Measures & procedure

Participants were asked to complete an online survey hosted on Gorilla including the following measures. The order of the measures and the order of the questions within each measure were randomised.

Perceived structural stigma associated with the Equality Act 2010 changes to the

Definition of Sex was measured using a tool developed by the authoring team and consultation with the lived experience panel from for Study 1 which comprised four TGD people. Questions around the following domains were drafted and the feedback sought: Legal uncertainty (e.g., "I have felt legal protections for trans and gender diverse people have weakened"), Legitimisation of Discrimination & Social Safety (e.g., "I feel less safe in social spaces given the publics reactions") and, Access to Key Services and Spaces (e.g., "I expect it will or it has become more difficult for me to access safe and appropriate healthcare because of the ruling.").

The lived experience panel provided written feedback on the content, language, and readability of the initial 23 items, advising on the inclusion of topics (e.g., social safety) and accessibility, and were offered a voucher for their time. After feedback and revision of items, the tool comprised 19 items rated on a 5-point rating scale (1=strongly disagree;5= strongly agree).

Items 3, 13 and 18 were reverse scored and a higher score represented a greater perceived impact of the changes to the definition of sex under the Equality Act. All items can be found in in the open materials document on the OSF. Loneliness was measured using the Three-Item Loneliness Scale (Hughes, Waite, Hawkley, & Cacioppo, 2004). Participants were asked to rate their agreement with each statement (e.g., "How often do you feel that you lack companionship?") on a scale ranging from 1 ("Hardly ever") to 3 ("Often"). Higher values represented higher loneliness. In the general population, this measure has been found to be internally consistent (α = .72) and possesses both convergent and discriminant validity (Hughes et al., 2004). In a TGD sample, reliability has been demonstrated (α = .75; Jones et al., 2024; 2026). Anxiety was measured using the GAD-7 (Spitzer, Kroenke, Williams, & Löwe, 2006).

Seven items (e.g., "Feeling nervous, anxious or on edge") assessed anxiety during the preceding two weeks on a 4-point rating scale from 0 ("Not at all") to 3 ("Nearly every day").

Higher scores indicated higher anxiety. The scale has been found to have good reliability and validity in both general public and clinical samples, globally (Löwe et al., 2008). With TGD people, the measure has also been found to have excellent internal consistency (w = .91; Jones et al., 2026). Scores greater than or equal to 8 indicate clinically relevant anxiety (The National Collaborating Centre for Mental Health, 2024) Depression was measured using the PHQ-9 (Kroenke & Spitzer, 2002). Participants rated nine statements based on their feelings over the previous 2 weeks (e.g., "Little interest or pleasure in doing things") on a 4-point rating scale ranging from 0 ("Not at all") to 3 ("Nearly every day"). Higher scores indicated greater depression. Previous studies have shown the PHQ-9 has very good reliability (.87) and construct validity in a psychiatric sample (Beard et al., 2016). In a TGD sample, very good internal consistency has also been found (w = .90; Jones et al., 2026). Scores on the PHQ-9 that are greater than or equal to 10 indicate clinically relevant depression (The National Collaborating Centre for Mental Health, 2024). Suicidality was assessed using the Risk Assessment Suicidality Scale (RASS; Fountoulakis et al., 2012). The RASS is a self-report measure assessing suicidal ideation, intention, and history, with higher scores indicating greater suicide risk. Questions are rated on a 4-point rating scale ranging from 0 ("Not at all") to 3 ("Very Much"). Internal consistency was reported as good for the Intention subscale (α = .85), acceptable for the Life subscale (α = .69), and lower for the History subscale (α = .52).

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.6.0). The anonymised data supporting our findings and the analysis code are freely available on the OSF. A total score was computed where a participant answered at least 80% of a scale's items; on this rule missing data were low (2% for loneliness, 1% for social gender role recognition).

Our hypotheses were tested using lavaan (Rosseel, 2012). Mental distress (i.e., poorer mental health) was modelled as a latent factor indicated by PHQ-9 and GAD-7 total scores. Both loadings were fixed to one. Indicators were, therefore, retained on their original scale, and unstandardised estimates are reported with fully standardised estimates alongside. Residual covariances were estimated among the three outcomes. Our initial pre-registered model fitted poorly, and the modification indices suggested theoretically meaningful changes. These changes included entering resilience as a predictor of the mediator and of each outcome.

Loneliness and resilience were mean centred before computing their product term. Moderated mediation was tested formally using the index of moderated mediation. Age and self-reported gender were covariates throughout. Models were estimated using maximum likelihood, with full information maximum likelihood used for missing data. Indirect effects were tested using the product-of-coefficients approach with percentile 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals based on 5000 resamples. An indirect effect was inferred where the interval excluded zero and both component paths were significant (Yzerbyt et al., 2018). Fit was judged against CFI and TLI of at least .95, RMSEA of .06 or below and SRMR of .08 or below (Hu & Bentler, 1999).

General Discussion

The present paper employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods design to examine the psychological consequences of the FWS ruling for TGD people. Table 3 presents a joint display of findings from Studies 1 and 2 including areas of convergence or divergence across the two. In this section, we will discuss the findings from both studies in combination to demonstrate how, together, they advance our understanding, and below we discuss how, together, they advance our understanding.

While a substantial body of minority stress research has documented the harmful effects of trans negativity on TGD wellbeing, much of this evidence has focused on interpersonal experiences of prejudice, discrimination, and victimisation (Price et al., 2024; Testa et al., 2017). Comparatively less attention has been paid to structural forms of stigma embedded within legal, political, and institutional systems, especially. This emphasis risks obscuring the ways in which broader socio-political contexts shape TGD lives in the UK and may inadvertently frame oppression as the product of individual prejudice rather than systemic inequality. The present study contributes to this literature by demonstrating that structural stigma, in the form of the FWS ruling, was associated with both distal and proximal minority stress processes. At the distal level, participants described the ruling as legitimising transphobia, increasing public scrutiny of gender diversity, generating uncertainty about legal protections, and fostering concerns regarding discrimination, healthcare access, and social participation.

These findings support research conducted in the US (e.g., Hughto et al., 2022; Restar et al., 2024) and suggest that socio-political developments may themselves be experienced as stressors, even in the absence of direct discriminatory encounters.

At the proximal level, participants in Study 1 reported heightened vigilance, concealment, self-monitoring, and pressures to conform to normative gender expectations, alongside fears of future discrimination and rejection. Consistent with Gender Minority Stress Theory (Hendricks & Testa, 2012), these findings suggest that structural stigma may become psychologically embodied through expectations of trans negativity, concerns about safety, and restrictions on authentic self-expression. The quantitative findings support this interpretation.

Perceived structural stigma was associated with greater loneliness, poorer mental health, higher suicidality, and lower social gender role recognition, while loneliness emerged as a significant mediating pathway linking structural stigma to these outcomes, with perceived structural stigma associated with poorer mental health, higher suicidality, and reduced perceptions that others recognise and affirm one's gender. Levels of distress were also notably high, with 66% and 65% of participants exceeding the clinical cut-offs for anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 8) and depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10), respectively, aligning with growing evidence that the escalating structural stigma experienced by TGD people in the UK constitutes a significant public health concern (Connolly et al., 2025). Taken together, the findings extend minority stress theory by illustrating how structural conditions are not merely the backdrop against which minority stress occurs but may actively generate and sustain both distal and proximal stress processes. In doing so, they provide empirical support for conceptualising anti-trans stigma as a systemic phenomenon that operates through legal, political, and institutional structures as well as interpersonal interactions.

Our findings are also consistent with emerging evidence that the psychological harms of discriminatory legislation extend beyond current material conditions to encompass anticipated future losses (Dhanani & Totton, 2023; Hughto et al., 2022; Restar et al., 2024).

Participants expressed concerns about future employment opportunities, access to healthcare, legal recognition, and even whether they could continue living safely within the UK. Such concerns suggest that structural stigma may undermine what might be termed democratic wellbeing by reducing confidence in the fairness of institutions and in one's ability to participate as an equal citizen. Longitudinal research is now required to determine whether these future-oriented concerns translate into subsequent changes in wellbeing over time.

In our studies, loneliness emerged as a key explanatory mechanism through which perceived structural stigma was associated with psychological distress. Although previous scholarship has theorised that structural discrimination may undermine wellbeing through its impact on belonging and social safety (Barreto et al., 2024; Lattanner et al., 2022), our findings provide greater insight into why and how these processes may operate in the context of significant legal change affecting TGD people in the UK. Participants in Study 1 described the FWS ruling as diminishing their sense of social recognition, acceptance, validation, and citizenship. Uncertainty about whether they would be accepted, challenged, or excluded in everyday settings, including single-sex spaces, led many to alter their public presentation and to avoid wider social and public spaces, behaviours likely to further exacerbate loneliness.

These qualitative findings help explain the quantitative evidence that loneliness mediated associations between perceived structural stigma each outcome, consistent with previous research demonstrating that loneliness mediates the relationship between stigma-related experiences and mental health among TGD people. Given the well-established links between loneliness and suicidality (Joiner, 2005), together with concerns regarding elevated suicide risk among TGD people following the FWS ruling (Samaritans, 2026), there is a pressing need for further research examining suicide prevention and intervention strategies for TGD people in the UK.

Although identity-affirming resilience did not moderate the association between loneliness and mental health as hypothesised, mirroring findings from our other moderation analyses (Jones et al., 2026), both studies nevertheless underscored the importance of community as a psychosocial resource. The absence of buffering was somewhat unexpected given that Gender Minority Stress Theory proposes resilience resources can attenuate the effects of minority stressors (Hendricks & Testa, 2012; Meyer, 2003). However, it mirrors our previous findings that affirmative experiences did not attenuate the effects of gender minority stress on mental health (Cahill et al., 2026; Jones, McDermott, et al., 2026), and broader evidence that resilience factors more consistently show direct than buffering effects (Mezza et al., 2024). One explanation is that structural stigma differs qualitatively from the interpersonal stressors that resilience is typically conceptualised as protecting against. As Meyer (2015) argues, stressors embedded within social, legal, and political systems may ultimately require structural rather than individual responses, and because the ruling applies to all TGD people, even sources of affirmation and support exist within the same sociopolitical climate.

Participants described LGBTQ+ communities as sources of safety, belonging, solidarity, and collective action. Quantitatively, greater community resilience was associated with lower levels of loneliness and suicidality. These findings align with the Social Identity Approach to Health, which proposes that wellbeing benefits arise through access to meaningful group memberships and shared social identities (Haslam et al., 2018). Rather than buffering the adverse effects of loneliness once it is established, community resources may operate earlier in the causal pathway by fostering belonging, strengthening social connectedness, and reducing the likelihood of loneliness developing in the first place. This interpretation warrants further investigation, particularly through longitudinal research capable of establishing the temporal ordering of these relationships.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, data collection occurred shortly after the ruling and publication of the EHRC guidance, at a time when most organisations had not yet implemented changes to policy or practice. As such, participants' responses may primarily reflect the immediate uncertainty surrounding the ruling rather than its longer-term consequences. Future research should examine experiences following the wider implementation of guidance and any associated changes to access to single-sex spaces.

A limitation of Study 1 concerns the use of asynchronous audio interviews. While this approach provided participants with flexibility and anonymity when discussing a potentially sensitive topic, it did not allow for real-time probing or follow-up questions. Consequently, some nuance and complexity within participants' experiences may not have been fully captured.

Future research may benefit from combining asynchronous and synchronous interviewing methods to facilitate deeper exploration of emerging themes.

A further limitation relates to the measure of perceived structural stigma developed for Study 2. Although the measure was informed by theory and refined through patient and public involvement with TGD community members, it has not undergone extensive psychometric validation. The findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary evidence regarding perceptions of structural stigma associated with the FWS ruling, pending further validation of the measure.

Finally, trans women were underrepresented in Study 2, comprising only 18% of the sample. This is particularly important given that trans women are most frequently positioned at the centre of anti-trans narratives and debates concerning access to single-sex spaces in the UK (Bracco et al., 2024; Connolly et al., 2025). Although this pattern is consistent with previous UK-based TGD research, including our own studies (Cahill et al., 2026; Cahill et al., under review), the findings may not fully capture the experiences of the group most directly affected by these policy changes. Future research should prioritise recruiting larger samples of trans women.

Conclusion

Across two convergent studies, the findings suggest that the ruling is not experienced merely as a legal or policy change, but as a form of structural stigma with meaningful consequences for wellbeing. Qualitative accounts highlighted how the ruling was perceived as undermining recognition, acceptance, and citizenship, while quantitative findings demonstrated that greater perceptions of structural stigma were associated with poorer mental health, increased suicidality, lower social gender-role recognition, and greater loneliness.

Importantly, the findings identify loneliness as a key mechanism linking structural stigma to adverse psychological outcomes. By integrating qualitative and quantitative evidence, this study extends existing minority stress theory by demonstrating how legal and policy developments may shape wellbeing through their effects on belonging, social connectedness, and everyday participation in public life. At the same time, both studies highlighted the protective role of community. Although community resilience did not moderate the relationship between loneliness and mental health, stronger connections to LGBTQ+ communities were associated with lower loneliness and suicidality, underscoring the importance of collective identities and community resources during periods of social and political uncertainty.
