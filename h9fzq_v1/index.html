Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Full text

Abstract

Can a chimpanzee that has learned words for colors and objects find a blue pencil among pencils, straws, and Lego pieces in red, green, and blue? This seemingly simple task requires more than recognizing individual words: it requires integrating the meaning of a color adjective with that of a noun to identify an object possessing both properties. Children typically master this ability by 2.3 years. By age 3, they can integrate two modifiers, such as size and color, to follow instructions like "Find a large green straw." These achievements mark important milestones in the development of language comprehension, allowing children to combine multiple words into a unified mental representation of an object. Chimpanzees, by contrast, can learn hundreds of words referring to objects, colors, and actions. Yet, despite decades of research, they have not been demonstrated to reliably integrate even a color with a noun. In this paper, we propose a simple neurocognitive account of adjective-noun comprehension. We argue that this ability depends on the development of neural connections that allow the prefrontal cortex to modify sensory representations of objects in posterior cortical regions. This framework offers a testable explanation for a fundamental difference between human and chimpanzee language comprehension.

1. Introduction: the blue pencil problem

Most discussions of imagination, particularly in comparative research, treat it as a single capacity that a species either has or lacks. On this view, the question "Do chimpanzees have imagination?" has a simple answer, and the answer is yes. Chimpanzees almost certainly dream, recall absent objects, anticipate familiar events. Their closest relative, the bonobo, has even been shown to track objects in someone else's pretense 1 . But if imagination is a single capacity, and chimpanzees clearly have it, then it is impossible to explain how human imagination differs from chimpanzee imagination.

The question in our title is deliberately narrower. A blue banana does not exist, so a chimpanzee cannot have seen one; it can only be assembled in the mind from two independently known parts, "blue" and "banana." Asking whether a chimpanzee can imagine a blue banana is therefore asking not whether it has imagination, but whether it can voluntarily combine two stored representations into a novel one. We argue that this capacity is an overlooked prerequisite of even the most elementary syntax: understanding an adjective-noun phrase.

Consider the blue pencil test. A subject sees a tray holding pencils, straws, and Lego pieces, each in red, green, and blue. Told "blue pencil," the subject must select the one object that is both blue and a pencil. Using "blue" alone leads to the blue straw or the blue Lego piece as often as the blue pencil. Using "pencil" alone leads to a red or green pencil as often as the blue one. Only a subject that combines both meanings into a single target succeeds above chance across all combinations.

The difficulty is not perceptual. Chimpanzees are comparable to humans in basic visual processing, including visual acuity, trichromatic color vision, brightness perception, contrast sensitivity, and temporal resolution, as well as in the perception of basic forms, texture patterns, and shape from shading 2 . They also readily learn to discriminate colors and shapes, and to associate a lexigram, plastic token, or manual sign with either a color or an object 3 . The difficulty lies in composition: holding in mind the object named by the noun and altering it with the property named by the adjective. In children this composition appears early and predictably. Children typically follow color-noun instructions by about 2.3 years, size-noun instructions by about 2.6 years, and two-modifier instructions such as "Find a large green straw" by about 3 years 4 . This review develops its argument in four steps. First, objects are represented in posterior cortex as distributed ensembles of feature-coding neurons (Section 2). Second, the long-range connections between the lateral prefrontal cortex (LPFC) and these posterior regions differ markedly between humans and chimpanzees, in both anatomy and developmental timing (Section 3). Third, deliberately recoloring an object representation requires the LPFC to reach and reorganize those ensembles through long-range fibers, which chimpanzees lack (Section 4). Fourth, these differences explain why decades of ape language research have documented vocabularies of hundreds of words but no convincing case of adjective-noun integration (Section 5). We then turn to apparent counterexamples: dreams, in which a chimpanzee may well see a blue banana (Section 6), and ape behaviors that resemble voluntary imagination (Section 7). Next, we place adjective-noun comprehension within a developmental hierarchy of combinatorial tasks (Section 8) and describe clinical populations in which these abilities are selectively lost (Section 9). We then examine two ways a subject can answer correctly without integrating the words, memorized compounds (Section 10) and sequential filtering (Section 11). Finally, we propose a test that rules out both (Section 12) and discuss the account's limitations.

2. Image representation in the cortex

A familiar object is encoded in posterior cortex not by a single neuron but by a distributed ensemble of potentially millions of neurons 5 . Following Hebb's cell-assembly idea, we call such an ensemble an object-encoding neuronal ensemble 6, 7 . A banana is represented in posterior cortex by the coactivation of neurons coding its curved elongated shape, its yellow color, its texture, and its typical size 8.

The neocortex encodes these features in partly separate territories. Color is encoded most prominently in area V4 and adjacent ventral occipitotemporal cortex 9, 10 ; damage in these areas produces cerebral achromatopsia, the loss of color perception with preserved shape vision 11 . Object shape is encoded in the inferotemporal cortex 9, 12 ; damage in these areas produces visual object agnosia with preserved color 13 . These double dissociations show that "yellow" and "banana-shaped" are encoded in different places, and that a whole object is the bound activity of several regions. How do distributed features cohere into one object? A long-standing proposal is that neurons coding features of the same object fire in temporal synchrony, while neurons coding features of different objects fire out of phase 14 . Gamma-band synchronization accompanying perceptual grouping supports this view [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] , though the binding-by-synchrony hypothesis remains debated 19 . For our argument the precise binding code matters less than one consequence: whatever mechanism ties "yellow" to "banana shape," a different mechanism is needed to tie "blue" to it instead.

Recalling an object reactivates many of the same posterior regions used to perceive it 8 . Imagining faces activates the fusiform face area, and imagining places activates the parahippocampal place area 20 . Imagining colored objects engages color-selective ventral regions 12, 21 . Two kinds of imagery should be distinguished, however. (1) Recall of a familiar object reactivates an ensemble whose parts were bound together by experience. A yellow banana can be retrieved as a stored whole, through associative pattern completion within posterior cortex. (2) Deliberate construction of a novel object requires parts that were never bound together to fire as one ensemble. No experience has wired "blue" to "banana," so the binding must be imposed from outside the posterior cortex. The consensus is that the second kind of imagery is controlled by the LPFC 22, 23 ; we have called the mechanism of this control Prefrontal Modification (PFM) 24 . Understanding an unfamiliar adjective-noun combination, such as "blue banana," is the simplest case of PFM 25.

3. Frontoposterior connections in chimpanzees and humans

Humans and chimpanzees differ less in the posterior cortex that stores object features than in the white matter linking frontal and posterior regions (Figure 1). Three lines of evidence point to this difference: tract anatomy, prefrontal white matter growth, and the timing of myelination and synaptic plasticity. Diffusion tractography comparing humans, chimpanzees, and macaques shows that the human arcuate fasciculus/superior longitudinal fasciculus (AF/SLF) is not simply a larger version of the ape tract. In humans it terminates extensively in middle and inferior temporal cortex, regions involved in word meaning and object encoding. In chimpanzees its temporal terminations are far more limited, and in macaques more limited still [26] [27] [28] [29] . This fiber tract provides an anatomical route by which the LPFC can reach temporal regions involved in object representation.

The human prefrontal cortex is larger than the chimpanzee's in absolute terms 30 , and its underlying white matter is disproportionately expanded relative to gray matter 31 . Together, these differences suggest that the human LPFC has far greater capacity to control activity in posterior cortex than the chimpanzee LPFC, for two reasons: it has more cortical territory from which control can originate, and more of its fibers reach the regions that encode object features.

The most striking difference is when these connections mature. Histological work indicates that chimpanzee neocortical myelination approaches adult levels around sexual maturity, whereas human myelination continues into the third decade 32 . Gene-expression studies report that synaptic-development genes in the human prefrontal cortex peak at around 5 years, compared with under one year in chimpanzees and macaques 33 . This prolonged plasticity in humans matters because frontoposterior connections, like other experiencedependent circuits, are refined by use 34 . Combinatorial conversation and oral storytelling repeatedly ask a child to mentally assemble novel scenes from words. Human children get years of this practice while LPFC-to-posterior pathways are still modifiable. Linguistically deprived children fit the same picture: with little combinatorial language early on, they may later learn many words yet struggle with complex syntax, as a critical period predicts [35] [36] [37] [38] [39] [40] . In chimpanzees, by contrast, the window of prefrontal plasticity closes early, and frontoposterior tracts terminate sparsely in the regions that encode color and shape.

4. Why chimpanzees cannot deliberately recolor an object

Without long-range LPFC-to-posterior connections, we do not expect the LPFC to be able to modify representations encoded in posterior cortex. A chimpanzee may therefore recall a yellow banana, and may recall the color blue, but cannot deliberately make the banana blue in its mind's eye. Understanding "blue banana," or finding a "blue pencil" on instruction, requires exactly that operation.

There are four steps of adjective-noun comprehension: 1.

Noun activation. Hearing "pencil" activates the pencil ensemble in posterior cortex through a learned word-object association. Chimpanzees do this routinely.

2. Adjective activation.

Hearing "blue" activates neurons that code the color blue. Chimpanzees do this routinely too.

3. Prefrontal Modification.

The LPFC binds the blue-coding neurons to the pencil-shape neurons into one ensemble and suppresses the pencil's default colors. The result is a target template that never existed as a single memory.

4. Guided search. The template biases visual attention toward items that match it until the blue pencil is found.

Our claim is that step 3 is where chimpanzees fail. Steps 1, 2, and 4 rely on associative retrieval within posterior cortex and on attention, both of which chimpanzees have.

It is important to separate building the template from using it. When a chimpanzee is shown a blue pencil as a sample, it can find a matching blue pencil among distractors in a matching-to-sample task 41.

Here the conjunction arrives already bound by perception, and no neuronal ensemble modification is needed. The same animal, given the words "blue" and "pencil," must construct the conjunction internally. The account therefore predicts a sharp contrast: success when the target is shown, failure when the target is only named.

If the noun and adjective act separately, a chimpanzee should behave as though it heard one word or the other. On a tray with three object types in three colors, errors should fall overwhelmingly on items sharing only the color or only the object class, with roughly chance-level selection among the items consistent with the dominant word. A subject that truly integrates the words should make few errors of either kind. This makes the hypothesis falsifiable with existing lexigram-trained apes.

5. The ape language record

Over half a century of ape language research has produced large vocabularies but no controlled demonstration that a chimpanzee or bonobo can select an object from a novel adjective-noun phrase across a full set of color-object combinations 3, [42] [43] [44] [45].

Kanzi deserves particular attention because his comprehension test is the strongest in the literature. We are not aware of an adjective-noun integration test, but the bonobo was reported to understand some syntactically reversible sentences 45, 46 . For example, he arranged stuffed animals as his caretaker requested, both for "make the doggie bite the snake" and "make the snake bite the doggie." This success, however, can be explained by routinization, a learned procedure that requires no mental image of the described event. In both sentences, the first noun names the agent doing the biting. Kanzi could therefore respond algorithmically: 1) pick up the first-named animal and open its mouth, then 2) pick up the second-named animal and place it next to the first.

The limitation of a routinized procedure is its rigidity. It handles instructions in canonical word order (e.g., "put the red cup inside the green cup") but fails with non-canonical word order (e.g., "inside the green cup, put the red cup"). Our recent study illustrates this limitation by comparing typically developing children (about 5 years of age) with autistic adolescents (about 18 years of age) attending a special education school 47 . The children understood canonical and non-canonical instructions equally well. The autistic adolescents all completed the canonical cup-stacking task, but only half of them completed the same task with non-canonical word order, even though they were not time-limited and the instruction was repeated several times. Those who failed usually selected the correct cups but assembled them incorrectly. Their success with canonical instructions likely reflects routinization rather than understanding. By the same logic, Kanzi's success with reversible sentences does not show that he understood them, and a non-canonical version of his test would be the decisive check.

The African grey parrot Alex, studied by Pepperberg, also learned labels for both colors and objects [48] [49] [50] [51] . Shown an object, he could name its color and say what it was. Naming two properties of an object in view, however, differs from combining two properties that are only named. Alex's naming therefore does not establish PFM.

To our knowledge, no nonhuman animal has demonstrated PFM. One caution applies. This is an argument from absence, and absence of evidence is weak if the right test was rarely run. That is precisely why we propose the PFM test in Section 12.

6. Dreaming of a blue banana

A chimpanzee may well dream of a blue banana. Dreams combine features that were never bound in experience, yet they do so without voluntary prefrontal control. The capacity at issue in language comprehension is not experiencing a novel combination but producing a specified one on instruction.

During REM sleep, imaging studies show high activity in visual association cortex and medial prefrontal cortex together with deactivation of the LPFC 52, 53 . Dream content is correspondingly bizarre: objects morph, colors shift, and people merge into composites. These recombinations arise from spontaneous activity and associative drift among recently active ensembles 54, 55 . With the LPFC largely offline, nothing selects which feature attaches to which object. Consistent with this, patients with LPFC lesions report little change in their dreams 56 , suggesting that dream recombination does not depend on the LPFC. Mammals other than humans almost certainly dream 57, 58 and there is no reason to deny chimpanzees vivid, recombinant dreams. A banana might appear blue by chance, just as a waking hallucination might present an impossible object.

The distinction is between involuntary modification during dreams, which posterior cortex can produce on its own, and PFM, which voluntarily builds a specific combination chosen by an external instruction. This is why we call the mechanism Prefrontal Modification rather than simply Mental Modification: dreams show that mental images can be modified without the LPFC, so what chimpanzees lack is not modification itself but its prefrontal control. Understanding "blue banana" demands the PFM. The chimpanzee may see a blue banana in its sleep but cannot summon one when asked to.

7. Do apes show voluntary imagination elsewhere?

Several ape behaviors look like deliberate imagery, and a recent experiment indicates that a bonobo can track pretend objects 1 . We argue that in each case the ape recalls, replays, or tracks a familiar representation; none requires building a novel combination of features on command. The distinction is narrow, but it is the whole of our claim. PFM, as defined here, is required only when two conditions hold. First, the target combines features never experienced together. Second, the subject must deliberately build that combination in the mind, rather than having it assembled outside the head, whether by sorting objects in view, by following prompts from the surroundings, or by watching someone else's actions. Imagery that recalls a familiar object, replays a familiar event, or follows objects in someone else's pretense does not meet both conditions. Our account therefore does not deny apes mental imagery. It denies them one specific neural mechanism of imagery: the ability of the LPFC to deliberately modify the features of a mental object, such as its color or size.

Cathy Hayes reported that Viki, a chimpanzee raised in her home, repeatedly behaved as if dragging a toy on a string that was not there, at one point appearing to untangle the invisible string 59 . Taken at face value, the report still shows a familiar motor routine performed with a familiar object. Pull toys were part of Viki's daily life, and no features are recombined.

Santino, a male chimpanzee at Furuvik Zoo in Sweden, gathered stones in the calm morning hours and cached them along the shore facing the visitor area 60 . Hours later, when visitors arrived and he became agitated, he threw them. He later concealed caches under hay and moved closer to visitors before throwing. This is impressive behavior, which Osvath and Karvonen interpreted as planning for a future mental state 61 . Yet the scene Santino anticipates is one he has lived through many times: visitors, arousal, throwing. Recalling a familiar sequence and acting in advance of it requires future-oriented recall, which our account allows. The concealment innovation is more striking but could have been shaped incrementally, since hay was at hand and hidden stones went unnoticed by staff. In neither case does Santino construct an object or scene whose features never co-occurred.

Chimpanzees in the Goualougo Triangle bring two kinds of tools to subterranean termite nests: a stout stick to puncture the soil and a flexible probe to fish 62, 63 . They use them in a fixed order. In captivity, apes select a suitable tool and save it for use hours later 64, 65 . These are impressive, but they are procedural chains and stored tool-task pairings. Each step is cued by the state the previous step leaves: a punctured tunnel invites the probe.

In an experiment with Kanzi, the language-trained bonobo, an experimenter pretended to pour juice from an empty jug into two empty cups, then pretended to empty one of them 1 . Asked where the juice was, Kanzi chose the cup that still held pretend juice well above chance. He still preferred real juice to pretend juice when offered both, so he did not mistake the pretense for reality. Similar results were obtained with pretend grapes. The authors conclude that a bonobo can hold a representation of an imaginary object, a capacity often considered uniquely human. We accept this conclusion, and it sharpens our claim. Three features of the task keep it outside PFM: (1) The content is familiar. Juice and grapes are well-known objects. Kanzi imagined them as they normally are; nothing was recolored or reshaped. (2) The experimenter builds the scene. Each pretend pour and pretend emptying is a visible action. Kanzi follows a narrative scaffolded step by step, much as apes track a real object through hidden displacements. (3) The binding is object to location. Tracking which cup holds the juice may rely on spatial indexing of a familiar object, not on prefrontal modification of the object's feature ensemble.

What would change our view? The four cases share a pattern. Apes recall absent objects, replay familiar events, anticipate recurring scenes, and follow objects through someone else's pretense. What has not been shown is an ape producing, on instruction, an image whose features it has never seen together. A single well-controlled demonstration of that in language comprehension would falsify the account (Section 12).

8. A hierarchy of combinatorial tasks

Children acquire prefrontal operations in a fixed order, each asking more of the connections between the LPFC and posterior cortex (Table 1) 4.

(1) Prefrontal Modification (PFM) changes the features of one mental object, such as making a pencil blue or a straw large and green. These features are encoded in ventral occipitotemporal cortex, so PFM needs LPFC connections to that region alone (Figure 2). It is acquired first, by about 3 years.

(2) Prefrontal Synthesis (PFS) combines two or more independent objects into a novel scene with a specified relation, such as a cup placed under a box 24 . It must coordinate several object representations in the ventral occipitotemporal cortex and bind each to a location encoded in the parietal lobe, all at once. PFS therefore depends on more extensive and better-timed LPFC connections than PFM (Figure 3), and it is acquired later. PFS of two objects, which allows children to understand instructions relating two nouns, such as "inside the blue cup, put the green cup," emerges at around 3.7 years.

(3) PFS of three objects, which allows children to understand recursive descriptions relating three nouns, such as "the box under the cup behind the desk," emerges at around 5-6 years.

In chimpanzees, expression of synaptic-development genes in the prefrontal cortex peaks within the first year rather than at around 5 years as in humans 33 . On this account, chimpanzees never enter the window in which PFM emerges and they should stall at the second row of Table 1, however much training they receive.

The human timeline also suggests a critical period. Children deprived of combinatorial language during these years, through neglect or a late start in sign language, may later learn many words yet struggle with combinatorial comprehension, particularly spatial prepositions and complex syntax [66] [67] [68] [69] [70] [71] [72] [73] [74] [75] [76].

9. Neurocognitive disorders: the human dissociation

If PFM and PFS depend on specific frontoposterior operations, they should be selectively lost in some humans who retain individual word meanings. Several clinical populations appear to show this dissociation.

Damage to the LPFC or to the frontoparietal and frontotemporal tracts frequently impairs PFM and PFS while leaving word meanings intact 77 . In primary progressive aphasia, degeneration of the dorsal language tracts predicts impaired comprehension of syntactically complex sentences, independent of single-word comprehension 78.

A similar dissociation appears in development. A subgroup of autistic individuals can label hundreds of objects and colors yet fail instructions combining them. Parent-report studies of large autism cohorts find that some individuals never acquire the ability to understand adjective-noun combinations despite substantial vocabularies. In this subgroup the noun is understood, the adjective is understood, and the combination is not 79, 80 (Figure 2).

In the autism intervention literature, this pattern is known as stimulus overselectivity, or a lack of multiple-cue responsivity: a tendency to respond to only one feature of a complex stimulus, such as one word of an instruction, rather than integrating several [81] [82] [83] [84] . Our account offers a mechanism for this behavior in language: without PFM, each word activates its own representation, but the representations cannot be combined.

Across these groups the key observation is the same: knowing words is not enough. Each population retains vocabulary while losing, or never gaining, the capacity to deliberately alter a stored representation.

10. Memorized combinations: "bluepencil" as one word

The account does not forbid a chimpanzee from responding correctly to "blue pencil." It forbids only correct responses that depend on composing the two words. Any particular adjective-noun pair can be learned as a single unanalyzed label, exactly as a child might learn "hotdog" without imagining a hot dog. With enough trials, a chimpanzee could associate the sound sequence "blue pencil" with the blue pencil itself, through the same associative mechanism it uses for any noun. This possibility creates a serious methodological trap. Training on a pair and testing on the same pair cannot distinguish composition from memorization. Even training on some pairs and testing on others can mislead if test items share salient cues with trained items. Three design features rule out the memorized solution: 1.

Novel combinations at test. Each test phrase combines an adjective and a noun that were never paired during training, though each word was trained with other partners.

2. A full feature matrix. Every object class appears in every color, so every wrong item shares exactly one feature with the target. A one-word strategy then yields predictable, measurable errors.

3. First-trial scoring. Only the first presentation of each novel phrase is scored, before feedback can build a new association.

Under these constraints, a subject relying on memorized compounds performs at chance on novel pairs while remaining accurate on trained ones. A subject that composes performs well on both. The gap between trained and novel accuracy is the diagnostic measure.

11. Sequential filtering: solving the task outside the head

A subject can find the blue pencil without ever combining "blue" and "pencil" in its mind. It first collects every blue object, then picks the pencil from that blue set. Each word is used alone, one after the other, and the conjunction is built in the world rather than in posterior cortex. No PFM of an object representation is required. The same result can be reached by testing one object at a time. The subject takes or looks at an object and checks whether it is blue; if it is, the subject checks whether it is a pencil; if both answers are yes, the correct object has been identified; otherwise the subject moves on to the next object. This version differs from filtering only in order: both features are tested on each item in turn, rather than one feature across the whole tray. It too needs no combined template, because each word is checked separately against the object in view.

The strategy is a familiar kind of cognitive offloading. People often use their surroundings to hold intermediate results so the mind does not have to, as when a long multiplication is worked on paper, with each partial product written down instead of held in memory. Here the tray serves as an external workspace. Physically gathering the blue items, or simply fixating them one by one, stands in for the bound blue-pencil template that PFM would otherwise build.

Sequential filtering can make an ape appear to have PFM when it does not. An ape that passes a visibletray adjective-noun test might be filtering rather than using PFM. Any future success must therefore be checked against this route before it is taken as evidence of PFM.

The strategy also clarifies what the account claims. We do not claim chimpanzees cannot respond correctly to "blue pencil." We claim they cannot respond correctly by constructing a blue pencil in the mind's eye. Memorized compounds (Section 10) and sequential filtering are two ways to give the right answer without PFM.

Two manipulations make filtering impractical. The first separates thinking from searching. The phrase is given while the tray is out of view, and the subject may take as long as it likes to prepare. When it signals that it is ready, for example by touching a start key, the tray is shown only briefly. Because filtering can begin only once the objects are in view, the brief exposure leaves no time to sort the tray, scan it in two stages, or check its objects one at a time. A subject that has already built a combined template, by contrast, can make one direct selection.

The second manipulation increases the number of different objects and colors on the tray. Every added object type or color enlarges the subset left after the first filter, which lengthens the second search and increases the chances of error. For one-at-a-time checking, every added item is one more object to test. A subject guided by a combined template should be much less affected, because only one item on the tray matches it. Like memorized compounds, sequential filtering sharpens the prediction rather than weakening it. A chimpanzee that passes a standard visible-tray test only through filtering should fail when the tray is shown briefly, and should lose accuracy as objects and colors are added. A typically developing 3-yearold should pass both versions.

12. A proposed test of PFM in apes

The account stands or falls on one experiment: whether a language-trained ape can select an object named by a novel adjective-noun phrase when memorized compounds and sequential filtering are ruled out. The test needs no imaging and little equipment, and it can be run with apes that already know the relevant words.

The objects form a full matrix: every object class appears in every color. A basic set uses three classes (pencils, straws and Lego pieces) in three colors (red, green and blue). Every wrong item on the tray then shares exactly one feature with the target. Objects within a cell are identical, so nothing but color and class distinguishes them. A larger 5 × 5 set is prepared for the large-array condition.

The test has four phases: (1) Vocabulary check. The subject is asked for single words: "blue" (any blue object) and "pencil" (any pencil). Testing continues until the subject reaches criterion on every word. This confirms that any later failure is not due to vocabulary. (2) Task familiarization. The subject learns the task format with a subset of adjective-noun pairs, with feedback. The remaining pairs are withheld and never presented during this phase. (3) Novel-pair test. Each withheld pair is presented on a visible tray, and only the first trial of each novel pair is scored, before feedback can build a new association. Trained pairs are interleaved as a reference, and rewards are given on all trials regardless of accuracy, so that the test trials teach nothing. (4) Anti-filtering conditions. The phrase is given while the tray is out of view. The subject may prepare for as long as it likes, then signals readiness by touching a start key. The tray is shown briefly, and the first touch counts. In a second condition, the 5 × 5 tray replaces the 3 × 3 tray. Both conditions defeat sequential filtering and one-at-a-time checking.

Every named trial in phases 3 and 4 is paired with a matching control trial. Instead of hearing "blue pencil," the subject is shown an actual blue pencil, which is then removed. Everything else stays the same: the same tray, the same distractors, the same delay and the same brief exposure. Only the way the target is specified differs, by words or by sight. In the control trial, perception has already combined color and object, so the subject never needs PFM. The control therefore checks everything except PFM: color vision, memory for the target over the delay, quick selection from a brief display, and motivation in the presence of many distractors.

The account predicts that language-trained apes fail novel adjective-noun phrases in this design. A subject that uses only one of the two words picks among the three items matching that word, so on a 3 × 3 tray it should be correct on about one trial in three. Apes should score near this one-word level on novel pairs, while staying accurate on trained pairs, and their errors should share a single feature with the target. First-trial accuracy on novel pairs well above that level, with few single-feature errors, would falsify the account. The same apes should succeed on the matching control trials. If they fail those too, their failure on named trials points to a perceptual, attentional or memory limit rather than to the absence of PFM, and the test says nothing about the account.

Children provide the reverse check. The account predicts that most children pass the named condition by 3 years of age, on both the brief-exposure and the large-array trays. If children who pass a standard visible-tray version (phase 3) fail these conditions, the design may be too demanding, and the ape results would need to be read with that in mind.

First-trial scoring yields few trials per subject: a 3 × 3 matrix with three familiarization pairs leaves only six novel pairs. Larger matrices, additional object classes, and several exemplars per cell increase the number of novel trials.

The same design extends up the hierarchy. Two modifiers ("the large green straw") use a three-way matrix of size, color and object.

PFS can be tested with spatial instructions in non-canonical word order ("inside the blue cup, put the green cup"), which defeat routinized responding. An ape that fails the basic test should fail every extension.

Limitations

PFM need not be conscious. People with aphantasia report no voluntary visual imagery yet understand "blue banana" 85 . Our claim concerns the modification of neural representations in posterior cortex, not the vividness of the image in the "mind's eye." One possibility is that in aphantasia these representations are formed but do not reach conscious awareness, for example because attention is not directed to the activity of visual areas. The PFM process may run without conscious awareness of it.

Alternative explanations for ape failure include limited working memory, low motivation in tasks with many distractors, and training regimes that never rewarded composition. Each can be addressed. Working memory is unlikely to be the limit: Kanzi readily followed commands such as "put the ball on the pine needles," "put the ice water in the potty," "give the lighter to Rose," and "take the ball outdoors" 86 . These commands contain more words than a two-word adjective-noun phrase and so place greater demands on auditory working memory. Yet they need not rely on PFM or PFS. Kanzi had only to identify the objects named; the action did not need to be combined with them, because it was predictable from the objects themselves. Low motivation is tested by the matching control, which uses the same tray, the same distractors and the same timing; an ape that succeeds there is not deterred by the task itself. Lack of relevant training is addressed by the familiarization phase, which explicitly rewards correct responses to adjective-noun pairs before the novel pairs are tested.

Several links in the argument remain untested. The comparative anatomy is correlational: it shows that humans and chimpanzees differ in frontoposterior connections, not that this difference causes the difference in comprehension. Great-ape imaging studies rely on small samples. Most importantly, to our knowledge, the decisive behavioral test has not yet been run. The account should therefore be read as a hypothesis designed to be tested, not as a settled conclusion. Its value lies in making a clear prediction that a single experiment, described in Section 12, can confirm or refute.

Conclusion

Our proposed answer to the title question is "not on demand." A chimpanzee can know "blue," know "banana," and perhaps dream of a blue banana, but we argue it cannot deliberately build one in response to an instruction. That operation requires the LPFC to reorganize neuronal ensembles in posterior cortex, and in chimpanzees the frontoposterior connections that permit it reach far less of the posterior cortex, are sparser, and also mature for a shorter period than in humans. This framing locates a fundamental human-ape difference not in vocabulary, perception, or memory, but in the voluntary recombination of stored representations. It explains why decades of ape language work yielded large lexicons without adjective-noun composition, why some humans with intact vocabularies lose or never acquire composition, and why dreams and pretend play are not counterexamples.
