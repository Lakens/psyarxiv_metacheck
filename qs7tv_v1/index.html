Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Much like meditation, breathwork is an umbrella term for a vast school of practices that affect (neuro)physiology and phenomenology. Recently, breathwork has moved rapidly from Yoga and wellness studios into offices and mobile apps, and emerging data suggest it can reduce stress and improve quality of life. Yet practices, durations ('doses'), settings and facilitators differ widely, making comparison difficult. Moreover, no widely adopted, secular, graded framework offers the general population a full breath toolkit. Safety is also unpredictable, especially when faster-paced breathwork is taught without rigorous screening, expert instruction and integration. Before Mindfulness-based Stress Reduction (MBSR) emerged in 1979, Western meditation faced unregulated, fragmented and predominantly spiritual practice. What changed was not a new technique but an eight-week, secular, manualised and uniform structure that could be repeatedly tested and scaled; trial evidence followed the framework, not the reverse. We argue breathwork now requires such a moment to flourish into a scalable and accessible intervention. We clarify the model of MBSR and propose an adapted new framework, Breathwork-based Stress Reduction (BBSR), which parallels MBSR's weekly core (macro)practices with breathwork equivalents, alongside brief micropractices and integration into daily life. BBSR is grounded in the modulation of the autonomic nervous system, progressing from foundational resting breathing to slow-paced practices, movement, retentions and faster-paced breathing (think: sitting, walking, running, sprinting). Breathwork zones (BWZs) rise progressively (e.g., BWZ-1; noticing, BWZ-2; physiological sigh, BWZ-3; retention, BWZ-4; conscious connected breathing), supported by rigorous screening and skilled instructors. If necessary, all participants are invited to soften their experience, prioritising safety, tolerability and agency. Participants finish by building their own individualised toolkit. As MBSR is regarded as the 'gold standard' for meditation interventions in the West, BBSR should aim to become the gold standard of structured breathwork. In sum: breathwork now needs its MBSR moment.

Introduction

Chronic stress is linked to numerous physical and mental health conditions, from high blood pressure and vascular disease to depression and anxiety (Mariotti, 2015). It can keep the autonomic nervous system (ANS) in a state of sympathetic ('fight-or-flight') dominance, which can alter regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and cortisol secretion, contributing to allostatic load and increased risk of cardiovascular, metabolic and mental health problems (McEwen, 2017). Many people practise meditation to relieve stress, stress-related illnesses and augment quality of life (Goyal et al., 2014). Recently, voluntarily controlled breathing techniques, or breathwork, have been dubbed the 'new meditation' within the wellness world; people are gravitating towards this modality in response to modern-day chronic stress due to its accessibility and acute effects (Ap, 2025), such as increasing heart-rate variability (HRV) (Lehrer and Gevirtz, 2014), often interpreted as greater parasympathetic ('rest-and-digest') activity (Gerbarg et al., 2019). The first step of controlled breathing (breathwork) is to become aware of the breath in the first place (meditation). However, the former arguably offers a more accessible state due to immediate physiological perturbations, rather than solely a manipulation of attention/cognitive control (like the latter meditation).

In Latin (spiritus), ancient Greek (pneuma), Sanskrit (prana) and ancient Chinese (qi), a single word carries the meanings of breath, spirit, life force and vital energy all at once. Breathwork is often traced back more than 10,000 years to shamanic teachings (Brown and Gerbarg, 2022), but its best-known form is found in Pranayama, a classical limb of Yoga, which means the regulation of vital energy (prana = life force; ayama = control) (Paul, 1888). In the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, meditation is part of a broader, holistic practice that includes breath regulation, which is seen as preparation for meditative contemplation (Bryant, 2009). Breathwork can now be found across yoga and wellness studios, gyms, corporate wellbeing schemes, retreats and mobile apps, under labels ranging from pranayama, coherent breathing and cyclic sighing to the Wim Hof method, conscious connected breathing and holotropic breathwork. The science is emerging but encouraging. A 2023 meta-analysis of randomised-controlled trials (RCTs) showed breathwork improved subjective stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms (Fincham et al., 2023d), the same year a landmark RCT found just five minutes of either daily cyclic sighing, hyperventilation-with-retention or box breathing for four weeks improved mood more than a particular form of mindfulness meditation (Balban et al., 2023), and reviews highlight reliable physiological changes during slow-and fast-paced breathwork (Russo et al., 2017; Sevoz-Couche and Laborde, 2022; Fincham et al., 2023a).

However, in real-life settings, breathwork practices' durations ('doses'), settings and instructors can vary considerably such that outcomes are difficult to compare (Bentley et al., 2023; Fincham et al., 2023d). This heterogeneity both reflects and perpetuates the absence of an agreed, replicable curriculum or set of practice standards, which in turn limits the generation of robust data on efficacy and safety. The unregulated breathwork world could be described as the 'Wild West', with innumerable techniques, some trademarked or proprietary, making it overwhelming, confusing and potentially dangerous for those who wish to learn more about these practices. Few harms of breathwork are reported so far (Fincham et al., 2023d), although there is a risk that adverse events have been underreported due to lack of shared standards for the delivery and safety monitoring of breathwork practices. Yet, such reports are essential to guide the design of safe protocols and rigorous safety/inclusion criteria. Careful standardisation of breathwork practices and protocolised reporting of adverse events will ultimately ensure consistent care, better health outcomes and detection of facilitatorrelated harm.

An evidence-base is needed to inform decisions regarding who and how breathwork should be offered to, both within and beyond healthcare services, and to underpin standards for practitioner training, accreditation and participant screening. Few real attempts at integrating breathwork into healthcare settings for stress and mental health (e.g., the National Health Service (NHS) in the UK) have been made (not for want of trying), with many organisations focusing on a specific or narrow category of practices, sometimes charging fees which make breathwork inaccessible. Nonetheless, breathwork should offer a low-cost way to reduce suffering, with many reporting perceived subjective effects of feeling more relaxed, calm, focused and less stressed.

A recent appraisal of the current breathwork literature regarding the management of stress and mental health has shown that some techniques may suit particular people or outcomes better, but none have yet been shown to be superior (Fincham et al., In press). Nearly five decades ago in the West, there were enthusiastic meditation practitioners yet no widely adopted clinical way to deliver it. What changed was not a new technique, but a programme. Before Mindfulness-based Stress Reduction (MBSR; see next section), meditation encountered similar criticisms that breathwork faces today; it was perceived by the mainstream as excessively spiritual, unregulated and divided into opposing lineages. This paper argues that the breathwork field has got to the point that it now requires a framework, and the history of MBSR provides an appropriate roadmap. As N-of-1 trials and personalised approaches gain attention in meditation and breathwork research (Vetter et al., 2026) and beyond (Kas et al., 2025), offering complementary ways to investigate individual responses alongside the population-level evidence provided by RCTs, a multi-faceted research programme is needed. We propose such a programme, dubbed Breathwork-based Stress Reduction (BBSR). Much like MBSR, BBSR standardises the structure and teaching of the course. Furthermore, BBSR extends the notion of precision medicine to provide participants the opportunity to personalise their choice of breathing practices afterwards. Truth is one, paths are many, and like different meditation practices, different breathwork techniques may work for different people. We offer one potential structure for this decision-making process. Just as MBSR may be deemed the 'gold standard' for secular, educational mindfulness practice in the Western world (Foale et al., 2024), BBSR could become the gold standard for breathwork.

Mindfulness-based Stress Reduction

Despite meditation being practiced for millennia, only a handful of studies were available in the 1970s compared to today (Goleman and Davidson, 2017), with virtually no integration into Western healthcare settings. That changed in 1979 when Dr. Jon Kabat-Zinn, a molecular biologist and meditation practitioner, opened the Stress Reduction Clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center, secularising and delivering Buddhist-derived mindfulness (awareness which arises with focused, nonjudgmental attentiveness to present moment experience) techniques in a clinical setting (Kabat-Zinn, 1990). The main goal was to see if it was feasible to construct a vehicle for the successful guidance of medical patients in fairly demanding mindfulness meditation, also comprising mindful Hatha Yoga, and its direct application to the stress, pain and illness they were experiencing (Kabat-Zinn, 2003). The result was a communal eight-week course, "MBSR", with weekly ~2.5 hour group classes, daily home practice of ~45 minutes and one all-day retreat, built around the core techniques of body scanning, sitting meditation and mindful movement (e.g., yoga) (Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust, 2026), along with integrating mindfulness into everyday life with brief practices.

From the first published evidence in 1982 as a pilot for chronic pain patients (Kabat-Zinn, 1982), research into MBSR continues. A 2004 meta-analysis showed medium effect sizes in both controlled and uncontrolled studies across various clinical and nonclinical contexts, whilst stating the limitations of the included empirical studies (Grossman et al., 2004). Likewise, a 2015 meta-analysis in healthy individuals found very similar effect sizes, with MBSR being reported as moderately effective in improving stress, anxiety, depression and quality of life (Khoury et al., 2015). Meta-analyses on MBSR suggesting benefit are continuously published across various health domains to this day (Geiger et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2025).

Numerous features made MBSR scalable as opposed to a compelling one-off. MBSR was: (a) Standardised (a clear structure/syllabus), (b) Lay and embedded within an educational framework, grounded in direct experiential understanding (instructors practise the teachings), and (c) Verifiable, as a set weekly framework can be manualised, repeated and tested. It is important to reiterate that this course existed prior to trial evidence. The standardised framework is what allowed such later substantiation. We believe this needs to happen for breathwork. However, the analogy has limits: MBSR practices may carry lower acute risk, whereas the upper end of BBSR (faster practices) requires screening and adverse-event monitoring, even though many people already practise such techniques recreationally.

In addition to integrating meditation into the mainstream of the West (hundreds of thousands of people having now taken a course (UMass Memorial Health, 2026)), MBSR inspired the emergence of other Mindfulness-based Interventions (MBIs). Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), for example, was developed by Drs. John Teasdale, Zindel Segal and Mark Williams. MBCT replicated the general framework of MBSR with similar practices but added more precision via integration of elements of cognitive therapy, first focusing on depression (National Institute for Health and Care Research, 2022). The evidence-base, including a 2016 individual patient data meta-analysis of randomised trials (Kuyken et al., 2016), built up to the point that NICE guidelines include the option of group-based MBCT for managing depression (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2022), thereby displaying further benefits of manualised mindfulness meditation.

It is critical to note that adverse experiences are also reported in MBIs -which are often assumed to be benign. It has been suggested that such reports may be more frequent than earlier work implied, although estimates vary widely across studies and definitions (Matko and Van Dam, 2026). The most commonly reported effects include anxiety, low mood and trauma-related symptoms, and the factors that predict who is affected are not yet well understood. The safeguards discussed for breathwork, such as screening, informed consent and monitoring during practice, are therefore likely to be relevant to MBIs too, although the evidence for their effectiveness in this context is still developing.

While MBSR and MBCT are centred on mindfulness and can both improve mental and emotional health, they are intended to serve distinctive purposes and focus on different populations; MBSR is an adaptable course devised for overall stress reduction and wellbeing augmentation whereas MBCT is more clinically oriented, focusing on recurring depression and anxiety (Brown University School of Professional Studies, 2023). As such, there are usually fewer participants in MBCT (8-15) compared to MBSR (15-30) with 1-2 teachers. Such courses are now available online as well as in-person. While the MBSR and MBCT meditation practices are similar and appear to benefit wellbeing, some techniques may have greater adherence (Fincham et al., 2023b). This will likely be the same with breathwork, with engagement depending on the individual. Akin to exercise and diet, the best breathwork you can do is the one that is effective for your goals, that you enjoy, and that you can practise consistently. Timing, pace and dose are also likely to play a role, and there may be different practices for clinical and subclinical populations.

Meditation Paving the Way for Breathwork

Notably, becoming aware of breathing (a universal aspect of meditation) is the first stage of breathwork. We believe that breathwork research could benefit from following a similar path to mindfulness research, particularly the development and evaluation of MBSR, to establish its therapeutic potential and support wider clinical scaling. One needs to become aware of the breath (i.e., mindfulness) to then control it (i.e., breathwork). However, such sustained single-pointed attention of the present moment can be extremely challenging; in fact, Kabat-Zinn says it can be "some of the hardest work in the world" (Kabat-Zinn, 2005). In the current attention economy, we are bombarded by information distracting and competing for our attention, and so letting thoughts come and go before placing attention back on breathing can be extremely challenging -especially for beginners. Control of breathing may provide an alternative and more accessible approach.

Breathing is unusual among vital biological processes, like heartbeat or digestion, in that it can be regulated both automatically and voluntarily. To take control of breathing acutely (and inherently) directs attention to it and can thus be easier for many novices than attention to automatic breathing (notwithstanding the 'Observer Effect' on the breath (Western and Patrick, 1988)). In fact, in many traditions, breathwork is used before meditation practices as a tool to prime the system for deeper contemplative work. For instance, kapalabhati (skull shining breath) pranayama is typically categorised as a shatkarma, one of the six cleansing kriyas in hatha yoga, representing preparatory purification practices that come before meditation (Swathi et al., 2021).

The ability to control breathing impacts both parasympathetic and sympathetic parts of the ANS. Slower breathwork tends to favour the former, and intense, faster breathing the latter (Laborde et al., 2022; Fincham et al., 2023a). Breathwork is thus strongly rooted in physiology. Heart rate typically increases and decreases with inhales and exhales respectively, a phenomenon known as respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) or respiratory heart rate variability, which is a major contributor to overall HRV. RSA primarily occurs because the vagus nerve, the main brake on heart rate, eases off during each inhale and re-engages during each exhale (Berntson et al., 1993). The sympathetic nervous system is too slow to keep pace with individual breaths, so RSA largely reflects the ebb and flow of this vagal brake and is a major contributor to overall HRV (Cacioppo et al., 2007). As stress is robustly associated with low HRV (Kim et al., 2018), this provides a simple illustration of how breathwork might… work.

Voluntarily slowing the breath strengthens vagal influence and amplifies RSA, a parasympathetic shift that can be felt as calming or grounding. Faster, more vigorous breathing does the converse: less vagal engagement tips the balance toward sympathetic activation. Critically, the shift from automatic to deliberate breathing activates brain regions beyond the brainstem's automatic rhythm generators, including frontal, insular and cingulate cortices (Herrero et al., 2018), areas that also support our awareness of internal bodily signals and our capacity for emotional regulation (Critchley and Harrison, 2013).

Choosing to alter the breath therefore creates a feedback loop between action and awareness: a deliberate change in breathing produces a measurable shift in heart rate and autonomic balance, which is itself sensed, reinforcing both the physiological change and the felt experience of control (Boyadzhieva and Kayhan, 2021). This loop has a direct line to the body's core machinery. RSA is predominantly generated by central feedforward mechanisms within the brainstem's respiratory networks (Skytioti and Elstad, 2022); suppressing central respiratory drive while preserving cyclical lung stretch reduced RSA by approximately 60%, corroborating a central origin over peripheral reflex. Voluntary breathing therefore acts on the very source of this heart rate modulation, not just its downstream effects (Farmer et al., 2016; Herrero et al., 2018). This capacity to reach into one's own autonomic regulation at its origin may be what distinguishes breathwork from passive exposure to autonomic shifts and what underpins its emotional and contemplative potential.

A key difference between most meditation and breathwork practices in general is that the latter can become psychophysiologically demanding (thus carrying contraindications (Fincham et al., 2023a)). If you attend four different breathwork classes or workshops, you may get four profoundly diverse experiences, all the way from sleepiness during soft box breathing to hyperventilation-induced intense experiences. Accordingly, we propose an evolution of breathwork zones (BWZs), including BWZ-1 (e.g. noticing), BWZ-2 (physiological sigh), BWZ-3 (retention), BWZ-4 (conscious connected breathing) to help novices navigate the breathwork world. An explanatory parallel or metaphor is: sitting, walking, running, sprinting; though ironically the posture taken (as practices increase in intensity) is directly oppositional to the analogy we provide. For example, the high demand of a 'sprinting' session would require a participant to be supine during the session. Moreover, to enhance accessibility and safety, all participants can be invited to soften the practices if necessary, while contraindications are handled through comprehensive screening (see below).

MBSR is precisely standardised into a uniform eight-week syllabus with weekly progressions (body scan, gentle yoga, sitting meditation, etc.). A breathwork equivalent requires a set procedure; for instance, moving from basic foundational breathing and biomechanics in week 1, to modulating the ANS with a focus on the parasympathetic branch in weeks 2-4, to controlled stressors and focus on sympathetic responses in weeks 5-7 (all at the participants' preferred intensity). This provides a whole breath toolkit for people to learn what they enjoy the most and what they can deploy in real-time for desired psychophysiological states.

Kabat-Zinn intentionally removed spiritual language from mindfulness by reframing and introducing MBSR as a secular tool for stress reduction. To make it more accessible, breathing practices also need a unified curriculum like BBSR. The idea here is not to detract from the richness of breathwork, nor mindfulness meditation and MBSR; they are much more than a few basic practices and stress reduction. This is simply a start in trying to offer a framework that makes breathwork more accessible to the general public in a structured format by safely exposing recipients (in order of progressive physiological demand) to a spectrum of non-linear, heterogeneous, breathwork-related benefits that can be tailored to individual need. Similar to the core practices in MBSR, we will now discuss some of the potential core practices which could be implemented into this novel BBSR framework.

Breathwork-based Stress Reduction

The practices listed in Table 1 and described below are not rigid or irrevocable and could be finalised through a Delphi study (Beiderbeck et al., 2021) to reach consensus on what is included and the order of the programme. The ideal panel would include breathwork researchers and teachers, clinicians, medical and safety experts and people with lived experience; consensus would also be sought on explicit screening, modification and exclusion criteria (mainly for BWZs 3-4; retentions and/or faster breathing). The framework can be centred around helping participants understand and progressively learn to work with different autonomic and chemoreflex responses to breathwork practices, including changes in heart rate, cardiac vagal activity and responses to rising carbon dioxide levels. Additional emphasis would be made on how these responses depend on the specific technique, its intensity and the person. The progression could be organised along the proposed BWZs (see Figure 1), their nuanced benefits and their respective physiological demands, all of which would be summarily introduced in week 1.

Safety will be prioritised, with a thoughtfully curated set and setting and at least two well-trained facilitators present. Each weekly class will last approximately two hours (including psychoeducation, instruction, macro-and micro-practices, discussion and integration), with around 40 minutes of daily home practice, groups of 15-30 participants, and a day-long retreat after the programme. Appropriate music playlists will be curated and there will be minimal need for equipment (to further increase accessibility). The gentle BWZ-1 ('sitting') and BWZ-2 ('walking') practices could be done seated, lying down, standing or moving -and participants would be encouraged to explore what is best for them during home practice. BWZ-3 ('running') and BWZ-4 ('sprinting') practices will only be performed supine. Safety addendums are summarised in the next section.

On top of the core macropractice(s) of each week, in line with MBCT which has the '3-minute breathing space', micropractices of breathwork will be encouraged. This could depend on the techniques taught in previous weeks (along with their respective BWZs) and participants could personalise to themselves; for example if they wish to experience relaxation before a stressful experience or bedtime, they could practise 3-5min cyclic sighing (extended exhales to decrease heart rate, and enhance HRV) whereas if they wish to feel more energised they could perform bhastrika pranayama (bellows breath). The ultimate aim will be that by the end of the course they can design their own personalised breathwork structure.

All breathwork techniques will be guided in the first instance in real-time by trained facilitators regardless of physiological intensity as depicted by breathwork zones (BWZ-1 to 4; sitting, walking, running, sprinting). Week 1: Under the theme of "Foundation and awareness", this would involve an introduction to the course, along with assessing one's current resting respiratory pattern (i.e., in terms of mouth vs. nasal, upper chest vs. abdominal, and the rate at which one breathes using remarkably simple methods like the Hi-Lo test (Shimozawa et al., 2023)). Home practice could involve dedicated times to check in with participants' breathing at rest (noticing throughout the day when scrolling devices; some people might experience 'email apnoea' where they hold their breath unconsciously whilst focusing on screens (Stone, 2008; Gupta, 2023), though this remains to be formally studied). Such reminders could be achieved and delivered through the development of a simple app. The encouragement would be towards nasal breathing (unblocking exercises could be used) and slower, lower 'diaphragmatic' breathing (placing one hand on chest and belly and trying to make the bottom hand rise whilst keeping the top still).

If nose breathing is not possible then pursed lip breathing through the mouth will be encouraged to slow the rate of breath. This matters because slower breathing at rest has been associated with reduced anxiety and other beneficial outcomes in controlled breathing interventions (Nicolò et al., 2020; Czub et al., 2024). Simply being aware of breathing (i.e., mindfulness meditation) may be connected to lower respiratory rates and thus greater wellbeing (Wielgosz et al., 2016; Karunarathne et al., 2023; Kral et al., 2023). Rate along with mechanics are important, with nasal and abdominal or 'diaphragmatically' being preferable to mouth and upper chest. Importantly, even in athletes, dysfunctional breathing patterns were reported to be associated with worse pulmonary function test results (Sikora et al., 2024), hence cardiovascular fitness does not equal superior breathing. It is logical to posit that breathing patterns -i.e., relaxed slower and lower vs. faster and shallower -may plausibly influence long-term health (Fincham et al., In press). Instructors will emphasise not to worry about perfect breathing, very much aligning with Kabat-Zinn's statement (and the title of week 1 in MBSR): "As long as you are breathing, there is more right with you than wrong with you" (Brown University School of Professional Studies, 2023).

Week 2: "Expiratory emphasis" could involve simple practices for slowing down the breath via extending the exhale. Exhale-dominant techniques are designed to activate the parasympathetic nervous system, aid sleep, relaxation and recovery from stress. This could include simple pursed lip breathing (Nield et al., 2007) to start, along with other forms of resistance breathing such as Ujjayi pranayama (ocean breath) (Mason et al., 2013) and humming (Weitzberg and Lundberg, 2002). There is also extended exhale box breathing (4-4-6-2; inhale:hold:exhale:hold) (Lavretsky et al., 2016), 4-7-8 breathing (Vierra et al., 2022) and cyclic sighing. Interestingly, the latter has been shown to reduce resting respiratory rate (Balban et al., 2023). Micropractices could involve a dedicated five-minute practice of whichever of these participants enjoy most when needed (e.g., at bedtime), or can be combined with BWZ-1 (week 1) via noticing breathing throughout the day (again with slower, lower breathing encouraged; the intention at all times being to calm and not stress the system).

Week 3: "Rhythmicity" would involve coherent breathing (also known as resonant-frequency or 0.1 Hz breathing), which perhaps has the largest evidence-base behind it at present (Sevoz-Couche and Laborde, 2022). It involves a simple rhythm of breathing (usually ~5-6 breaths per minute with no pauses) -and is extensively found across traditions throughout the world (Qigong, yoga, mantra and prayer, as well as meditation) (Lehrer et al., 1999; Bernardi et al., 2001; Nestor, 2020; Brown and Gerbarg, 2022). It is proposed to result in coherence/resonance of the breathing cycle with circulatory functions, increasing HRV and leading to improvements in stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms (Sevoz-Couche and Laborde, 2022), though further in-person research is needed (Fincham et al., 2023c). It is extremely important that coherent breathing is guided in the correct manner since it can cause mild hyperventilation without proper instruction (Szulczewski, 2019; Marchant et al., 2025). Additionally, coherent breathing can also be combined with visualisation of moving the breath, Nadi Shodhana pranayama (alternate nostril breathing) (Yadav and Mutha, 2016) and extending the inhale:exhale to a 1:2 ratio. Another balancing tool -and potential micropractice -would be box breathing in a classic 4-4-4-4 or personalised ratio based on the purported 'carbon dioxide tolerance' test (Balban et al., 2023).

Week 4: "Moving" evidently starts to involve movement (similar to MBSR/MBCT and mindful hatha yoga). Movement-informed breathwork (Caddye et al., 2026) would be employed, where muscular contraction is synced with the breathing cycle (contracting specific muscles -usually in a predefined sequence -on inhales and relaxing on exhales). This also revisits week 2 as it involves inhaling nasally and exhaling through pursed lips. Further reasoning behind the inclusion of movement-informed breathwork is that emerging literature is starting to suggest that coordinated muscular contraction augments HRV during coherent breathing (Shaffer et al., 2022; Tatschl and Schwerdtfeger, 2022). Moreover, even slower exhale-dominant resistance breathing may augment HRV further (Tatschl et al., 2026). Movement-informed breathwork combines the best of both worlds (muscular contraction and slow-paced breathing). A micropractice could be any technique most enjoyed from weeks 1-4 (BWZ-1 to 2).

Week 5: "Breath-holding and interoception" would introduce the idea of no breathing at all. This would be where the intensity of the programme may be deemed moderate (but all volitional), with participants asked to prospectively predict how long they can hold their breath in a supine position, then retrospectively guess how long they held it, to examine discrepancies between predicted, perceived and actual duration. This aspect of breath hold timing metacognition has received little attention in the literature. Participants will then be guided in freediving principles through how to relax during breath holds to see how this improves performance (which is never the sole aim). Previous relaxing practices from weeks 2-4 (BWZ-2) can be used to see if this helps them with breath holds during home practice (keeping a log). Beyond performance, breath-holding provides an opportunity to experience the growing urge to breathe (air hunger, a component of dyspnoea) while retaining control over when to end the hold. Breath-holding has previously been used as an interoceptive exposure exercise in Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) for panic disorder (Schmidt and Trakowski, 2004), and repeated exposure to dyspnoea has been suggested as a possible active component of breathing interventions (Meuret et al., 2018).

Week 6: "Faster breathing and holds" would logically follow on from week 5, introducing perhaps one of the most popular breathwork practices in the West coming from the Wim Hof method. It involves hyperventilation followed by breath holds (in this case causing intermittent hypoxia), usually 3-4 rounds, with the aim of triggering short-term stress to improve resilience to it (Blades et al., 2024; Crosswell et al., 2024; Lewis-Healey et al., 2024; Epel et al., 2025). Again, more in-person research is needed on this (Fincham et al., 2024). Because it involves retentions, this is classified as low-dose high ventilation breathwork. The idea would be to increase arousal/sympathetic activity, as opposed to lowering it. This could lower HRV acutely but, through the potential mechanism of hormesis (adaptation after brief, controllable stress (Canello et al., 2026)), it could cause a rebound effect later. In line with the previous week's theme, it may also act as a kind of exposure therapy (again to a controlled stressor; one's own volitional breath that they have complete agency over). Micropractices of bhastrika and kapalabhati (Dhruva et al., 2012) could then be used like 3-minute breath spaces -advised earlier on in the day. Interestingly, cyclic sighing can also be performed quickly (in a two-stage pranayama style) as an invigorating practice.

Week 7: "Exploration" is the culminating session of weeks 1-6 and arguably most psychophysiologically intense, but participants will have been prepared thoroughly for it through BWZs 1-3 (sitting, walking, running) before BWZ-4 (sprinting). In this week, participants will experience conscious connected breathing -an extended period of continuous fast breathing without retentions in between (high-dose high ventilation breathwork). This practice may allow people to experience unconventional or altered states of consciousness, which share some features with psychedelics (Bahi et al., 2024; Erdmann et al., 2026; Fincham et al., 2026; Lueken et al., 2026; Schmitz et al., 2026), but usually (and crucially) on a particular dimension representative of a positive blissful-like state. Again, it is likely HRV will decrease acutely (Kartar et al., 2025) but potential post-session HRV improvements may be associated with catharsis (emotional release) (Canello et al., 2026). Research suggests that just being in this set and setting whilst breathing normally may still elicit altered states (Havenith et al., 2025).

Week 8: "Integration" ('Sitting' to 'Sprinting') will be the conclusion of the course, consolidating learnings and teachings from weeks 1-7, and doing shorter 'refresher' sessions of the core macropractices. These breathwork practices are now the participants' own and can be deployed when appropriate. They can also develop their own routine. Practice-supporting resources will be provided to participants. Home practice will be whatever they feel like or feels best on the day, and they will be encouraged to plan the best times (i.e., in response to certain situations in life, but allowing flexibility) for their macro-and micropractices to increase adherence/engagement.

Retreat Day: This will comprise longer forms of the macropractices, performed in the progression of BWZs 1-4. Discussions will be held on progress and participants can revisit their engagement and adherence with both the core macro-and micropractices. Another refresher could summarise how different breathing practices might be used depending on individual needs, preferences and circumstances, encouraging participants to explore what works for them, avoiding universal prescription of specific techniques for particular situations or desired outcomes. The all-day retreat comes after the full programme so that all macropractices can be experienced deeply. In MBSR the all-day session occurs within the programme after most, if not all, core techniques have been practised.

Safety considerations

Contraindications for BWZ-3 and BWZ-4 practices would be screened for, and the invitation to soften the experience will be offered to all participants regardless (i.e., slow, nasal breathing with short pauses; no competition). Again, some conditions, however, might require exclusion from these or medical clearance (see below). Practices involving breath holds and/or fast breathing will only be performed lying down. No breath-holding or hyperventilation practice should be performed in or near water, while driving or operating machinery, or in any situation where fainting could cause harm -and this will be stated explicitly in the home practice guidance.

It is important to note that every contraindication might not be managed by softening the practice. Some circumstances may acceptably require a modified version of breathing, whereas others may require medical clearance (e.g., in the case of pregnancy, epilepsy, cardiovascular disease, a history of psychosis or mania and panic disorder). Slow breathing is a low-risk intervention but should not be presented as risk-free for everyone. Screening will be carried out before the course by trained facilitators using a standardised health questionnaire, with medical clearance sought where indicated, and anyone excluded from BWZs 3-4 could be offered a modified practice or other appropriate support.

Regarding weeks 5-6 involving breath-holding, participants will be told that air hunger is normal and that they can end retentions at any time, and facilitators will be prepared to support anyone who becomes anxious or panicky. This will be the first introduction to trying to remain calm in the presence of respiratory discomfort, rather than necessarily eliminating it.

Concerning fast breathing practices (weeks 6-8), participants can practise in whichever way they please and feel comfortable doing. Facilitators will be trained to recognise and support participants who find the experience overwhelming (for example by guiding a return to normal breathing and grounding), and, as mentioned above, participants with relevant contraindications will be managed according to a predefined protocol.

Discussion

Stress is abundant in today's society, with global trends indicating rising levels worldwide (Canaletti et al., 2026). Breathwork may offer an alternative and, for some people, a more preferable option to mindfulness for reducing this. However, we are not arguing for breathwork as a replacement but as another toolkit for stress reduction complementary to meditation. This paper offers foundational work which could lead to the development of a manual for breathwork as a whole, not just one technique or school of practices, emphasising both accessibility and scalability.

Though most of the available support for the therapeutic potential of breathwork is still based on anecdotal, uncontrolled and potentially biased reports, more rigorous testing of breathing practice effects has started to transpire. Breathwork is a promising but still emerging intervention that is easy to access, with encouraging early evidence: meta-analyses of RCTs report small-to-medium effect sizes for subjective mental health improvement (Fincham et al., 2023d), and Sudarshan Kriya Yoga (SKY) has been shown to be non-inferior to cognitive processing therapy for Post-traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) (Bayley et al., 2022). Slow-paced breathing is generally safe, whereas fast-paced breathwork carries greater and lesser-known risks and is contraindicated in epilepsy, significant cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease. The dose-response relationship remains poorly defined and the minimum effective dose needed for sustained benefit has not been established (Fincham et al., In press). Outcomes appear to be best when instruction is led by a person, delivered over multiple sessions and followed by long-term practice (Bentley et al., 2023), which is exactly why BBSR may prove efficacious.

Whilst RCTs and mechanistic studies focusing on individual techniques continue to be essential to address specific questions related to the effects of each component of any larger breathwork programme, we argue that it is also important to work collaboratively towards the development of a standardised BBSR framework for the general public. Inspired by MBSR, which piloted a standardised programme and paved the way for mindfulness meditation in the West, here we propose the development of a programme that will combine various core macropractices and smaller dosed micropractices into a structured, safety-screened programme, that should be designed to be as accessible and scalable as possible.

Since MBSR is a standardised, manualised eight-week protocol, now heavily validated by research, a breathwork-based equivalent (BBSR) should match its structural rigour from the outset. The paradox, however, is that the latter emerged for MBSR from practice-based evidence (Gabbay and May, 2010) and Kabat-Zinn along with other names mentioned are pracademics, i.e. researcher-practitioners (Hollweck et al., 2021). The same could happen for breathwork and may be the best way to have the field progress, calibrating hype with evidence, and allowing the data to inform how the field grows and evolves. Currently, the closest breathwork-equivalents to MBSR are SKY (Korkmaz et al., 2024) and the Breath-Body-Mind (BBM) programme (Gerbarg et al., 2015), founded by guru and spiritual leader Sri Sri Ravi Shankar and integrative psychiatrists Drs. Patricia Gerbarg and Richard Brown, respectively. Both are structured, manualised multiday courses, including some of the practices mentioned above (coherent breathing is the core practice for BBM, whereas SKY is a fixed sequence, though in a spiritual context, combining slow and fast practices culminating in cyclical Sudarshan Kriya). To our knowledge, no open, secular programme offers the general public a toolkit graded by psychophysiological demand, from which participants can flexibly choose the practices that suit them most.

Whilst rolling out BBSR, pilot/feasibility studies could be conducted before large scale trials and, if proving successful, later including dismantling studies and N-of-1 RCTs to personalise and adapt it to individuals and specific populations (similar to MBCT for depression, and MBSR for hypertension). First, broader highstress sampling would be beneficial before adapting and making it specific to conditions. Pilot work should include systematic monitoring and reporting of adverse events (for example fainting, panic, dissociation or psychotic symptoms). BBSR also provides a novel research opportunity to collect data not just on the programme as a whole (zooming out) and its effectiveness, but also efficacy surrounding individual practices (zooming in). Outcome measures could be related to specific breathwork practices and provide granularity around which techniques may be more suited to specific cohorts or individuals.

The BBSR programme is structured around a progression of breathing practices, from basic awareness and controlled breathing patterns to movement-based exercises, breath-holding, and more physiologically demanding high ventilation breathwork techniques. The BWZs 1-4 (sitting, walking, running, sprinting) sequence here is intended to support both accessibility and safety, while recognising that different practices engage distinct physiological mechanisms. Through an interoceptive lens, and for the safe adoption by the general public, such a progression may enable participants to learn to understand changes in bodily sensations as normal. This parallels how MBSR could provide an antidote for people wanting to try mindfulness but then end up doing an intense 10-day silent Vipassana (insight) meditation retreat (Hart, 2011), which can be cheap and accessible. This could be viewed as the equivalent of 'sprinting' in a meditative context which many may not be prepared for.

Of course, further challenges exist. While secularisation may be important for enhancing accessibility of breathwork in the Western world, it is important that we do not divorce it from its roots and provide ample education around the traditions and cultures which birthed these techniques (particularly yoga and pranayama). To not do so risks another form of so-called 'McMindfulness'. It is important to find ways to balance the commercialisation, commodification and secularisation of ancient spiritual practices whilst enhancing accessibility. The good thing is: everyone breathes. It is also important to note that, whilst distillation of mindfulness into a standalone wellness practice has helped bring it into the mainstream, removing it from both its community-based settings and broader complementary practices such as breathwork and yoga may limit its impact, given the preparatory role of pranayama in cultivating concentration.

Earlier meta-analytic work emphasised that robust evidence must precede standardisation -that only through rigorous research demonstrating breathwork's efficacy could an evidence-based canon emerge -grounded in standardised and manualised materials (Fincham et al., 2023d). However, examining MBSR's development reveals a different pathway: methodological rigour and standardised protocols do not follow from evidence accumulation; they enable it. This is also similar to the evolution of Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing (EMDR) therapy (Laliotis et al., 2023). MBSR's eight-week manualised structure was not born from prior evidence but created the conditions for systematic, comparable research to follow. Breathwork requires this same framework-first approach. Rather than waiting for the evidence-base to mature before standardising practice, we propose establishing a structured, manualised intervention that can then be rigorously tested, refined and scaled.

MBSR has been great for the progression of mindfulness research in the clinic and beyond from the late 1970s. Building on its revolutionary groundwork, we can use a variety of techniques to progress and optimise BBSR further. Precision psychiatry and personalised approaches may allow for physiological markers to predict or implement specific styles of breathwork for individuals, which could refine the course and its application. For example, lower pre-treatment end-tidal carbon dioxide levels have been shown to predict poorer outcomes following cognitive behavioural therapies (CBTs) for anxiety disorders, even after controlling for baseline symptom severity (Davies and Craske, 2014). Embedding digital interventions, like app-based nudging, for example to facilitate noticing (BWZ-1) in the earliest stage of BBSR, could also prove extremely helpful. Not only that, but the programme could evolve to a point where psychophysiologically-informed combinations of techniques could be run as pre-defined packages (or 'journeys') that have specific combination effects that parallel other modalities, including contrast therapy (hot and cold, e.g., sauna and cold plunge), psychedelic effects (rapid activation followed by gradual decline), progressive overload (controlled stress exposure, acclimation, activation, and so on), deeper exploration (techniques combined with dedicated awareness periods), or emergencies (grounding techniques followed by noticing).

From an operational perspective, MBIs are well-established, although such programmes may now be reaching structural saturation as people who first encountered mindfulness training in educational settings move on to adult health and workplace services. Breathwork could be delivered through the same infrastructure, extending existing wellbeing provision across healthcare, education and workplaces. Small pilots in each setting could help determine whether and how the approach should expand as the evidencebase for each develops. Embedding quality improvement (QI) into these pilots would support ongoing evaluation and refinement, helping teams assess feasibility, acceptability and outcomes in each setting. A streamlined version could be adapted for children and young people, building on breathwork techniques such as box breathing that are already used in social, emotional and mental health (SEMH) provision (Gross and Seleznyov, 2024). Reasonable adjustments may be needed for participants with asthma, a tendency toward panic reactivity, or other relevant health conditions.

In terms of scalability, high-quality practitioner training will be needed. Fortunately, despite the heterogeneity in breathwork schools, many practitioners are skilled across mutual areas like screening, trauma-informed delivery, safety/first aid and group facilitation. And the simplicity of breathwork and its standardisation through BBSR could allow it to scale quickly. Excellent real-time guidance is key in the first instance with all breathwork practices (Fincham et al., In press). Eventually there is a chance of online delivery but, to start, and in today's society, we need more in-person experiences; the communal aspects of a breathwork programme may be important for supporting integration and embedding healing. Delivery costs (two facilitators, venue and the retreat day) will also need to be considered so that fees do not themselves become a barrier to access. Guided facilitation is especially important for breathwork, where self-help guidance may reduce the observed effects (Fincham, 2024). In-person is best for this. Nonetheless, breathwork is already being adapted for conditions like social anxiety (Cavarra et al., 2024).

In conclusion, we have highlighted a notable gap: breathwork lacks a widely adopted, secular and graded framework for the general population, equivalent to what MBSR has provided since 1979. Breathwork requires a practice-based evidence protocol, BBSR, to move past wellness trends and gain clinical credibility. A standardised curriculum and dosage model would inform rigorous research instead of relying on dramatic anecdotal claims. Self-guided sessions or unregulated (breath)workshops could push participants too fast into intense hyperventilation or intense emotional responses without proper support; a structured framework emphasises trained instructors who know how to handle psychophysiological distress. Like MBSR's blend of formal practice and 'informal mindfulness', a structured breathwork model needs to teach micropractices which aid facing everyday stress, rather than just during the weekly class formats. Mindfulness may be viewed as the first key step of breathwork (to control your breathing, you must first become aware of it), and BBSR could likewise provide the key step towards rigorous breathwork research. Just as MBSR may be viewed as the gold standard for meditation, BBSR could be the gold standard for breathwork.
