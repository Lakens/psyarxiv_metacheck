Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Subjective experiences featuring altered self-awareness, temporal perception, or spatial boundaries-termed Non-Ordinary Experiences (NOEs)-occur across clinical neurology, psychiatry, and psychedelic medicine, including in insular ecstatic seizures, meditation, breathwork, hypnosis, and psychedelic therapy.

Objective:

To synthesize neurobiological and clinical literature on NOEs for neurologists by presenting three heuristic frameworks: 1.

A six-dimension phenomenological taxonomy; 2. A tri-level descriptive taxonomy (computational "software", neuromodulatory-autonomic "firmware", and network "hardware"); 3. The Trigger-Dynamic Psyche-4E Appraisal-Outcome Model, which maps how acute neurodynamic shifts resolve into adaptive growth or clinical distress without reducing existential meaning to neural activity.

Narrative synthesis of systematic reviews, coordinate-based meta-analyses, and key theoretical frameworks sourced via PubMed/MEDLINE.

Conflating spirituality, religion, and NOEs causes construct contamination in neuroimaging, compounded by unvalidated single-item measures and non-independent cohorts. An fMRI metaanalysis of meditation (34 studies, N=700) reveals aggregate recruitment of the right rolandic operculum, left insula, supplementary motor area, and hippocampus, arguing against a singular "spiritual center". First-person reports are mapped using a six-dimension schema (experiential content, temporal dynamics, spatial dynamics, volitional dynamics/agency, ownership/embodiment, cognitive stance). Mechanistically, states are organized across three bidirectionally coupled levels: computational inference ("software"), neuromodulatory-autonomic state-setting ("firmware"), and large-scale networks ("hardware"). Five entry routes (meditation, psychedelics, breathwork, hypnosis, focal neurological events) are hypothesized to share shifts in Bayesian precision weighting and network decoupling within the master Trigger-Dynamic Psyche-4E Appraisal-Outcome Model. Crucially, long-term outcomes (integration vs. distress) are not dictated by acute brain states alone, but are mediated by contextual filtering through the 4E Appraisal Prism (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended dimensions).

Neurospirituality bridges network neuroscience, clinical neurology, and person-centered care. Clarifying biological substrates does not resolve existential validity. Clinicians must maintain a strict distinction between biological mechanisms, subjective experiences, and personal meanings (the Epistemological Invariant) to avoid pathologizing adaptive states or romanticizing neurological disease.

1.Introduction: From Neurotheology to Neurospirituality

The scientific investigation of spiritual and transcendent experiences possesses a deep history, evolving from early localizationist hypotheses into modern network neuroscience. Early phrenological attempts, such as Franz Gall's mapping of spiritual organs, sought biological substrates for transcendence but lacked empirical validity. The phenomenological foundation was established by William James, who demonstrated that first-person qualitative reports could be systematically analyzed as empirical psychological data (as reviewed in Harper, 2019). Concurrently, early psychoanalytic paradigms offered contrasting interpretations of the "oceanic feeling": Sigmund Freud, in Civilization and Its Discontents (1930), framed it as a regressive preservation of infantile narcissism, whereas Carl Jung (1969) viewed such transcendent states as transformative encounters with archetypal structures essential to psychological individuation (both cited and discussed in Unterrainer, 2025). These foundational perspectives established an enduring conceptual tension between biological reductionism and spiritual transcendence.

Terminology in this field evolved alongside its empirical methodologies. The term "neurotheology," initially appearing in fiction by Aldous Huxley, was formalized into an academic framework by James Ashbrook in the 1980s. A decisive empirical milestone occurred at the turn of the millennium, when Andrew Newberg and Eugene d'Aquili utilized SPECT imaging to measure regional cerebral blood flow shifts during meditation and prayer (Newberg et al., 2001; Newberg et al., 2003). Over the subsequent two decades, the discipline shifted away from basic localizationism toward large-scale network neuroscience-focusing on dynamic interactions among the Default Mode Network (DMN), Salience Network (SN), and Central Executive Network (CEN)-complemented by 4E cognition (embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended frameworks) (Harper, 2019; McNamara & Grafman, 2024; Jedlicka & Havenith, 2025; Timmermann et al., 2023; Unterrainer, 2025).

To maintain scientific precision, three frequently conflated terms must be explicitly demarcated: • Neuroscience of religion and spirituality: Basic empirical research investigating the neural and cognitive correlates of spiritual phenomena.

• Neurotheology: An interdisciplinary hermeneutic enterprise synthesizing neuroscientific findings with theological and philosophical frameworks.

• Neurospirituality: A translational clinical framework grounded in personcentered healthcare and palliative medicine, applying neuroscientific insights directly to the existential and spiritual dimensions of patient care, particularly within clinical neurology (da Mota Gomes, 2024; da Mota Gomes et al., 2026).

Differentiating these domains prevents category errors, such as deriving direct clinical management protocols from raw neuroimaging correlations.

To integrate these disparate literatures into clinical practice, this review advances three original heuristic contributions, each offered as a conceptual device pending formal validation: 1.

The Six Phenomenological Dimensions of Non-Ordinary Experiences (NOEs): An operationalized descriptive schema extending the neurophenomenological work of Timmermann et al. (2023) to categorize first-person qualitative reports along six dimensions (experiential content, temporal dynamics, spatial dynamics, volitional dynamics/agency, ownership/embodiment, and cognitive stance), without reducing experience to biological mechanisms.

2.

Tri-Level Descriptive Taxonomy of Consciousness States: A descriptive framework contrasting ordinary waking states with non-ordinary states across three mutually constraining, bidirectionally coupled levels: computational inference ("software"), neuromodulatory-autonomic state-setting ("firmware"), and large-scale network substrates ("hardware"). The computational designations serve strictly as a heuristic mnemonic and pragmatic organizing device-not as a literal computer architecture or a dualistic separation of mind and brain.

3. The Master Trigger-Dynamic Psyche-4E Appraisal-Outcome Model: An integrative architecture mapping the complete trajectory from Trigger Initiation (Stage 1), through Biological Substrate Mapping across the tri-level taxonomy (Stage 2), to Contextual Outcome Mediation via the 4E Appraisal Prism (Stage 3), culminating in Differential

Outcomes of adaptive growth, practice-induced adverse events, or primary clinical trajectories (Stage 4).

Central to this overarching architecture is an Epistemological Invariant: mapping the biological mechanism of a transcendent state constrains its physical substrate but does not dictate its existential validity or personal meaning. A detailed operationalization of this model and its six-step clinical reasoning heuristic is provided in Section 5 and Section 5.6.

2. Conceptual Foundations

2.1.Spirituality, Religion, and NOEs

Four terminological commitments establish the conceptual foundation for this review, beginning with a deliberate choice of nomenclature. This review prefers neurospirituality over the neuroscience of religion because NOEs are not inherently religious: an experience of ego dissolution, oceanic boundlessness, or ecstatic certainty may be interpreted religiously, spiritually, secularly, or not interpreted at all. The term is religion-neutral by design-neutral with respect to religion, not opposed to it-able to accommodate religious framing without requiring it and secular framing without excluding the religious. That neutrality is what makes the vocabulary usable across theistic, non-theistic, and culturally diverse clinical settings.

The remaining commitments concern what the words mean. Spirituality, as used here, is a meaning-making orientation involving perceived connection to something beyond the individual self-transcendent (the divine, the sacred), natural, interpersonal, or a blend of these. For a secular patient, "beyond self" may mean community, nature, or future generations rather than God; the formulation holds either way. Religion refers to the organized beliefs, rituals, doctrines, and communities through which many people express their spirituality. The two overlap heavily-spiritual experiences happen inside religious traditions, and religious life involves far more than doctrine-but they are not the same thing, and the habit of collapsing them into a single "S/R" measure, pervasive in the neuroimaging literature, makes it impossible to know which dimension is actually driving which neural outcome (Rosmarin et al., 2022; Maraldi et al., 2024).

Two further distinctions are maintained throughout, because the clinical recommendations of this review depend on them. Spirituality is treated as a disposition-for the purpose of choosing instruments, a relatively enduring orientation-whereas spiritual well-being is a state: the felt sense of alignment with whatever a person holds as personally, communally, or transcendentally significant. The former is the broader construct; the latter is what the clinician actually assesses and what outcomes research measures. And spiritual meaning (connection to something perceived as sacred or beyond the self) is distinguished from existential meaning (purpose, dignity, and value drawn from secular sourcesrelationships, work, legacy). Existential suffering can occur without any spirituality at all, which is why the clinician should assess existential concerns regardless of the patient's spiritual identification.

These definitions are pragmatic, adopted because they can be operationalized in clinical neuroscience-not because they claim cross-cultural validity. They reflect a largely Western, Christian-inflected understanding of spirituality; other traditions conceptualize the territory differently, as interconnectedness with ancestors in Ubuntu philosophy, harmony with cosmic order in Indigenous frameworks, or alignment with natural forces in Taoist and Buddhist thought. The definitions earn their keep by making measurement possible, and spiritual assessment should always proceed with cultural humility.

NOEs are defined phenomenologically rather than etiologically: subjective experiences involving identifiable alterations in the content, organization, or sense of self of consciousness, relative to a person's ordinary waking state. The category gathers mystical, transcendent, oceanic, ecstatic, and psychedelic states, together with selected experiences arising from hypnosis, meditation, breathwork, and neurological conditions. It is a descriptive umbrella, not a claim about mechanism. Nothing about inclusion implies a shared cause, an equivalence of phenomenology, or a comparable fate.

Three qualifications carry through everything that follows. NOEs are not inherently pathological, though some occur alongside psychiatric or neurological illness. They are not synonymous with spiritual or religious experience, since unusual experiences arrive without spiritual interpretation all the time. And they are not homogeneous: their phenomenology, significance, and consequences differ profoundly across contexts (Timmermann et al., 2023; Maraldi et al., 2024).

For analytical purposes, it helps to distinguish four levels that are related but never interchangeable: enduring dispositions and orientations toward meaning and the sacred; the practices and contexts in which these find expression, from prayer to psychoactive substances; the particular experiential episodes themselves; and the interpretations and consequences that follow. These levels interact over time-prior beliefs shape how an experience is read, and experiences in turn reshape beliefs-but neither direction of influence should be assumed in advance. A deeply spiritual person may never report an NOE; a diehard materialist may have one and be shaken by it. The distinction is conceptual separability, not a demonstration of statistical independence.

2.2 Six Phenomenological Dimensions

To compare non-ordinary experiences across diverse contexts without flattening their unique features, the six phenomenological dimensions taxonomy synthetically extends Timmermann et al. (2023) across six analytical axes: experiential content (sensory, imagistic, and affective qualities), temporal dynamics (felt duration, flow, and continuity), spatial dynamics (distance, extension, and boundaries), volitional dynamics or agency (authorship and control over thoughts and actions), ownership, embodiment, and self-experience (running from ordinary bodily ownership to radical self-dissolution), and cognitive stance (the fused, detached, or meta-aware relation to ongoing thoughts). Offered as a shared descriptive vocabulary rather than a set of established orthogonal variables, this framework systematically specifies phenomenological shifts without assuming fixed valence or structural conflation.

The instrument that comes closest to this comparative ambition-though it realizes it only partially-is the Altered States of Consciousness (ASC) rating-scale family, whose etiology-independent design and broad experiential coverage make it the most comprehensive validated instrument currently available. Its most recent revalidation, the 3D-ASCr (Stocker et al., 2026), integrates eleven lower-order subscales (42 items) into three coherent higher-order dimensions: Positive Effects (PosE), Distressing Effects (DisE), and Perceptual Effects (PerE). This hierarchical structure organizes experience by valence and content-positively valenced and meaningful experiences, distressing states, and perceptual phenomena-and is validated for classic serotonergic psychedelics across a wide dose range, with its dimensions closely approximating Dittrich's original OB, AED, and VR.

A dimension-by-dimension comparison, however, reveals that even this instrument covers only part of the sixfold space. It provides strong coverage of experiential content and substantial coverage of ownership, embodiment, and self-experience, but captures volitional dynamics only in their negative pole (impaired control), leaving the graded exercise of agency unmeasured; temporal and spatial dynamics fare worst, surviving only as indirect traces; and no subscale operationalizes cognitive stance as the relation to ongoing thought. This asymmetry generalizes to the other major multidimensional instruments-the PES48 and the Hallucinogen Rating Scale-whose shared tripartite structure likewise organizes experience by valence and content rather than by structure, and to narrower single-construct tools (EDI, MEQ30, PCI), each of which captures only one pole or one fused facet of the structural axes.

The comparison thus yields a precise division of labor. The ASC family offers what the sixfold schema deliberately does not: quantified, psychometrically validated, outcomelinked measurement. Conversely, the six-dimension taxonomy offers what no current instrument provides: systematic, valence-free coverage of the structural axes-graded agency, felt time and space, and cognitive stance-that cut across the valence-based domains of the validated scales. Neither substitutes for the other: validated instruments serve as mandatory anchors for quantification, while the sixfold schema functions as the common descriptive vocabulary into which their subscales can be translated-the precondition for linking phenomenological report and neural measurement on a single, explicit map. To demonstrate the qualitative utility of this schema, Box 1 applies these parameters to three contrasting historical and literary reports. A note for readers: Box 1 only illustrates how the six dimensions can be used to describe an experience. The schema was built partly to fit classical accounts, so the box is not evidence for the schema or for any brain mechanism.

Box 1. Qualitative Benchmarking: Applying the Six-Dimension Schema to Historical and Literary Texts

To illustrate how the six-dimension schema operationalizes first-person reports without reducing them to biological mechanisms, three contrasting cases are benchmarked below. These accounts serve strictly as qualitative demonstrations of descriptive classification-not as empirical biological proof. The three cases were deliberately selected for contrast across induction vectors; the selection was not systematic and confers no evidential weight on the schema itself.

Case A: Marguerite Porete (Âme Anéantie) -Primary and secondary sources: Porete, The Mirror of Simple Souls (c. 1300); Mieth (2025). Marguerite Porete, a 14th-century Beguine mystic executed for heresy in 1310, described in The Mirror of Simple Souls the soul's total annihilation in divine love (âme anéantie)-a state of loving "without why" (sans pourquoi) that surrenders individual self-will. Mapped onto the framework: • Ownership and Embodiment: Radical dissolution of personal self-identity and self-will.

• Volitional Dynamics: Deliberate surrender of agency, rather than involuntary loss of control.

• Cognitive Stance: Complete detachment from egoic self-narratives (parallel to Meister Eckhart's Abgeschiedenheit; Mieth, 2025).

• Methodological Caution: Finding these dimensions in Porete provides no evidence for neural mechanisms such as DMN decoupling, because the schema was constructed partly to accommodate classical contemplative accounts.

Case B: Fyodor Dostoevsky (Ecstatic Aura) -Primary source: Dostoevsky, The Idiot (1868); secondary medical/theoretical sources: Gschwind and Picard (2016); Picard and Friston (2014). The ecstatic aura is attested in two distinct kinds of sources, which should be kept apart. The first is fiction: in The Idiot, Prince Myshkin recounts moments of sublime harmony and beauty culminating in an "ecstatic, prayerful merging with the highest synthesis of life." This is a fictional character's account, not direct testimony from Dostoevsky. The second is secondary biographical and medical literature (e.g., Gschwind & Picard, 2016), which attributes similar experiences to Dostoevsky's own seizures. Because that attribution is retrospective, the epilepsy diagnosis rests on historical reconstruction rather than clinical examination, and the literature variously describes the condition as temporal lobe epilepsy and as insular or ecstatic epilepsy. Mapped onto the schema: • Experiential Content: Intense, overwhelming feelings of bliss, serenity, and inner wholeness.

• Cognitive Stance: Absolute subjective certainty and clarity without rational doubt.

• Temporal Dynamics: Subjective time dilation and a collapse of felt duration into expanded present-moment awareness.

• Neurobiological Context: Ecstatic seizures are proposed to originate in a network centered on the anterior insular cortex. On the zero-error hypothesis of Picard and Friston (2014), hyper-synchronous epileptic discharge may disrupt the insular comparator that evaluates incoming sensory signals against predictions, transiently suppressing prediction-error generation and locking the brain into an artificial, zero-error "stable state" experienced as certainty and bliss (Picard & Friston, 2014; Gschwind & Picard, 2016). This hypothesis has not been experimentally confirmed in clinical cohorts (see Section 3).

Case C: Romain Rolland (The Spontaneous "Oceanic Feeling") -Primary source: Rolland (1927 letter to Freud, as cited in Maharaj, 2017). In his 1927 correspondence with Sigmund Freud, the French writer Romain Rolland described the "oceanic feeling" as a simple, direct feeling of the "eternal"-an unmediated sensation of limitless, boundless oneness with the whole universe (as analyzed in Maharaj, 2017). Mapped onto the framework: • Spatial Dynamics: Vast, unconstrained spatial expansion without perceptible limits.

• Temporal Dynamics: Time experienced as timelessness, as "the eternal." • Ownership and Embodiment: Dissolution of rigid egoic boundaries into total cosmic unity.

• Cognitive Stance: A direct intuitive stance wholly independent of formal religious dogma, credos, church organizations, or sacred texts.

• Experiential Content and Affective Tone: A spontaneous state experienced as a constant source of vital renewal and energy.

• Methodological Caution: Rolland's report illustrates secular or non-dogmatic selftranscendence and shows how ego boundaries loosen in a specific first-person account. A single anecdotal report, however, cannot demonstrate that ego boundaries loosen without clinical distress as a general rule.

Takeaway and Comparative Summary: Comparing contemplative surrender (Porete), focal epileptic discharges (Dostoevsky), and spontaneous mystical report (Rolland) shows that identical phenomenological signatures-bliss, time dilation, boundary dissolution-can emerge across contexts while their induction vectors and clinical trajectories remain fundamentally distinct.

Similar Phenomenology Does Not Imply Identical Trajectories

Ego dissolution occurs in monasteries, in psychedelic sessions, and on psychiatric wards. The descriptions can be strikingly similar; the fates are not. Two people may report a diminished sense of self while differing in bodily awareness, agency, emotional tone, and functional capacity, and the same label may prescribe entirely different courses. Phenomenological comparison is therefore necessary, but never sufficient for judging causal or clinical equivalence, and outcomes cannot be sorted into a tidy binary of growth versus pathology-a single experience can carry both benefit and harm, and its meaning may be renegotiated for years afterward (Maraldi et al., 2024). What tends to matter for the trajectory is not the content of the experience but its context: pre-existing psychological resources, social support, expectations, interpretive frameworks, and how the experience is ultimately woven into a life. These are candidate moderators and mediators of outcome-variables that do not alter the acute neurodynamic state itself, but shape what that state comes to mean and what it leaves behind. The 4E Prism therefore operates at two analytically distinct points: it shapes the acute state through set and setting (as the empirical literature on contextual modulation of psychedelic and meditative states documents), and it filters the aftermath through appraisal and integration. Until they are measured directly-through baseline heart rate variability, interoceptive accuracy tasks, set-and-setting inventories, and semantic coping frameworks-the field will continue to confuse similar phenomenology with identical trajectories, and will continue to mistake the content of an experience for its cause (Jedlicka & Havenith, 2025; Maraldi et al., 2024; Timmermann et al., 2023; Unterrainer, 2025).

2.3.Epistemological Demarcation

Neural mechanism, phenomenal experience, and existential meaning are distinct levels of analysis. Finding the neural correlate of an experience constrains its physical substrate; it does not tell us what the experience was like for the person, and it settles nothing about its significance. The intensity of an experience, conversely, is not evidence for any particular mechanism, let alone for a metaphysical conclusion. This review rejects both directions of overreach with equal force: neurobiological reductionism, in which a brain description is treated as settling every existential question, and its mirror image, in which the felt meaning of an experience is taken as evidence about biology or metaphysics (Jedlicka & Havenith, 2025). The distinction does not make the levels causally independent-brain states contribute to the conditions of experience, experiences alter beliefs, beliefs alter attention and memory-but it does mean that each level must be investigated with methods suited to it. One sentence, repeated at the close of this review, captures the commitment: causal mechanism is not phenomenological experience, and phenomenological experience is not spiritual meaning.

This invariant is not only a philosophical commitment; it is a clinical instruction. At the bedside, it requires the clinician to assess three things separately:

•

The mechanism: what is happening in the brain and body (e.g., anterior insular discharge in ecstatic epilepsy).

• The experience: what the patient reports phenomenologically (e.g., feelings of absolute unity and bliss).

• The meaning: what the patient takes the experience to signify existentially or spiritually.

Collapsing any two of these produces a characteristic error-reducing a meaningful experience to discharge activity, or inflating a neural correlate into a metaphysical proof. As demonstrated in Dostoevsky's case (Box 1), a neurological patient's report of transcendent bliss is neither reducible to discharge pathology nor evidence of metaphysical contact. It is a phenomenological event with a constraining biological substrate-and the patient requires clinical neurological care for epilepsy regardless of how the aura is personally interpreted. Mechanism, experience, and meaning hold their distinct places even at the moment they seem most entangled.

2.4.Oceanic States: Historical, Philosophical, and Existential-Neuroscience Frames

"Oceanic states"-defined by ego dissolution, felt unity, and timelessness-exist at the intersection of psychopathology, artistic expression, and self-transcendence. The concept originated in a 1927 letter from Romain Rolland to Sigmund Freud following the publication of Freud's The Future of an Illusion (as discussed in Maharaj, 2017). Rolland described a spontaneous "oceanic" feeling of limitless oneness with the universe, existing independently of dogmas or church structures. Citing Sri Ramakrishna and Swami Vivekananda, Rolland maintained that oceanic states foster dynamic action and intellectual vigor rather than psychological regression. Conversely, Freud dismissed the experience in Civilization and Its Discontents (1930) as a regressive illusion rooted in infantile narcissism and pre-Oedipal helplessness (Freud, 1930 , as cited in Unterrainer, 2025).

Re-examining Rolland's biographies Vie de Ramakrishna (1929) and Vie de Vivekananda (1930), Ayon Maharaj (2017) demonstrates that Rolland's primary intent was not to solicit a psychoanalytic validation of mysticism, but to challenge psychoanalytic reductionism directly. Rolland leveled three core critiques against psychoanalysis: first, an extroverted bias that pathologizes mystic introversion, thereby losing what Plotinus termed the "function of the real"; second, the misapplication of clinical regression theories (e.g., Théodule Ribot) to mystics, conflating infantile pre-discursive intuition with mystical post-discursive intuition (Édouard Le Roy, as cited in Maharaj, 2017 and Unterrainer, 2025)-a conflation later formalized by Ken Wilber as the "pre/trans fallacy"; and third, a superficial model of the unconscious that reduces transcendent states to sexual repression or Oedipal dynamics while ignoring the underlying "Ocean of Being" (l'Être océanique). Maharaj (2017) clarifies that scholars like William Parsons misread Rolland's phrase psychophysiologie mystique ("mystical psychophysiology") as an endorsement of "mystical psychoanalysis." In truth, Rolland rejected psychoanalysis in favor of a Yogic "new science of the mind" (une nouvelle science de l'esprit). Drawing on Patanjali's Rāja-Yoga (particularly pratyāhāra or detached observation) and Vedāntic Jñāna-Yoga-later modernized by Vivekananda and Sri Aurobindo-Rolland framed mystic introversion as an empirical method grounded in the Plotinian identity of seer and seen. This critique presaged major twentieth-century transpersonal frameworks: Sri Aurobindo criticized psychoanalysis for attempting to explain the "superconscient" lotus purely through subconscious "mud"; Swami Akhilananda (1948, as cited in Unterrainer, 2025) advocated Yogic concentration over pathologizing clinical generalizations; and Ken Wilber exposed Freud's reduction of transrational states to prepersonal adualism as a classic pre/trans fallacy.

Beyond Freudian reductionism, Carl Jung (1969, as cited in Unterrainer, 2025) reinterpreted ego dissolution as a transformative encounter with the collective unconscious mediated by the numinosum (mysterium tremendum et fascinans). In art theory, Anton Ehrenzweig (1967, as cited in Unterrainer, 2025) described oceanic feeling as a "de-differentiated perception" enabling creative "bisociation" (Arthur Koestler), a liminal space balancing visionary insight against psychic breakdown, as illustrated by Vincent van Gogh, Louis Wain, and Antonin Artaud. Existential and Eastern philosophies ground these states further: ego dissolution manifests as a confrontation with existential nothingness (Jean-Paul Sartre, 1956, as discussed in Unterrainer, 2025), an authentic disclosure of Being through a rupture in Dasein (Martin Heidegger, 1962, as discussed in Unterrainer, 2025), or a return to primordial embodied unity (Maurice Merleau-Ponty, 2002, as discussed in Unterrainer, 2025), echoing Buddhist anattā and contemporary self-model theory (Thomas Metzinger, 2003 , as discussed in Unterrainer, 2025).

From an existential-neuroscience perspective, Unterrainer (2025) frames oceanic states as a dynamic reorganization of self-processing networks rather than brain dysfunction. Cortically, ego dissolution correlates with functional decoupling within the DMN (mPFC, PCC, precuneus, angular gyrus), conceptualized as belief relaxation under the REBUS (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019) and ALBUS (Safron et al., 2025) Unterrainer, 2025). While therapeutic in psychedelic settings, unintegrated oceanic experiences carry risks of spiritual emergency (Grof & Grof, 1989, as cited in Unterrainer, 2025) or psychotic collapse.

3.Mechanisms: Three Levels of Description

The neurobiological literature on NOEs is scattered across imaging, pharmacology, electrophysiology, and computation. To hold it together, this review uses a three-level descriptive heuristic, coupled bidirectionally with phenomenal consciousness. The gloss is an analogy, a pragmatic organizing device, not a literal architecture, and emphatically not a veiled dualism of mind and brain. The three levels are levels of description, not levels of causation: they name three vocabularies in which the same system can be described, not three substances or three independent causal strata. The bidirectional coupling between them is what prevents the metaphor from hardening into a hierarchy: repeated engagement may induce plastic changes in neural tissue, which alter neuromodulatory dynamics; the computational description then changes accordingly. This is why psychosocial contextappraisal, setting, meaning-making-can have biological consequences rather than merely interpretive ones.

The first level, the network substrate, is the physical infrastructure: the triple-network architecture of DMN, SN, and CEN, within which the self is best conceived not as a thing but as an emergent, ongoing process of network interaction (Menon, 2011). Thalamic gating, insular hubs, and subcortical structures provide entry points that constrain how the cortical networks configure themselves (Picard & Friston, 2014; Dost et al., 2025; Unterrainer, 2025). McNamara and Grafman (2024) extend this picture to religious experience specifically, proposing that such experiences involve repeated cycles through salience, default mode, and frontoparietal evaluation, with the salience network acting as the switch-a dynamic picture compatible with everything argued here.

The second level, neuromodulatory and autonomic state-setting, comprises the systems that tune the network substrate. Brainstem arousal networks and vagal afferents set baseline readiness and interoceptive tone; vagal tone, indexed by heart rate variability, tracks emotion regulation capacity and is hypothesized-though not established-to moderate the valence of non-ordinary states: a body that feels safe may be more open to surrender, a body on alert more prone to panic. A distinction must be maintained here, because the literature routinely conflates two dissociable variables. Resting vagal tone-measured at baseline, before any induction-is a trait variable: it indexes pre-existing autonomic flexibility and belongs analytically to the appraisal stage, where it is hypothesized to predict how an experience will be met and integrated. Phasic HRV change during the experience itself is a state variable: it is a state-setting signature of the acute neurodynamic perturbation, and it may transiently decrease under sympathetic surge or vagal withdrawal. However, because HRV is often reported to increase during slow-paced breathing and certain meditation practices, reduced HRV cannot be listed as a general feature of non-ordinary states. The two are not interchangeable, and treating them as a single "HRV" measure obscures precisely the dissociation the model needs. This is not a measurement artifact: systematic reviews of trait mindfulness report consistent trait-level neural correlates-amygdala reactivity, insular thickness, DMN connectivity-alongside inconsistent state-level EEG and between-network findings (Treves et al., 2024; Calderone et al., 2024). The divergence between trait and state is therefore a feature of the underlying physiology, not a failure of the instruments. Serotonergic 5-HT2A activation raises cortical signal entropy, while dopaminergic signalling determines which of the newly available states get flagged as salient and significant-an interaction that may explain why psychedelic experiences are at once so meaningful and so different from person to person (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). REM-sleep-like mechanics-PGO waves and cholinergic surges promoting associative imagery-remain a speculative candidate mechanism for the felt presences reported across cultures (McNamara & Grafman, 2024). And the periaqueductal gray, classically studied for pain and defense, has emerged from lesion-network mapping as a node of unexpected breadth, implicated in carerelated affect, awe, and trait spirituality: a structure operating at the most basic embodied level, where the boundary between self and world, safety and surrender, can be radically redrawn (Unterrainer, 2025). This characterization of the PAG, including its role as a node between embodiment and transcendence, is an interpretive extension resting on lesionnetwork mapping and a single review; it is a hypothesis, not an established finding.

At the level of computational inference, the brain functions as a predictive processing network, generating top-down expectations that interpret incoming bottom-up sensory signals, with neuromodulatory gain control adjusting the balance between expectation and evidence. Within this framework, the REBUS model (Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics) proposes that 5-HT2A receptor activation weakens high-level cognitive expectations, flattening cortical hierarchies and allowing unconstrained sensory information to flood awareness. Rather than a proven mechanism, REBUS remains a testable hypothesis with domain-specific and dose-dependent empirical support. Addressing these limitations, the ALBUS model (Altered Beliefs Under Psychedelics) suggests that high-level expectations are not solely relaxed under psychedelics; they can also be amplified and entrenched through a complementary process termed SEBUS (Strengthened Beliefs Under Psychedelics). Crucially, ALBUS positions context-including dose, mindset, and clinical environmentas a primary variable determining whether cognitive expectations loosen or harden. While ALBUS and SEBUS were originally formulated for psychedelic compounds, extending this framework to non-pharmacological interventions such as meditation, hypnosis, or breathwork represents a broader theoretical generalization.

From a clinical and neuroscientific standpoint, REBUS, ALBUS/SEBUS, and related computational accounts-such as Picard and Friston's zero-error hypothesis-must be evaluated as heuristic models rather than confirmed pathophysiological mechanisms. To avoid circular reasoning where any observed outcome fits the framework, these models require clear, falsifiable parameters. Specifically, directionality must be pre-specified based on baseline clinical variables: for instance, high affective threat and rigid baseline priors should explicitly predict SEBUS-type belief strengthening, whereas supportive environments and high interoceptive accuracy predict REBUS-type relaxation. Ultimately, these computational theories require the same empirical rigor applied to clinical neuroimaging, including construct validation, independent dataset replication, blinding, and systematic auditing before they can be accepted as established neurobiological facts.

Each level invites its own methods-connectivity analysis for the network substrate, pharmacology and autonomic measurement for the state-setting level, computational modeling for the inference level-and the coupling between them runs both ways. Repeated engagement may induce plastic changes in neural tissue, which alter neuromodulatory dynamics; the computational description then changes accordingly: a recursive loop that is a plausible candidate mechanism for the durable effects of contemplative practice and psychedelic therapy.

4.Empirical Evidence

4. Empirical Evidence

4.1.What Meditation Imaging Actually Shows

The quantitative benchmark in contemplative neuroimaging derives from Baten et al. (2026), whose whole-brain coordinate-based fMRI meta-analysis synthesized 34 unique studies (N=700) across four practice families: focused attention, open monitoring, mantra recitation, and loving-kindness. Across this aggregate dataset, meditation consistently recruits a core neural substrate comprising the right Rolandic operculum, left insula, right superior temporal gyrus, left supplementary motor area, and right hippocampus/parahippocampal gyrus. Functional decoding links this substrate to interoceptive processing, self-monitoring, and cognitive reappraisal alongside reduced visual-perceptual loading, reflecting a systematic neural shift away from external sensory processing toward internal awareness.

Beyond this shared core, distinct practices exhibit tailored functional signatures. Focused attention hyperactivates the left insula, bilateral supplementary motor area, right hippocampus, and right superior frontal gyrus while hypoactivating the right middle cingulate cortex. Open monitoring recruits the left Rolandic operculum/insula, left middle temporal gyrus, left inferior frontal gyrus, and left superior frontal gyrus, accompanied by left thalamic hypoactivation. Mantra recitation selectively engages the right putamen and insula, whereas loving-kindness recruits prefrontal networks, the anterior cingulate cortex, and the cerebellum. This functional divergence provides empirical evidence against naive neurolocalizationism or a singular "spiritual center." However, applying a symmetrical skeptical standard requires acknowledging that this foundational meta-analysis carries its own methodological constraints-namely, reliance on coordinate-based spatial estimates, task heterogeneity across primary paradigms, and modest sample sizes within specific practice sub-cells-which compound broader field-wide limitations such as varying practitioner expertise, heterogeneous control conditions, and unassessed publication bias (Calderone et al., 2024).

These empirical data further qualify network-level generalizations regarding DMN dynamics. Although Baten et al. (2026) demonstrate consistent engagement of insular, opercular, motor, and hippocampal nodes, their findings do not establish global DMN decoupling as a universal neural signature of meditation. Instead, involvement of DMNadjacent structures reflects dynamic attentional state fluctuations intrinsic to meditationparticularly during focused attention-as well as subtle subsystem variations between practice styles. Broader claims regarding meditation-induced DMN decoupling must therefore be interpreted within these explicit functional boundaries.

4.2.The S/R Neuroimaging Literature: An Audit

If the meditation literature is young and still methodologically limited (Section 4.1), the spirituality-and-religion imaging literature is methodologically immature and subject to high risk of bias. Rosmarin et al. (2022) systematically reviewed 18 neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies of S/R, mental health, and neurobiology, and what they found should unsettle anyone citing this literature as established. The clinical evidence concentrated heavily on depression-and most of the depression studies turned out to be sub-studies of a single multigenerational family cohort (Weissman et al., 2005). What looks like a broad evidence base is, on inspection, largely one dataset counted several times, from a sample selected for depression risk that may not represent anyone else. The celebrated association between personal religiosity and cortical thickness reported by Miller et al. (2014)-which demonstrated greater regional thickness in parietal and occipital cortices among adults at high familial risk for major depression (N=103; 67 high-risk, 36 low-risk)-rests on a single multi-generational lineage (the Columbia/Weissman cohort). Because key neuroimaging and clinical reports in this line of literature draw from this same underlying participant pool, their overlapping sample base limits their status as fully autonomous corroborations, plausibly weakening the overall evidentiary base. Crucially, however, dataset non-independence does not demonstrate that Miller et al.'s primary neuroanatomical finding is an artifact. Rather, it shows that the finding has not yet been independently replicated in an autonomous cohort. The distinction matters: an unreplicated finding may well be valid, and the appropriate scientific response is prospective replication in independent populations, not summary dismissal.

The measurement situation is no better, and the numbers deserve to be stated plainly. Of the eighteen studies reviewed by Rosmarin et al. (2022), only three used psychometrically validated, multidimensional instruments-the Daily Spiritual Experiences Scale, the TCI-R Self-Transcendence subscale, or the DUREL. Eight relied on unvalidated single-item selfreports assessing general "importance of religion," five measured external religious attendance alone, and the remaining two relied on unvalidated, study-specific questionnaires or experimental prayer paradigms. The consequence is that the regressor labeled "religiosity" or "spirituality" in most of this literature is not a construct but a placeholder-and there is no way to know which dimension of the experience, if any, the neural signal tracks. The S/R imaging literature is therefore not merely young; it is structurally underdetermined by its own instruments.

4.3.Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy in Life-Threatening Illness

Studies of psychedelic-assisted therapy in patients facing life-threatening illness offer a preliminary window into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying altered states of consciousness. A systematic review by Ferreira and Reis-Pina (2025) synthesizing six clinical trials (𝑁 = 140 , predominantly oncology patients) reported consistent improvements in spiritual well-being and mystical-type experiences following psilocybin and MDMA-assisted interventions. Mechanistically, these subjective changes mirror neuroimaging patterns characterized by reduced within-network connectivity, heightened global inter-network communication, and altered thalamic sensory filtering-a profile compatible with cortical entropy and network decoupling models. Nevertheless, substantial methodological constraints, including small sample cohorts, high overall risk of bias, and functional unblinding driven by prominent psychoactive effects, mean these data serve as hypothesisgenerating insights for human neuroscience rather than established clinical evidence.

Translating altered-state practices into safe clinical practice requires explicit risk management and triage protocols. Non-pharmacological interventions like breathwork, while mechanistically relevant, induce hyperventilation-mediated hypocapnia that carries genuine risks for patients with cardiac arrhythmias, pregnancy, severe panic disorder, or a lowered seizure threshold. Additionally, when managing practice-induced or drug-associated adverse events, clinicians require structured routing criteria: persistent depersonalization, hallucinogen persisting perception disorder, or severe psychological crisis warrant formal psychiatric referral, whereas newly emergent focal neurological signs, paroxysmal spells, or unprovoked seizures require an immediate standard epilepsy and neurological workup.

4.4.A Computational Working Hypothesis: Precision-Weighting Convergence Across Induction Vectors

At the level of computational inference, the brain operates as a hierarchical Bayesian prediction engine. It continuously generates top-down expectations (priors) to interpret incoming sensory and interoceptive data, updating these expectations when prediction errors occur. Within this framework, "precision weighting" acts as a neuromodulatory gain-control mechanism that adjusts the relative weight assigned to top-down predictions versus incoming sensory evidence. In non-ordinary experiences (NOEs), this balance shifts: certain states attenuate the influence of top-down expectations, allowing unconstrained sensory and affective signals to dominate awareness, whereas other contexts reinforce top-down predictions.

Non-ordinary states can be elicited through five distinct induction vectors, each operating via a unique primary entry point: 1.

Hypnosis: Operates via sociocognitive suggestion, attenuating voluntary executive control and increasing absorption through top-down frontoparietal modulation.

2. Meditation: Represents a neuroplastic, training-dependent trajectory where sustained attentional practice gradually alters interoceptive awareness and self-monitoring.

3. Classical Psychedelics: Act pharmacologically via 5-HT 2A receptor agonism on deep-layer cortical pyramidal neurons, elevating cortical signal entropy and relaxing highlevel narrative priors (the REBUS model).

4.

Voluntary Hyperventilation (Breathwork): Utilizes respiratory mechanicsspecifically transient hypocapnia and reduced arterial CO 2 saturation-to alter autonomic state and induce mystical-type phenomenology. From a clinical safety perspective, hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia carries specific risks (e.g., lower seizure thresholds, cardiac arrhythmias, or panic decompensation) that require pre-screening.

5. Focal Neurological Events: Arise from pathological hyper-synchronous epileptic discharges (such as anterior insular ecstatic seizures) or structural lesions. These organic events must not be assumed to share identical mechanisms with voluntary or pharmacological practices.

A central working hypothesis proposes that despite these disparate entry points, all five vectors may converge on a shared neurodynamic state defined by altered precision weighting and reduced coupling across large-scale intrinsic networks. Subjectively, this manifests as altered sense of agency, shifting spatiotemporal boundaries, and ego dissolution. However, this convergence remains an inductive heuristic rather than an established biological reality. Direct empirical measures of precision weighting currently do not exist for any of these five routes. Furthermore, existing neuroimaging proxies reveal clear contradictions: meta-analyses in meditation do not demonstrate universal default mode network (DMN) decoupling, nor does hypnosis induce global DMN disintegration.

Addressing the limitations of simple prior-relaxation models, the ALBUS (Altered Beliefs Under Psychedelics) framework demonstrates that top-down expectations are not exclusively relaxed during altered states; they can also be amplified and entrenched through a process termed SEBUS (Strengthened Beliefs Under Psychedelics). Rather than viewing belief strengthening as an ad-hoc exception, REBUS (relaxation) and SEBUS (strengthening) represent competing parallel computational pathways. Which pathway dominates is governed downstream by contextual factors: environments characterized by high affective threat, rigid baseline priors, or unsupportive settings trigger reactive SEBUS-type belief hardening to minimize overwhelming prediction error, whereas supportive settings facilitate REBUS-type relaxation. Pre-specifying these contextual predictors renders the framework empirically falsifiable.

Possible Unfoldings: Testing the Hypothesis Directly

Testing this convergence hypothesis directly requires prospective, within-subject comparative studies that utilize model-derived measures of precision weighting across different induction vectors. The hypothesis would be disconfirmed if different vectors produce conflicting shifts in precision weighting that cannot be accounted for by prespecified contextual variables. Focal neurological events, being non-experimental by nature, serve as observational clinical comparators rather than experimental conditions.

5.The Integrative Model: From Triggers to Outcomes

5. The Integrative Model: From Triggers to Outcomes

5.1. Stages

To synthesize mechanistic, phenomenological, and clinical data without theoretical overload, the trajectory of non-ordinary states can be organized into a four-stage master architecture designated as the Trigger-Dynamic Psyche-4E Appraisal-Outcome Model.

Stage 1 encompasses the primary perturbations that disrupt baseline neurodynamics. These include voluntary contemplative practices such as meditation, ritual, hypnosis, and hyperventilation breathwork; pharmacological agents, principally classical psychedelics; acute boundary events including psychological trauma, near-death experiences, and severe sleep deprivation; and focal organic events such as ecstatic epilepsy or structural brain lesions.

Stage 2 models the acute biological reconfiguration across three mutually constraining operational levels. At the substrate level ("hardware"), the state is characterized by large-scale network reconfigurations, including variable Default Mode Network (DMN) decoupling, altered triple-network interactions among the DMN, SN, and CEN, reduced thalamic sensory gating, and periaqueductal gray (PAG) recruitment. At the neuromodulatory-autonomic level ("firmware"), it is defined by altered serotonergic and dopaminergic signaling, elevated cortical signal entropy, shifted salience attribution, altered heart rate variability (HRV/RMSSD), and speculative ponto-geniculo-occipital (PGO) wave activity. At the computational level ("software"), this dynamic is conceptualized as the attenuation of high-level narrative priors and altered precision weighting, permitting bottomup sensory and affective information to flood conscious awareness.

Stage 3 introduces the 4E Appraisal Prism, operating on the premise that acute neurodynamic shifts do not directly dictate final psychological outcomes. Instead, these shifts interact bidirectionally with the Prism, which functions as a real-time contextual filter integrating four interdependent facets: embodied (somatic baseline and interoceptive accuracy, such as resting HRV), embedded (physical environment and perceived social safety), enacted (procedural technique, ritual structure, and behavioral framing), and extended (symbolic, cultural, or spiritual cognitive frameworks that assist in sense-making). When these 4E factors are misaligned-such as through coercive settings, technique overefforting, or destabilizing interpretive frameworks-the Prism operates as a vulnerability matrix that precipitates clinical distress.

Stage 4 resolves these filtered neurodynamic states into three primary outcome trajectories. Adaptive trajectories manifest as integrated psychological insights, posttraumatic growth, and enhanced spiritual well-being. Practice-induced adverse trajectories encompass transient or persistent depersonalization, derealization, hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD), or spiritual crises arising from contextual misalignments. Primary clinical trajectories involve the acute exacerbation or unmasking of underlying psychiatric disorders, such as primary psychosis or mania. Clinically, differential triage requires evaluating functional impairment and symptom etiology: persistent functional deficits or severe distress mandate formal psychiatric referral, whereas new-onset focal neurological symptoms, paroxysmal spells, or unprovoked seizures require immediate neurological evaluation and electroencephalographic workup.

5.2. The Epistemological Invariant

Threaded across this entire model is the Epistemological Invariant: identifying the neural mechanisms or computational dynamics of a non-ordinary state does not resolve its ultimate existential or metaphysical validity. Documenting biological mechanisms, phenomenological reports, and existential meaning as distinct operational domains prevents two clinical errors: reducing meaningful human experiences to simple discharge pathology, and inflating neuroimaging correlates into metaphysical claims. 6. Challenges and a Research Agenda

Identifying the methodological and theoretical vulnerabilities of a discipline is medically useful only if the diagnosis directly transforms how future clinical trials, observational studies, and neuroimaging paradigms are designed. In the emerging field of neurospirituality and NOEs, a systematic audit reveals profound structural weaknesses: systematic construct conflation, unvalidated single-item regressors, sample nonindependence-such as the repeated scanning of the Columbia/Weissman multigenerational depression cohort (Weissman et al., 2005; Miller et al., 2014)-and a pervasive habit of driving functional MRI analyses with unvalidated proxies of religiosity. As documented in the systematic review by Rosmarin et al. (2022), out of eighteen neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies examining spirituality, mental health, and neurobiology, thirteen relied on unvalidated single items or external attendance frequency, while only three incorporated psychometrically validated, multidimensional instruments. For the clinical neurologist, these are not mere academic quibbles; they represent foundational threats to diagnostic accuracy and mechanistic clarity. The basic premise of clinical neuroscience holds that valid empirical inferences require valid psychometric constructs, and a body of neuroimaging literature built upon single-item self-reports cannot be salvaged simply by acquiring larger volumes of similarly flawed data.

To establish an empirically defensible framework for neurological practice, future research must systematically address these diagnostic weaknesses through three primary avenues. First, conceptual conflation-the reflex to mistake a raw, acute phenomenal state for its post-hoc cultural, religious, or psychiatric interpretation-must yield to structured neurophenomenological protocols, deploying standardized clinical interviews with published inter-rater reliability to separate immediate qualitative experiences from downstream narrative appraisals (Timmermann et al., 2023). Second, construct validity must become a mandatory inclusion criterion in neuroimaging paradigms, requiring psychometrically validated, multidimensional instruments; no patient or research participant should enter a scanner without such psychometric quantification. Third, localizationist reductionism must yield to dynamic network neuroscience, resisting the search for hypothetical "spiritual centers" to examine how large-scale systems like the DMN, SN, and CEN interact with subcortical gating structures such as the anterior insula and periaqueductal gray (Picard & Friston, 2014; Unterrainer, 2025).

Overcoming the clinical translation gap further requires directly addressing widespread sampling biases and longitudinal deficits in the current literature. The existing evidence base relies disproportionately on small convenience samples from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) populations, severely limiting its generalizability to diverse clinical and cross-cultural cohorts (Maraldi et al., 2024; Timmermann et al., 2023). Establishing multi-center, cross-cultural research consortia alongside prospective longitudinal designs is essential to track patient trajectories over time, providing the empirical foundation needed to identify the specific baseline variables that moderate whether an acute neurodynamic state resolves into adaptive growth or clinical distress.

7. Limitations and Epistemic Status

As a narrative synthesis based on targeted literature searches rather than an exhaustive systematic review, this paper is subject to potential selection bias and precludes quantitative data pooling. The generalizability of the underlying empirical literature is further constrained by a heavy reliance on WEIRD study populations, leaving cross-cultural validity unestablished. Furthermore, widespread publication bias across clinical and contemplative neuroimaging risks overstating regional neural specificity, while clinical trials of psychedelic interventions face significant methodological challenges due to functional unblinding driven by prominent psychoactive effects.

To prevent ad-hoc hedging, address these literature-wide constraints, and maintain a clear distinction between well-replicated data and original conceptual proposals, the claims throughout this synthesis are organized into three explicit epistemic tiers. Tier 1: Established Empirical Benchmarks. Replicated findings backed by coordinate-based meta-analyses and validated psychometric instruments, such as core neural networks recruited during meditation, the established factor structures of the Altered States of Consciousness rating scale family (e.g., 3D-ASCr / 5D-ASC), and the four-factor structure of the Revised Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ30). Tier 2 represents Supported / Preliminary Evidence, consisting of real clinical and physiological findings derived from small-cohort trials, including psychospiritual improvements in psychedelic-assisted palliative care and state shifts induced by hyperventilation-mediated hypocapnia. Although grounded in empirical data, Tier 2 findings remain qualified by modest sample sizes, heterogeneous control conditions, and unisolated contextual set-and-setting factors. Tier 3 designates Conceptual & Speculative Heuristics, which gather original theoretical models introduced in this review to structure a fragmented domain, including the six-dimension phenomenological taxonomy, the tri-level neurodynamic architecture ("software," "firmware," "hardware"), the Trigger-Dynamic Psyche-4E Appraisal-Outcome Model, the 4E Appraisal Prism, the five-vector precisionweighting convergence hypothesis, and proposed periaqueductal gray or heart-rate variability mechanisms.

Crucially, the operational parameters embedded within these Tier 3 frameworksincluding baseline heart-rate variability, interoceptive accuracy tasks, setting inventories, and the six-dimension phenomenological profile-have not yet undergone formal psychometric testing for reliability or prognostic utility. Furthermore, no empirical study has directly measured precision-weighting shifts across all five induction routes simultaneously, and major interpretive extensions-such as applying predictive processing models (like ALBUS/SEBUS) to non-pharmacological induction vectors or attributing central valencedetermining roles to periaqueductal gray signaling-remain unverified hypotheses. Finally, as a single-author synthesis reconciling disciplines of vastly different methodological maturities, theoretical integration inevitably reflects specific interpretive decisions. Explicitly categorizing these frameworks into Tier 3 ensures that clinicians and neuroscientists can utilize them as testable research heuristics for prospective inquiry without mistaking speculative formulations for established diagnostic tools or confirmed pathophysiology.

8. Conclusions

This work provides general neurologists with a practical, non-reductionist framework for evaluating Non-Ordinary Experiences (NOEs)-states characterized by altered selfawareness, temporal perception, or spatial boundaries arising from contemplative practices, psychoactive substances, or focal neurological events. The overarching synthesis connects three conceptual heuristics: a six-dimension phenomenological taxonomy for mapping firstperson reports of what an experience was like, independent of any downstream interpretation; a tri-level descriptive taxonomy contrasting normal and non-ordinary states across computational inference, neuromodulatory-autonomic state-setting, and large-scale network substrates; and the master Trigger-Dynamic Psyche-4E Appraisal-Outcome Model, a process architecture in which acute triggers perturb neurodynamics across the tri-level taxonomy, the resulting dynamics unfold within the patient's psyche, and long-term trajectories toward adaptive growth or clinical distress are selected by the patient's bodily state, social setting, technique, and interpretive beliefs (the 4E Appraisal Prism). Notably, the six-dimension schema extends the foundational phenomenological framework established by Timmermann et al. (2023), and it functions within the model as the descriptive language for characterizing the Dynamic Psyche stage-the experiential state that lies between trigger and appraisal.

Two primary hypotheses anchor this theoretical framework, neither of which is yet empirically established: first, the Precision-Weighting Convergence Hypothesis, which posits that five distinct induction routes (meditation, 5-HT2A classical psychedelics, hypocapnic circular breathwork, hypnosis, and focal insular seizures) share a common neurodynamic shift toward reduced top-down prior precision and large-scale network decoupling (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Havenith et al., 2025; Picard & Friston, 2014; Timmermann et al., 2023), although direct computational measurements across all five vectors are lacking and network findings remain inconsistent across modalities (Baten et al., 2026); and second, the Contextual Outcome-Mediation Claim, which asserts that acute neural perturbations do not independently dictate long-term results, but rather whether an experience resolves into integrated growth or triggers depersonalization, hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD), or spiritual crisis depends on contextual filtering via the 4E Appraisal Prism.

Three falsifiable predictions follow from these frameworks: first (P1), first-person NOE reports decompose into the hypothesized six latent dimensions rather than a different latent structure; second (P2), baseline 4E appraisal variables statistically mediate the triggeroutcome relationship above trigger intensity alone; and third (P3), the five induction vectors exhibit a convergent shift in precision-weighting proxies. The failure of any of these predictions in prospective testing would refute the corresponding framework.

Methodological rigor must be applied symmetrically across the discipline. Neuroimaging research on spirituality requires psychometrically validated multidimensional tools rather than unvalidated single items or oversampled cohorts (Rosmarin et al., 2022; Miller et al., 2014; Weissman et al., 2005). Similarly, computational models such as REBUS (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019), ALBUS/SEBUS (Safron et al., 2025), and the zero-error hypothesis of insular ecstatic seizures (Picard & Friston, 2014; Gschwind & Picard, 2016)and, no less, the two hypotheses and Trigger-Dynamic Psyche-4E Appraisal-Outcome Model articulated in this review-must be treated as productive hypotheses rather than established biological truths until subjected to strict prospective validation.

In clinical practice, understanding mechanisms like anterior insular hyper-synchrony in ecstatic epilepsy enables neurologists to treat underlying seizure disorders while respecting the patient's subjective reality (Picard & Friston, 2014). The six-step clinical reasoning heuristic offers a research-stage tool that walks the clinician through the model in practice: phenomenological profiling across the six dimensions, trigger identification, 4E appraisal analysis, dynamics and controllability, functional impact, and risk screening-guiding appropriate clinical routing or integration support. At the core of this framework is a fundamental distinction: brain biology, subjective experiences, and personal meanings are three separate levels of analysis. Describing the neural activity associated with a transcendent state clarifies its physical substrate without reducing its personal meaning or proving metaphysical claims. Maintaining this distinction prevents both the reflexive pathologization of adaptive experiences and the romanticization of underlying neurological disease.
