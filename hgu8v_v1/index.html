Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

Patterns are designed to capture:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

This study examined the complexity of cyberbullying and its relationship with traditional bullying, school climate, and social support using network analysis. The sample consisted of 1,269 participants (359 boys), including 770 emerging adults and 499 teens, with a mean age of 18 (SD = 2.5). We measured 11 forms of cyberbullying across different roles (victim, aggressor, defender, bystander), five factors of school climate, social support (from parents, friends, teachers), and traditional bullying roles. Undirected weighted network models revealed strong interconnections between various types of aggressive behaviors and cyberbullying victimization.

Unauthorized sharing of personal media showed negative relationships with online verbal attacks, while online group exclusion strongly associated with social isolation. The bystander role positively related to both verbal aggression and victimization. Defenders in cyberbullying were more likely to be victims of personal information exposure and verbal attacks, and strongly associated with defending in traditional bullying. School climate and social support demonstrated weak connections to cyberbullying, with student-student relationships, fairness in school, and parental support showing the most direct interconnections. Network comparisons revealed subtle structural differences between genders and between teens and emerging adults. Findings suggest prevention programs should focus on strengthening school relationships while supporting student defenders who may become targets themselves.

Frequent use of the internet increases the likelihood of privacy violations, making individuals more vulnerable and exposed to the attention of strangers (Lloret-Irles et al., 2022).

Cyberbullying is understood as intentional aggressive behavior carried out by a cyber-aggressor using digital devices and online applications (Blinka et al., 2023). It often occurs anonymously, through digital tools, and aims to destroy and psychologically humiliate the victim (Saladino et al., 2020). This negative social phenomenon is characterized by a power imbalance (Auriemma et al., 2020), identity anonymity, a larger audience (Martínez et al., 2020; Tabares et al., 2024), the victim's inability to defend themselves, and the absence of physical contact (Yurdukal & Bütün Ayhan, 2023). It also involves a lack of nonverbal cues (such as facial expressions, body language, and tone of voice) and asynchronous communication between the victim and aggressor in the online environment (Graf et al., 2022), as well as the possibility of continuous or prolonged attacks on the internet (Martínez et al., 2020; Estévez et al., 2020; Sousa et al., 2024).

Cyberbullying can take various forms, including trolling, happy slapping, flaming, griefing, online harassment, and exclusion from online communities (Draganić et al., 2024; Iqbal & Jami, 2022; Niklová et al., 2019; Shukla & Chouhan, 2023), and represents a serious threat to adolescents. It involves a range of reactions and consequences for young people (Saladino et al., 2020). Cyberbullying is associated with serious psychosocial problems for both victims and aggressors, most commonly symptoms of depression, increased psychological stress, substance use, and school avoidance (Ranjith et al., 2023; Zsila et al., 2018).

Research shows that traditional forms of bullying often precede cyberbullying among young people (Forssell, 2016). Cyberbullying shares some characteristics with traditional bullying (intentionality, repetition, aggressive behavior) (Viau et al., 2020), yet has distinct features. Individuals with experiences of offline victimization, especially women, are more likely to engage in cyberbullying (Kapitány-Fővény et al., 2022; Zsila et al., 2019).

Other social variables, such as social support and school climate, have been analyzed as potential indicators of cyberbullying. For example, it has been found that teenagers who perceive greater social support are less likely to become victims of cyberbullying (Arató et al., 2022; Castaño-Pulgarín et al., 2022). Relationships and support from the social environment act as protective factors against cybervictimization, particularly among girls (Hellfeldt, 2019; Harris, 2024). Social support serves as a mediator between cyberbullying, psychological well-being, and mental health (Espino et al., 2023). Seeking social support, together with self-efficacy, are important coping strategies for dealing with cyberbullying (Stegmair & Prybutok, 2024). Parental and peer support play an important role in coping with the consequences and preventing cyberbullying (Hellfeldt, 2019).

School climate is broadly defined as a student's perception of their school's safety, friendliness, and level of support (National School Climate Center, 2017), referring to the quality and nature of social interactions at school. It is defined by the norms, values, rules, organizational structures, and patterns of relationships that are unique to each school (Cohen et al., 2009).

School climate is defined as the perceived quality and nature of school life (Kosciw, Greytak, Giga, Villenas, & Danischewski 2016). Cyberbullying is also reflected in academic performance and the school environment. Students who have been victims of cyberbullying have poorer academic performance compared to students who have not been cyberbullied (Tsitsika et al., 2015). School climate may be a key factor in cybervictimization (Azami & Taremian, 2021; Li et al., 2025). Individuals who have experienced cyberbullying show a decrease in psychological well-being, school functioning, interpersonal relationships, and emotional stability (Yurdukal & Bütün Ayhan, 2023). Perceptions of the school environment and safety are significantly positively related to students' cybervictimization, and on the other hand, predict the perpetration of cyberbullying (Harrison-Pavlik, Cashel, & Kroner, 2024). Experience with cyberbullying brings various psychosomatic problems, problems in peer relationships, and impaired achievement (Sourander et al., 2010). Cyberbullying is associated with serious psychosocial problems for both victims and aggressors, most often with symptoms of depression, increased psychological stress, substance use, and school avoidance (Ranjith et al., 2023; Zsila et al., 2018).

Recent international data highlight the widespread nature of cyberbullying among young people. According to a UNICEF poll, more than one third of young people in 30 countries report having been victims of online bullying, with many also feeling unsafe at school as a result (https://www.unicef.org/press-releases/unicef-poll-more-third-young-people-30-countries-report-being-victim-online-bullying). This underscores the urgent need to better understand the mechanisms and risk factors associated with cyberbullying in adolescent and emerging adult populations.

Traditional research on bullying and cyberbullying has often focused on isolated variables or direct relationships between specific factors. However, these phenomena are inherently complex, involving multiple roles (such as victim, aggressor, defender, and bystander), various forms of aggression, and a web of social and environmental influences. Network analysis offers a powerful methodological approach to capture and visualize these intricate interconnections (Borsboom et al., 2021). By modeling the relationships among different forms of bullying, roles, and contextual factors, network analysis can reveal central elements, bridge variables, and unique patterns that might be overlooked by conventional methods. While the network approach is predominantly used in psychopathology research (Borsboom, 2017), it is increasingly being applied to the study of cyberbullying (Vieta-Piferrer et al., 2024; Molero et al., 2023; Rajamani & Iyer, 2023; Lin et al., 2025). This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of how cyberbullying and traditional bullying are embedded within broader social systems, and how factors like school climate and social support interact within these networks.

Despite extensive research on cyberbullying, there is still limited understanding of how different forms of cyberbullying, traditional bullying, school climate, and social support interact within complex networks. It is also unclear how these relationships differ across age groups and genders. Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore possible dynamics of cyberbullying behavior and experience in the context of the social school environment. Specifically, the aims were to: (1) examine the network structure of cyberbullying; (2) analyze the interconnections of cyberbullying with traditional bullying, school climate, and perceived social support factors; and (3) compare the analyzed networks in girls and boys as well as in teens and emerging adults.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

Data collection was carried out from January to March 2022 in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration for psychological research involving human subjects. The research project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Trnava University in Trnava (No.

3/2022). Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and parental consent was obtained for participants younger than 16 years. Data were collected both online and in person by the research team. Inclusion criteria required to be 13 and older and to have no diagnosis of ADHD or specific learning disorders..

Measures Exposure to Bullying Online and Offline

The questionnaire is based on previous instruments that examined cyberbullying and bullying (Aizenkot & Kashy-Rosenbaum, 2018; Heiman & Olenik-Shemesh, 2015). It was later adapted to specifically assess cyberbullying and cybervictimization (Aizenkot & Kashy-Rosenbaum, 2020). The questionnaire consists of 11 items and includes subscales for cybervictim, cyber-defender, and cyber-bystander roles. Cybervictimization covers various forms of aggressive behavior, such as sharing personal photos or online exclusion from a group.

Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 -never, 5 -quite often).

Olweus Bully/Victim Questionnaire

The questionnaire is one of the most commonly used tools for assessing bullying and victimization among students (Olweus, 1996; 2013). It consists of 6 items. After providing a definition of bullying, the questionnaire focuses on analyzing specific role groups (aggressor, victim, defender, bystander). The items ask students how often they have experienced bullying, been victimized, defended a bullied student, or witnessed bullying. According to Olweus (2003), bullying is defined as occurring if it happens at least two or three times per month.

School climate

School climate was assessed using a modified version of the Delaware School Climate Survey-Student (Bear et al., 2011), which includes 23 items covering five key dimensions: teacher-student relationships (8 items), student-student relationships (4 items), fairness of rules (4 items), school safety (3 items), and school connectedness (4 items). For this study, we utilized 4 items to evaluate student-student relationships, 4 items for teacher-student relationships, 3 items for perceived fairness, 4 items for school satisfaction, and 1 item addressing respect for others.

Participants responded using a 4-point Likert scale, with options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree).

Social support

Social support was measured using 3 items, each focusing on a different source of support: parents, teachers, and friends. Respondents rated the level of social support on a 4-point Likert scale (1 -very poor, 4 -excellent).

Data analysis

Following this step, scores for the scales were summed, and descriptive analyses were conducted, including different roles in bullying. To examine the relationship between selected factors as potential moderators of the cyberbullying network, Welch t-tests were used for gender and age categories, while polychoric correlations were applied for ordinal variables. The structure of cyberbullying (i.e., the connections between different behavior or situations related to cyberbullying) was analyzed using network analysis. In this approach, indicators are represented as nodes, and their relationships are depicted as edges. Thicker edges indicate stronger relationships, with solid blue lines representing positive relationships and dashed red lines indicating negative ones.

An undirected weighted network was estimated, treating variables as ordinal. The EBICglasso method with a high tuning parameter was applied to regularize weak relationships and produce a sparse network. Strength (the degree to which a node is directly connected to others) and closeness (the degree to which a node is indirectly connected to others) indices were calculated. Non-parametric and case-drop bootstrapping were used to assess the accuracy and stability of edge weights and centrality indices. The correlation stability (CS) coefficient was also estimated, indicating the proportion of cases that could be removed while maintaining a correlation of at least 0.70. Differences between networks across selected subgroups were evaluated using a network comparison test with 1,000 iterations and Bonferroni-adjusted pvalues.

Missing data were inspected, revealing an average missing rate of 1.4% across analyzed items. The highest rates of missingness were 9% for traditional bullying items. Maximum missingness per participant was 16% in items from analyzed scales. Overall, the level of missing data was low. For cyberbullying networks estimation and t-tests, a pairwise deletion approach was used to handle missing data. For exploring moderating factors of cyberbullying networks, an averaged dataset obtained through multiple imputation was utilized.

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3; R Core Team, 2025) using the following packages: psych (Revelle, 2025), qgraph (Epskamp et al., 2012), bootnet (Epskamp et al., 2018), NetworkComparisonTest (van Borkulo et al., 2021), mice (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the dynamics of cyberbullying within the social school environment by examining its network structure, connections to traditional bullying, school climate, and social support, and comparing these patterns across gender and age groups. The findings revealed strong links between cyberbullying and traditional bullying roles, with victims and aggressors in one domain often overlapping with their counterparts in the other.

Cyberbullying behaviors were negatively associated with perceived social support and a positive school climate, highlighting the importance of these factors in mitigating such behaviors.

Network analyses further underscored the centrality of aggressor and victim roles, as well as audience behaviors like defending and bystanding, in potentially shaping the dynamics of cyberbullying. While no significant structural differences were found between genders or age groups, notable variations in the density and interconnections of behaviors were observed, suggesting nuanced differences in how cyberbullying manifests across these groups. Cyberbullying Networks

Network analysis of cyberbullying behaviors revealed complex interconnections, with certain roles, particularly victims and aggressors, emerging as central to the network's structure.

Verbal aggression and social exclusion demonstrated high centrality, which may underscores their pivotal roles in sustaining cyberbullying dynamics. Verbal aggression is among the most frequent forms of cyberbullying (Bauman, 2013) and is not limited to aggressors. It can also spread to bystanders, especially those lacking strong social ties or confidence in handling cyberbullying (Ferreira et al., 2020). Social exclusion, another common behavior, is often used deliberately to ostracize individuals from online groups. This behavior increases victims' feelings of isolation, loss of agency, and psychological distress (Ademiluyi et al., 2022).

Our findings suggest that interventions targeting central behaviors such as verbal aggression and social exclusion may have a broader impact on the overall network of cyberbullying. Because these behaviors have high centrality, addressing them could have a cascading effect and potentially reduce the prevalence of other related cyberbullying behaviors.

Strong interconnections were observed among different victim experiences, such as being verbally attacked, excluded from groups, and exposed to inappropriate content. This indicates that victims often experience multiple forms of cyberbullying at the same time. Similarly, aggressor behaviors, including verbal aggression, unauthorized sharing of media, and exclusion, were tightly clustered. This pattern may reflect that individuals who engage in one type of cyberbullying are likely to perpetrate others as well. Other authors have also found that different types of cyber-aggression, as well as different forms of cyberbullying victimization, are strongly related to each other. Individuals often engage in cyberbullying for multiple, overlapping motives such as rage, revenge, reward, and recreation (Thomas et al., 2018; Graf et al., 2022). These findings highlight that cyberbullying is a complex phenomenon, consisting of an interplay of various types of behavior and experience, in which multiple roles are involved.

In our study, strong links between bystander and defender behaviors highlight the importance of audience roles in either perpetuating or mitigating cyberbullying. Audience roles, particularly defenders and bystanders, appeared as significant components of the network, reflecting their active involvement in cyberbullying dynamics (Polanco-Levicán & Salvo-Garrido, 2021; Sarmiento et al., 2019). Defenders, though less studied, play a crucial role in mitigating cyberbullying by supporting victims (Chen et al., 2024). The positive association between defender behavior and various types of victimization suggests that defenders may be more likely to intervene in situations where victimization is more prevalent. This underscores the importance of empowering bystanders to take constructive action. However, defenders also risk becoming targets themselves, as they may be victimized by the bullies of the victims they defend (Huitsing et al., 2014). This relationship highlights the importance of protecting defenders.

Combined network analysis demonstrated strong links between cyberbullying and traditional bullying roles. This indicates that individuals' involvement in one context is closely related to their roles in the other. Previous research has also shown significant overlap between traditional and cyberbullying, with involvement in one form increasing the likelihood of involvement in the other (Hemphill et al., 2012; Modecki et al., 2014; Casas et al., 2013; Zych et al., 2015; Wegge et al., 2014). These findings suggest that prevention and intervention strategies should address both online and offline bullying behaviors in an integrated manner, rather than treating them as separate phenomena (Cantone et al., 2015).

Another interesting finding from the combined network was the role of social support and school climate. Although the connections between these factors and cyberbullying were generally weaker than those among bullying behaviors themselves, variables such as student-student relationships and parental support still showed direct links to cyberbullying experiences. The centrality of school climate variables, including teacher-student relationships and perceived fairness, underscores the potential of fostering a positive school environment as a protective factor against both traditional and cyberbullying. Social support, particularly in the school context, also appears to be an important factor in cyberbullying dynamics (Akturk, 2015; Arató et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2020; Schultze-Krumbholz et al., 2020).

Some unexpected or paradoxical findings were also observed. For example, studentstudent relationships were not only negatively associated with cyberbullying victimization but also positively linked to the exclusion of others from online groups. This suggests a complex role of peer dynamics. This finding aligns with ingroup-outgroup theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979), which explains how strong group bonds can both protect members and exclude outsiders in digital settings. Another unexpected finding was the positive connection between perceived fairness in school and exposure to age-inappropriate content. One possible explanation is that when students perceive their school environment as fair and open, they may also feel more tolerant or less restricted regarding age-controversial content online.

Age and Gender as Moderators of Cyberbullying Networks

Moderated network analyses indicated no significant overall differences in the structure of cyberbullying networks between girls and boys, or between teens and emerging adults. This suggests that the general pattern of relationships among variables is comparable across gender and age groups. However, previous research has shown that cyberbullying dynamics are more likely to occur within same-gender and same-age groups (Wegge et al., 2014). Other studies have also reported gender (Cornell et al., 2013; Wang, Wang, & Lei, 2019; Lee et al., 2022) and age (Lopéz-Castro et al., 2023; Wang, Yogeeswaran et al., 2019; Barlet & Coyne, 2014) differences in cyberbullying behavior and experience.

Despite the lack of significant global differences, the boys' network appeared denser, with stronger interconnections between victim and aggressor roles, as well as between personal information exposure and social isolation online. Defender behavior in boys was more closely tied to personal information exposure and exclusion of others. In contrast, in girls, defender behavior was directly connected to both online verbal aggression and victimization, which highlights a particular sensitivity to verbal forms of cyberbullying. Parental support was more protective for boys, whereas peer relationships played a stronger protective role for girls.

Among teens, the network was notably denser, with various types of cyberbullying behaviors more closely interconnected, especially between audience roles and both victimization and aggression. Defender behavior in teens was directly connected with all types of victimization, suggesting that younger individuals may be more likely to intervene across a broader range of experiences. In emerging adults, aggressive behaviors were more strongly interconnected, indicating a tendency for multiple forms of cyberbullying to co-occur. For teens, cyberbullying was more directly linked to the school environment and traditional bullying, which emphasizes the centrality of school context in adolescent experiences (Wegge et al., 2014).

These results highlight the importance of considering both gender and age differences when developing cyberbullying prevention and intervention programs. This emphasizes the need for strategies tailored to the unique experiences and responses of different groups.

Limitations and Further Directions

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample was not fully balanced in terms of gender, with boys representing only 28% of participants, which may limit the generalizability of gender-related findings. The majority of participants were emerging adults, and the results may not fully capture the experiences of younger adolescents. The cross-sectional design of the study does not allow for causal inferences or examination of changes over time. Data were collected through self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to social desirability bias and recall inaccuracies. Additionally, the study was conducted in a specific cultural and educational context, which may affect the applicability CYBERBULLYING, BULLYING AND SCHOOL SOCIAL ENVIRONMENT of the results to other populations. Despite that, our results emphasize the complex interplay between individual behaviors, social support, and school climate in understanding cyberbullying.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the complex network of cyberbullying behaviors among adolescents and emerging adults. The findings highlight the central roles of verbal aggression and social exclusion, as well as the importance of audience roles such as defenders and bystanders. The results also underscore the interconnectedness of cyberbullying and traditional bullying, and the influence of contextual factors like school climate and social support. Although no major differences were found in the overall network structure across gender and age groups, some specific patterns emerged, emphasizing the need for tailored prevention and intervention strategies. Addressing central behaviors and fostering supportive environments may be key to reducing the prevalence and impact of cyberbullying.
