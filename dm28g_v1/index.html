How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Introduction

Students' reactions to errors are increasingly recognized as central to learning processes. A substantial body of research shows that errors can foster learning by highlighting knowledge gaps and triggering deeper cognitive and metacognitive processing (see e.g., Lubin et al., 2015; Metcalfe et al., 2025; Ruthig et al., 2017; Zhang & Fiorella, 2023). However, learners do not benefit from errors automatically, and may rather react to them differently, ranging from seeing them as learning opportunities to perceiving them as threats (Pan et al., 2020; Tulis et al., 2016), with important consequences for achievement (Soncini et al., 2022). To account for these differences in how learners respond to errors, recent research has conceptualized error processing as involving a dynamic interplay of affective, motivational, and self-regulatory processes. Adaptive responses include maintaining motivation, engaging in error analysis, and persisting despite setbacks, whereas maladaptive responses (e.g., in people with high perfectionism) involve avoidance, disengagement, or reduced effort (Tulis et al., 2016). Importantly, experimental evidence shows that these responses can be causally shaped by learners' beliefs about errors. For example, emphasizing the negative consequences of errors reduces persistence and metacognitive engagement, whereas framing errors as learning opportunities promotes effort investment and adaptive regulation (Soncini et al., 2021; Tulis & Dresel, 2025). Together, these findings indicate that the effects of errors on learning are mediated by learners' psychological responses rather than by errors per se.

Despite this growing body of research, the role of individual differences in sensitivity to errors (as a more general and habitual tendency to perceive and react to errors across situations compared to situation-specific reaction to individual errors) in shaping students' motivation and self-perceived ability remains poorly understood, with sensitivity to errors mostly discussed in the framework of anxiety (see e.g., Likhanov et al., 2026). For example, concerns over one's errors and doubts about one's actions have been found to be positively linked with mathematics anxiety (r=.38) and negatively associated with self-efficacy in mathematics (r=-.28; McCaughey et al., 2022) -due to increased skipping of complex problems and decreased help-seeking behaviors after error commission (e.g., Wang et al., 2025a). In addition, teacher attitudes toward errors and students' shared perception of the usefulness of errors for learning have been shown to influence classroom-level motivation, with no effect on achievement (Käfer et al., 2019).

Error sensitivity overlaps conceptually with perfectionistic concerns, and may be especially relevant to achievement goals because goal adoption reflects both competence-related expectations and fear of failure (Elliot & Church, 1997; Seidah et al., 2002). Studying error sensitivity alongside achievement goals and self-perceived ability can therefore show how students' concerns about mistakes relate to their perceived competence and the goals they pursue: prior research links positive ability self-concepts and mastery goals to more adaptive responses to errors, and performance-avoidance goals to less adaptive responses (e.g., Steuer et al., 2013).

Although research has begun to examine the links among error sensitivity, motivation, achievement and self-perceptions about ones' ability, the literature remains limited, particularly with respect to the directionality of these relationships and the role of individual differences over time. The present study addresses this gap by examining the longitudinal relations between error sensitivity, achievement goals, and self-perceived ability in French and mathematics, with a specific focus on directionality of their associations.

Error sensitivity

Error sensitivity refers to the extent to which individuals react negatively to errors or failure and has been proposed as a key psychological factor that may profoundly shape academic trajectories (e.g., Pekrun, 2024). Many students show a notable aversion to making errors, seeing them as threats rather than learning opportunities (Pan et al., 2020). Excessive sensitivity to errors, closely related to fear of failure or a high concern over errors and especially in comparison with others, can lead to maladaptive outcomes such as anxiety, avoidance of challenging tasks, or disengagement from learning, which in turn may undermine performance (Giel et al., 2020). For example, Johansson et al. (2024), analyzing data from over 200,000 children, found that sequential errors increased the probability that students would disengage from an online learning task, reflecting a decrease in motivation. Importantly, there were large individual differences in this effect: some students showed only a small drop in persistence after errors, whereas others were highly sensitive and disengaged quickly after making mistakes. These individual differences were stable across tasks, suggesting that error sensitivity may reflect a trait-like characteristic. Yet, as the authors note, "little is known" about why such differences exist or how they develop.

One possible source of differences in error sensitivity may be teacher-and peer-related supports, such as tolerance, constructive analysis, low ridicule, and encouragement of risk-taking.

These supports are often conceptualized as a positive error climate (which could contribute to lower error sensitivity at individual level) and have been studied as predictors of achievement-related variables. For example, Steuer et al. (2022) found that perceived positive error climate predicted students' affective-motivational adaptivity, with strong standardized effects (β ≈ .48-.55 across subjects; N = 937 students; 48 classrooms). Longitudinal evidence further indicates that students' adaptive reactions to errors tend to decline on average across grades, but that a more positive error climate is associated with a less negative developmental trajectory of error-related adaptivity over time (i.e., lower sensitivity to errors; N = 1,641; 69 classes; five waves; Dresel et al., 2025). At the outcome level, perceived positive error climate has been linked to better mathematics grades indirectly through more adaptive responses to errors (N = 563; 32 math classes), consistent with a pathway from error climate to responses to learning outcomes (Soncini et al., 2022).

Taken together, these findings suggest that error sensitivity may play a central role in shaping how learners engage with errors and regulate their behavior in response to them. However, despite its potential importance, little is known about how error sensitivity relates to various components of motivation, such as achievement goals, and self-perceived ability, particularly over time and in terms of the directionality of these associations.

Achievement goals

Achievement goals represent a central framework for understanding students' motivation and have been extensively studied in educational psychology. A widely used conceptualization is the 2 × 2 achievement goal framework, which distinguishes between mastery and performance goals, each with approach and avoidance components (Elliot & McGregor, 2001). Within this framework, mastery-approach goals reflect a focus on developing competence and mastering tasks, whereas performance-approach goals emphasize demonstrating competence relative to others. In contrast, mastery-avoidance goals involve concerns about failing to fully understand or master a task, and performance-avoidance goals reflect concerns about appearing incompetent compared to others. This framework has been particularly useful for capturing the complexity of students' motivational orientations and their distinct implications for learning and achievement (Butera et al., 2024).

A large body of research has shown that these goal orientations are differentially associated with students' affect, motivation, and academic outcomes. Mastery-approach goals are associated with the use of deeper learning strategies, higher intrinsic motivation, and greater academic effort and perseverance, which in turn support better performance (Guo et al., 2023; Kaplan et al., 2002; Katz-Vago & Benita, 2024). Performance-approach goals may be associated with higher performance in the short term (e.g., (Elliot & McGregor, 2001; Loose et al., 2012; but see Crouzevialle & Butera, 2017), but in the long run they are associated with higher anxiety (Elliot & Murayama, 2008), surface learning strategies (e.g., rote learning), and lower academic self-esteem (Senko et al., 2011 (Senko et al., , 2013;; Shim et al., 2012). In contrast, avoidance-oriented goals are consistently associated with less adaptive outcomes. Both mastery-avoidance and performance-avoidance goals are associated with higher levels of anxiety and disengagement (An et al., 2022; Bruno et al., 2019; C. Darnon et al., 2007; Elliot & McGregor, 2001; but see Senko & Freund, 2015, for positive effect of mastery-avoidance goals).

Performance-avoidance goals, in particular, have been identified as the least adaptive type, producing maladaptive motivational patterns such as disorganized studying and an increased fear of failure and focus on one's errors (see discussion in Elliot & McGregor, 2001; Moller & Elliot, 2006; Świątkowski & Dompnier, 2021). Some evidence suggests that mastery-avoidance goals may share some maladaptive features with performance-avoidance tendencies. For example, metaanalytic results indicate that both performance-avoidance and mastery-avoidance goals are associated with higher negative affect (worry, dissatisfaction) in students (Baranik et al., 2010; Huang, 2011). However, mastery-avoidance goals remain relatively understudied due to the complexity of the construct and concerns regarding its reliability (see Fiévé et al., 2025 for discussion), highlighting the need for further investigation. Another meta-analysis further underscores the asymmetry between approach and avoidance orientations, showing that masteryapproach goals were negatively linked with anxiety (-.10), while performance avoidance demonstrated a stronger positive association (r = .25; Diaconu-Gherasim et al., 2024). Despite this, to our knowledge, there are no studies that directly investigated the link between achievement goals and error sensitivity.

Taken together, research discussed above suggests that students who are overly worried about errors may also tend to avoid making errors in front of others (performance-avoidance) or avoid failing to achieve full mastery (mastery-avoidance).

Self-perceptions of academic achievement

Beyond motivational orientations, students' own perceptions of their academic abilityoften termed academic self-concept or self-perceived achievement -have emerged as powerful predictors of educational success. A growing body of evidence indicates that how students evaluate their competence in academic domains is strongly associated with objective outcomes such as grades (see some discussion in Núñez-Regueiro et al., 2026). For example, Steinmayr and colleagues (2019) found that students' domain-specific self-concepts in mathematics and language arts were among the strongest predictors of their grades in those subjects, outperforming even their achievement goal orientations in predictive power. Such findings highlight the importance of students' self-beliefs: when learners feel capable and successful academically, they tend to achieve more.

Over the past decades, researchers have increasingly emphasized the role of self-related constructs, recognizing that academic self-perceptions can both drive engagement and reflect prior successes in a reciprocal cycle (see reviews, Marsh & Craven, 2006; Marsh & O'Mara, 2008). For example, several meta-analyses of longitudinal relations found that students who perceive themselves as competent (i.e. higher academic self-concept / self-efficacy) tend to achieve better academic results over time, with almost equal effect in the opposite direction -those with higher achievement demonstrated higher academic self-concept later (Huang, 2011; Wu et al., 2021).

Although this bidirectional relationship is widely supported, the relative strength and directionality of these effects remain debated (see a recent exchange of commentaries; Núñez-Regueiro et al., 2025; Sorjonen et al., 2025b Sorjonen et al., , 2025a)).

One recent study investigated 28 anxiety-related variables in a large sample of vocational education students using network analysis (Likhanov, Fillon, et al., 2026). The results revealed a tightly interconnected cluster that included anxiety, error sensitivity, and imposter syndrome. This cluster was negatively connected to self-perceived ability in mathematics and French, and positively associated with achievement goals. Notably, mastery-approach goals were directly linked to error sensitivity and self-perceived ability, suggesting that these constructs may be embedded within a broader motivational system. However, these findings were based on crosssectional data, precluding establishing the directionality of the effects. It thus remains unclear whether the relations between self-perceived ability and motivational constructs are unidirectional (perceiving oneself as capable in mathematics leading to higher endorsement of mastery achievement goals or vice versa) or reciprocal (a cycle of effects that unravel in time). A similar question arises for error sensitivity: whether perceiving oneself as academically capable leads to lower error sensitivity and greater endorsement of approach-oriented goals, or whether lower error sensitivity fosters more positive self-perceptions and adaptive motivational orientations over time.

Potential pathways linking error sensitivity with motivation and self-perceived ability

Several theoretical pathways may underlie the relations between error sensitivity, achievement goals, and self-perceived ability; and suggest some directionality of effects. A first pathway concerns how errors are appraised and emotionally processed. As suggested by controlvalue theory, competence and value appraisals generate distinct achievement-related emotions, which in turn influence effort, strategy use, and persistence (Pekrun, 2024). In this context, error sensitivity may bias these appraisals, leading students to interpret errors as more threatening and more diagnostic of their ability. Such interpretations are likely to reduce perceived control and increase anxiety/shame, especially in high-value domains, thereby decreasing both motivation (especially approach aspect of Achievement goals) and self-perceived ability.

A second pathway involves post-error regulation. In educational error-learning models, maladaptive reactions to errors involve drops in motivation and increases in dysfunctional emotions, whereas adaptive reactions maintain motivation and support regulatory behaviors such as analyzing the error and persisting (Dresel et al., 2025). If error sensitivity increases the subjective cost of errors, students may minimize future exposure by avoiding difficult items, reducing helpseeking, or choosing low-risk tasks. These patterns are consistent with avoidance-oriented motivational profiles and may, over time, reduce learning opportunities (see e.g., a vicious cycle in mathematics anxiety; Wang et al., 2025).

A third pathway concerns contextual amplification or buffering via classroom error climate.

A positive error climate (teacher tolerance, supportive responses, peer non-ridicule, and constructive analysis) can reframe errors as informative rather than identity-threatening (reducing error sensitivity), increasing risk-taking and sustaining students' affective-motivational adaptivity (Steuer et al., 2022). This climate pathway is particularly relevant for language learning, where participation and communicative risk-taking are central to progress and errors are socially public (Teimouri et al., 2019). Similarly, higher levels of approached-oriented motivations and selfperceived ability can be protective against errors (e.g., via ego-busting mechanisms, see e.g., research on narcissism being a protective factor against general anxiety; Papageorgiou et al., 2026), and thus result in lower error sensitivity.

There is also some evidence, coming from electroencephalography (EEG), that provides insights into neuroscientific mechanisms underlying individual differences in error processing and its links with learners' motivation. Specifically, evidence from studies using event-related potentials (ERPs) suggests that individuals' level of motivation can systematically shape errormonitoring signals (this brain excitability in relation to errors is conceptually close to error sensitivity), especially the error-related negativity (ERN/Ne), and, in some cases, the later error positivity (Pe). For example, when errors are made more motivationally significant (e.g., via monetary incentives or reward/punishment contexts), ERN amplitudes tend to increase, which is consistent with enhanced performance monitoring when people are trying harder (Maruo et al., 2016; Pailing & Segalowitz, 2004). In addition, when individuals become mentally fatigued or disengage over time, ERN amplitude tends to decrease, suggesting reduced investment in monitoring/cognitive control (Boksem et al., 2006).

The current study

To sum, the associations among error sensitivity, achievement goals and self-perceived ability and the directionality of these associations remain unclear. This gap is critical, as error sensitivity may represent a key mechanism through which students interpret academic challenges and regulate their engagement, with potential implications for both their motivational orientations and their self-perceptions of competence. The present study addresses this issue by examining for the first time the longitudinal relations among error sensitivity, achievement goals, and selfperceived ability -to assess the directionality of effects. Based on the reviewed literature, the current study examined whether: RQ1: higher error sensitivity leads to decrease in approachoriented motivations and self-perceived ability; RQ2: error sensitivity function as an antecedent of avoidance-oriented motivation; RQ3: higher levels of motivational orientations-particularly those involving social comparison (e.g., aiming to perform better than others, such as performanceapproach) lead to heightened sensitivity to errors; RQ4: higher levels of avoidance motivations (especially performance -due to it social component) lead to higher error sensitivity; RQ5: error sensitivity is more strongly associated with avoidance-oriented goals than with approach-oriented goals; and RQ6: whether higher self-perceived ability is associated with lower error sensitivity over time, consistent with the idea that more positive self-perceptions may buffer the perceived threat of errors.

The study draws on a large sample of vocational education (VET) students who are often omitted from motivational research (Bardach et al., 2019). VET students constitute a particularly relevant population for examining error sensitivity, as vocational tracks are often perceived as a less prestigious alternative to general education pathways (European Centre for the Development of Vocational Training., 2017; Jonsson & Beach, 2015; Weller et al., 2025). Moreover, up to 60% of VET students did not choose the vocational stream as their first choice. These structural characteristics have been associated with increased risks of school dropout, behavioral difficulties and emotional challenges, including higher anxiety and lower motivation (Arrighi & Gasquet, 2010; Bernard & Troger, 2012; Jellab, 2017; Solberg et al., 2023). In such conditions, students may be particularly vulnerable to errors due to more fragile self-perceptions and prior academic difficulties. As a result, this population provides a particularly informative case for examining how error sensitivity is related to motivational orientations and self-perceived ability over time.

Methods

Participants

A total of 10,163 students (59% females; age mode: 16) were recruited from 109 vocational schools in France that volunteered to participate in a nationwide multi-lab longitudinal experiment conducted over three years in two cohorts (2017 and 2018) (PROFAN project; see Riant et al., 2024; Rudmann et al., 2024). The project aimed to examine the effects of two cooperative learning approaches (Jigsaw classroom and another form of collaborative learning), compared to standard instructional practices. The data analyzed in the present study were drawn from this larger project and focus on three measurement occasions spanning two academic years.

Participants' age at Time 1 ranged from under 15 to over 20 years, with the majority being 16 years old. Students were enrolled in three vocational tracks: MELEC (electricity, industrial systems and maintenance; N= 2,441, 1.76% females), ASSP (nursing and care; N= 3,454, 88.85% females), and COMMERCE (sales, promoting and managing retail space; N= 4,268, 49.39 % females). Given the substantial variation in gender composition across vocational tracks, educational track and gender were accounted for in the analyses.

Socio-economic status (SES) of participants was assessed using the highest level of parental education, with majority of participants coming from families with secondary education (~62%): "Lower-secondary certificate/completed lower secondary education" (6.4%), "Short vocational upper-secondary qualification" (25.1%), and "Upper-secondary school-leaving qualification" (31.12%). Parents of one third of participants (~30%) had higher education: "Short-cycle tertiary degree/diploma" (19.24%), and "University or other higher-education degree" (11.08%). 7.06% of parents reported that they had "no diploma".

The sample size of the current study is comparable (N~10,000) to previous research using the planned Random Intercept Cross-Lag Panel Models analysis (see below) with three-wave designs (Núñez-Regueiro et al., 2026; Speyer et al., 2023). However, these studies used bivariate rather than trivariate models -as in the current study. The studies that utilized trivariate models generally used smaller samples (Kristensen et al., 2023; Marsh et al., 2022; Nobakht et al., 2025).

Thus, we deemed our sample sufficient to test our hypotheses.

Procedure

The present study uses data collected at three time points: Time 1 (September-October, beginning of the school year, Grade 11), Time 2 (end of Grade 11), and Time 3 (end of Grade 12). Time 1 data were collected prior to the implementation of the experimental instructional conditions in classrooms. The lag between T1 and T2 (~7.4 months) was comparable to the lag between T2 and T3 (~9.4 months).

The ProFAN experimentation was commissioned and authorized by the French Ministry of Education under an official ministerial decree (Arrêté du 21 mars 2017). Formal participation agreements were signed between each participating school and the corresponding Academic Directorate (Rectorat). All data were anonymized prior to analysis, and data collection and processing complied with French data-protection regulations, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Students completed an online survey in school computer rooms, containing multiple scales, a subset of which is analyzed in the current study (see below).

Measures

The study included measures of achievement goals, error sensitivity, self-perceived ability in French and mathematics, collected over three time points. In addition, students provided selfreported grades in these two subjects at Time 1. All measures were adapted to French, validated and demonstrated sufficient reliability in previous research as well as in the current sample. Table 1 provides an overview of the measures, including the number of items, example items, and reliability coefficients at Time 1.

Analytical strategy and data preprocessing

To investigate the longitudinal links among achievement goals, error sensitivity, and selfperceived academic ability measured, we estimated classical Cross-Lagged Panel Models (CLPMs) and more advanced Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models (RI-CLPM) across three time points.

CLPM allows to test directional (quasi-causal) hypotheses by estimating whether a variable X predicts later changes in Y above and beyond prior levels of Y, while simultaneously testing the reverse path (Lüdtke & Robitzsch, 2022; Selig & Little, 2012). The lagged paths are essential because they impose temporal precedence and control for autoregressive stability, allowing the cross-lagged coefficients to approximate prospective influence rather than mere contemporaneous association.

Given ongoing methodological discussions regarding limitations of traditional Cross-Lagged Panel Models, including their inability to disentangle stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations over time (e.g., Hamaker et al., 2015; Orth et al., 2021), we additionally estimated Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models (RI-CLPMs; Hamaker et al., 2015). RI-CLPMs separate stable between-person variance from within-person deviations around individuals' expected levels, thereby allowing a more precise examination of temporal withinperson processes. Consequently, the estimated autoregressive and cross-lagged effects reflect whether changes in one variable relative to an individual's typical level predict subsequent changes in another variable, rather than conflating these dynamics with stable differences between individuals. The size of lagged effects is typically evaluated using standardized coefficients, confidence intervals, and explained variance, interpreted relative to autoregressive paths and emerging empirical benchmarks (e.g., small ≈ .03, medium ≈ .07, large ≈ .12), alongside robustness checks across alternative models (Orth et al., 2024).

In order to use CLPMs and RI-CLPMs analyses, we first assessed missing data, ensured measurement invariance across time points and deleted outliers.

Missing data

Attrition rates ranged from 13.45% to 13.52% between T1 and T2 and from 16.74% to 16.77% between T2 and T3, depending on the variables (see Table S3 in SOM). Little's MCAR test with all 3 timepoints suggested data were not missing completely at random (MCAR). Therefore, analyses relied on MAR-robust estimation with auxiliary variables. Logistic regression analyses showed that Gender, Educational track, T1_Math_grade, T1_French grade, and Experimental Condition significantly predicted missingness at T2 and T3 (p<.05), and, thus, were included in the SEM models (model 3 and model 4) to account for systematic missingness. Missing data were handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML), which treats missing data by fitting the model to all non-missing data for each observation. It yields the least biased and most reliable estimates (Graham et al., 2007; Peugh & Enders, 2004). All statistics reported in this article were estimated using FIML.

Measurement invariance

Prior to fitting the cross-lag models, longitudinal measurement invariance (MI) was tested for all the measures across time. MI was assessed for the four achievement goals (Mastery Avoidance, Mastery approach, Performance avoidance and Performance Approach), self-perceived ability in Maths and French and Error sensitivity, by using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), following established procedure (Mõttus et al., 2015). Analyses were conducted on a subsample of 9064 participants with sufficient data across time points. Overall, scalar invariance was supported for the models of Performance Approach, with only partial scalar invariance supported for the remaining constructs (see details in SOM Table S1).

Outliers

Outliers were deleted using the interquartile range (IQR) criterion: [25 th percentile] -1.5 × IQR and [75 th percentile] + 1.5 × IQR (McGill et al., 1978). The number of outliers was quite significant for some variables (around 10%, see thought a simulation paper that such situation could be normal in applied contexts; Reimann et al., 2005), with the highest for two variables at T2 -Mastery approach (~11%) and Mastery avoidance (~10%). Table S2 in SOM presents exact numbers for each variable. This could be related with the nature of the sample, which comprises vocational students who usually show lower academic motivation and disengagement (see discussion in Likhanov et al., 2025). After outliers' deletion, all variables met the normality assumption. Skewness and kurtosis were in acceptable range (<2; (George & Mallery, 1998).

Sensitivity analyses

A series of sensitivity analyses was conducted to assess the robustness of the main RI-CLPM findings to experimental condition, cohort, and the exclusion of outliers.

Accounting for experimental condition in RI-CLPM

RI-CLPM model 4 tested whether accounting for experimental condition through a multigroup specification of the model would improve fit (see e.g., Muthén et al., 2024). The multigroup specification did not improve model fit (showed better RMSEA, but worse CFI compared with model 3), and no meaningful differences in the magnitude of the path coefficients were observed across conditions.

The overall pattern of associations was therefore considered robust across experimental conditions. Although some paths reached statistical significance in only one or two groups, these variations were not interpreted as evidence of between-group differences because the corresponding coefficients did not differ meaningfully in magnitude. Complete multigroup results are reported in Tables S14-S17 in the SOM.

Accounting for Cohort in RI-CLPM

Although the cohorts differed in the absolute mean scores of some study variables (e.g., Error sensitivity at T1 and three of the four achievement goals at T3; See Table S18 in SOM), adding Cohort as a clustering variable changed the fit indices (RMSEA increased, CFI decreased in all 4 traits), but the model did not convert properly for neither of 4 Achievement goals (was not identified with the smallest eigenvalue smaller than zero). This may mean that the uncertainty around the estimates cannot be estimated properly by lavaan, usually because some parameters are redundant, highly dependent, at a boundary, or not uniquely identified. This allows the lavaan to calculate path coefficients from the candidate solution, the model discrepancy function and fit indices (e.g., χ², RMSEA, and CFI) but does not allow to obtain a valid information matrix and trustworthy standard errors. Despite that, the path coefficients did not change compared to RI-CLPM model 3 in full sample and did not differ as a function of Cohort. Results on this analysis is available on request.

Full sample vs. Outliers deleted sample

The main RI-CLPM analyses were conducted after excluding outliers. To address the robustness of the findings, model 3 was reestimated in the full sample, including outliers. The results remained unchanged across all the four achievement goals, indicating that the findings were robust to the inclusions of outliers. Detailed results are available from authors on request.

Power simulation

In addition, we conducted a Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis using the powRICLPM package (Mulder, 2023) to estimate power at the achieved analytic sample size. Given the package's bivariate scope and the same sample size for pairs withing a trivariate model, we ran a three-wave RI-CLPM simulation for the one focal pair -achievement goals with error sensitivity -evaluating the cross-lagged paths in both directions under the specified parameter assumptions of Model 2 (since the package cannot account for exogenous variables). These pairwise analyses provide approximate sensitivity estimates and do not represent power for the full trivariate model.

At the achieved analytic sample size (N = 9,676), three waves, and α = .05, Monte Carlo simulations with 2,000 replications estimated power to detect a standardized cross-lagged weak effect of β = .03. For error sensitivity → achievement goals, estimated power was .858 at ICC = .30 and .782 at ICC = .50; for achievement goals → error sensitivity, power was .867 and .789, respectively. When restricting to medium effect of β = .07, for error sensitivity → achievement goals, estimated power was .998 at ICC = .30 and .985 at ICC = .50; for achievement goals → error sensitivity, power was .999 and .985, respectively.

Discussion

The current study examined the directionality of connections among the four achievement goals, self-perceived ability, and error sensitivity in a large sample of vocational education students.

RI-CLPM results revealed a clear distinction between between-person associations and prospective

within-person variations. At the between-person level, error sensitivity, self-perceived academic ability, and achievement goals were strongly associated. At the within-person level, however, prospective associations were small, mostly unidirectional, and did not support a general reciprocal cycle between error sensitivity and achievement goals. Specifically, positive within-person variations in prior error sensitivity and self-perceived ability predicted modest subsequent increases in mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, and performance-approach goals. These three goals did not, in turn, predict subsequent changes in error sensitivity or self-perceived academic ability.

Performance-avoidance goals showed a different pattern: they were predicted by neither prior error sensitivity nor self-perceived ability, but they predicted a small subsequent decrease in error sensitivity.

Contrasting conclusions from RI-CLPM and traditional CLPM

A noteworthy finding concerns the different conclusions produced by traditional CLPMs and RI-CLPM. Traditional CLPMs showed positive reciprocal links between achievement goals and error sensitivity. However, these reciprocal paths disappeared in the RI-CLPM after stable between-person differences were separated from within-person variations. This discrepancy suggests that the apparent reciprocal links identified by the traditional CLPMs probably reflect differences between students (that were captured by relatively high correlations between random intercepts in RI-CLPM), rather than "true" causal effects from on time point to another (see Hamaker et al., 2015 for discussion of such effects). Importantly, the traditional CLPM results were theoretically plausible and could be interpreted as reflecting a self-reinforcing cycle in which heightened sensitivity to errors strengthens goal striving, which, in turn, amplifies emotional reactions to errors (Daniels et al., 2009). The RI-CLPM results indicated that this theoretically compelling interpretation was not supported at the within-person level. This illustrates the importance of distinguishing between-person differences from within-person variations before drawing conclusions about reciprocal longitudinal associations. For this reason, the following discussion is based on the RI-CLPM results.

Between-person links between achievement goals, error sensitivity and self-perceived ability

The strong positive between-person associations between the four achievement goals and error sensitivity are consistent with prior cross-sectional research. Endorsing achievement goals, especially avoidance-oriented goals for which the associations with error sensitivity were stronger (.57-.69 vs. .45-.53 for approach goals -partly supporting prediction of RQ5), may increase the perceived stakes of success and failure, thereby making errors more salient and emotionally charged (e.g., Giel et al., 2020). Conversely, students who are highly sensitive to errors may adopt multiple achievement goals as strategies for coping with the possibility of failure. Self-perceived ability correlated with most goals weakly and positively (.04-.17 -highest correlation with mastery approach), with only exception being mastery avoidance goals for which correlation was negative (-.07); and with error sensitivity negatively and weakly (-.08).

From a perfectionism perspective, these associations suggest that error sensitivity may be embedded in a broader dispositional system involving chronic self-evaluation, perceived competence, and standards for defining success and failure (see clusters resulted from network analysis in; Likhanov, Fillon, et al., 2026). For example, concern over mistakes has been shown to reflect not only dislike of errors but also a tendency to equate them with personal failure and anticipated negative judgment from others (Smith et al., 2022a). Self-perceived academic ability may constitute another component of this system, as academic self-concept covaries with motivation and performance over time. Thus, at the between-person level, it is theoretically plausible that students who generally feel more competent, but are also more vigilant about mistakes, would report stronger and more differentiated achievement-goal profiles than peers with lower standing on these constructs (e.g., Steinberg et al., 2024). Such a pattern also suggests that concerns over mistakes, academic self-beliefs, and goal orientations may cluster together in meaningful ways. For example, perfectionistic profiles characterized by stronger evaluative concerns, especially when combined with family pressure, have been associated with greater endorsement of performance-avoidance and work-avoidance goals. In contrast, high standards without the same level of external pressure aligned more with mastery-approach goals and stronger academic performance (Fang & Sotardi, 2026). Future research needs to investigate whether distinct profiles combine error sensitivity, approach vs. avoidance motivations, and self-perceived ability, and how these profiles develop over time (see e.g., recent study on AG profiles, Fiévé et al., 2025).

Within-person associations between achievement goals, error sensitivity and self-perceived ability

Although the within-person effects were much smaller than the between-person associations, they are theoretically quite informative and supplement the results above by suggesting some directionality to the links discussed above. First, higher prior error sensitivity predicted modest subsequent increases in mastery-avoidance, mastery-approach, and performance-approach goals.

Thus, changes in students' sensitivity to errors were followed by small changes in their motivation to develop competence, avoid failing to master a task, or demonstrate competence relative to others, suggesting that higher error sensitivity may not be purely debilitating -contradicting our prediction on higher error sensitivity leading to decrease in approach oriented motivations (RQ1); supporting the claim that error sensitivity will contribute to avoidance-oriented motivations (RQ2) and contradicting our prediction of higher levels of motivation (particularly performance-approach) leading to heightened sensitivity to error (RQ3).

This interpretation, however, fits contemporary perfectionism theory, which distinguishes high standards from maladaptive evaluative concerns while recognizing that the two often co-occur in students (Smith et al., 2022b). When a student becomes temporarily more alert to the consequences of error, that state may activate a wish both to improve the self and to demonstrate competence, especially when this still feels controllable. Recent research similarly indicates that personal standards and perfectionistic concerns can jointly shape mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, and performance-approach goals through distinct pathways (e.g., Cecalupo et al., 2026). In this sense, heightened error sensitivity may function as a short-term catalyst for intensified striving, even if its long-run costs are substantial.

It should be noted that the changes in error sensitivity were followed only by modest changes in achievement goals, and the reverse paths were not supported (unlike in regular CLPM). Such small effects are expected in RI-CLPM logic (Hamaker et al., 2015): once stable individual differences are partialled out, the remaining wave-to-wave deviations in motivational states are usually subtle.

Experience-sampling research also supports these results by showing that achievement goals are indeed dynamically adjustable in the short run, but retain substantial overall stability (Yu et al., 2025). In addition, these effects were almost similar for different goals -precluding discussion of larger susceptibility for change of a particular goal on avoidance vs. approach or mastery vs.

performance opposition, suggested by RQ4.

Second, self-perceived academic ability showed a similar asymmetric pattern. Higher prior selfperceived ability predicted subsequent increases in mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, and performance-approach goals (RQ6), whereas none of the four goals predicted subsequent changes in self-perceived ability. Feeling more competent may therefore increase students' willingness to invest in achievement and pursue a range of competence-related standards. This finding is broadly consistent with previous research showing that feeling capable promotes stronger endorsement of achievement goals, particularly approach-oriented goals (Ciani et al., 2011; Moller & Elliot, 2006; Seaton et al., 2014). It also aligns well with evidence that academic self-concept plays an important role in the relationship between achievement and goal orientations, even though goal orientations do not consistently mediate reciprocal self-concept-achievement relations (Steinberg et al., 2024).

The absence of reverse paths may reflect the high temporal stability of perceived competence and its strong dependence on prior achievement and evaluative feedback. Controlling for baseline competence, as in the present study, substantially reduces the variance available for achievement goals to explain subsequent changes in self-perceived ability (Marsh & Craven, 2006; Niepel et al., 2014).

A distinct pattern for performance-avoidance goals: a defensive mechanism against social comparison and stress in relation to errors?

Unlike the other three achievement goals, Performance-avoidance goals were predicted by neither prior error sensitivity nor prior self-perceived ability. More strikingly, performanceavoidance was the only achievement goal to predict subsequent error sensitivity (supporting prediction of RQ4): at the within-person level, higher prior performance-avoidance predicted a small decrease in subsequent error sensitivity. At first glance, this negative path seems counterintuitive. Given that performance-avoidance goals are generally associated with greater anxiety and internalizing symptoms, lower intrinsic motivation, and poorer achievement outcomes (Diaconu-Gherasim et al., 2024; Möcklinghoff et al., 2023), one might have expected stronger performance-avoidance to heighten students' vigilance towards mistakes and thereby increase, rather than decrease, subsequent error sensitivity.

One possible explanation is that increased performance-avoidance promotes defensive disengagement from error-related information. Because students high in performance-avoidance goals are motivated to avoid appearing incompetent, attending closely to their mistakes may expose them to precisely the evidence of low ability and negative evaluation that they seek to avoid. They may therefore reduce their vigilance towards errors, process them less deeply, or withdraw from evaluative situations in which such errors become salient. This interpretation is supported by several lines of research. For example, one of the classical studies on achievement goals showed that students high in performance-avoidance goals tend to rely more on surface-level and disorganized study strategies, rather than deep processing, which may make errors less likely to be used as informative feedback for learning (Elliot, 1999). Another study showed that fear of failure indirectly predicts procrastination through emotion-regulation difficulties, fitting the idea that avoidance can function as short-term relief from aversive self-evaluative states (Duru et al., 2024).

Likewise, evaluative-concerns perfectionism is associated with more avoidance and rumination after failure and with less acceptance of failure experiences (Van Der Kaap-Deeder et al., 2016).

Performance-avoidance goals have also been found to be closely tied to self-handicapping, a defensive strategy that protects self-worth by creating excuses for possible failure (Schwinger & Stiensmeier-Pelster, 2011). Because performance-avoidance goals are organized around avoiding the appearance of incompetence relative to others, such students may also disengage from errors and avoid evaluative situations or unfavorable social comparisons that could expose low ability (Darnon et al., 2007; Elliot & McGregor, 2001). This result is especially important for vocational education students, who often have a history of unfavorable social comparisons and an unsuccessful academic track record (see discussion in Likhanov et al., 2026).

Limitations

The current study has a number of limitations. First, the data were collected in the context of an educational intervention, which may have affected the variables and their longitudinal associations. However, experimental conditions showed negligible effects in previous studies (e.g., Riant et al., 2024) and were therefore statistically controlled -brining no meaningful differences in lagged paths in the current study. Second, the temporal design warrants consideration. The time intervals between measurement waves were unequal, which could have affected the magnitude of cross-lag effects. To address this, we equalized the cross-lag loadings, which resulted in improved model fit and suggested that the effects were stable across time. In addition, the study included three measurement occasions, the minimum required to estimate an RI-CLPM (Hamaker et al., 2015; Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). Although sufficient for model estimation, three waves provide only a limited representation of within-person dynamics and may not capture more transient or complex patterns of change. Another related issue is that Mathematics and French grades were available only at T1, precluding investigation of their cross-lag effects. Future longitudinal studies including more measurement occasions and repeated measures of achievement would allow a more detailed and stable characterization of these dynamics. Third, mathematics and French selfperceived ability scores were averaged to create a domain-general measure, consistent with the domain-general assessment of achievement goals and error sensitivity. However, this aggregation may have obscured domain-specific effects, which have been documented in prior research (e.g., Fiévé et al., 2025; Susperreguy et al., 2018). This loss of domain specificity might partly explain the absence of reciprocal associations between achievement goals and self-perceived academic ability. Fourth, the RI-CLPM analyses were based on total scale scores rather than item-level latent variables. This may have affected the estimates by ignoring measurement error and potential itemlevel heterogeneity. To address this concern, we applied a correction based on composite reliability at each wave, following (Kline, 2016). We also estimated item-level models, but they failed to fit adequately, likely due to their substantially greater complexity. Reliability-corrected composite scores therefore provided a reasonable compromise between accounting for measurement error and maintaining model parsimony. The within-person associations remained small, and their theoretical and practical significance should not be overstated. Effects of this magnitude are yet common when stable between-person differences are separated from within-person variations (Hamaker et al., 2015), but future studies using fully latent models are needed to obtain more precise estimates.

Confidence in the main findings is strengthened by the consistency of the overall pattern across achievement goals, experimental conditions, and analyses including versus excluding outliers.

Finally, gender and educational track were included as covariates, but their potential moderating effects were not examined due to unbalanced group compositions. This limits our ability to determine whether the longitudinal associations differ across these groups, despite previous evidence of moderation by gender and educational track (Likhanov, Fillon, et al., 2026).

Nevertheless, including both variables as covariates allowed the main estimates to account for mean-level differences associated with these group characteristics.

Methodological, theoretical, and practical implications

Despite these limitations, the present study has several important methodological, theoretical, and practical implications. At the methodological level, the contrast between traditional CLPMs and RI-CLPMs demonstrates that the choice of longitudinal model can substantially alter the conclusions drawn. Previous cross-sectional and two-wave studies may yield associations consistent with theoretically plausible bidirectional relations, and traditional CLPMs can further reinforce this interpretation by producing reciprocal cross-lagged paths. However, such converging evidence does not establish that these processes unfold within individuals, because these designs do not adequately separate stable between-person differences from within-person variations. The present findings illustrate the added value of the RI-CLPM: reciprocal associations between error sensitivity and achievement goals disappeared once these two sources of variance were distinguished. This methodological issue extends beyond the constructs examined here. Recent research on academic self-concept and achievement similarly shows that conclusions about reciprocal relations depend on separating stable and dynamic components and on aligning model specifications with the timescale of the underlying processes (Núñez-Regueiro et al., 2026). This does not imply that RI-CLPMs should invariably replace traditional CLPMs; rather, different models capture different sources and timescales of variation and should not be interpreted interchangeably (Hamaker et al., 2015; Núñez-Regueiro et al., 2026).

At the theoretical level, ruling out a general reciprocal within-person process changes how the relations among achievement goals, error sensitivity, and self-perceived academic ability should be conceptualized. Rather than forming a self-reinforcing motivational system, these constructs appear to follow an asymmetric organization in which perceived competence and sensitivity to errors contribute to subsequent goal endorsement, whereas there was no evidence that achievement goals directly predict subsequent changes in these beliefs and error-related responses.

One possible interpretation is that achievement goals influence perceived competence and error sensitivity only indirectly, through the experiences associated with goal pursuit, such as engagement, goal attainment, success or failure, and evaluative feedback (Arens et al., 2017; Hübner et al., 2025; Theobald et al., 2023). These potential pathways were not examined in the present study and should be tested in future longitudinal research. The contrast between performance-avoidance and the other three goals also suggests a possible refinement of the 2 × 2 achievement-goal framework. Although mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, and performanceapproach goals occupy different positions within this framework, they showed the same general pattern of longitudinal associations, possibly because they all involve continued investment in attaining, maintaining, or demonstrating competence. Performance-avoidance goals showed a distinct pattern and may instead involve protecting the self from evidence of incompetence by disengaging from errors and threatening evaluative information. Achievement-goal theory may therefore benefit from considering not only how competence is defined and valenced, but also whether students remain actively engaged with competence-relevant information or defensively withdraw from it.

These theoretical implications, in turn, inform several practical recommendations. Because self-perceived academic ability preceded stronger endorsement of three achievement goals, teachers may support students' motivation by creating credible experiences of competence through appropriately challenging tasks, progressive scaffolding, opportunities to improve, and feedback that makes individual progress visible (Belland et al., 2013; Jang et al., 2010; Ryan & Deci, 2020).

However, strengthening perceived competence should not be expected, by itself, to produce more constructive responses to errors. Similarly, promoting adaptive achievement goals-even masteryapproach goals-may benefit engagement and learning but is unlikely to be sufficient to change how students respond to mistakes. Error processing should therefore be addressed directly.

Teachers can ask students to identify the source of an error, explain the reasoning that produced it, compare alternative strategies, correct their response, and apply what they have learned in a subsequent attempt. At the same time, they should reduce classroom conditions that foster performance-avoidance, including public social comparison, excessive emphasis on normative evaluation, and feedback that presents errors as indicators of low ability. Framing errors as informative and revisable and providing feedback privately when appropriate may help students remain engaged with potentially threatening information. These practices could reduce defensive disengagement without encouraging indifference to mistakes, while supporting both students' sense of competence and their constructive use of errors (Steuer et al., 2022; Tulis & Dresel, 2025). Given the small longitudinal effects and observational design of the study, these recommendations should nevertheless be tested directly in classroom interventions.

Conclusion

The present study investigated the longitudinal associations among achievement goals, error sensitivity, and self-perceived academic ability. The findings from traditional CLPM suggested that they form a reciprocal, self-reinforcing motivational system. However, once stable differences between persons were separated from within-person variations in RI-CLPM, their longitudinal relations proved selective and asymmetric, with performance-avoidance showing a particularly distinct pattern. More broadly, adaptive motivation cannot be inferred from stronger goal endorsement or lower error sensitivity alone, but should be understood in terms of students' continued and constructive engagement with competence-relevant feedback.
