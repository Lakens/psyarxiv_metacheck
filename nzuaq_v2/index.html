Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Motivation shapes how people allocate cognitive control, including whether they persist with a current task or flexibly switch to another. Although reward transitions have been shown to influence this balance, prior work has largely relied on monetary incentives. We tested whether changes in appetitive food reward motivate voluntary task switching, and whether this effect varies with subjective reward valuation and eating-related traits. A total of 112 undergraduate participants completed a voluntary task-switching task in which cues signaled low (1 point) or high (10 points) rewards exchangeable for candy. Access to the candy rewards was manipulated between subjects, with a free access group and a restricted group that could see, but not access, the received candy rewards. Trial-level choices were analyzed with generalized linear mixed models. Participants switched tasks more often when reward value changed between trials than when it remained consistently low or high. This reward-transition effect was larger among participants reporting greater candy liking and wanting. It was also stronger among those more susceptible to external food cues and weaker among those reporting greater routine restraint. Candy-access condition and progress toward the next candy did not improve model fit in the primary choice model. These findings identify reward dynamics as a motivational influence on cognitive flexibility and extend reward-transition accounts of control allocation to primary food incentives. Individual differences in incentive valuation and eating-related tendencies further shape how reward cues guide flexible behavior.

Appetitive Food Rewards and Cognitive Flexibility: Reward Transitions, Voluntary Task Switching, and Individual Differences

Cognitive control is essential for healthy, goal-directed functioning in daily life, enabling people to stay on task when distractions arise while also adapting when circumstances change (Diamond, 2013). This capacity reflects a dynamic interplay between cognitive stability and cognitive flexibility (Musslick & Cohen, 2021). Cognitive stability is characterized by the capability to maintain goals and task-relevant information while averting distractions, whereas cognitive flexibility encompasses the ability to engage in innovative thinking, redirect attention, and alternate between tasks in response to fluctuating environmental requirements (Banich, 2009; Egner, 2023; Fröber et al., 2018).

Although both processes are crucial, cognitive flexibility seemingly imposes significantly greater cognitive demands than maintaining stability: response times are increased for the same task when participants have to mix two or more tasks opposed to performing single task blocks, there are performance costs for switching tasks compared to repeating tasks (Monsell, 2003; Rogers & Monsell, 1995; Wylie & Allport, 2000), and when given the choice to repeat or switch tasks, participants are more likely to choose to repeat the same task (Arrington & Logan, 2004 , 2005; Mayr & Bell, 2006). Given these substantial cognitive costs, task switching is often considered aversive (Dreisbach & Mendl, 2024). Indeed, when asked to rate cues that were used to indicate switching versus repeating, participant report a greater dislike for switch cues (Vermeylen et al., 2022). However, other data shows that objective costs, i.e., the greater temporal cost of slower responses on switch vs. repeat trials, may better explain choice behavior compared to objective costs (Dreisbach & Jurczyk, 2021).

This aversion to task switching can be understood through the lens of cost-benefit analysis. Before engaging in any task or decision, individuals naturally weigh the required effort against anticipated rewards (Croxson et al., 2009; Kool et al., 2010; Shenhav et al., 2013). Indeed, cognitive effort is treated as an intrinsic cost that people are motivated to avoid, such that they will forgo rewards to select less demanding courses of action (Kool et al., 2010). This principle is formalized in the Expected Value of Control (EVC) framework, which proposes that the brain-particularly the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex-computes whether to allocate cognitive control by integrating the anticipated payoff of a control-demanding action against its intrinsic effort cost (Shenhav et al., 2013).

When perceived effort outweighs potential benefits, people typically avoid engagement (Yee & Braver, 2018). However, motivational manipulations, particularly performance-contingent rewards, can fundamentally alter this calculation by increasing the expected value of exerting control. High reward expectations have been shown to reduce switch costs and accelerate reaction times in task-switching paradigms by enhancing proactive control mechanisms (Chiew & Braver, 2013; Yee & Braver, 2018). These effects elicit pupil dilation in response to increased cognitive effort and engagement, with the magnitude of enhancement modulated by individual differences in reward and punishment sensitivity (Chiew & Braver, 2014). Thus, despite the inherent cognitive costs of task switching, the prospect of reward can motivate individuals to overcome their natural tendency toward cognitive stability and engage in more demanding flexible behavior.

An emerging body of work has shown that trial-to-trial dynamics of reward can differentially bias participants to stability or flexibility depending on the context. With dynamic rewards (increases or decreases), participants are more likely to engage in flexibility with reduced switch costs and/ or a greater proportion of voluntary switches; when rewards remain stable, especially when they remain high, participants are more likely to engage in stability (Balcazar & Orr, 2025; Fröber & Dreisbach, 2016; Fröber et al., 2018; Shen & Chun, 2011). Such reward dynamics may provide an important meta-control signal to mediate the balance between stability and flexibility (Fröber & Dreisbach, 2021).

Given that these effects of reward dynamics are contingent on participants' experienced reward value, it is necessary to examine the characteristics of the incentives used. Reward valuation is intrinsically subjective and may be attributed to any stimulus that has innate or learned positive value (Schultz et al., 1997). The aforementioned studies on reward transitions relied predominantly on monetary incentives, supplemented by social-competitive reward contingencies (Fröber & Dreisbach, 2016 , 2021; Shen & Chun, 2011). While secondary rewards such as these are easy to quantify and manipulate parametrically (Elliott et al., 2003), there are important differences between abstract secondary incentives and biologically relevant primary incentives such as food (Beck et al., 2010; Rosati & Hare, 2016). Monetary rewards-particularly checks or gift cards that constrain when and where they can be redeemed-are comparatively abstract: although adults may know their nominal value, the concrete benefit depends on how (and whether) they are ultimately spent, and redemption typically requires additional time and effort. By contrast, food has intrinsic value. At the same time, money rewards tend to be more broadly transferable across contexts, while food rewards are shaped by hunger and satiety levels, as well as individual preferences (Banica et al., 2023; Simon et al., 2017).

The present study investigated the extent to which appetitive food rewards modulate the trade-off between cognitive stability and cognitive flexibility. To implement dynamic reward transitions while minimizing the potential confounding effects of satiety-related devaluation, each trial could yield 1 or 10 points, and participants received a piece of candy for every 50 accumulated points. A progress bar displayed continuously at the bottom of the screen provided online feedback on the proximity to the next candy delivery and was reset after each dispensation. Participants selected their preferred candy type (chocolate M&M's (Mars, Incorporated) or Original Skittles (Mars, Incorporated) fruit candies) prior to task performance. To dissociate the hedonic valuation ("liking") from incentive motivation ("wanting") (Morales & Berridge, 2020; Robinson & Berridge, 1993 , 2025), the participants provided repeated ratings of (a) how much they liked the candy they had received and (b) the degree to which they wanted additional candy.

Method Participants

A sample of 140 participants completed the study procedure. These participants were recruited from the Texas A&M University Psychology Subject Pool. The final sample included 127 participants after removing participants with poor performance or dispenser malfunctions, as described below. The final sample included a majority of female participants, between 19 and 22 years old; 49.1% of the participants identified themselves as non-White (see Table 1). All participants provided their informed consent prior to starting the study and received course credit for their participation. The study was determined to be exempt by the Texas A&M University Institutional Review Board, as it involved only benign behavioral interventions in accordance with the Revised Common Rule.

Materials and Stimuli

Before starting the experimental tasks, the participants completed several self-report questionnaires, including a demographic survey, the PROMIS Alcohol Use Item Bank (Pilkonis et al., 2013), the PROMIS E-cigarette Nicotine Dependence Item Bank (Hansen et al., 2014), the PROMIS Substance Use Severity Short Form (Pilkonis et al., 2013), a 24-hour food log, and the Weight-Related Eating Questionnaire (Schembre et al., 2009, WREQ). The WREQ includes four subscales that measure how individuals control their eating: Routine Restraint (WREQ-RR), the deliberate restriction of food intake to manage weight; Compensatory Restraint (WREQ-CR), overeating followed by restraint to compensate; Susceptibility to Extenal Cues (WREQ-EX), eating in response to external cues regardless of internal hunger or satiety signals; and Emotional Eating (WREQ-EE), eating in response to negative emotions. In addition, participants completed the Behavioral Inhibition and Behavioral Activation Systems (BIS/BAS) Scales (Carver & White, 1994), a 24-item self-report questionnaire that assesses individual differences in sensitivity to punishment and reward, with four subscales: BIS, sensitivity to punishment; BAS-Reward Responsiveness (RR), sensitivity to rewards; BAS-Fun Seeking (Fun), impulsive willingness to approach potentially rewarding events; BAS-Drive, persistent pursuit of desired goals.

The experiment was programmed in PsychoPy (Peirce et al., 2019 (Peirce et al., , p. v. 2025.1) .1) and presented on a 22 inch LCD monitor. Before completing the experimental task, participants completed a training phase designed to familiarize them with the reward values associated with the task cues. The cues were blue and orange circles, which were associated with either 1 or 10 points; the color-point mapping was counterbalanced across participants. On each training trial, one circle of each color was presented, with the circles positioned to the left and right of screen center (10 % of screen height from the center).

Each circle had a diameter equal to 8 % of screen height, and the left-right locations of the colors were randomized across trials. The pair of circles remained onscreen for up to 3,000 ms or until the participant selected one using the up or down arrow keys. Feedback indicating the number of points received was then displayed for 1,500 ms. If no response was made within 3,000 ms, participants were instructed to respond more quickly.

Cue-reward validity during training matched that of the main task, although rewards in the training phase were not contingent on participants' responses. Trials were separated by a 1,000-ms intertrial interval (ITI). Participants completed two blocks of 10 training trials.

Each trial in the main task followed the sequence shown in Figure 1: presentation of a centrally displayed reward cue (blue or orange circle; 1,500 ms), a target display, and feedback. The target display contained one digit (125, 132, 139, 146, 160, 167, 174, or 181) and one letter (B, D, F, H, S, U, W, or Y), presented above and below fixation, respectively, or vice versa. Targets were positioned 10 % of screen height from the center of the display and were presented in Arial font at a size equal to 5% of screen height. For the digit task, participants indicated whether the digit was less than or greater than 153. For the letter task, they indicated whether the letter was closer to the beginning or end of the alphabet. Participants responded to the upper stimulus using the left-hand keys ("D" and "F") and to the lower stimulus using the right-hand keys ("J" and "K"); the mapping of target position to response hand was counterbalanced across participants. The target display remained onscreen until a response was made or 3,000 ms had elapsed, after which feedback was displayed for 1,500 ms.

During both training and the reward portion of the experiment, a reward progress bar was continuously displayed at the bottom of the screen. The bar filled as participants earned points and reset after they accumulated 50 points, at which point a candy reward was dispensed. Before beginning the task, participants selected either chocolate M &M's (Mars, Incorporated) or Original Skittles (Mars, Incorporated) as their reward. Candy rewards were delivered using a modified MedAssociates ENV-702 food-pellet dispenser (Fairfax, VT, USA), positioned within arm's reach of the participant and modeled after the design described by Deweese et al. (2015). PsychoPy controlled the dispenser through a USB relay (ADU200; OnTrak Control Systems Inc., Canada). When participants accumulated 50 points, a TTL pulse triggered delivery of a single piece of candy simultaneously with the feedback display. Although the dispenser's infrared sensor was designed to prevent the release of multiple candies, it occasionally dispensed more than one piece; these occurrences were not monitored or recorded consistently. Candy was dispensed into a metal bowl lined with a paper coffee filter for hygiene. Although participants were instructed to maintain fixation on the screen, the audible sound of the candy entering the bowl indicated that a reward had been delivered.

As shown in Figure 2, the study included two candy-access conditions. In the Free Access condition, participants could view and access the candies they earned throughout the session, although they were instructed to wait until the next break before consuming them. In the Restricted Access condition, the dispenser was placed inside a previously concealed clear acrylic box with a locking lid after the training phase. Participants could view the earned candies but were informed that they would not be able to access them until the end of the experimental session.

Procedure

Following the training phase, but before starting the primary task, participants individually practiced the two tasks. They were given eight trials to reach 80% accuracy; if they did not meet criterion, the experimenter reviewed the instructions, confirmed understanding, and the practice was repeated. After successful practice of each task, participants practiced switching between the two tasks ("Try to perform a mix of both tasks and not just focusing on a single task"). Participants completed 16 trials and repeated this practice if they did not achieve at least 80% accuracy and/or if their switch rate fell outside 15%-85%; before each repeat, the experimenter again verified that the participant understood the task and emphasized balancing the two tasks. If a participant failed to meet criteria after three attempts in any practice phase, the session was terminated because persistent noncompliance was taken to indicate that the participant was not making a good-faith effort to perform the task.

In order to set reaction time thresholds for fast responses in reward trials, participants first completed 64 trials without performance contingencies and without rewards. Although the cues were presented, participants were instructed to ignore them.

No progress bar was shown, and the feedback contained information only about the accuracy of the response. From this block, an initial threshold of the 40th percentile was set for the rewarded blocks. Separate reaction time thresholds were calculated for each task and task transition (repeat or switching). The thresholds were updated after each block, based on all cumulative correct trials. On error trials, the feedback said "Error," and on trials in which a response was not made within 3000 ms, it said "Please respond!!".

Participants completed 8 blocks of 32 trials during the reward phase. In line with studies by Fröber, Dreissbach, and colleagues (Fröber & Dreisbach, 2016; Fröber et al., 2018), for 1-point trials, there was no speed requirement to receive the point, only an accuracy requirement. For 10-point trials, points were given only if the response was correct and faster than the speed threshold. If the response was correct, but not fast enough, the participant received 0 points and feedback that said "Respond Faster!". If participants made an error or did not respond within 3000 ms, they lost 5 points, and the progress bar receded.

Before the training phase, the dispenser was partially filled with the candy that the participants chose. They were asked to indicate on a continuous slider how much they liked the candy they chose. The slider contained 5 tick marks, and the beginning and end were anchored by "Not At All" and "A Lot", respectively. Then they were asked to indicate how much they wanted to eat the candy, using the same slider. After each block, they were asked how much they wanted additional candy, and, in the Free access version, they were also asked how much they liked the candy they just received.

Analysis

All analyses were carried out in R (R Core Team, 2026). Voluntary task choice, i.e. the decision to repeat or switch tasks, as well as reaction times, were analyzed using (generalized) linear mixed models using trial-level data. Accuracy was not analyzed because the performance was near ceiling (Median = 0.972, IQR = 0.031).

To examine task switch proportions, we used a generalized linear mixed model with task transition (repeat: 0, switch: 1) as a binary dependent measure and a binomial distribution family with a logit link function. We evaluated multiple models to determine which provided the best fit. Each model examined the effect of reward transition, i.e., the interaction between the previous trial's reward outcome (hereafter referred to as 'previous trial reward') and the current trial's reward prospect (hereafter referred to as 'current trial reward'). We also evaluated additive and interactive terms for the candy access version (free vs. restricted) and a continuous indicator of how many points remained until the next candy reward (i.e., reward progress). Reward progress was included to assess whether participant's behavior was affected by proximity to the goal, i.e. candy receipt. Model fits were compared using the Akaike Information Criteria corrected for small sample bias and likelihood ratio tests to identify significant differences. For each model, we identified the most parsimonious random-effects structure following recommendations by Bates et al. (Bates et al., 2018) beginning with a maximal structure and simplifying it when convergence problems occurred. We included the block number as a random factor to account for the variability throughout the duration of the experiment, such as fatigue and satiety for the candy rewards.

To examine whether the reward transition effect was moderated by how much the participant 1) liked the candy rewards and 2) wanted more candy, we ran separate analyses with these ratings. Because participants were unable to eat candy during the restricted access version, we restricted the analysis of the liking ratings to the free access version. For the analysis of the wanting ratings, we evaluated whether treating candy access version as an additive and interactive term best fit the data. To examine individual differences, we performed separate analyses that included responses to self-report questionnaires (demographics, substance use, BIS-BAS, and the Weight-Related Eating Questionnaire).

We only included those who self-reported male and female gender, excluding those who identified as non-binary or transgender due to low response numbers. Gender was also coded as a sum contrast. Because the BIS-BAS and the Weight-Related Eating Questionnaire subscales were correlated, we performed a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce complexity and collinearity (see details below). To examine individual differences in reward-guided cognitive flexibility, separate models were fit for each of the PCA component scores. In order to interpret models where PCA scores had a significant contribution, we compared models with a given PCA score included as an additive factor or an interactive factor.

As part of data cleaning procedures, we considered descriptive statistics for reaction time, accuracy, task choice (number or letter), and probability of switching tasks, excluding participants with values marked as extreme outliers through the boxplot method 1 . This resulted in the removal of one participant due to low accuracy (56%) and nine due to mainly performing only a single task (> 80% of the trials). An additional four participants were excluded due to the candy dispenser jamming or otherwise not dispensing the candies. This resulted in a final sample of 127 participants In addition to removing participants classified as outliers, trials with reaction times less than 200 ms, error trials, and trials following errors were removed prior to analysis. This resulted in the loss of 6.52% of the trials.

The models were fit using lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), with indices of model quality and goodness of fit assessed with the performance package (Lüdecke et al., 2021).

Model-estimated average marginal estimates and post-hoc hypothesis testing was performed using the marginaleffects package (Arel-Bundock et al., 2024). Unless otherwise mentioned, multiple comparisons were corrected using the Holm method.

Results

Questionnaires -Principal Component Analysis

To facilitate the interpretation of individual differences and reduce collinearity between the eight BIS-BAS and WREQ subscale scores, PCA-derived component scores were examined. Based on the scree plot and cumulative variance explained (approximately 80%), four components were retained (see Supplemental Table A1 for factor loadings). The first component (PC1) showed moderate positive loadings on nearly all subscales except BIS, suggesting a general tendency toward increased motivational responsiveness. The second component (PC2) was characterized by moderate negative loadings on the BAS subscales, particularly Reward Responsiveness, and moderate positive loadings on the WREQ subscales, particularly Routine Restraint and Emotional Eating, reflecting greater responsiveness to eating-related and affective influences alongside lower responsiveness to rewarding outcomes. The third component (PC3) was defined by a strong positive loading on BIS, reflecting a greater sensitivity to potential negative outcomes. The fourth component (PC4) exhibited moderately strong positive loadings on Routine Restraint (WREQ-RR) and moderately strong negative loadings on Susceptibility to External Cues (WREQ-EX), indicating a tendency to restrict food intake rather than respond to external food cues in the absence of internal hunger or satiety signals. Subsequently, these component scores were used to examine whether individual differences in motivational and eating-related tendencies moderated the probability of the switch (see below).

Choice Behavior

The best fitting model for switch probability did not contain interactive terms for candy access version or reward progress. The model parameters are reported in Table 2 and the model comparisons are reported in Supplemental Table A2. There was a significant two-way interaction of previous reward outcome and current reward prospect. This indicates that the effect of current reward prospect on the odds of switching depended on the previous reward outcome. Specifically, the odds ratio for current high versus low reward was 44% lower following a high previous reward than following a low previous reward. As shown in Figure 3, and supported by a planned contrast of changing reward versus consistent reward, participants switched tasks 20.1% more often when the reward increased or decreased than when the reward was consistently low or high. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons found significant differences between all four reward transitions, although the difference between decreasing and increasing reward (hi.Lo -lo.Hi, ∆p = 0.036) and the difference between consistent high and consistent low reward (hi_Hi -lo_Lo, ∆p = -0.054) were small. The pairwise comparisons are reported in Supplemental Table A3

Liking and Wanting Ratings

We then examined how the degree to which participants reported liking the candy and wanting more candy influenced switch behavior. Due to the procedure of collecting participants' self-reported levels of liking and wanting prior to each experimental block, there is variability in these ratings both within and between participants. This variability is adequately addressed using a hierarchical mixed model, as implemented in the present study. Two separate models were run with like and want ratings (see Supplemental Tables A4 and A5 for model fit comparisons for like and want models, respectively). Because participants were restricted from eating the candy in the restricted candy access version, the analysis of like ratings only used the free candy access version data. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, there were significant three-way interactions for previous and current reward and both the like and the want ratings. For want ratings, an additional four-way interaction was observed among previous and current trial reward, scaled want ratings, and candy access version. As shown in Figure 4, a higher liking/ wanting of the candy reward was associated with a larger reward transition effect than a lower liking/ wanting. To further examine these effects, we compared the reward transition effect between low and high (±1 SD) like/ want ratings. The reward transition effect was significant at both levels of like and want ratings, but larger for high than for low like and want ratings, as reported in Supplemental Table A6. The reward transition effect was qualitatively similar for free and restricted candy access versions, as shown in Supplemental Figure B1. Within each combination of want rating and candy access version, the reward transition effect was significant, while the difference between high and low wanting was significant in the free version, but not in the restricted version (see Supplemental Table A7 for detailed statistics of the comparisons).

Trait Level Measures

We were also interested in how individual differences captured by the BIS-BAS and WREQ subscales moderated the effect of reward transition. In separate analyses, we included factor loadings for PC1, PC2, PC3, and PC4 in the model of the main analysis described above. Model fit comparisons for each of these analyses are reported in Supplementary Table A8. A moderating effect of the individual difference measure was only present for PC4; the full parameter estimates for the PC4 model are reported in Table 5. The PC4 component was characterized by a positive loading of routine restraint in eating (WREQ-RR) and a negative loading of cue-driven eating (WREQ-EX). As shown in Figure 5, the reward transition effect was greatest at low levels of PC4 and smallest at high levels of PC4. To interpret this finding in terms of the original variables, we derived predicted probabilities from the fitted model by substituting implied PC4 scores. For each variable (WREQ-RR, WREQ-EX), we computed the PC4 score that would result from that variable being at -1SD (Low) , 0SD (Average), or +1SD (High) while holding the other two variables at their mean (0 after scaling). These implied PC4 scores were entered into the original model to generate predicted switch probabilities across all combinations of previous reward and current reward, marginalized over candy access version. This approach allows the contribution of each variable to the three-way interaction to be examined without fitting additional models, while respecting the correlational structure that motivated the PCA. The predicted switch probabilities for the three subscales are shown in Supplemental Figures B2 and B3, and the comparisons of the reward transition effect within and between the levels of each subscale are reported in Supplemental Table 6.

The implied scores for WREQ-RR had a negative influence on the magnitude of the reward transition effect, with smaller reward transition effects at high than at average or low levels, as well as a smaller effect at the average level than at the low level, although the reward transition effect was large within each level (0.454-0.557 ). The pattern was in the opposing direction for WREQ-EX, with a larger reward transition effect at high than at average or low levels, as well as a larger effect at the average level than at the low level.

Reaction Time

For the reaction time analysis, the full model with interactive terms of previous trial reward outcome, current trial reward prospect, candy access version, and reward progress had the best fit (see Supplemental Table A9). As shown in Supplemental Table A10, candy access version did not interact with the reward factor. However, there was a four-way interaction of task alternation, previous reward outcome, current reward prospect, and reward progress (β=0.33, 95% CI=[0.06, 0.59], z=2.43, p=0.015). As previous studies have found reduced switch costs with changing reward compared to consistent reward, we first sought to test if this pattern was replicated here. To simplify the visualization of switch cost for each combination of previous and current reward, we combined previous and current reward to a single factor, reward transition. As shown in Figure 6, and confirmed by post-hoc testing, the switch cost was lower with changing reward (Increase and Decrease) compared to consistent reward (Remain Low and Remain High; ∆RT = 63.4 ms, 95% CI = [43.1, 83.8]). Adding the effect of reward progress, reaction times immediately following candy receipt were especially slowed for consistent high reward trials on which participants switched tasks, as shown in Supplemental Figure B4. Finally, we evaluated whether reaction time effects were moderated by like or want ratings and the individual difference components, and although the full models with interactive factors of like and want ratings were the best fitting, the only significant effects involving individual differences were like (want) interacting with progress to candy reward. The parameters for these models are reported in Supplemental Tables A11 and A12. Finally, none of the models with interactive factors of the Principal Components had a better fit than the model without these factors.

Discussion

This study tested whether dynamic changes in point amounts-where one candy was earned for every 50 points-influence the trade-off between cognitive stability and flexibility, as previously shown with monetary incentives. Despite important differences between food and money, point transitions robustly altered control allocation: participants voluntarily switched tasks more often when point values changed than when they remained constant. This reward transition effect scaled with individual differences in reward sensitivity, including liking and wanting ratings and eating-related traits. Together, these results show that sensitivity to changing reward value plays an important role in reward-guided cognitive flexibility.

Reward transitions guide stability and flexibility

Previous work indicates that transitions in reward availability act as a meta-control signal regulating the stability-flexibility trade-off, with increased voluntary switching and reduced switch costs when reward changes relative to a consistent context. These effects are attenuated or absent under probabilistic reward schedules with cue-outcome validity below 80% (Balcazar & Orr, 2025; Orr et al., 2025). The present data extend this signal to a primary reward. A three-way interaction among task alternation, previous reward, and current reward showed that switch costs were reduced when reward levels changed relative to when they stayed the same, supporting our first hypothesis. Importantly, choice behavior mirrored this pattern: participants switched more when rewards changed, consistent with prior studies showing that changing reward structure encourages flexibility and exploration (Fröber & Dreisbach, 2016; Shen & Chun, 2011). Participants also switched less for repeated high rewards than for repeated low rewards, and more when rewards decreased than when they increased.

Two interpretations could help explain this pattern. First, reward transitions may signal environmental change, shifting control toward a more exploratory mode, as in adaptive gain theory (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005). Second, repeated high rewards may foster satisfaction that reduces the motivation to seek alternatives, whereas a decrease in reward signals that the current strategy is suboptimal. A reduction in an expected reward can also produce frustrative non-reward, which may serve as a catalyst for behavioral adjustment (Abler et al., 2005; Amsel, 1992). This account fits evidence that bottom-up influences on task switching are typically limited to task-relevant stimuli (Jurczyk et al., 2021).

Reaction times also decreased as participants neared the candy reward, particularly in the high-reward condition. Because the progress bar was continuously visible, this pattern is more consistent with the salience hypothesis of goal monitoring-in which monitoring and effort increase as a goal becomes imminent-than with the uncertainty hypothesis-in which progress is checked regularly to reduce uncertainty (Devine et al., 2025). It is also consistent with studies that show that participants increased effort near goal completion when progress was visible (Sayalı et al., 2023). Nevertheless, we did not measure how often participants looked at the progress bar, so this interpretation remains tentative.

The reward-transition effect on reaction-time switch costs was not specific to restricted candy access: switch costs were lower for changing than for consistent reward transitions, but this effect did not differ significantly by access version. Adding access version also did not improve fit in the primary choice model. However, access effects were not uniformly absent: in the wanting rating analysis, the reward-transition effect varied jointly with wanting and access version. Delay can reduce the subjective value of a reward, but clear progress toward a goal can sustain motivation despite the delay (Howe et al., 2013). This may help to explain why there were not more robust effects of the candy access version.

The benefits of reward transitions were tied to the reward environment. Under restricted access, but not free access, we replicated monetary findings of reduced switch costs for changing versus consistent rewards (Fröber & Dreisbach, 2016; Shen & Chun, 2011). Restricted candy access and typical monetary paradigms share a delay in reward receipt: participants received the candy only after the experiment, like a delayed gift card.

However, delay alone cannot explain our results, because a previous study with delayed monetary rewards found no differences in switch costs across reward transitions (Orr et al., 2025). D What distinguishes the present paradigm is that a primary reward was visible but withheld. Palatable food cues without consumption induce anticipatory autonomic and endocrine responses as well as craving (Power & Schulkin, 2008; Smeets et al., 2012), similar to the cue reactivity seen in addiction research (Betts et al., 2021; Carter & Tiffany, 1999). Restricting access to the candy therefore likely created a motivationally engaging state, with a persistent conflict between urges and the no-eating rule that secondary rewards do not readily produce.

Individual differences

Our second hypothesis was also supported: individual differences in reward responsiveness and eating styles significantly modulated the relationship between reward transitions and cognitive control. First, participants who reported higher levels of liking and wanting the candy showed a greater effect of reward transitions on task switching. This finding highlights the role of individual differences in motivational salience and aligns with neurobiological models of reward processing. Liking generally refers to the pleasurable, hedonic enjoyment experienced when consuming a reward. Wanting, in contrast, describes the motivational drive to seek out and obtain that reward and is more closely tied to dopaminergic signaling (Morales & Berridge, 2020; Pool et al., 2016).

Notably, the influence of wanting on behavior was slightly stronger than that of liking, echoing findings from addiction research. It is possible to strongly desire a reward without necessarily deriving pleasure from its consumption, a phenomenon well-documented in substance use research (Grigutsch et al., 2019; Koranyi et al., 2017; Robinson & Berridge, 2025). In our case, participants who reported high levels of wanting may have been driven more by the motivational pull of the candy reward than by the actual anticipated enjoyment of consuming it, thereby showing greater sensitivity to reward transitions and a stronger tendency to switch tasks.

We also examined whether individual differences measured by the BIS/BAS personality scales and the Weight-Related Eating Questionnaire (WREQ) were associated with the reward-transition effect. The moderating component identified in these analyses was characterized most strongly by self-reported routine restraint and susceptibility to external food cues. Participants reporting greater routine restraint showed a smaller reward-transition effect, whereas those reporting greater cue-driven eating showed a larger effect. Because the WREQ assesses reported eating behaviors, these findings are best CANDY REWARDS & FLEXIBILITY 20 interpreted as associations between eating-related tendencies and task choice, rather than as evidence that personality traits caused differences in switching.

For people highly sensitive to external food cues, salient restricted food cues can heighten craving and arousal (Carter & Tiffany, 1999). Resisting the visible reward then demands strong top-down prefrontal control (Kober et al., 2010). Sustained prefrontal recruitment to regulate cue-reactivity leaves fewer resources for task switching, selectively impairing cognitive flexibility and increasing rigidity during reward transitions. By contrast, individuals low on affect-driven scales may use reduced reward value or disappointment as a signal to adapt: rather than draining control, low reward or errors appear to increase sensitivity to reward changes and promote task switching to seek better options, likely due to more balanced reward-system activation.

Limitations

First, this was a behavioral study and was not designed to test neural or physiological mechanisms; eye tracking of progress-bar monitoring, EEG/fMRI, and skin conductance could test the mechanisms proposed here. Second, reward variety was limited to two candies (M&Ms and Skittles) because of the dispenser's physical constraints, and some participants may not have found these rewarding. Third, we did not assess hunger at the start of the session; time of day, recent food intake, and eating schedules may have influenced the initial desirability of the candy.

Future Directions

Future studies could examine whether people with difficulty controlling eating, such as those with strong cravings or a history of disordered eating, or with a history of addiction, show different reward-mediated flexibility, given their altered reward sensitivity and cognitive control. It would also be useful to determine whether effects arise from sugar or from other features of candy (flavor, texture, emotional associations) by comparing candy with pure sugar and by developing dispensers for savory, non-sugary foods.

Conclusions

Collectively, these findings suggest that reward dynamics involving primary food incentives can shape voluntary task switching, with effects that vary across individuals.

The present analyses do not show that restricted access improves behavioral adaptation relative to free access, nor do they establish that food rewards affect cognitive control differently from monetary incentives. Potential neurobiological and physiological mechanisms remain hypotheses for future research.

For individuals characterized by affect-driven eating behaviors, the prefrontal demands of regulating appetitive cravings in highly rewarding, restricted-access environments may deplete executive resources, leading to cognitive rigidity. In contrast, for others, the disappointment of delayed or suboptimal rewards acts as a motivating cue for exploration and adaptation. This work contributes to a deeper, more integrated understanding of how cognitive effort is shaped by motivational contexts, offering valuable implications for understanding both adaptive goal pursuit and maladaptive self-regulation failures.
