Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Adult developmental theory has the potential to contribute across many fields. This paper, authored by two developmentalists and a neuroscientist studying meditators, argues that developmental models are increasingly needed as contemplative science enters a "third wave" concerned with advanced meditation including highly skilled practitioners, deep meditative states, and enduring transformations. This leading edge also overlaps with wisdom studies, spiritual and transpersonal inquiry, and trauma theory, exposing important conceptual tensions. We argue that these arise from a common assumption that cognitive complexity and spiritual clarity or psychological wholeness necessarily develop together along a single vertical axis of developmental "altitude." To clarify and resolve these tensions, we propose a framework distinguishing two quasi-independent developmental movements: ascending structural growth, the cumulative and hierarchical pg. 2 construction of cognitive capacities, and descending structural release, the loosening, deconstruction, or seeing-through of cognitive structures. Release exposes constrained or inaccessible cognitive resources, enabling deconditioning, reintegration, healing, and/or insight. We apply this framework to problems including the fallibility of spiritual adepts; relationships among states, stages, and stable "vantage points"; requisite complexity and adverse effects of intensive contemplative practice; disputes over maps of contemplative attainment; and the legitimization of traditional and Indigenous knowledge. The framework offers a heuristic bridge among contemplative science, developmental psychology, and adjacent fields, while generating theoretically and empirically testable hypotheses about increasingly deep possibilities for human development.

[div-01]

As academic fields mature, each discovery potentially reveals more questions than it answers, and new phenomena of study emerge that may stretch and strain the field's norms, sometimes spilling into the boundaries of other disciplines. As new opportunities for interdisciplinary inquiry arise, so do the challenges inherent to inter/cross/trans-disciplinary scholarship (Klein, 1990). We argue that adult development and contemplative science are at this type of juncture. Developmental principles and measurements have been applied to many fields, and we argue that current trends in contemplative science warrant its rich integration with developmental theory.

Contemplative science has always had an interdisciplinary bent, but much of its early phases were confined by the methods, theories, and norms of that time, as it worked to establish its legitimacy as a scientific field. Presently, as an established science with its scope expanding, it overlaps with or engages a diverse set of fields, including spiritual studies, somatic trauma theories, wisdom studies, and psychedelic studies, in addition to developmental psychology.

Though advantageous for all the fields involved, integration across academic fields can be difficult due to differences in concept definitions, metaphysical/ontological assumptions, norms for assessing usefulness and validity, and the themes habitually foregrounded and omitted (Newell et al., 2001; Klein, 1990; Stein, 2007). We will elaborate on this juncture in contemplative science's incorporation of adult development, noting a key bottleneck or conceptual tension to cross-discipline work, and propose a framework that relieves these tensions, potentially also clarifying conceptual conundrums within each field separately. Though our main focus is on applying adult development to contemplative science, our suggestions also facilitate its application to the other fields mentioned.

As the study of contemplative practices and meditation outcomes matures, more attention is paid to advanced practices, longer-term practitioners, or more accomplished adepts. Lieberman & Sacchet (2026) describe this as a "third wave" of meditative science. Prior studies (in the first and second waves) tended to focus on 'mindfulness' and its health and therapeutic effects, performance and cognitive enhancements, and clinical interventions; while neuroimaging and physiological studies often examined novice or intermediate meditators, and short-term effects. Though there were notable early studies focused on these themes, contemporary research finds increasing attention to these areas: 1.

Continuing to broaden the inquiry beyond novice practitioners and short-term interventions to include intermediate and advanced practitioners and mastery-related effects, including meditative endpoints (Sacchet et al., 2024; Van Lutterveld et al., 2024; Chowdhury et al., 2023; Ehmann et al., 2024; Sezer & Sacchet, 2024). 2. Continuing to widen practice contexts beyond basic meditative methods and states to include advanced meditation and related states, including advanced concentrative absorption meditation (Chowdhury et al., 2024; Treves et al., 2024; Potash et al., 2025; Potash et al., 2024; Demir et al., 2025), advanced investigative insight meditation (Yang et al., 2024a,c), and other "pinnacle" states (Schoenberg et al., 2018). 3. Studies of state-based outcomes are extending to include the development of persistent traits and developmental stages in what we have called meditative development (e.g., Singleton et al., 2021; Galante et al., 2023). 4. Mixed methods research that combines quantitative measurements with phenomenological and interpretive reports from practitioners and teachers (e.g., Sparby & Sacchet, 2022 , 2024). 5. An increasing appreciation of the potential value of "wisdom traditions," including traditional texts that focus on spirituality (i.e. practices and principles about the transcendent or sacred) as providing insights into practice nuances, phenomenological markers, developmental pathways, and pro-social/ethical applications (e.g., Walsh, 2015; Wright et al., 2023; Churchill & Murray, 2020). 6. Expanding the focus from stress-(or suffering-) reduction and therapeutic interventions to include broader dimensions of well-being and human potential, including goals such as thriving, pro-social attitudes, and self-understanding, and awakening (e.g., Schoenberg et al., 2018; Lou et al., 2017; Davis & Vago, 2013; Chowdhury et al., 2023; Van Lutterveld et al., 2024). 7. An increasing interest in (subjective or cultural) "interiors" in addition to (objective) exterior measurements and benefits, including an interest in outcomes related to virtue and wisdom (e.g., Schoenberg et al., 2018; Yaden et al., 2017; Sparby & Sachet, 2022). 8. An interdisciplinary broadening to include comparisons of meditation with psychedelic states and so-called flow states and other altered states of consciousness (e.g., Sparby & Sacchet, 2022; Sacchet et al., 2024; McAlpine et al., 2025; McAlpine et al., 2024). 9. An increasing appreciation and study of potential adverse, challenging, or unintended effects of contemplative practice (e.g., Wright et al., 2024 Wright et al., , 2025;; Britton et al., 2019; Schlosser et al., 2019; Hugemark et al., 2026).

These trends signal both a maturing of the field and an increased openness to new questions that were once considered outside of proper scientific study. They also extend the interdisciplinary reach of meditative studies to allow for more cross-discipline influences and synergy. While the "silos" of specialized disciplines can be helpful (e.g., allowing depth, standardization, and efficient collaboration), complex phenomena operate through multiple mechanisms, levels, and scales that no single discipline or ontology can capture (Frodeman et al., 2017; Bhaskar, 2010; Price, 2016). Based on trends such as those noted above, Murray et al. (in pg. 4 submission) argue for the importance of integrating developmental psychology into contemplative science. Here we extend this work to speak to issues that inhibit the integration of contemplative psychology with additional areas of study.

Over recent decades, academia has been increasingly open to inquiries into spiritual, esoteric, and "transpersonal" themes, drawing on religious traditions and "perennial philosophy" (Ferrer, 2002; Hanegraaff, 2012). These inform the liberatory and ethical motivations behind meditative practices and traditions and provide maps for the experiences and insights that are encountered. The scholarly treatment of wisdom is also implicated here, as it can give a developmental perspective on the maturation of the combination of self-awareness, moral complexity, and philosophical/existential sophistication implied at the intersection of all these fields (Walsh, 2015).

Finally, psychotherapy and trauma theory are implicated in these trends. The release or healing of maladaptive beliefs and habits is a common theme in the meditation (e.g., mindfulness) and psychotherapeutic (including self-help) literatures (Van der Kolk, 2014; Porges, 2001). These processes have more recently been informed by trauma and somatic theories that articulate how negative experiences lodge in the nervous system to create limitations and lacunae, which we will call "occlusions." The release (or "ablation") of limiting occlusions is germane to the "cleaning up" of habitual or constraining mental patterns, and to the deeper releases implied in more advanced meditative achievements such as boundlessness, selflessness, and nondual apprehension. Trauma theory is also germane to studying adverse effects of meditation, and the potential risks in exploring advanced states or practices too quickly or too soon in one's development. The convergent overlap and potential cross-fertilization of these fields is illustrated in Figure 1. While these emerging interdisciplinary encounters can invite fruitful collaborative inquiry, any attempt to integrate several fields risks superficiality and partiality. Here we follow a modest approach by articulating a single yet key tension that can inhibit dialogue among these fields -i.e. unexamined assumptions around the relationships between complexity capacity and spiritual maturity. We offer an "ascending/descending" (growth vs. release) developmental framework that helps clarify and integrate some of the conceptual differences and conundrums found in translating between the fields. We expect our model to be useful in the crossdisciplinary convergence of any subset of these domains, but we primarily focus on what is needed for contemplative science to mature through interdisciplinary encounters.

Conundrums of advanced states and stages

Several conundrums commonly arise in inquiries at the boundary between science, psychology, and spirituality, especially concerning what it means to achieve advanced states or stages of human potential, and how these relate to wisdom, virtue, and/or morality. These conundrums can impede conceptual and scientific clarity in and between contemplative science, adult developmental science, and spiritual and transpersonal studies. Such issues can be overlooked in contemplative science when its focus is limited to basic mindfulness, cognitive and health benefits, or psychotherapeutic healing, but they become more salient in studies of advanced practices and long-term practitioners. Note that some of these quandaries arise more frequently in the application or common interpretation of meditation theories than in research per se. The conceptual and foundational conundrums at the boundary between these fields include: 1.

Many development-oriented models propose increasing complexity and nuance with advancing development (for example, in perspective-taking, systemic thinking, or selfunderstanding) (e.g. Commons, 2008; Kohlberg, 1973). In contrast, many models describe later stages as having increased simplicity or emptiness (e.g., Cook-Greuter, 2013; Debold, 2002). Though there is some evidence for this non-linear progression, it contradicts some foundational models of development (as we explain later). The paradigm of "simplicity on the other side of complexity," by itself, explains away this paradox without explaining it or clarifying the distinct cognitive and functional processes involved. 2. "Growth to goodness" assumptions or grandiose attributions are often projected onto later stages, creating surprise and outrage when individuals experiencing meditative endpoints or considered "awakened" or "enlightened" commit ethical breaches or exhibit lapses in judgment-despite many decades of evidence that this phenomenon is not uncommon (Stein 2007 (Stein , 2019;; Forman, 2012). Though the lesson that our esteemed teachers "are only human" is a reasonable practical conclusion, it still leaves open the question of just what advanced spiritual or contemplative achievement is and is not. 3. There is often confusion about the implications of transient-state achievement vs. stablestage achievement (DiPerna, 2014). Individuals experiencing profound states can have grandiose beliefs about such experiences and the resulting status, insights, or capacities, sometimes bordering on delusional or narcissistic (Picciotto et al., 2018; Masters, 2010; Fossella, 2011). 4. In contemplative and religious studies, one finds controversies and disagreements over meditative maps, attainment claims, and the ordering or relative profundity of contemplative states (Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026; Brown, 2006; Analayo, 2020; Grabovac, 2015). Though neuroscience can inform these inquiries, scholarship will also benefit from the developmental maps and conceptual framings we discuss here.

5. Contemplative Science is among a number of fields that, in drawing from ancient, traditional, or indigenous knowledge, can struggle to legitimize such knowledge within a scientific paradigm (Davis & Vago, 2013; Wright et al., 2023; Dorjee, 2016). Our framework includes mechanisms suggesting that the advance of knowledge and complexity in society necessarily occludes important wisdom that then calls to be reintegrated-knowledge that non-modern cultures have preserved, pointing to foundational capacities that can be recovered. 6. Given the documented adverse outcomes of contemplative practices (Cebolla et al., 2017; Schlosser et al., 2019; Britton et al., 2021; Lindahl et al., 2017), the field could use better models to explain these outcomes and prescribe methods and prerequisites that are sensitive to those predisposed to negative outcomes. Current research offers only a limited account of practitioners' readiness for intensive or advanced contemplative practice, and of the adverse effects that may occur when particular practices or attainments are pursued too far, too quickly, or without adequate preparation and support. 7. The focus on "altitude" or higher/later states and stages can foster modes of "bypassing" (Masters, 2010; Sharf, 1995) that can inhibit paying attention to fields tied more closely to daily life or human suffering, including psychological trauma and shadow.

Unexamined assumptions behind such issues can have unseen (including "paradigmatic", see Kuhn & Hawkins, 1963) effects on research motivation and planning, hypothesis framing, the integration of findings across studies and domains, and the practical outcomes of research. We propose that all seven of these conundrums, and more, spring primarily (though not completely) from the first conundrum. That is, many theorists in several fields (contemplative science, adult development, transpersonal psychology, and perennial spirituality) assume that contemplative/spiritual maturation and developmental/complexity maturation should track together into higher/later stages of maturity or achievement.

Addressing these conundrums requires clearer distinctions between, and better explanation of, these interrelated phenomena: (A) Cognitive states versus developmental stages; (B) temporary states versus stable vantage-points (or state-stages); and (C) growth versus release mechanisms in maturation processes (i.e.. ascending versus descending modalities). Our ascending/descending model focuses on (C) but, in so doing, addresses (A) and (B) as well.

As noted in Stein & Heikkinen (2009) and Cook-Greuter (2013), there is significant metaphorical "upward" bias in interpretations and uses of both developmental narratives and spirituality narratives-a gazing towards or grasping for "verticality" or "altitude" that easily leads to an egoic appropriation of transcendence. Similar concerns appear in critiques of spiritual ranking, hierarchical perennialism, meditative-attainment discourse, spiritual bypassing, and spiritual materialism (Trungpa, 2002; Sharf, 1995; Ferrer, 2011). The ascending narrative is understandable, considering that metaphors of vertical ascent align with modernist narratives of positivism and progress, as well as with religious soteriology.

Though "higher" (or later) in developmental narratives generally refers to stages of greater complexity and nuance, and "higher" in spiritual narratives generally refers to more advanced stages of spiritual unity and freedom, many treatments (including some in transpersonal psychology, perennial philosophy, and adult development) suggest that both of these occur together. Our goal here is not so much to critique the vertical or hierarchical orientation (already done well by the cited authors), as to critique the conflating of increasing complexity capacity with that of increasing spiritual unity and freedom-as both occurring with increased "altitude."

As one example of the theorists struggling to understand the relationship between ascending and descending phenomena, Suzanne Cook-Greuter (2000) describes her dialogue with Chris Alexander on the nature of higher states and stages (and see Alexander et al., 1990). Alexander comes from a Hindu Vedic spiritual background, which is a "top-down" paradigm that "starts in the purely spiritual, non-material Unity Consciousness at its most fundamental [and most elevated] level and ends at the bottom with the body consciousness of infancy as the most material, gross form of mind" (p. 229). Meanwhile Cook-Greuter, like all developmentalists, uses a bottom-up bio-psycho-social model describing "growth of the self from undifferentiated early infancy to self-actualized mature adulthood." Both are seen to point to something important about late-stage maturity, but the discussion about the nature of these stages and states, and the relationships between psychotherapeutic and contemplative practices, ends inconclusively, with Cook-Greuter concluding that more research is needed. We believe out framework resolves these questions.

To address these conundrums, we describe a simple yet analytically powerful distinction between two quasi-independent movements: the growth of cognitive structures and the release of cognitive structures, where growth is associated with accumulative learning and hierarchical integration, and release is associated with the "unlearning," loosening, or seeing-through of mental structures that is a primary goal of both psychotherapy and contemplative practice.

Key to this framework is articulating the interplay between structural growth and structural release, which can but do not necessarily occur together. When they co-occur in specific ways (described later) they produce the type of robust wisdom development described by models that conflate the two movements as necessarily (or presumably) conjoined. We thus shift from a vertical/hierarchical metaphor to a "volumetric" metaphor for healthy/well-adapted psychospiritual growth, which combines the (ascending) "height" of increased structural complexity with the (descending) "depth" of ever-deeper structural release. This framework is theoretical and heuristic and in the companion paper we describe empirical work that could test some of its aspects. Before we describe the details of our ascending/descending model, we will delve more deeply into the interdisciplinary crossfertilization, or need for more cross-fertilization, between the fields noted in Figure 1.

Growth and Release in Developmental Matura)on

Structural Growth

Though the main innovation of our model is in highlighting structural release, we will first give an account of structural growth, both to summarize foundations of mainstream developmental theory, and because our model of structural release depends on principles of structural growth.

Adult developmental psychology studies the progression of the following overlapping constructs through the lifespan: meaning-making, perspective-taking, self-understanding, identity, wisdom, world-view, cognitive complexity, moral capacity, and depth of insight (e.g. Piaget, 1971; Kohlberg, 1973; Loevinger, 1976; Kegan, 1994; Fischer, 1980; and Torbert, 2014; Cook-Greuter, 2000; Commons & Richards, 1984; Fowler, 1981). These constructs have been tested and refined over 50 years, with assessments demonstrating strong psychometric properties (see Murray, 2017, for an overview). Each theory may focus on a differently defined construct within this set of overlapping constructs. Here we focus on theories broadly related to the development of wisdom skills, including ego development models, though all developmental theories can be said to share foundational principles.

Though most developmental theories map out specific sequences of skills and capacities, fundamentally, developmental theory concerns the rigorous mapping out of prerequisites-i.e. it is a science of discovering necessary orderings among skill and sub-skill acquisition. Michael Commons' Model of Hierarchical Complexity (MHC) and Kurt Fischer's Skill Theory (which are substantially similar) are generally considered the most rigorous or foundational theories of the mechanisms of cognitive development (see Fischer, 2008; Commons, 2008; Dawson-Tunik, 2005). Commons' definition of development defines the "complexity order" of a capacity in terms of how many hierarchical levels of one thing building upon another that a task or skill implies (Commons & Pekker, 2009). For example, one must learn to: crawl before standing, stand before walking, walk before running, and run before playing football. Each skill necessarily builds upon and develops after its prerequisites, and learning a weak version of a subskill and rushing (or being rushed) into performance at the next hierarchical level constitutes a weak skill foundation that can lead to brittle or dysfunctional performance at later stages.

Although the cumulative nature of learning may appear self-evident, this ("constructivist," von Glasersfeld, 1995) principle is surprisingly neglected. All too often, people are expected to perform a task or learn a new task when, unbeknownst to learners and leaders, key prerequisite building blocks are missing because the careful mapping of prerequisites is rarely appreciated. For example, academic or conceptual learning often mistakes "learning about" for "learning how" and ignores the foundational sensorimotor and social-emotional knowledge needed for knowledge to be deeply understood and flexibly applied (Vervaeke & Ferraro, 2013; Immordino-Yang & Damasio, 2007). As another example, a nation might be advised to adopt a democratic-style government without a sufficient analysis of, or preparation for, the skills citizens need for such a system to function. Those lacking a developmentally informed framework can bring misguided and self-defeating expectations to endeavors in many domains, including conflict resolution, teaching, parenting, and leadership (Kegan, 1994; Selman et al., 1986; Hennighausen et al., 2004; Rooke & Torbert, 2005).

The developmental perspective ties together cognitive phenomena associated with infancy (such as differentiating self from other), the magical/mythical mind (important to intuition, embodiment, and creativity), modes of social convention and relationality (the water we all swim in and must negotiate), abstract and rational thought ("adult" cognition, including metacognition), and so-called post-or trans-rational capacities (and the most advanced stages of wisdom maturity). Because later-stage capacities "transcend and include" earlier stages, which, in a sense, are always present and operating, an understanding of the psychology of any stage informs an understanding of all later stages. Thus, for example, in studying advanced contemplative practices or expert practitioners, aspects of the entire spectrum of development may be relevant.

Structural Release

In contrast to structural growth (hierarchical learning), structural release is the loosening, deconstruction, seeing through, or healing of cognitive structures. We use the term occlusion for the aspect of a cognitive structure that blocks, inhibits, or distorts cognitive resources (knowledge, skill, belief, capacity, etc.), and ablation for the apophatic process of letting go of, releasing, unlearning, or decentering an occlusion. Development involves a complex dialectic choreography between ascending and descending processes. For example, release allows previously occluded capacity to be recovered ("re-membered," liberated, revealed, etc.), and then reintegrated (connected and synergized in new ways)-which is an ascending process.

We think of structural release as more of a reduction in the habituation, grip, attachment to, or identification with a cognitive structure, rather than its complete erasure or destruction. Phenomenologically, ascending structural growth has the sense of gaining a new capacity "that I never had before," whereas descending structural release and recovery has the sense of revelation or insight wherein "a part of me always knew this, but I forgot." Following the "wisdom skill" framework (Murray, 2019; 2021; 2025) we maintain that all learning, that is, all cognitive structures, not just unwanted or harmful ones, hide or occlude cognitive content and capacity as well as, ostensibly, provide positive new capacities. Concepts such as shadow or trauma are typically used to indicate maladaptive structures (occlusions), which are usually thought to be entrenched and arising from either painful experiences or deepseated habits of mind. Related terms include psychic injury, limiting ideas, negative beliefs, frozen narratives, defenses, attachments, identification, illusions, biases, splits, fusions, traps, boundaries, etc. (as found in the psychotherapeutic literature, e.g. Van der Kolk, 2014; Maté, 2012; Levine, 2010; Sapolsky, 2004; Porges, 2001, and the contemplative/ transpersonal literature, e.g. Shapiro et al., 2006; Schuman-Olivier, 2020; Dahl et al., 2015). In a sense, only structures that are experienced as problematic "call to be released." When a structure (capacity, skill, belief) is perceived to provide significant benefit and little harm, then, by definition, the fact that the structure occludes certain capacities is not seen as a problem.

The cognitive, neurological, and psychotherapeutic mechanisms and processes involved in structural release are indicated in many pockets of the related interdisciplinary literature (beyond our scope here, but addressed in a companion paper "Adult development, meditation, wisdom, and unlearning: Toward a science of advanced meditation", in submission). Decentering, reorganization, and unlearning are referenced frequently in the fields of psychotherapy, meditation, spirituality, personal growth, psychedelics, and trauma recovery. 9 10

The domain of descending structural release encompasses themes including humility, acceptance, forgiveness, unknowing, bare presence, reenchantment, ensoulment, disidentification from the "consensus trance" of cultural delusions, and contemplative accounts of egotranscendence. We follow others in contemplative and psychotherapeutic fields who propose that simply bringing focused, non-attached attention to a maladaptive phenomenon is a key element of release processes (Brahinsky et al, 2024; Wilber, 2000; Laukkonen et al., 2024; Laukkonen & Slagter, 2021). 11 Fields including meditation studies, spiritual/religious studies, and trauma studies emphasize the descending and overlook the important contributions of developmental psychology. Meanwhile, many developmental models either ignore descending processes or conflate them with ascending processes within their ascending framework. The differentiation is important because ascending and descending processes may involve different neurological mechanisms and practice regimens and require different precautions against unintended or adverse outcomes. For example, if one conflates ascending with descending, one runs the risk of (1) addressing a problem that requires releasing structures through practices that build structural complexity (without releasing anything); or (2) addressing a problem that requires building complexity, nuance, or sophistication through practices that actually release structures. And, as noted, the differentiation of ascending and descending processes is key for a robust interdisciplinary integration of contemplative science with developmental theory (Murray, 2025).

An Ascending/descending Model

Research shows how many human capacities can be ordered quasi-developmentally within any skill area, by order of prerequisite or composition (roughly from earliest to latest, see Fischer, 1980; Commons, 2008; Rochat, 2003). The Wisdom Skills framework proposes that structural-release phenomena may be mapped homologously onto developmental sequences. This shifts the interpretation of contemplative (or transpersonal) processes and outcomes away from a single question of "how high" they are-whether in terms of altitude, transcendence, or presumed developmental superiority-and toward the question of what depth of primordial structure they release, disclose, or make newly accessible. In this way, the framework offers a two-axis model for analyzing contemplative effects and outcomes, distinguishing complexity capacity from release depth, rather than collapsing both into a single measure of developmental altitude.

A. Disappointing gurus and the independence of ascending and descending processes

The bulk of this paper describes how growth in developmental complexity and the recovery of "spiritual clarity" do not track together but can be described as quasi-independent ascending and descending movements of structural growth and release.

The assumption that greater hierarchical complexity necessarily corresponds to, or tracks with, spiritual or transpersonal growth creates conceptual confusion and ethical risk. Contemplative traditions have long warned against conflating temporary state experiences, such as bliss, certainty, or expanded self-confidence, with more durable developmental attainments, such as wisdom, compassion, ethical maturity, and virtue (Brown & Engler, 1980). Similarly, Stein (2019) critiques the "growth-to-goodness" fallacy: the assumption that later-stage development necessarily entails greater happiness, wisdom, or ethical conduct. These confusions appear in both developmental and contemplative/spiritual contexts, where excessive wisdom or authority may be attributed to teachers, adepts, or theorists who claim advanced states or stages, even when such claims are not matched by corresponding ethical, relational, or epistemic maturity (Caplan, 2014; Hassan, 2020; Martin-Smith, 2022; Žižek, 2001; Cohen, 2023).

Understanding spiritual attainment in terms of the descending dimensions of wisdom development, and understanding profound spiritual states as involving the release of early, primordial cognitive structures, helps explain the recurrent fallibilities of esteemed spiritual leaders. Such states (and vantage points) do not necessarily imply increased complexity capacity, nor do they guarantee the integration or release of intermediate-level personality, relational, or shadow structures. The question shifts from "how could they" fail to meet our high expectations of wisdom and ethics, to "why would one assume" that spiritual state achievement necessarily instills the complexity and nuance associated with wisdom? B. What lies at the "top" of development?

Many developmental models place phenomena such as ego release, concept deconstruction, seeing through conventional worldviews, emptiness of time or space, and realizations of nonduality at the top of their developmental sequences (DiPerna, 2014; O'Fallon, 2012; Wilber, 2017). This can be problematic because it conflicts with the most adequate (modern and elegant) theories of development-hierarchical complexity frameworks. They rigorously define development in terms of successive levels that build upon (or coordinate) lower-level structures (Fischer, 2008; Commons, 2008)-while such developmental processes produce ever greater diversity and uniqueness (similar to biological evolution). This contradicts models that gesture toward an ascending ultimate "Omega Point" unifying human or earthly consciousness or forms-a singular apex as proposed by de Chardin (de Chardin et al., 1965) and later taken up by Wilber (2017) and others.

It is the descending direction that points toward what all have in common and what unifies across individuals or types of being. 12 While the higher/later levels of hierarchical development lead to ever more complexity, nuance, uniqueness, and diversity, the ultimate reality or perfection that are endpoints in spiritual narratives point in the opposite direction, toward releasing boundaries, splits, and complexity-which is "descending" for us, though communities are free to use ascending/transcending metaphors for such processes. (We discuss the "volumetric" metaphor of combining both later.) Regardless of the metaphors used, what is important for this interdisciplinary work is to differentiate structural growth from structural release, and explicate how they interrelate.

For one, releasing movements can feel energetically ascending. In addition, both complexity capacity and spiritual clarity are associated with increased wisdom and skills acquired cumulatively over a lifetime. O'Fallon (2020), based on her analysis of thousands of sentence completions from developmental assessments, goes further to hypothesize that certain states are necessary, but not sufficient, for each stage of development.

Contributing to the confusion and conflation of state and stage is the existence of phenomena that incorporate aspects of both-namely, the ability to reliably access a state or the insights associated with it. This is a learned skill and thus a stage-like phenomenon. We can thus, following contemporary theorists, including Brown (2006), Wilber (2000), and DiPerna (2014), differentiate the developed skill of reliably accessing profound states and insights from the development of other capacities associated with wisdom. We will use the term "vantage point" (from DiPerna and Brown) to indicate a learned skill of reliable access to an advanced state (Wilber [2000] uses the term "state-stage"; Dorje (2016) uses "existential modes," and Khema [1994] uses "fruition state/attainment").

Though contemplative practice can contribute to building skills and higher complexity capacities (i.e., ascending development), such as self-monitoring, self-control, metacognition, attentional skills, and perspective-taking, we speculate that most of the states (and vantage points) in contemplative studies can be associated with descending release. These include movement toward the "emptiness" (apophatic) direction of deconditioning or releasing ego, attachments, problematic ideas and ideations, etc.; and experiencing timelessness, boundlessness, formlessness, etc., which, as noted, temporarily release the constraints imposed by the most primitive learned structures. But even peak contemplative experiences associated with "fullness" such as bliss, oneness/unity, clarity, abundance, and luminosity, can be understood as releasing learned constraints to recover capacities dampened by social conditioning and difficult experiences.

Our ascending/descending model supports the view that experiences do not imply stable learning or wisdom but are the first step in developing stable traits. The narrative around the independence of state vs. stage achievement can lead to an "apples to oranges" categorical confusions. It is better to compare (stable, ascending) stage acquisition to (stable, descending) vantage point acquisition.

D. Developmental volume and requisite complexity

Though we propose that they are independent, ascending structural growth and descending structural release influence each other and act in combinatorial ways. As noted, we frame wisdom development as a particular combination of ascending and descending movements, using a volumetric "higher and deeper" metaphor. We propose that in the most natural or opportune path, descending depth and ascending complexity happen together and support each other. This is another probable reason the ascending and descending movements are conflated.

In O'Fallon's (2020) language, particular levels of structural release may be "necessary but not sufficient" for particular levels of ego/wisdom development. While practitioners at any developmental level can engage in deconstructive practices aimed at releasing elements of any previously constructed strata of the psyche, each major fulcrum of ego development may require the release of a particular structure.

We hypothesize that ever-higher levels of ego development nominally track with everdeeper layers of release. As hierarchical growth builds layer upon layer, the lower strata have more built upon or relying upon them. The deeper excavation of releasing lower levels could be expected to accompany higher risk of destabilization and disorientation, even as they enable more radical transformation (Britton et al., 2021; Lindahl, 2017; Cebolla et al., 2017; Schlosser et al., 2019). We propose that tolerating, managing, and adapting to greater levels of chaos, ambiguity, and novelty requires higher levels of maturity and complexity capacity. For example, a realization that the traditional "self" does not exist is more generally destabilizing than realizing that one's father was not a terrible person. Deep structural releases may be harmful or not fully integrated if the subject has not developed the requisite complexity of meaning-making for that depth of release.

The "higher complexity suggests deeper release" principle of healthy maturation is generally supported by ego and meaning-making developmental models (Kegan, 1994; Torbert, 2014; Cook-Greuter, 2000; O'Fallon, 2020). Development from conventional to postconventional and "post-post conventional" stages involves this typical sequence of releases: questioning and disembedding from cultural norms; releasing limiting habits of persona and belief; questioning the validity of logical and linear thought processes; decentering the self/ego; decentering and releasing the reification of conceptual categories; and (in O'Fallon's [2020] model ) releasing spaciotemporal structures and deep splits between self-vs-other, self-vs-world, interior-vs-exterior, and individual-vs-collective. This principle is suggestive and conjectural, and more work is needed to precisely analyze how reported stage sequences in ego development map to the release of ever-lower cognitive strata.

Our mapping of state experiences and contemplative milestones with structural release at particular early developmental fulcrums has the potential to inform controversies concerning the relative priority, profundity, or ordering of achievements and practices found in the literature. Analogues found among the phylogenetical, developmental, neurobiological, microgenetic, and phenomenological pathways noted above may suggest a robust depth ordering of cognitive structures that can be to address current and perennial controversies. For example, the Jhanic sequence of contemplative practice (Chowdhury et al., 2024; Demir et al., 2025), sometimes described as relaxing successively deeper cognitive processes, could be linked, not only to brain regions or functions, but to developmentally and evolutionarily earlier capacities, releasing structures built ever earlier in infant development.

E. Dangerous heights and depths

Many have documented the risks of intensive meditation practice, which include dissociative disorders such as depersonalization and derealization (Yaden, 2017; Cebolla et al., 2017; Schlosser et al., 2019; Britton et al., 2021; Lindahl et al., 2017). Similarly, in psychotherapy, suppressed material can be coaxed out of the unconscious too rapidly, leading to re-traumatization, regression, or other setbacks (Berk & Parker, 2009). Thus, descending release can create adverse effects under certain conditions.

On the ascending side, developmentalists have cautioned that rushing development into higher stages can leave earlier stages anemic (under-developed or lacking breadth) and can also intensify narcissism or over-specialized growth (Torbert & Erfan, 2020). The growth of hierarchical complexity can sometimes lead to increasingly abstract or complicated thinking that distances the individual from concrete or ethical realities (Murray, 2025; Winther, 2014). Deconstructing the self or meaning-making apparatus can also leave one uninterested in "developing" themselves and freeze them at their current level of skill in various aspects of wisdom (Torbert & Erfan, 2020).

Though we argue that nominal or healthy development progresses in a quasi "middleout" progression, with later stages implicating release at lower strata, we also note that structural growth and release are independent. In practice, complexity and abstraction often develop without the concurrent release processes that would establish wisdom; and release processes can likewise occur without the increases in complexity capacity necessary for wisdom.

One can move too fast or too far in either ascending or descending movement and risk traumatization, psychopathology, decompensation, grandiosity, or losing touch with the pragmatic and ethical realities of life. Progress in either direction should serve the current life context and the pace of change best tolerated by the individual.

Beyond disappointing teachers, general questions of "spiritual bypassing" are informed by our model. Spiritual bypassing is an overemphasis on superlative states, boundary loosening, or ideation about the transcendent or grandiose, as used to avoid uncomfortable feelings, challenging truths, or practical and ethical realities (Forman, 2012; Masters, 2010). It can result from an overemphasis on state experiences over stages of wisdom development. It can also result from an overemphasis on ascending or transcending themes, when what is needed is the recognition of problematic structures that call for descending release. Levit (2018, p. 586) notes that "psychoanalytic treatment is itself inherently disruptive. If growth is to occur, the painful and conflictual must be opened up and the internal status quo must be explored and challenged [which can be] detrimental for people with certain vulnerabilities." The same might be said for many forms of contemplative practice. Our model may be useful in managing such risks. We can hypothesize that when one releases or deconstructs a psychic structure one "falls into" the structures hierarchically below it, and that one should not dismantle any structure for which a healthy stable "landing space" does not exist "below" it.

The related concept of "regression in the service of transcendence" (Washburn, 2000) may actually come from confounding ascending and descending processes. True regression (as that can happen in states of shock, delirium, intoxication, mesmerization, psychosis, hypnagogia, etc.) suppresses later level capacities, leaving the individual in a compromised "less developed" state of less awareness and response-ability; while structural release, under our definition, releases the grip or habituation of a structure, while leaving one's fully developed metacognitive awareness and wisdom at-the-ready as the situation requires.

Combining these caveats about rushing transformation with our notion of requisite complexity, we can hypothesize a correspondence between each higher stage of growth and specific lower levels of release that defines a natural or healthy progression. Change outside of this volumetric pairing may carry additional risk, a hypothesis that calls for further theoretical and empirical investigation.

Anthropologists, Indigenous scholars, and cultural philosophers have argued that Indigenous and ancient societies preserve ways of knowing that have been attenuated in modern industrial societies (Descola, 2013; Henrich, 2020; Viveiros de Castro, 2015). Our model says that occluded capacities eventually "call to be recovered" when their absence creates noticeable tension or suffering, but the release of problematic shadow structures is far from guaranteed, and can require unusual degrees of focused attention and resolve. The modeling and teaching of indigenous minds can clarify, motivate, and guide the recovery and reintegration of occluded forms of wisdom.

We are also reminded that structures can not only be learned, occluded, and recovered, but that they must sometimes be actively preserved, maintained, and protected-against external and internal forces that would erode, distort, appropriate, or subordinate them.

For example, some scholars argue for the "re-enchantment of the lifeworld," or a return to perceiving the world of everyday life as intrinsically meaningful, value-laden, relational, alive, and sacred, rather than reducing it to utility, mechanism, abstraction, consumption, or control (Berman, 1981; Price, 2016; Latour, 2005; Taylor, 1991). This is not a call to regress into prerational thinking, but a call into descending recovery of aspects of the magical/mythical strata of cognition that are typically occluded by the rational/modern strata. Early cognitive processes related to "magical thinking" or mystical experiences are discredited as irrational and fanciful, yet hold vital instinctive, symbolic, poetic, and intuitive resources essential to creativity, existential meaning, ethical imagination, and a more participatory relationship with the living world (Donald, 1993; Hutson, 2012; Bird-David, 1999; Jung, 1966; van Elk, 2026). These scholars challenge the modern splits between mind vs. world, human vs. nature, fact vs. value, and material reality vs. sacred significance. In terms of our model, they call for release of these structural splits and the recovery of modes of knowing free of the edicts of such splits. They call for more fluid access to information and perception from the so-called magical and mythical developmental strata of meaning-making, suppressed deep into the unconscious in most modern contexts.

Though questions of inter-cultural exchange, appropriation, and mutual benefit are complex and beyond our scope, we maintain that developmental theories combined with our ascending/descending framework can inform the practical, philosophical, and ethical aspects of such knowledge transfer. Conclusions

Contemplative science's expanding attention to advanced meditation including meditative development, deeper meditative states, ethical outcomes, and adverse effects places it increasingly in conversation with developmental psychology, psychotherapy and trauma theory, wisdom studies, and spiritual and transpersonal scholarship. These interdisciplinary encounters are promising precisely because each field foregrounds phenomena that the others tend to leave implicit. Yet the same encounters can generate conceptual confusion when constructs developed for different purposes are treated as equivalent. We have focused on one such confusion: the tendency to place increasing cognitive complexity, psychological maturation, spiritual realization, and contemplative attainment along a single vertical axis of "higher" development.

The ascending/descending framework proposed here separates two processes that may cooperate but need not develop together. Ascending structural growth is the familiar developmental movement of building increasingly differentiated, integrated, and hierarchically complex capacities. Descending structural release is the loosening, deconstruction, or reduced pg. 24 grip of already-constructed structures, allowing previously occluded capacities to become accessible and potentially reintegrated. The distinction is analytic rather than absolute: release can enable new integration, and growth can create the metacognitive and regulatory capacities needed to tolerate deeper release. Nevertheless, treating the two movements as quasiindependent provides a clearer vocabulary for phenomena that are otherwise forced into a single metaphor of developmental altitude. This distinction helps resolve several conundrums that become especially salient in research on advanced contemplative practice. Profound states need not imply stable traits, later developmental stages, or broad wisdom; the capacity for reliable access to a state-a vantage point or state-stage-should itself be distinguished from other dimensions of developmental maturity. Likewise, contemplative or spiritual attainments involving selflessness, boundlessness, nonduality, or other forms of deconditioning do not by themselves guarantee the complexity, relational integration, ethical judgment, or shadow work associated with mature wisdom. This helps explain why accomplished practitioners or teachers may display striking contemplative capacities alongside ordinary or serious limitations, without requiring either the dismissal of their attainments or the inflation of them into global claims of maturity.

The framework also reframes the apparent paradox that later development is sometimes described as both more complex and more simple. Hierarchical growth tends toward greater differentiation, nuance, and contextual adequacy, while deep release can reduce fixation on distinctions, categories, narratives, and even primordial structuring assumptions. What appears as "simplicity on the other side of complexity" may therefore reflect the coordinated operation of two different developmental movements rather than a reversal of the logic of hierarchical development. We have called their healthy coordination developmental volume: wisdom may be better represented by both the height of complexity capacity and the depth of release than by either dimension alone. This volumetric perspective also has practical and safety implications. Too-rapid growth can produce brittle, overly abstract, narcissistic, or poorly grounded forms of functioning when prerequisite capacities are weak. Too-rapid or too-deep release can destabilize identity, meaningmaking, affect regulation, or ordinary functioning when adequate integrative capacities and supportive contexts are absent. The hypothesis of requisite complexity therefore suggests that particular depths of contemplative or psychotherapeutic release may require corresponding developmental resources if they are to be integrated adaptively. Rather than assuming that more intensive practice or more radical deconstruction is inherently better, research and training may benefit from asking what forms of preparation, scaffolding, support, and subsequent integration are appropriate for a given person and practice.

Finally, the same framework offers one way to approach knowledge preserved in traditional and Indigenous cultures without either romanticizing it or reducing it to an earlier developmental status. Increasing cultural complexity and abstraction generate powerful capacities, but they generally occlude embodied, relational, ecological, imaginal, and participatory modes of knowing. Many traditional practices and worldviews may therefore preserve capacities that modern societies have attenuated or lost. From this perspective, mature cultural and individual development is neither a simple return to the past nor an indefinite accumulation of complexity, but a process of preserving, recovering, and reintegrating foundational capacities within increasingly adequate forms of understanding.

The model remains theoretical and heuristic. Its key claims-especially the relative independence of structural growth and release, the possibility of mapping release depth onto developmental strata, and the proposed relation between deeper release and requisite complexity-require sharper operationalization and empirical testing. Such work (described in more detail in the companion paper) could combine developmental assessment, detailed phenomenology, longitudinal designs, behavioral measures, and neuroscience in studies of contemplative practitioners at different levels of experience. It could also compare contemplative, psychotherapeutic, psychedelic, and other transformative processes to determine where apparently similar experiences arise from different mechanisms or lead to different developmental outcomes.

As contemplative science moves further into the study of advanced states, enduring transformations, wisdom, and human flourishing, its questions will increasingly exceed the boundaries of any single discipline. A productive interdisciplinary science will therefore need distinctions that are simple enough to travel across fields without erasing the phenomena that make those fields distinct. The ascending/descending framework is offered as one such distinction. By separating the building of structure from the release of structure-and then examining how they interact-it shifts attention from the question of how "high" a person, practice, or state is to the more discriminating questions of what has developed, what has been released, what has been recovered, and how well these changes have been integrated into a wise, ethical, and functional life.

Glossary

• Contemplative studies. The field of research and theory of meditation and related contemplative practices, including psychological, psychotherapeutic, phenomenological, and neuroscience approaches.

• Meditative development: Refers broadly to any change resulting from contemplative practice over time. It can include non-hierarchical learning and changes in state experience, behavior, or physiology.

• Developmental theory (or cognitive developmental): In this paper, refers to theories of increasing cognitive (including logical, metacognitive, and social/emotional) skill that mature through hierarchical transformative stages, theorized to progress through processes of differentiation followed by integration. Cognitive developmental theories encompass childhood and adult development, and a variety of skill domains or narrow as well as broad constructs "such as ego development. We focus on "adult developmental theories." • Hierarchical complexity theories of development. Very general theories of developmental mechanisms that apply to all skill domains (including mathematics, music, sensory-motor, moral reasoning, etc. ( Fischer 2008; Commons & Richards, 1984). We describe them here because these foundational growth (ascending) processes set the stage for our notion of release (descending) processes.

• Ego Development theory: A lineage of adult developmental theories that include validated assessments, originating with Jane Loevinger, that track how self-sense, meaning-making complexity, perspective-taking, and generally "consciousness," mature over time, given the right conditions. (Closely related to Robert Kegan's "orders of consciousness" theory.) We propose that they explicitly include ascending growth and implicitly include descending growth processes.

• Wisdom skills/development: An umbrella term, not tied specifically to any model or assessment method, for mature levels of ego or meaning-making development that encompasses capacities referred to in contemplative or spiritual traditions and in the philosophy of applied ethics. Its development also involves ascending and descending movements. Though there are many non-developmental theories of wisdom, we propose that ego development stands out within developmental approaches as a model of wisdom skill.

• Cognitive structure (or "structure"): here used to refer broadly to explicit and tacit beliefs, knowledge, mental and behavioral habits, capacities, skills, preferences, memories, etc. These can include "shadow structures." • Structural shadow (or occlusion). Has two senses. The first refers broadly to how all developed cognitive structures occlude (block, distort, hide, or inhibit) some prior structures, i.e., "put into shadow." The second is the more general notion of maladaptive cognitive structures, compensatory and defense patterns, repressed or suppressed mental content, trauma conditioning, etc., that occlude needed or important prior structures.

• Ascending structural growth: Refers to processes of increasing hierarchical complexity, i.e., traditional models of cognitive development and skill maturation. Includes all learned structures, including those eventually deemed maladaptive.

• Descending structural release (or ablation): Refers broadly to processes of loosening or dismantling, deconstructing or seeing through cognitive structures. Often used in the context of releasing structures no longer deemed useful, but can apply to any structure. "Ablation" is used to emphasize the release of an "occlusion," which then can lead to processes of recovery, reintegration, reorganization, and/or insight.

• State: An ephemeral state of cognitive or brain activity, associated with phenomenological experience and/or neuronal "firing." • Stage (or developmental stage): A stable cognitive trait built through developmental processes of learning, associated with stable skill acquisition and neuronal "wiring.
