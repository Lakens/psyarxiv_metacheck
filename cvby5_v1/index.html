Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Comparing Time-and Event-Contingent Ecological Momentary Assessments of Daily Stressors

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies aim at the repeated collection of real-time data on behavior and experiences in people's natural environments (Shiffman et al., 2008). Although they have been a part of psychological research since the early 1990s, their number has multiplied over the past decade with the availability of new data-collection technologies (Delooz et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2024). The popularity of EMA studies in psychological research is not surprising as they offer a unique access to within-person variability and change processes in people's real-life contexts. Overall, the EMA approach reduces distortions due to recall biases, which are common in retrospective assessments, and maximizes the ecological validity of findings (Shiffman et al., 2008; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). However, within the scope of EMA studies, there are different assessment methods which vary in the frequency of assessments, the degree of retrospection and the role participants play in determining the timing of data collection. Each of these methods offers particular strength and weaknesses with respect to different applications.

In time-contingent EMA designs, people receive a predetermined number of prompts per day, either following a fixed schedule (e.g., at 10am, 3pm and 8pm) or at random within predefined time-intervals (e.g., between 9 and 11am, between 1 and 3pm, etc.). Ideally, the sampling schedule should match the anticipated frequency of the phenomenon of interest, hence, this design requires certain prior knowledge in this regard (Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023).

In event-contingent designs, on the other hand, data is only collected when the phenomenon of interest occurs. Typically, participants make this decision by themselves and initiate the corresponding survey in the assessment tool. These EMA designs are applied when targeting specific or rare events which, or when the timescale of the construct of interest is unknown (Doherty et al., 2020; Fritz et al., 2024). Although event-contingent approaches offer compelling advantages for many applications, we know surprisingly little about compliance in these designs, the reliability with which individuals report the phenomena of interest, or the qualitative features of these reports. The current study aims to fill this gap based on a 14-day EMA study, which combines time-and event contingent assessments of daily stressors. We investigate the frequency and reliability of event-contingent assessments over the course of the study, examine person variables associated with compliance and event-contingent reporting, and evaluate qualitative difference between time-and event-contingent assessments.

Compliance in Ecological Momentary Assessment Studies

When conducting longitudinal studies intensively following participants over several weeks with multiple assessments per day, compliance is key for obtaining high quality data, which are the basis for reliable and valid results (Rintala et al., 2019; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). A participant is compliant to the study protocol, if he completes a survey when he is supposed to (Waddell, 2026). In theory, the frequent prompts received in time-contingent designs increase the burden on participants, which can have a negative impact on study compliance as compared to event-contingent designs, where participants actively trigger the survey only in relevant situations (Hultgren et al., 2020). Compliance rates reported for EMA studies are typically between 70% and 80% (Fritz et al., 2024; Howard & Lamb, 2024; Rintala et al., 2019; Waddell, 2026). However, evidence on compliance in event-contingent designs is sparse, as its evaluation requires additional time-contingent reports as reference or objective (e.g., physiological) measures of the phenomenon of interest (Waddell, 2026).

For the design of EMA studies, the question how compliance changes over the course of the study, is even more important. Such information can help determine at which point participants might need some extra reminders or incentives to stay motivated (Rintala et al., 2019). Multiple time-contingent EMA studies indicate, that response rates systematically decrease over the course of the study (Janek et al., 2025; Rintala et al., 2019; Howard & Lamb, 2024; Runyan et al., 2013). Based on these findings, the number of event-contingent reports should also systematically decrease towards the end of the assessment period.

However, a recent study by Ji et al. (2025) found that participants in an event-contingent EMA design showed relatively stable compliance rates throughout the study. Moreover, research suggests that the awareness for the phenomenon of interest can increase over the course of the study, which may lead to even higher response rates in event-contingent designs towards the end of the assessment period (Runyan et al., 2013).

Beyond such general trends, one specific concern with respect to event-contingent approaches is that participants may not always be able or willing to initiate surveys in relevant situations (Waddell, 2026). This bears a risk of confounding: Covariates of substantive interest to the study may influence the ability to detect events or to initiate the survey in the respective situation. Studies on alcohol consumption already found missing event-contingent assessments to be associated with increased alcohol use and intoxication (Hultgren et al., 2020). The same problem may arise in EMA studies of daily stressors, because stress itself might prevent the person from responding to a survey.

Due to this great uncertainty regarding study compliance in event-contingent EMA designs, it is particularly important to understand whether there are any person-level variables associated with the frequency of event-contingent reporting. However, knowledge about person-level predictors of compliance in EMA studies is sparse, especially in eventcontingent designs (Waddell, 2026). Previous studies mostly focus on demographic information, like gender, age, or mental health status. While some studies report higher compliance for female and older participants (e.g., Rintala et al., 2019 , Schüz et al., 2014), others found no such effects (Howard & Lamb, 2024; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). With respect to mental health status, studies usually report no effect on compliance (Howard & Lamb, 2024; Rintala et al., 2019; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). Overall, the body of research in this area is rather limited and consists of only a few studies on demographic predictors of compliance in time-contingent designs.

Qualitative differences between time-and event-contingent assessments

Time-and event contingent assessments may not only differ in compliance, they also offer some unique qualitative features that need to be considered. As compared to eventcontingent approaches, which assess the phenomenon of interest directly in the relevant situation, time-contingent assessments often involve a certain degree of retrospection, which may introduce recall-bias in the assessment (Fritz et al., 2024; Neubauer et al., 2020; Zygar-Hoffmann & Schönbrodt, 2020). Empirical evidence suggests, that ratings of the respective phenomenon may be more extreme in retrospective assessments (Neubauer et al., 2020).

Time-contingent assessments may also show a greater variability as the behavior or experiences are assessed in more heterogenous contexts as compared event-contingent approaches. One study by Dawood et al. (2020) indeed found greater variability in signalcontingent assessments of affect, while mean ratings were more extreme in event-contingent designs. However, we found no study to systematically investigate qualitative differences between time-and event-contingent assessments across different dimensions, even though such evidence is crucial for selecting the appropriate assessment method for the research question at hand.

The current study

Although event-contingent approaches offer particular advantages for many phenomena and research aims, we know almost nothing about the factors that influence the successful application of these designs and the qualitative differences between event-and time-contingent assessments (Waddell, 2026). Only few studies combine time-contingent and event-contingent assessments, which is necessary to separate study compliance and event-reporting frequency (Dawood et al., 2020; Himmelstein et al., 2019). The few studies which do combine both assessment methods, rarely investigate person-level predictors or qualitative differences (Rintala et al., 2019).

The first aim of this study was to investigate the frequency and reliability of eventcontingent EMA assessments of daily stressors over the course of the study. Specifically, we examine how frequently people self-initiate assessments over a study-period of 14 days (RQ 1.1), which types of stressors are reported particularly frequently in event-contingent assessments (RQ 1.2) and whether the number of event-contingent reports increases or decreases over the course of the study (RQ 1.3). While research suggests that overall compliance tends to decrease over the course of the study (Janek et al., 2025; Rintala et al., 2019 , Howard & Lamb, 2024), the awareness for stressful events might increase (Runyan et al., 2013). We also address a concern frequently raised in the literature -that participants might not be able or willing to answer a survey in a relevant situation, for example, when they are stressed (Hultgren et al., 2020; Tate et al., 2024; Waddell, 2026). We thus examine, whether retrospective time-contingent assessments indicate missed event-contingent assessments in high-stress situations (RQ 1.4).

As mentioned above, there is only limited research on person-level predictors of compliance in EMA studies and even less is known about person characteristics associated with reporting frequencies in event-contingent designs. The second aim of this study is therefor to examine whether stress beliefs (RQ 2.1), personality (RQ 2.2) and mental health (RQ 2.3) predict compliance in time-contingent assessments and whether these factors also predict the frequency of event-contingent reports when controlling for compliance (RQ 3.1 -3.3).

The third aim of this study is to investigate whether event-contingent reports qualitatively differ from time-contingent reports. While one study suggest that time-contingent reports may show greater variability (Dawood et al., 2020), there is conflicting evidence on whether mean ratings should be more or less extreme in event-contingent reports (Dawood et al., 2020; Neubauer et al., 2020). We are thus going to investigate, whether timeand event-contingent assessments differ in terms of mean and variability with respect to stress intensity (RQ 4.1), the appraisal of stressors (RQ 4.2), affect (RQ 4.3) and somatic symptoms (RQ 4.4).

Study Design and Participants

Participants of this study were recruited from German universities using university online platforms for research participation, notifications via lecturers and mailing lists between March and September 2024. To be eligible to participate in the study they had to a) be able to comprehend the German language, b) be at least 18 years old and c) provide informed consent (inclusion criteria). We excluded people who participated in another (intervention) study during the study period. Participants had the chance to win gift certificates as compensation. Data collection was carried out with the application M-Path using participant's own mobile devices. At the beginning of the study, participants filled out the baseline questionnaire comprising demographics and various person-and health related variables.

From the following day, they took part in an EMA study on daily stressors over 14 consecutive days with three time-contingent assessments per day for which they received prompts at randomly selected times within predefined time intervals (morning: 8 a.m. -12:20 p.m., afternoon: 12:20 p.m. -4:40 p.m. and evening: 4:40 p.m. -9 p.m.). In these assessments, participants were asked to retrospectively report the experience of stressors in the previous time interval since the last assessment. Additionally, participants were instructed to initiate event-contingent assessment whenever they experienced stressful events. To create a common frame of reference, a definition of stressful events (accessible at all times) was provided via the app: "Being stressed means that you are in a situation that is personally meaningful to you and whose management places physical and/or psychological demands on you. This may involve everyday, one-time, recurring, or extraordinary situations or events.

Examples of potentially stressful situations include hurrying to arrive at work on time; exerting oneself during an exam to achieve the best possible grade; working faster or longer to meet a deadline; performing at one's best in an athletic competition in order to win; meeting new people; experiencing an interpersonal conflict; a separation; illness or injury; being threatened, insulted, discriminated against, or ignored; bereavement; financial difficulties; or moving." At the end of the 14-day EMA period, participant filled out the follow-up questionnaire comprising the same measures as the baseline questionnaire (except for demographics). Data collection was ended when the sample size approached the target number of about 200 participants and only few new participants could be recruited.

Of the 177 people who participated in the study, 4 were excluded from the analyses for not providing data at baseline, hence, the final sample consisted of N = 173 individuals. All participants were students of German universities with a mean age of 22.63 (SD = 4.79). Most of them (86%) identified as female. Less than half (40.4%) were in a relationship and only 6 of the students reported to have one or more children. Measures

Baseline and Follow-up Questionnaire

Personality. Personality traits were assessed with the Big Five Inventory 10 (BFI-10, Rammstedt & John, 2007). The inventory comprises 10 items, 2 for each of the Big Five personality dimensions. The Items (e.g., Neuroticism: "I see myself as someone who gets nervous easily.") are answered on a 5-point scale from 1 (Disagree strongly) to 5 (Agree strongly). Reliabilities range from α = .37 (Agreeableness) to α = .74 (Extraversion).

Anxiety. Anxiety was assessed with seven items of the General Anxiety Disorder 7 scale (GAD-7; Spitzer et al. 2006). Participants are instructed to rate how often they feel affected by the listed symptoms (e.g., "Excessive worry about various matters"). Items are answered on a 4-point scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day). A higher value on the total (mean) score indicates higher anxiety, the reliability was Ω = .84.

Depression.

Depressiveness was measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire 8 (PHQ-8, Kroenke & Spitzer, 2002). Participants rate how often they feel affected by the listed symptoms (e.g., "Depression, melancholy, or hopelessness"). The eight items are answered on a 4-point scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day). Higher values indicate higher depressiveness, the reliability of the total (mean) score was Ω = .82.

Beliefs about Stress.

Stress Beliefs were assessed with the Beliefs About Stress Scale (BASS, Laferton et al., 2018). The BASS captures positive stress beliefs (BASS-P, four items, example: "Being stressed enables me to work in a more focused manner"), negative stress beliefs (BASS-N, eight items, example: "Being stressed affects my health in the short term"), and controllability beliefs (BASS-C, three items, example: "Being stressed is something I am able to influence through my actions"). Items are answered on a 4-point scale from 1 (completely disagree) to 4 (definitely agree). Reliabilities were Ω = .76 for negative beliefs, Ω = .85 for positive beliefs and Ω = .75 for controllability beliefs.

Chronic Stress.

Chronic Stress was assessed with the nine-item version of the

Inventory of Chronic Stress (TICS-9; Petrowski et al., 2019). Participants were instructed to rate to which extend the listed experiences (e.g., "I have to fulfill obligations that I inwardly reject") apply to them on a 5-point scale from 0 (never) to 4 (very often). The reliability of the total (mean) score was Ω = .77, higher values indicate stronger chronic stress.

EMA Assessments

Stress Intensity. Stress intensity was assessed with the Brief Daily Stressors Screening Tool (BDSST, Scholten et al., 2020). The ten items (e.g., "Difficulties with family obligations (e.g., household chores, caregiving, raising children, school)") of the BDSST cover stressors from various life domains. Participants are instructed to rate strain from the listed stressors on a 5-point scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very strong). Higher values of the total (sum) score indicate a higher stress intensity, the reliability was Ω = .83.

Stress Appraisals.

Stress appraisals in terms of threat and challenge were assessed based on the Stress Appraisals of Acute Stress scale (SAAS; Mendes et al., 2007) with two items following the adaptation of Yeager et al. (2022). One item for threat ("I felt threatened by this situation") and challenge ("I felt that the situation challenged me in a positive way") were rated on a 7-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Affect.

Positive and negative affect were assessed with five items each using the short form of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS-SF, Watson et al., 1988).

Participants were asked to report how they were feeling (e.g., "inspired" for positive affect) on a 5-point scale from 1 ("very little or not at all") to 5 ("extremely"). Reliabilities of total (mean) scores were Ω = .87 for positive affect and Ω = .79 for negative affect.

Somatic Symptoms. Somatic symptoms were assessed with eight items of the Somatic Symptoms Scale (SSS-8, Gierk et al., 2014). Participants are instructed to rate to what extent they feel affected by the listed symptoms (e.g., "Headaches"). Items are answered on a 5-point scale from 0 ("not at all" to 4 "very strong"). Higher values on the total (sum) score indicate stronger somatic symptoms, the reliability was Ω = .83.

Analyses

The first aim of this study is to investigate the overall frequencies of time-and evencontingent reports over the course of the 14-day EMA period and by stressor domain (RQ 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3). We calculate the respective statistics and illustrate the trends in frequencies and compliance rates over the course of the study with plots. To clearly separate compliance trends over the course of the study from study drop-out, these plots are based on studycompleters. We additionally examine whether retrospective time-contingent reports indicate missed event-contingent assessments in high-stress situations (RQ 1.4). To answer this question, we estimate multilevel models predicting event-contingent reports (1 = an event was reported,0 = no event was reported) in the time-interval before a time-contingent assessment by a) the occurrence of at least one very stressful event (score of 4) and b) the overall stress intensity. As a second source of evidence, we regressed the person-specific number of eventcontingent reports over the 14-day period on the chronic stress score at follow-up. We applied a negative binomial regression model to predict this count outcome, as most participants triggered less than three event-contingent assessments and an alternative Poisson model indicated overdispersion.

The second aim of this study was to examine person variables associated with compliance and the number of event-contingent reports. Compliance was operationalized as the number of answered time-contingent assessments. With three fixed assessments per day and a study duration of 14 days, 42 assessments would indicate perfect compliance. To predict compliance, we estimated separate linear regression models for stress beliefs (RQ 2.1), personality traits (RQ 2.2) and depression and anxiety (2.3). We conducted homoscedasticity checks due to the non-normal distribution of the count data. To predict the person-specific number of event-contingent assessments (RQ 3.1 -3.3) we again estimated negative binomial regression models to account for the distribution of the event-contingent counts including the same predictors as above, but additionally controlling for compliance in time-contingent assessments.

Finally, to examine qualitative differences between time-and event-contingent assessments, we compared the mean ratings of stress intensity (RQ 4.1), stress appraisal (RQ 4.2), affect (RQ 4.3) and somatic symptoms (RQ 4.4) with t-test. In case the assumption of equal variances was violated, we use a Welch t-test. Variances of time-and event-contingent assessments were compared using Levene's Test. In order to ensure a fair comparison, we only included time-contingent reports which actually indicated the occurrence of stressors (stress sum score > 0) in these analyses. We used the standard alpha-level of .05. All analyses were carried out in R (R Core Team, 2026). Analyses scripts and data are openly accessible via the Open Science Framework (blinded link: https://osf.io/wdy4s/overview?view_only=d314d095cb634eb2bc18963955cb9cf1).

Transparency and Openness

All research questions and analyses have been preregistered (blinded link: https://osf.io/m7ezw/overview?view_only=a6ecd38e481846e28b7340a76ba0a698). Some deviations from our preregistration were necessary after inspecting the data. First, we noticed that some participants continued to occasionally trigger event-contingent assessments after the end of the 14-day EMA-period. We decided against including these observations in our main analyses as these assessments may be qualitatively different from the assessments within the study period and we could not ensure that our exclusion criterium was still met after the end of the study. However, we conducted some exploratory (not preregistered) analyses to examine these additional observations. Second, as most participants triggered very few (three or less) event-contingent reports over the 14-day EMA period, we had to simplify our analyses for RQ 1.3 and 1.4 and had to use negative binomial regression models whenever predicting event-contingent counts. We also decided against controlling for age and gender in person-level regression models as our sample was quite homogenous in this regard.

Results

Over the 14-day EMA period, participants received 42 prompts (3 per day) to complete time-contingent assessments. On average, they completed 27.78 (SD = 12.35) of these questionnaires, which corresponds to an overall completion rate of 66.1%. Participants were additionally instructed to self-initiate event-contingent assessments in case of stressful experiences, of which they triggered 2.39 on average (SD = 2.14). The trends of time-and event-contingent reports (only including study completers) over the course of the study are depicted in Figure 1. Both curves indicate a clear decrease of response rates over the course of the study, whereby especially the number of event-contingent reports dropped considerably after the first few days. Time-and event-contingent reports also showed strong concordance with respect to frequencies of stressor domains (see Table 2, for details). In both time-and event-contingent reports stress at the workplace was reported most frequently (31.4% / 30.6%), followed by stressors concerning ones living situation. Health problems were least frequently reported in both time-and event contingent assessments (17.3%).

To investigate whether event-contingent assessments may be missed in high-stress situations, we first predicted the self-initiated report of a stressful event within a time-period by the retrospective report of at least one very stressful event (intensity > 3) at the next timecontingent assessment. Second, we used the retrospective overall stress intensity (personmean centered) and the person-specific stress mean to predict event contingent reporting in the previous time-interval, the results can be found in Table 3. Both analyses indicate that the probability of self-initiating a stress assessment was significantly higher when more intense stress was retrospectively reported. We additionally examined whether the chronic stress assessment at follow-up was associated with the number of event contingent reports over the EMA-period, but found no significant association (Incidence Rate Ratio: 1.08, 95% CI [0.76, 1.53], p = .651).

The second aim of our study was to investigate person-level predictors of compliance and of the frequency of event-contingent reports, the results can be found in Table 4.

Compliance, the outcome in the first series of models, was operationalized as the number of time-contingent responses over the study period. Conscientiousness showed a significant positive effect on compliance, a one unit increase in Conscientiousness was associated with answering 3.01 assessments more over the study period. Extraversion showed a significant negative effect on study compliance. However, neither stress beliefs nor mental health (depression and anxiety) were associated with compliance in time-contingent reports. In the second series of models, we examined person-level predictors of the number of eventcontingent reports while controlling for individual compliance. In the first model, negative stress beliefs showed a negative effect and positive stress beliefs a positive effect on the number of event-contingent reports, indicating that people tend to report more events with more positive and less negative stress beliefs. With respect to personality, only openness was associated with increased event-reporting behavior. In terms of mental health, both anxiety and depression indicated significant effects. While anxiety was associated with initiating more event-contingent reports, depression showed a negative effect on the number of eventcontingent assessments.

The third aim of this study was to investigate qualitative difference between time-and event-contingent assessments in terms of stress intensity, stress appraisals, affect and somatic symptoms, which are reported in Table 5. We found significant mean differences between time-and event-contingent reports with respect to all of these dimensions: Event-contingent assessments indicated higher mean values with respect to stress intensity, threat appraisal, negative and positive affect, and somatic symptoms. Only challenge evaluations were higher in time-contingent reports. Moreover, event-contingent reports indicated a higher standard deviation for threat appraisal, negative affect and somatic symptoms.

Discussion

The present study combined time-and event-contingent assessments to investigate how reliable events are reported over the course of a 14-day EMA study of daily stressors.

Using both assessment methods we were able to independently examine person-level predictors of compliance and event-reporting frequency. Moreover, qualitative differences between time-and event-contingent assessments were examined across multiple dimensions from stress intensity to somatic symptoms. In the following, we discuss our findings in the light of the available evidence and derive recommendations for future research.

With about 66% answered time-contingent questionnaires, the compliance in our study was slightly below the rates of 70 -80% reported in the literature (Fritz et al., 2024; Howard & Lamb, 2024; Rintala et al., 2019; Waddell, 2026). While compliance can be expected to decrease over the course of the study (Janek et al., 2025; Rintala et al., 2019; Howard & Lamb, 2024; Runyan et al., 2013), it had been argued that people might develop an increased sensitivity to the phenomenon of interest over the course of the study, which would result in increasing or at least stable event-contingent reporting rates over time (Runyan et al., 2013).

However, we found no indications of such a trend. The number of self-initiated surveys strongly decreased after the first few days of the study, which lead to a very small number of event-contingent reports per person with most participants reporting only three or less events over the 14-day period. One explanation is that participants quickly realized they had enough other opportunities to report their stressful events during the regularly conducted timecontingent assessments three times a day. Future studies, which aim to focus on eventcontingent assessments, may emphasize their importance more strongly in the instruction and complement them by fewer time-contingent assessments, to obtain a sufficient number of event-contingent reports.

One specific concern raised in the literature with respect to event-contingent approaches is that the phenomenon of interest itself may hinder the reporting of events, which has already been found for the assessment of drinking behavior (Waddell, 2026). With regard to the assessment of stress, it can be hypothesized that people in high-stress situations may not be willing or able to answer a questionnaire. However, our findings do not indicate such difficulties. On the contrary, we found that the probability of self-initiating an assessment was higher when more intense stress was retrospectively reported. This also fits with our analyses on qualitative differences between time-and event-contingent assessments, which indicate higher stress intensity, more negative affect and stronger somatic symptoms in eventcontingent reports on average. Together, these findings contradict the notion that people in stressful situations are unable or unwilling to complete questionnaires. On the contrary, they suggest that particularly extreme events actually motivate people to report them in eventcontingent assessments.

With regard to person-level factors that may contribute to a successful participation in EMA studies, conscientiousness was positively associated with study compliance while extraversion showed a negative effect. We are unaware of any EMA study reporting effects of personality traits on study compliance, but our findings fit with research on compliance in high-frequency panel studies, which also indicate positive effects of conscientiousness and negative effects of extraversion on the number survey responses (Saw & Kapteyn, 2025). In line with previous EMA studies, we found no effects of mental health variables on study compliance (Howard & Lamb, 2024; Rintala et al., 2019; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023).

Beyond study compliance, we were also interested in the person-level covariates that may have affected the frequency of event-contingent reporting. First, positive stress beliefs were associated with a higher number of event-contingent reports over the 14-day study period. This seems plausible as stress-positive individuals might feel less overwhelmed by a stressful situation and may therefore have greater cognitive capacities to think about the study and answer the survey. This also fits with the positive effect found by openness. However, higher anxiety also contributed to an increased reporting behavior while depressive symptoms were negatively associated with the number of event-contingent reports. The fact that the number of event-contingent assessment provided by participants depends on certain person characteristics is an important information to consider for the design of future EMA studies.

Relying solely on event-contingent assessments could introduce bias in that observations of individuals with certain characteristics may be overrepresented in the sample. This suggests that such designs should be complemented by fixed assessments or that event-contingent reports should be balanced across participants.

The final aim of our study was to examine qualitative differences between time-and event-contingent assessments. In line with previous research (Dawood et al., 2020), eventcontingent assessments in our study were more extreme with higher mean values across all dimensions except for challenge appraisals. With respect to somatic symptoms, on average, about three times as many symptoms were reported in event-contingent assessments compared to time-contingent assessments. As mentioned above, more extreme situations may remind people of the study and motivate them to initiate a survey to report on their experiences. However, we found no support for the theory that time-contingent reports may show a higher variability because these assessments take place in more diverse settings. On the contrary, the variance in event-contingent tended to be larger, but this may be a methodological artifact as the variance of bounded scales depends on the mean, which was also higher in event-contingent reports (Mestdagh et al., 2018). In sum, it is important to consider that event-contingent reports not only represent a more economical alternative to time-contingent assessments. People seem to report a more extreme selection of events in event-contingent assessments or at least report them as more extreme in the relevant situation. This becomes even more apparent when inspecting the additional event-contingent assessment that were imitated after the end of the study, which indicated even higher mean values than the reports within the study period. These events were reported by a small group of participants who apparently only returned to the app during very extreme situations. Our results suggest that such extra observations should be excluded from the main analyses as they seem to represent a very specific group of events which qualitatively differ from those within the study period and only stem from a small and specific group of participants.

Limitations

Our results should be interpreted in the light of certain limitations, which suggest avenues for future research on this topic. First, this study is based on a quite homogenous sample in terms of age, gender and education. This severely limits the generalizability of our findings to other populations. We therefor strongly encourage researchers to replicate these findings based on a more diverse sample.

Second, in our study we combined time-and event contingent EMA assessments which allowed us to examine whether stressful events were reliably reported in eventcontingent assessments and systematically compare both approaches. However, the number of event-contingent assessments in this study initiated by participants was quite small and rapidly decreased after the first few days of the study. This required us to simplify some aspects of our analyses. One explanation is that participants decided to report their experiences only in the regularly conducted time-contingent assessments instead of triggering additional surveys. Future studies may instruct participants to give priority to eventcontingent reports or employ fewer time-contingent assessments, whenever event-contingent assessments are of primary interest.

Third, our study was solely based on self-reports of stressful events which limited our ability to investigate whether event-contingent assessments were actually rated as more extreme or people only reported particularly extreme events in event-contingent reports.

Future studies should complement self-reports by physiological measures of stress to clearly rule out one of these explanations. Independent physiological measures of stress would have also allowed us to examine the reliability of event-contingent reporting even more precisely.

Conclusion

This study combined time-and event contingent EMA assessments of daily stressors to evaluate the reliability of event-contingent reporting and to qualitatively compare both assessment methods. Our results suggest, that event-contingent assessments not only represent a more economical alternative to a time-contingent design, but that participants apparently choose these assessments to report a more extreme subset of stressful events. Although time-contingent assessments place a greater burden on participants, they may capture a more representative and balanced selection of experiences. Moreover, certain types of participants are particularly likely to self-initiate assessments and may therefore be overrepresented in purely event-contingent designs.
