Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Epilepsy is among the most burdensome neurological disorders worldwide, with an impact that extends far beyond seizure frequency alone. Depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment, and internalized stigma affect a substantial proportion of individuals with epilepsy, yet these comorbidities remain systematically undertreated in routine neurological care. Conventional psychoeducation, largely centered on passive information delivery, has shown limited effectiveness in addressing the psychological and social complexities of living with epilepsy. Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation for Epilepsy (NIPE) offers a fundamentally different approach: one that translates the neuroscience of seizure generation, network dysfunction, and neuroplasticity into accessible frameworks, shifting patients from passive recipients of information to active, metacognitively engaged participants in their own care. This chapter presents NIPE as both a clinical framework and an integrative layer across the continuum of epilepsy management. Beginning with the neurobiological mechanisms underlying seizures and epileptogenesis, it highlights how the same neural networks implicated in ictal activity also contribute to the psychiatric and cognitive comorbidities that shape patients' daily experiences. It further examines how NIPE interfaces with evidence-based psychological interventions, pharmacologic education, neuromodulation, epilepsy surgery, and the management of refractory epilepsy, as well as the unique needs of women and other populations with distinct clinical considerations. In addition, the chapter explores the digital expansion of NIPE, including adaptive telehealth platforms, wearable biosensors, digital phenotyping, and the emerging role of artificial intelligence in tailoring psychoeducational delivery. Importantly, it addresses current limitations and ethical considerations with transparency, including the absence of standardized NIPE protocols and the relative scarcity of randomized controlled trials specifically within epilepsy populations.

The chapter concludes with a forward-looking perspective on the infrastructure, research agenda, and cross-disciplinary collaboration required to embed NIPE into routine epileptology practice. NIPE is positioned not as an adjunct to epilepsy care, but as a unifying framework, a shared language that enables truly integrated, patient-centered care.

1. Introduction

Epilepsy carries a substantial psychosocial burden that extends well beyond the seizures themselves. Depression and anxiety are common psychiatric comorbidities across both pediatric and adult populations, highlighting a clear and persistent unmet need in neurological care. These conditions are not benign; they are consistently associated with worse clinical outcomes, including reduced quality of life and poor medication adherence. At the same time, many epilepsy specialists report that they lack the time, resources, or training to adequately address mood disorders in routine practice (Gandy et al., 2021; Valente et al., 2024).

More than one third of youth and adults with epilepsy will experience clinically significant depression or anxiety at some point in their illness, rates that exceed those seen in other chronic neurological conditions of similar severity (Scott et al., 2020). Stigma adds another, often less visible, layer of burden. Patients frequently conceal their diagnosis from employers, and relationships can be affected in subtle but meaningful ways. In some cultural contexts, epilepsy continues to be framed in terms of contagion or moral failure, and patients internalize these narratives, which in turn worsen psychiatric outcomes regardless of seizure control (Mayor et al., 2021).

2. Epilepsy as a brain network disorder: a neuroscience perspective

Clinical approaches to epilepsy have traditionally treated cognitive and psychiatric symptoms as separate from the neurological condition itself, a division that reflects how medicine is structured more than how the brain actually functions. A neuroscience-informed perspective challenges this separation. Epilepsy is not simply a disorder of recurrent seizures; it is thought to be a disorder of dynamic, network-level brain organization, and that distinction has real clinical implications (Ekhtiari et al., 2017).

In conditions characterized by behavioral and cognitive symptoms, modern psychoeducation increasingly directs attention to key neural networks: the Salience Network, Default Mode Network, Attention Network, and Executive Control Network. In epileptology, this framework shifts the focus away from isolated, episodic electrical events and toward the brain's continuous functional state between seizures.

This perspective becomes particularly important when considering that epileptogenic networks do not reset between seizures. The interictal period, often implicitly treated as neurologically "quiet," is in fact characterized by persistent network instability, altered connectivity, and ongoing functional disruption.

These changes directly contribute to the cognitive and psychiatric symptoms patients experience day to day. Seizures are often the visible expression of a process that is continuously active beneath the surface. For many patients, this reframing provides, often for the first time, a neurological explanation for persistent cognitive fog, emotional dysregulation, or fatigue, rather than attributing these symptoms to medications, aging, or personal limitation. The sections that follow explore the specific mechanisms through which this network instability develops and propagates (González et al., 2019; Avoli et al., 2022).

3. Neurobiological mechanisms underlying seizures and epileptogenesis

Under normal conditions, neuronal networks maintain a tightly regulated balance between excitatory glutamate and inhibitory GABA signaling. When that balance shifts, the effects are anything but subtle. Excess excitatory activity can overwhelm local inhibitory control, creating focal regions of hyperexcitability that form the substrate for seizure generation (Avoli et al., 2022; González et al., 2019).

The transition from a localized interictal disturbance to a clinical seizure depends on progressive ionic and network-level changes that may lower the seizure threshold. Beyond the underlying hyperexcitability that characterizes epilepsy, behavioral, social, and environmental factors can further modulate this state and trigger seizures. For example, it is well established that sleep deprivation and alcohol use can precipitate seizures in susceptible individuals. In certain, less common scenarios, seizures may occur in a cyclic pattern or be triggered by specific factors, as seen in reflex epilepsies (e.g., flashing lights, reading or writing, hot baths, or other visual stimuli). Recognizing and explaining these patterns to patients is critical, both for improving seizure control and for strengthening patient education.

Translating these mechanisms into clinically meaningful concepts enhances patient understanding. When patients recognize that seizure threshold is dynamic, and can be lowered by factors such as sleep deprivation, psychological stress, or missed medications, their experience becomes more coherent. 4.2 Cognitive comorbidities and neurodevelopmental impacts

Cognitive impairment affects approximately 30-40% of individuals with epilepsy, although this estimate masks significant variability based on epilepsy type, age at onset, and seizure burden (Novak et al., 2022). Memory is the most consistently affected domain, followed by attention and executive function. The specific pattern of impairment depends largely on the location of epileptiform activity: left temporal lobe involvement more often affects verbal memory and language, whereas right temporal or frontal lobe involvement disrupts visuospatial abilities and executive processes (Khalife et al., 2022).

The mechanisms extend beyond the seizures themselves. Interictal epileptiform discharges, present between seizures and often not perceived by patients, can continuously interfere with the encoding of new information. Working memory is particularly vulnerable, as it relies on precise temporal coordination across networks, the very process that chronic epileptiform activity disrupts (Khalife et al., 2022; Novak et al., 2022). In children, the impact is amplified by developmental timing. Seizure onset during critical periods interferes with synaptogenesis, white matter maturation, and the development of cognitive reserve, leading to deficits that may persist into adulthood even when seizures are later controlled (Operto et al., 2023).

Anti-seizure medications introduce an additional layer. Polytherapy, in particular, is associated with measurable cognitive effects, and patients often attribute their cognitive slowing primarily to medication without recognizing the contribution of the underlying network dysfunction. This distinction matters, both for adherence and for how patients interpret their own experience. Within a NIPE framework, presenting cognitive impairment as multifactorial, arising from network disruption, medication effects, and cumulative seizure burden, offers a more accurate and less self-blaming understanding (Kiriakopoulos et al., 2025; Rezapour et al., 2021).

4.3 Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES)/Functional seizures and network framing

Functional seizures (previously referred to as psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, PNES) are paroxysmal events that clinically resemble epileptic seizures but can be distinguished by an experienced neurologist and lack an EEG correlate. They are currently classified as a subtype of functional neurological disorder and are thought to arise from disrupted functional connectivity within networks involved in emotional processing, motor control, and self-regulation, rather than from epileptiform hyperexcitability (Szaflarski and LaFrance, 2022). Between 20% and 30% of patients referred to epilepsy centers for drug-resistant seizures are ultimately found to have functional seizures, many having been treated with anti-seizure medications for years before the correct diagnosis is made Patients may experience this change as a form of deception or reversal. Those who have organized their illness identity around a neurological diagnosis are suddenly repositioned, and this transition is often perceived as delegitimizing.

Historical terminology, particularly "pseudoseizures," has further contributed to this perception by implying fabrication or intentionality. A neuroscience-based framework offers a more constructive approach. Presenting functional seizures as a disorder of brain network function, in which real symptoms arise from genuine disruptions in connectivity, preserves the biological validity of the patient's experience while correcting the diagnosis (Demir and Sertel Berk, 2025).

Within a NIPE framework, functional seizures can be explained using the same network-based language already introduced in the context of epilepsy. Rather than being framed as a separate or more stigmatizing condition, they become another manifestation of network dysregulation: one involving emotional-motor integration rather than neuronal hyperexcitability. This distinction is clinically meaningful. Patients who understand functional seizures as a brain-based functional disorder are more likely to engage with treatment, particularly psychological interventions, which remain the primary evidence-based approach.

At the same time, this framing reduces the risk that patients will experience the diagnosis as a withdrawal of care (Michaelis et al., 2018; Szaflarski and LaFrance, 2022).

4.4 SUDEP and other comorbidities

Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is the leading cause of epilepsyrelated mortality in adults, with an estimated incidence ranging from 0.6 to 2.0 per 1,000 patient-years in the general epilepsy population, with higher rates observed in refractory epilepsy cohorts (Sun et al., 2023). The pathophysiology of SUDEP involves seizure-induced dysregulation of cardiorespiratory function, with centrally mediated apnea, autonomic dysfunction, and cardiac arrhythmias converging into a fatal sequence, most commonly following generalized tonicclonic seizures during sleep (Sun et al., 2023). Modifiable risk factors include seizure frequency, nocturnal supervision, and medication adherence, precisely the domains that NIPE targets. Framing SUDEP within a NIPE discussion shifts it from an unpredictable outcome to one in which patients have meaningful agency (Mesraoua et al., 2022; Wheless et al., 2018).

Despite this, surveys consistently show that the majority of patients have never been counseled about SUDEP, and many neurologists remain hesitant to raise the topic unprompted, often due to concerns about causing distress (Mesraoua et al., 2022). This reluctance may stem from a desire to avoid emotional discomfort for both the patient and the clinician, or at times from underestimating the clinical importance of the discussion. However, even if the conversation is uncomfortable, it remains essential. Addressing SUDEP is directly tied to improving adherence, follow-up, and risk reduction. Patients who are unaware of SUDEP cannot make informed decisions about lifestyle modifications or treatment strategies that meaningfully reduce their risk.

Beyond SUDEP, epilepsy carries a substantial burden of additional comorbidities that are often underrecognized in clinical practice. Sleep disorders, chronic pain, cardiovascular risk, and reproductive health issues all interact with seizure control and overall quality of life in ways that patients are rarely guided to connect. NIPE creates the space to make these links explicit, helping patients understand epilepsy as a systemic neurological condition rather than an isolated disorder of seizures (Gandy et al., 2021; Valente et al., 2024).

Patients with epilepsy often face challenges similar to those seen in other chronic medical conditions, including denial of the diagnosis, the need for long-term daily therapy, and the broader implications of living with drug-resistant epilepsy.

While thorough and thoughtful discussions in the clinical setting can help address these issues, they may at times evolve into anxiety or depression that require more targeted, professional intervention beyond routine clinical counseling.

The ILAE Psychology Task Force has identified cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) as the most robustly supported psychological intervention for both adults and children with epilepsy, with demonstrated benefits across depression, anxiety, and quality of life outcomes (Michaelis et For patients with functional seizures, this integration is particularly important.

CBT remains the primary evidence-based treatment for functional seizures, yet engagement and retention are often limited, in part because patients lack a framework that makes psychological treatment feel neurologically grounded.

Framing functional seizures as a disorder of network function, and CBT as an intervention that directly targets that network, helps address this barrier and improves treatment acceptance (Moro et al., 2024).

6.2 Pharmacological treatments

Anti-seizure medications (ASMs) remain the cornerstone of epilepsy management, yet a substantial proportion of patients are nonadherent.

Forgetfulness, side effect burden, and the perception that the medication is not working are among the most commonly cited reasons (Syam et al., 2024) NIPE addresses this at the level of mechanism. Explaining to patients, for example, that sodium channel-blocking medications stabilize the ionic environment that determines seizure threshold, or that GABAergic agents restore inhibitory tone to a network that is overly excitable, changes the clinical conversation. The medication becomes part of a coherent framework rather than a directive. Side effects, often experienced as arbitrary or concerning, also become more interpretable when patients understand the mechanism behind them. Cognitive slowing with topiramate, for instance, is less alarming and more manageable when patients understand why a medication that broadly reduces excitability may also affect processing speed (Syam et al., 2024; Mula et al., 2022).

It is also important to counsel patients that some side effects, particularly mild ones, are expected and often transient, frequently improving within 7 to 10 days. Setting this expectation in advance can improve tolerance and adherence. In addition, some patients discontinue medications abruptly after periods of remission under the mistaken belief that they are cured. Clarifying that these medications control network excitability, and are often required for sustained remission, is essential. This approach does not require pharmacological expertise from the patient. It requires providing enough context for the treatment to feel logical rather than imposed. That shift, from passive compliance to informed engagement, is one of the more reliable levers for improving long-term adherence in a condition that often requires ongoing therapy (Ekhtiari et al., 2017; Rezapour et al., 2021).

6.3 NIPE and medical refractory epilepsy

Drug-resistant epilepsy, defined as the failure of two appropriately chosen and tolerated anti-seizure medication regimens, affects approximately one third of people with epilepsy. In this group, the clinical picture is rarely limited to seizures alone. Rates of depression in refractory epilepsy are commonly reported between 30-50%, with rates as high as 54% in some presurgical cohorts (Friedrich et al., 2022). The combination of ongoing seizures, polypharmacy, and repeated treatment failures creates a distinct psychological burden that standard psychoeducation often does not adequately address (Friedrich et At the same time, in many patients, the exact reason for medical resistance remains unclear, which can make these discussions more complex. It is important to explain that identifying a precise cause is not always necessary to move forward with effective management. Shifting the focus toward alternative treatment strategies, such as surgical evaluation, neuromodulation, or dietary therapies, becomes central in these cases.

For patients with refractory epilepsy, NIPE also supports the transition from a cure-oriented mindset to one focused on quality of life. This shift can be difficult, particularly for patients who have not been given a neurobiological framework to understand why seizure freedom may not be achievable. That framework does not eliminate hope; rather, it redirects it toward achievable and meaningful goals (Friedrich et al., 2022; van Hezik-Wester et al., 2022).

6.4 NIPE and epilepsy surgery

Epilepsy surgery offers the possibility of seizure freedom for carefully selected patients with drug-resistant focal epilepsy, yet it remains chronically underutilized, with a significant portion of this underutilization rooted in psychological factors. Patients referred for surgical evaluation often present with misconceptions about risk, unrealistic expectations regarding outcomes, and an illness identity so centered around managing epilepsy that the prospect of seizure freedom itself can generate anxiety rather than relief. This phenomenon has been described as the "burden of normality," referring to the disorientation that may follow when a condition that has long structured a patient's life is suddenly removed (Mulligan and Carniello, 2023). NIPE across the surgical pathway operates at three distinct stages. Preoperatively, it supports informed decision-making by helping patients understand the purpose of the evaluation process, the importance of precise localization of the epileptogenic zone, and the range of realistic outcomes associated with different surgical approaches. Patients who understand that risks such as memory decline after temporal lobe surgery are predictable and quantifiable, rather than random complications, are better positioned to weigh these risks against the burden of ongoing seizures (Patrikelis et al., 2023). In this context, informed consent is not a single event but an ongoing educational process, and NIPE provides the framework that makes it truly informed rather than simply procedural.

Postoperatively, psychoeducational needs shift significantly. Functional recovery milestones, such as return to work, independent living, and stable relationships, Vagus nerve stimulation works by delivering rhythmic electrical impulses to the left vagus nerve, which projects through the nucleus tractus solitarius to modulate activity across limbic and cortical networks. Explaining this ascending neuromodulatory pathway to patients demystifies a device that many initially experience as counterintuitive: a wire in the neck that affects the brain. It also prepares patients for the gradual and variable response profile that VNS produces, reducing the premature abandonment that follows unmet expectations (Wheless et al., 2018).

Responsive neurostimulation operates as a closed-loop system, continuously monitoring electrocorticographic activity and delivering stimulation only when epileptiform patterns are detected. The improvement it produces accumulates over months to years, driven partly by long-term network reorganization.

Patients who understand this timeline and mechanism are more likely to persist through an early period that may show limited benefit, and to engage meaningfully with the data their device generates at clinic visits (Rao and Rolston, 2023; Wheless et al., 2018).

6.6 Multidisciplinary care (including the PACES model)

Epilepsy care, at its most effective, is not the work of a single clinician. The neurologist, psychologist, epilepsy nurse specialist, social worker, and, when appropriate, the caregiver each hold a perspective on the patient's experience that the others cannot fully capture. What is often missing is a shared language that allows these perspectives to come together into a coherent clinical picture. NIPE provides that language. It offers every member of the care team a common explanatory framework, while giving patients a way to connect what each clinician communicates into something integrated rather than fragmented (Michaelis et al., 2018; Gandy et al., 2021).

The PACES model offers the most rigorously evaluated example of structured group psychoeducation for adults with epilepsy. Delivered across eight weekly sessions co-led by a mental health professional and a trained peer with epilepsy, PACES addresses medical, psychosocial, cognitive, and self-management dimensions of the condition. Participants show sustained improvements in selfefficacy, epilepsy self-management, and mood at twelve months follow-up, with low attrition and high satisfaction ratings (Johnson et al., 2020). The peer cofacilitation component is not incidental. It operationalizes the NIPE principle that understanding a condition is more powerful when it comes from someone who has lived inside it.

In pediatric epilepsy, the caregiver is the primary recipient of psychoeducation, and their understanding directly shapes the child's illness experience. Caregiver burden, internalized stigma, and anxiety are all modifiable through structured psychoeducational intervention, and the evidence suggests that these gains translate into improved outcomes for the child (Kaçan and Sakız, 2025)

6.8 NIPE as the integrative layer

The subsections of this clinical integration chapter describe NIPE as it applies across distinct treatment domains: psychological intervention, pharmacotherapy, surgery, neuromodulation, multidisciplinary care, and special populations. Read individually, each presents a focused argument. Read together, they point to something broader: NIPE is not one component of epilepsy care among others; it is the layer that connects them all.

Epilepsy management is inherently multidisciplinary, and evidence consistently shows that integrated care models lead to better outcomes than fragmented approaches (Spanos et al., 2024). However, structural integration (shared records, joint case conferences, coordinated referrals) does not automatically translate into integration at the level of patient understanding. A patient may see a neurologist, psychologist, epilepsy nurse, and dietician within the same timeframe, yet still leave with fragmented or even conflicting explanations of their condition and its management. NIPE addresses this gap directly. It provides patients with a unified explanatory framework that integrates all aspects of their care, while also offering clinicians a shared language for communication within that framework (Michaelis et al., 2018; Ekhtiari et al., 2017).

7.2 Wearables and physiological monitoring

Wearable biosensors, including wrist-worn devices capable of detecting electrodermal activity, accelerometry, heart rate variability, and photoplethysmography, have moved from research settings into clinical use.

Their value within a NIPE framework is not limited to seizure detection. These devices generate continuous data streams that, when interpreted with appropriate psychoeducational scaffolding, transform the patient's relationship to their own physiology (Donner and Devinsky, 2024; Dunn et al., 2021).

A patient who understands that their wearable is tracking the physiological signatures of stress and sleep disruption, not just counting seizures, is positioned to engage actively with that data. The device becomes a tool for testing the relationship between daily behavior and seizure threshold rather than a passive alert system. Seizure diaries and forecasting tools derived from wearable data are most useful when patients understand enough about their own neurobiology to interpret what the patterns mean (Brinkmann et al., 2021; Dunn et al., 2021).

Without that interpretive framework, the data accumulates without changing behavior.

7.3 Digital phenotyping and individualized feedback

Digital phenotyping refers to the moment-by-moment characterization of individual behavior and physiology through data passively collected from personal devices. In epilepsy, this encompasses sleep architecture, physical activity, social engagement patterns, medication adherence timestamps, and stress-related physiological markers, all of which interact with seizure threshold in ways that are increasingly quantifiable (Gupta et al., 2022).

The clinical value of this data within NIPE is individualization. Population-level psychoeducation tells patients that sleep deprivation raises seizure risk. Digital phenotyping shows a specific patient that their own seizure frequency reliably increases following nights of less than six hours of sleep, measured across their own data. That shift from generic to personal evidence changes how patients engage with behavioral recommendations. It also changes the clinical conversation. The follow-up appointment becomes a data review as much as a symptom check, and the patient arrives as a participant in their own monitoring rather than a recipient of assessment (Gupta et al., 2022; Kiriakopoulos et al., 2025).

7.4 Visual tools, medical illustration, and animations

The neuroscience content at the core of NIPE is inherently spatial and dynamic. Network propagation, synaptic balance, limbic circuit connectivity, these are concepts that resist purely verbal explanation and benefit substantially from well-designed visual representation. The challenge is not simply making images simpler. It is making them accurate enough to carry clinical meaning while remaining accessible across the full range of health literacy levels that epilepsy services encounter (Ekhtiari et al., 2017; Rezapour et al., 2021). 10. Conclusion

The psychiatric and cognitive comorbidities associated with epilepsy are not merely secondary complications; rather, they reflect the same underlying network dysfunction that gives rise to seizures. As such, they warrant clinical attention that is proportionate to their impact. NIPE represents an emerging and critical approach to enhancing patient understanding of epilepsy, with the potential to significantly improve outcomes and overall prognosis.

What distinguishes NIPE from conventional psychoeducation is not merely the quantity of information delivered, but the explanatory framework it provides. A patient who leaves a clinical encounter knowing their seizure triggers is better informed. A patient who leaves with an understanding that their brain functions as a dynamic network, that their depression shares a biological substrate with their epilepsy, that their medications act by restoring measurable physiological balance, and that their brain retains the capacity for adaptation and change, is genuinely empowered. This empowerment is not incidental to clinical outcomes; it is a key mechanism through which improved outcomes become possible. Figure 2. Limitations, ethical considerations, and evidence gaps NIPE rests on a compelling theoretical framework and draws on a growing body of translational evidence, but its evidence base in epilepsy specifically remains limited. Most of the foundational work derives from the addiction medicine context, and while the neurobiological parallels are substantive, direct trials of NIPE as a defined intervention in epilepsy populations are sparse. The field lacks standardized NIPE protocols, validated outcome measures specific to the neuroempowerment model, and randomized controlled trials that isolate the contribution of neuroscience-informed content from the non-specific effects of structured psychoeducation more broadly (Rezapour et al., 2021; Michaelis et al., 2021). Until that evidence base is built, the claims made for NIPE in epilepsy remain partly extrapolated. Several patient-level barriers limit implementation in routine care. Postictal cognitive impairment following frequent or severe seizures means that standard NIPE delivery windows may be systematically poorly timed. Patients assessed or counseled in the immediate postictal period are operating with impaired encoding capacity, and psychoeducational content delivered at that point is unlikely to be retained. Baseline health literacy presents a related challenge. The neuroscience content at the core of NIPE is meaningfully more complex than conventional epilepsy education, and while the use of metaphor and visual tools mitigates this, low-literacy patients require substantially adapted materials that are not yet widely available across languages and healthcare settings (Novak et al., 2022; Kiriakopoulos et al., 2025). Cross-cultural variation in how brain-based explanations are received adds further complexity. In many communities, epilepsy remains embedded within cultural, spiritual, or moral frameworks that a neuroscience model does not simply displace. Presenting a network-level explanation of seizures to a patient whose illness is understood within a spiritual framework requires cultural humility and skilled facilitation, not just accurate neuroscience; neuroscience frameworks must respectfully interface with a patient's existing spiritual or cultural beliefs rather than simply displacing them (Mayor et al., 2021; GBD Epilepsy Collaborators, 2025). Poorly delivered NIPE risks invalidating the patient's existing explanatory framework without successfully replacing it with one that is clinically useful. Ethical considerations attach to several specific aspects of NIPE delivery. Disclosure of SUDEP risk within a psychoeducational framework raises questions about timing, framing, and patient readiness that are not yet resolved by evidence. Neuroscience framing of psychiatric symptoms, while intended to reduce self-blame, carries the risk of over-biologizing distress in ways that deprive patients of psychological agency. The use of digital phenotyping and wearable data in personalized NIPE raises privacy and data governance concerns that clinical implementation has not yet fully addressed (Gupta et al., 2022; Donner and Devinsky, 2024). These are not arguments against NIPE. They are the honest accounting that any emerging clinical framework requires before it can be responsibly scaled.

9.

Future directions of NIPE in epilepsyThe immediate priority for NIPE in epilepsy is building the evidence base that the framework currently lacks. Structured trials comparing NIPE-enriched psychoeducation against standard care, with outcomes measuring not just seizure frequency and medication adherence but stigma internalization, treatment engagement, and metacognitive function, are the next necessary step. Without these, the case for NIPE rests on conceptual coherence and extrapolated evidence from adjacent fields rather than on direct demonstration of clinical benefit in epilepsy populations. The field also needs standardized NIPE protocols that can be tested, replicated, and adapted, rather than the heterogeneous locally developed interventions that currently characterize the landscape (Rezapour et al., 2025; Michaelis et al., 2021). Gamification offers a genuinely promising avenue for extending NIPE reach, particularly in younger populations and in settings where sustained engagement with structured content is difficult to achieve. Interactive game-based platforms that embed neuroscience content within goal-directed tasks, progress tracking, and social comparison elements have shown early evidence of improving epilepsy knowledge and self-management behaviors (Lucas et al., 2024). The design challenge is maintaining clinical fidelity while keeping engagement high enough to compete with the wider digital environment patients inhabit. Artificial intelligence introduces possibilities that go beyond digitizing existing NIPE content. Large language model-based tools capable of generating individualized psychoeducational responses calibrated to a patient's specific epilepsy syndrome, medication regimen, literacy level, and previously expressed concerns represent a near-term technical possibility, provided clinical safeguards against misinformation are established (Lucas et al., 2024; Arora et al., 2024). The clinical and ethical infrastructure to deploy such tools responsibly, including validation of output accuracy, safeguards against misinformation, and clear boundaries between psychoeducation and clinical advice, requires deliberate development before these applications move into routine use. Continuous digital psychoeducation, where NIPE content is not delivered in discrete sessions but woven into ongoing app-based interactions triggered by patient-generated data, aligns NIPE delivery with how patients actually live with epilepsy. A patient whose wearable detects a pattern of sleep disruption preceding increased seizure frequency could receive targeted neuroscience content about seizure threshold at the moment that content is most personally relevant. That kind of just-in-time psychoeducation, timed to the patient's own physiological narrative, is more likely to change behavior than content delivered on a clinic schedule (Brinkmann et al., 2021; Gupta et al., 2022). The longer-term vision is a NIPE ecosystem embedded in routine epileptology practice: standardized core content delivered through multidisciplinary clinical encounters, extended and personalized through digital platforms, reinforced by wearable data feedback, and adapted continuously to the patient's evolving clinical situation. Getting there requires not just clinical research but investment in resource development, clinician training, health system infrastructure, and the kind of cross-disciplinary collaboration between neurologists, psychologists, educators, and digital health designers that epilepsy care has historically struggled to achieve.
