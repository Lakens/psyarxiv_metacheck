How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Visual perspective is a core feature of autobiographical remembering, yet it is typically measured at the level of individual memories, and relatively little is known about stable individual differences in own-eyes (OE) and observer-like (OB) perspective tendencies. The present study evaluated the Visual Perspective Questionnaire (VPQ), a brief four-item measure designed to assess these perspective tendencies. Data were drawn from 15,043 undergraduate students, including 3,010 participants with repeated VPQ administrations and 1,712 participants who completed measures of object and spatial imagery. Reliability and test-retest analyses provided qualified support for separate OE and OB scores. Critically, OE and OB showed only minimal association, and this relationship remained small after accounting for imperfect scale reliability.

Consistent with this finding, the joint distribution of OE and OB included individuals high or low on both dimensions as well as individuals showing opposing profiles. OE and OB were also modestly associated with broader imagery tendencies. Together, these findings provide construct-validation evidence for the VPQ and support measuring OE and OB as separate, coexisting dimensions of individual differences in autobiographical remembering rather than as opposite endpoints of a single continuum.

Introduction

Visual perspective is a fundamental feature of autobiographical remembering that determines the vantage point from which past events are mentally re-experienced (St. Jacques, 2019 , 2024a , 2025). Although people normally perceive the world through their own eyes, autobiographical memories can be reconstructed from both an own-eyes perspective (OE) and from an observer-like perspective (OB), in which individuals view themselves as part of the remembered scene (Nigro & Neisser, 1983; Rice & Rubin, 2009; Robinson & Swanson, 1993).

Visual perspective is therefore an important component of the reconstructed representation of a past event and may reflect properties of the scene-based representations that support remembering (Rubin & Umanath, 2015; St. Jacques, 2025).

Importantly, visual perspective also varies systematically between individuals (Berg et al., 2021; Siedlecki, 2015; Verhaeghen et al., 2018). Some individuals frequently experience autobiographical memories from both OE and OB perspectives, whereas others rarely or never report particular perspective (Radvansky & Svob, 2018; St. Jacques, 2024b). Visual perspective has been proposed to reflect both dispositional characteristics of the individual and contextual features of retrieval (Sutin & Robins, 2010), suggesting that stable differences between people may contribute to how autobiographical memories are remembered. Consistent with this account, greater use of OB perspectives has been associated with heightened self-consciousness (Niese et al., 2022; Nigro & Neisser, 1983), social anxiety (Spurr & Stopa, 2003), dissociation and posttraumatic stress (H. K. McIsaac & Eich, 2004), and depression in clinical samples (Williams et al., 2007). OB remembering has also been linked to age (Serino et al., 2023), culture (Cohen & Gunz, 2002; Martin & Jones, 2012), and gender (Huebner & Fredrickson, 1999). These findings suggest that visual perspective is not simply a property of individual memories, but may also VALIDATION OF THE VISUAL PERSPECTIVE QUESTIONNAIRE (VPQ) reflect enduring differences in how individuals reconstruct and re-experience autobiographical events. Yet, to our knowledge, there is currently no dedicated, psychometrically validated questionnaire for assessing individual differences in OE and OB visual perspective tendencies.

Despite growing evidence for stable individual differences in visual perspective, perspective has typically been assessed at the level of individual memories rather than as a stable individual-differences construct. Participants are typically asked to recall one or more autobiographical events and then to rate the perspective adopted for each memory. Critically, however, different approaches to measuring visual perspective embody different theoretical assumptions about the relationship between OE and OB perspectives (e.g., Rice & Rubin, 2009) Forced-choice measures imply a mutually exclusive theoretical framework, in which a memory is experienced from either an OE or OB perspective, but not both (McIsaac & Eich, 2002). Bipolar rating scales instead imply a continuum framework, in which OE and OB are opposing ends of a single dimension, such that stronger experience of one perspective necessarily corresponds to weaker experience of the other (Libby & Eibach, 2011). In contrast, separate OE and OB ratings are consistent with an independent framework, in which the two perspectives can vary separately and potentially co-occur during retrieval (Rice & Rubin, 2009; St. Jacques, 2025). Evidence from individual memories provides some support for this latter accountparticipants can report experiencing both perspectives within the same memory, and OE and OB ratings show different associations with other phenomenological characteristics such as vividness (Rice & Rubin, 2009). Thus, these measurement approaches represent competing accounts of how visual perspective is structured. Whether these same theoretical frameworks characterize stable individual differences in OE and OB tendencies, however, remains unknown.

The Present Study

The Visual Perspective Questionnaire (VPQ) is a brief questionnaire originally designed to study individual differences in the tendency to adopt OE and OB perspectives (St. Jacques, 2024b). Unlike measures that characterize the perspective of individual memories, the VPQ assesses the extent to which individuals generally experience autobiographical memories from each perspective. Critically, OE and OB tendencies are assessed separately, making it possible to evaluate competing theoretical accounts of their relationship rather than assuming in advance VALIDATION OF THE VISUAL PERSPECTIVE QUESTIONNAIRE (VPQ) that they are mutually exclusive or opposite ends of a single continuum. In the present study we administered the VPQ to over 15,000 participants as part of a mass-testing survey. We used the data to examine the psychometric structure of the VPQ, test competing theoretical models of visual perspective, and evaluate its convergent validity with established individual differences in mental imagery.

The VPQ consists of four items, with two items assessing OE tendencies and two assessing OB tendencies. Its brevity makes it well suited to large-scale testing but also places constraints on conventional psychometric evaluation, particularly when each proposed dimension is represented by only two items. We therefore evaluated the structure and reliability of the OE and OB items using methods appropriate for very short scales (Franzen et al., 2022; Lins De Holanda Coelho et al., 2020). More importantly, the separate assessment of OE and OB provided an opportunity to test the competing theoretical frameworks outlined above. Object-Spatial Imagery Questionnaire (OSIQ).

The OSIQ (Blajenkova et al., 2006), a 30-item questionnaire assessing individual differences in object and spatial imagery. Participants rated their agreement with statements about their typical imagery experiences on a 5-point Likert scale. The OSIQ yields separate object and spatial imagery scores. Higher object imagery scores indicate a greater tendency to form vivid, colourful, detailed, and picture-like mental images, whereas higher spatial imagery scores indicate a greater tendency to represent spatial layouts and relations among objects and to perform spatial transformations in a more schematic manner.

Procedure

The data were collected online using Qualtrics (Qualtrics, 2019 (Qualtrics, -2026) ) as part of a larger departmental mass testing survey conducted between 2018 and 2026. Participants first provided demographic information, including their age, gender, and ethnicity, and then completed a larger battery of questionnaires that included the VPQ. The OSIQ was included during a subset of mass testing, resulting in the convergent-validity subsample described above.

All participants provided informed consent prior to taking part in the study.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using R, version 4.3.2 (RStudio Team, 2024). We keyed items so that higher scores consistently reflected greater endorsement of the corresponding perspective tendency. Follow-up exploratory dimensionality analyses examining the latent structure of the VPQ are reported in the Supplemental Materials.

Analyses were conducted in three stages. First, we evaluated the psychometric properties of the VPQ and examined whether its four items could be scored as internally coherent OE and OB subscales, and the questionnaire test re-test reliability.

Associations among the four items were examined using Spearman correlations. Because each proposed subscale contained only VALIDATION OF THE VISUAL PERSPECTIVE QUESTIONNAIRE (VPQ) two items, reliability was evaluated using the inter-item correlation and Spearman-Brown coefficient rather than Cronbach's alpha (Eisinga et al., 2013).

Test-retest analyses examined the temporal stability of the VPQ at the item, dimension, and profile levels (Dolnicar et al., 2022). Second, we examined the relationship between OE and OB tendencies and evaluated whether the observed pattern of VPQ responses was more consistent with mutually exclusive, continuum, or relatively independent accounts of visual perspective. The association between OE and OB subscale scores was first quantified using a Spearman correlation. To provide theoretical benchmarks for interpreting their joint distribution, we generated bivariate normal distributions representing the three proposed frameworks. Each simulated distribution contained the same number of observations as the empirical sample and had unit variances. The mutually exclusive framework was represented by a strong negative correlation (ρ = -.90), whereas the continuum framework was represented by a moderate negative correlation (ρ = -.60). The independent framework was represented by a weak negative correlation (ρ = -.30). These values were selected to illustrate the differing structural constraints implied by the three theoretical accounts rather than to provide formal fitted models.

For the independent framework, we specified a weak negative association rather than zero, as complete independence did not fully reflect the theoretical account or observed data. This allowed OE and OB to coexist while retaining some degree of opposition without assuming mutual exclusivity.

For comparison with the simulated distributions, empirical OE and OB scores were standardized within the sample and plotted in a common z-score space. Small random jitter was applied to empirical observations for visualization only and did not affect any statistical analyses.

Participants were also classified into extreme OE-OB profiles using a ±1 SD criterion applied to the standardized scores. High OE-high OB participants scored ≥ +1 SD on both dimensions, low OE-low OB participants scored ≤ -1 SD on both dimensions, and the two discrepant profiles reflected high OE-low OB and low OE-high OB scores, respectively. Participants who did not meet any of these criteria were classified as medium or mixed. The same ±1 SD criteria were applied to the simulated distributions to permit comparison of extreme-profile frequencies across the empirical and theoretical distributions.

To quantify these comparisons, odds ratios were calculated comparing the frequency of extreme profiles in the empirical data with those generated under each theoretical framework.

Analyses considered the high OE-high OB and low OE-low OB profiles separately and also combined these as congruent profiles, with high OE-low OB and low OE-high OB combined as discrepant profiles. Within the empirical data, a 2 × 2 chi-square test of independence was used to test whether extreme OE and OB classifications were associated. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test examined whether the four extreme profiles occurred with equal frequency. Finally, a permutation analysis in which OB classifications were randomly reassigned was used to evaluate VALIDATION OF THE VISUAL PERSPECTIVE QUESTIONNAIRE (VPQ) whether the observed association between extreme OE and OB levels differed from that expected under statistical independence.

Third, convergent validity was evaluated using the OSIQ. Spearman correlations were used to examine associations between OE and OB scores and object and spatial imagery. We additionally examined whether object and spatial imagery predicted membership in the four extreme OE-OB profiles using separate multinomial logistic regression models, with the low OE-high OB profile specified as the reference category. Object and spatial imagery were examined in separate models rather than entered simultaneously. OSIQ predictors were entered as mean subscale scores on their original scale. Accordingly, slope coefficients represented the change in relative log-odds of profile membership associated with a one-unit increase in the corresponding OSIQ mean score. Omnibus likelihood-ratio tests were used to assess whether each OSIQ dimension predicted profile membership overall.

Follow-up one-versus-rest logistic regressions were then conducted to characterize the association between each OSIQ dimension and membership in each of the four extreme profiles.

For these analyses, each profile was contrasted with the other three profiles combined. Holm correction was applied across the four profile-specific tests separately for each OSIQ dimension.

Discussion

The present findings support a multidimensional measurement framework for individual differences in autobiographical visual perspective. Across a large validation sample, OE and OB were better characterized as distinguishable, coexisting tendencies than as opposite endpoints of a single bipolar construct. This distinction has direct implications for how visual perspective is measured. Combining OE and OB into a single continuum or difference score would obscure meaningful variation in each perspective tendency and in individuals who report relatively high or low levels of both. The VPQ provides a brief means of assessing these dimensions separately.

Across several analyses, OE and OB showed little overall association, the observed joint distribution included all four combinations of relatively high and low OE and OB, and the distribution more closely resembled a relatively independent than a strongly bipolar framework.

Test-retest analyses further indicated modest temporal stability in both dimensions, whereas associations with object and spatial imagery provided evidence that VPQ scores relate systematically, although modestly, to broader imagery tendencies. Together, these findings provide initial construct-validation evidence for the VPQ while also clarifying the structure of the individual differences it is intended to measure.

The Measurement Structure of Visual Perspective in Autobiographical Memory

The present findings were most consistent with accounts in which OE and OB represent partially separable tendencies rather than opposite endpoints of a single visual perspective construct.

The observed profile distribution more closely approximated the pattern expected under the independent framework than those expected under the mutually exclusive or continuum/complementary frameworks. Under strongly bipolar accounts, greater endorsement of one perspective should constrain endorsement of the other, resulting primarily in opposing VALIDATION OF THE VISUAL PERSPECTIVE QUESTIONNAIRE (VPQ) profiles such as high OE-low OB and low OE-high OB. Instead, the observed data included high OE-high OB and low OE-low OB profiles as well as the two opposing combinations. Thus, individuals were not restricted to a fixed trade-off between OE and OB tendencies. This pattern aligns with prior work suggesting that OE and OB perspectives can co-occur during autobiographical retrieval and need not be treated as simple opposites (Rice & Rubin, 2009), and the original observation that individuals vary in their use of visual perspectives (Nigro & Neisser, 1983).

An important feature of the results is that relative independence does not imply that all combinations of OE and OB should occur equally often. The four extreme profiles differed in prevalence, but extreme OE and OB classifications showed little evidence of statistical association. Thus, the unequal frequency of the profiles suggest that some combinations of perspective tendencies are more prevalent than others, whereas the association between OE and OB suggests that these tendencies are not organized primarily as opposite poles of a single continuum. This distinction is important theoretically as the data support a model in which OE and OB can vary jointly without being perfectly symmetrical or equally prevalent across individuals. Such a pattern is compatible with the broader view that visual perspective is flexible and reconstructive, rather than a fixed property of autobiographical memory (St. Jacques, 2025).

These findings suggest that OE and OB are best treated as separate continuous dimensions. This approach preserves information about individual differences in each perspective tendency without assuming that greater OE necessarily implies lower OB. The joint distribution of these two dimensions can also be examined descriptively using profile analyses, which highlight combinations such as high OE-high OB and low OE-low OB that would be VALIDATION OF THE VISUAL PERSPECTIVE QUESTIONNAIRE (VPQ) obscured by treating visual perspective as a single bipolar continuum. Thus, the profile analyses complement, rather than replace, analyses of continuous OE and OB scores.

The interpretation of OE and OB as partially separable, coexisting tendencies is consistent with evidence that visual perspective is associated with differences in the phenomenology and content of autobiographical remembering. Shifting visual perspective during autobiographical recall can alter the kinds of information retrieved. For example, adopting an OB perspective is associated with fewer internally focused details, such as thoughts, feelings, and personal meanings, and with more externally focused descriptions of appearance and setting (King et al., 2022; H. McIsaac & Eich, 2002). More broadly, visual perspective may function as part of the reconstructive process through which autobiographical memories are represented rather than simply serving as a label for how a memory is experienced (St. Jacques, 2025). The present findings extend this proposal by suggesting that individuals differ not only in their tendency to use OE or OB perspectives separately, but also in how these tendencies are configured across memories.

The joint configuration of OE and OB may also be informative about broader styles of autobiographical remembering. Rather than asking only whether an individual tends to report more OE or more OB, examining the two dimensions together captures how these tendencies cooccur within individuals. This may be important because visual perspective is associated with differences in scene-based characteristics of autobiographical memory. In St. Jacques (2024b), individuals characterized by high OE and low OB tendencies showed better scene memory, suggesting that particular combinations of perspective tendencies may be associated with differences in how the spatial context of autobiographical events is represented. More broadly, visual perspective has been linked to differences in the information retrieved, including how VALIDATION OF THE VISUAL PERSPECTIVE QUESTIONNAIRE (VPQ) much sensory and emotional detail is retrieved and how the remembered self is positioned within this scene (King et al., 2022; St. Jacques, 2025). Accordingly, different OE-OB configurations may correspond to different ways of reconstructing autobiographical events.

The present findings do not establish such distinct remembering styles directly, but they identify individual-difference configurations that can be tested against memory-level phenomenology in future work. Based on previous research, a high OE-low OB profile may be associated with stronger scene-based or internally anchored recollection, whereas a low OE-high OB profile may be associated with greater emphasis on viewing the remembered self from an external perspective (McIsaac & Eich, 2002; Rice, 2010; St. Jacques, 2024b). The high OE-high OB profile is particularly interesting because it may reflect a more flexible or dual-perspective style of remembering, in which individuals can access both embodied and self-observational aspects of the event. In contrast, low OE-low OB profiles may reflect weak reliance on either visual perspective tendency. These possibilities remain to be tested directly. An important next step will be to determine whether the four OE-OB configurations are associated with systematic differences in scene memory, event detail, self-representation, perspective flexibility, and other characteristics of autobiographical recollection.

Reliability and Validation

The reliability analyses provide qualified support for the use of OE and OB as individualdifference measures while also highlighting the limitations of very brief two-item dimensions. At the group level, OE and OB showed little evidence of meaningful systematic change across administrations. Rank-order stability was more modest, indicating that VPQ scores capture both stable individual differences and occasion-specific variability. Item-level changes were generally small, with most responses remaining within one scale point across administrations. Similarly, changes in profile classification predominantly reflected movement toward the central region of the OE-OB distribution rather than direct transitions between opposing extreme profiles.

Thus, the test-retest suggest that the VPQ captures reproducible individual differences in visual perspective, while exact scores and particularly extreme profile classifications should not be assumed to remain invariant over time.

The OSIQ findings provided evidence that VPQ scores are systematically related to broader individual differences in visual imagery. Object imagery was positively associated with both OE and OB perspective, with associations of similar magnitude. This pattern suggests that a tendency toward vivid object-based imagery may contribute broadly to visual autobiographical remembering rather than selectively favouring one visual perspective. In contrast, spatial imagery showed only a very small positive association with observer perspective and no significant association with OE, providing more limited evidence of convergency between spatial imagery and visual perspective. Thus, although the VPQ dimensions were related to established imagery constructs, these associations were modest and did not indicate a simple correspondence between object or spatial imagery and a particular perspective.

The profile analyses further suggested that broader imagery tendencies vary across particular combinations of OE and OB. Higher object imagery was associated with greater likelihood of membership in the high OE-high OB profile and lower likelihood of belonging to the low OE-low OB profile. Predicted probabilities showed a similar pattern, with increasing object imagery associated with greater predicted membership in the high OE-high OB profile.

Thus, object imagery was not associated simply with stronger OE or stronger OB tendencies but appeared particularly characteristic of individuals who reported relatively high levels of both perspectives.

Spatial imagery showed a weaker and more selective profile pattern. The most robust association was a reduced likelihood of belonging to the low OE-low OB profile as spatial imagery increased. Although higher spatial imagery was associated with greater predicted probability of high OE-high OB membership, this association was less robust in the inferential analyses. Taken together, the dimensional and profile-level findings suggest that object and spatial imagery related differently to autobiographical visual perspective, while also indicating that these relationships are modest and require replication.

Scoring and Use of the Visual Perspective Questionnaire (VPQ)

The VPQ is intended to assess individual differences in the tendency to adopt OE and

OB visual perspectives during autobiographical memory. Based on the present findings, OE and OB should not be combined into a single bipolar index in which greater endorsement of one perspective necessarily implies lower endorsement of the other. Instead, we recommend scoring and analysing OE and OB as separate continuous dimensions. For most research questions, continuous OE and OB scores should provide the primary approach to analysis. This allows researchers to test whether each perspective tendency is associated with constructs such as vividness of visual imagery, spatial imagery, emotion, selfconsciousness, or other autobiographical memory phenomenology while retaining the full variability in the sample. Difference scores may be less informative because they can assign similar values to individuals with quite different underlying configurations. For example, an individual who scores highly on both OE and OB could have a similar difference score to an individual who scores relatively low on both dimensions. Joint profile analyses may provide a complementary descriptive approach when a research question specifically concerns combinations of OE and OB tendencies. Standardized VALIDATION OF THE VISUAL PERSPECTIVE QUESTIONNAIRE (VPQ) scores can be used to classify individuals occupying theoretically informative regions of the joint distribution, including high OE-high OB, low OE-low OB, high OE-low OB, and low OE-high OB configurations. These profiles should not, however, be interpreted as diagnostic or naturally occurring categories. Classification thresholds should be specified and justified a priori where possible, and conclusions should be examined for robustness across reasonable analytic choices.

The VPQ should also be distinguished from memory-level ratings of specific events. It assesses typical tendencies across autobiographical remembering rather than the perspective adopted for a particular event. Event-specific ratings remain preferable when the research question concerns the perspective for individual memories, changes with memory age, or experimental induced manipulations of retrieval perspective. The VPQ is therefore best suited for examining individual differences in typical perspective tendencies and should not be treated as a substitute for event-level measures or as a clinical screening or diagnostic instrument.

Limitations and Future Directions

The VPQ is a brief scale, with a small number of items representing each perspective dimension.

This brevity facilitates its use in larger studies, but also constrains reliability and the breadth with which each construct can be assessed. With only two items, each response has a relatively large influence on the resulting scale score. At the same time, the brevity of the VPQ may also be a strength. Visual perspective is a relatively narrow phenomenological construct, and adding items solely to increase internal consistency could introduce redundancy or broaden the measure into related but distinct constructs, such as imagery vividness, spatial ability, emotional distancing, or self-consciousness. Future work should therefore examine whether additional items can improve reliability while preserving the conceptual specificity of OE and OB perspective tendencies.

A second limitation concerns the theoretical simulations. These distributions were intended as illustrative benchmarks representing different degrees of constraint between OE and OB rather than as formally fitted models of the empirical data. The correlations used to instantiate the theoretical frameworks therefore provide simplified representations of these theoretical accounts, and the simulated distributions assumed continuous bivariate-normal variables whereas empirical VPQ scores were derived from a small number of ordinal questionnaire items. Importantly, however, the conclusion that OE and OB are only weakly related did not depend solely on these simulations. The observed OE-OB association was close to zero, remained small after correction for attenuation due to imperfect reliability, and was substantially weaker than the negative association specified by the relatively independent benchmark. Thus, the simulations should consequently be interpreted as conceptual reference points that illustrate the implications of different theoretical accounts, rather than as formal model-selection tests. Future work using measures with a larger number of indicators could more directly compare alternative latent variable models of the relationship between OE and OB.

A third limitation concerns the quadrant-based profile approach. The ±1 SD criterion was deliberately used to identify relatively extreme combinations of OE and OB, meaning that the majority of participants were not classified into one of the four extreme profiles. The resulting analyses therefore characterize informative regions of the joint distribution rather than the sample as a whole and should be viewed as complementary to, rather than replacements for, continuous analyses of OE and OB. Profile frequencies will also depend on sample size, score variability, and the threshold used to define high and low scores. Future studies should test the replicability of the observed configurations and examine the robustness of conclusions across reasonable alternative thresholds.

Future research should also distinguish more clearly between visual perspective as an individual-differences tendency and visual perspective as a performance-based or task-based ability. The VPQ assesses how people typically report experiencing visual perspective across autobiographical remembering, but this is not the same as assessing whether individuals can deliberately adopt, maintain, or shift perspectives when instructed to do so. Future studies could combine the VPQ with experimental tasks in which participants retrieve specific memories from cued perspectives, attempt to shift between OE and OB, or maintain a perspective across recall.

This would help clarify whether VPQ profiles reflect stable preferences, differences in perspective flexibility, differences in imagery ability, or differences in the accessibility of particular forms of remembering.

Finally, future studies should examine whether the joint OE-OB configurations identified here correspond to distinct phenomenological or cognitive characteristics of autobiographical remembering. Studies combining the VPQ with ratings of specific memories could test whether profiles differ in scene construction, vividness, emotional re-experiencing, self-location, sensory detail, or representation of the remembered self. Such work could also determine whether VPQ scores reflect stable perspective preferences or differences in the ability to access and shift between OE and OB perspectives. Additional convergent and discriminant validation is also needed. The present associations with object and spatial imagery were modest, and future studies could examine related constructs such as visual vividness, scene construction, imagery control, and spatial transformation. This would help clarify which broader imagery processes are shared with OE and OB tendencies and which aspects of the VPQ are specific to visual perspective.

Conclusions

The present study provides large-scale construct validation evidence for the VPQ as a brief measure of individual differences in visual perspective.

The results indicate that OE and OB perspectives are better represented as separate dimensions than as opposite endpoints of a single continuum. Accordingly, researchers using the VPQ should retain separate continuous OE and OB scores, with joint profiles used only when combinations of perspective tendencies are of specific theoretical interest. Although further validation across tasks, populations, and measures is needed, the VPQ provides a practical tool for incorporating visual perspective into research on cognitive individual differences. More broadly, the findings demonstrate that the perspective with which people remember autobiographical memories varies systematically between individuals in ways that are not adequately captured by a single OE-to-OB continuum.
