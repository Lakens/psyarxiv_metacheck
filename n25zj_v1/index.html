Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Intensive longitudinal studies link parent-adolescent daily emotions and behaviors, yet whether these fast dynamics shape lasting change remains a fundamental question. We propose a crosstimescale framework centered on parent-adolescent coupling: how one family member's fluctuations track another's. We first distinguish between-from within-family associations and review evidence showing that within-family findings vary by timescale. We then review literature linking short-term processes to long-term adjustment, identifying a greater focus on adults over adolescents despite rapid developmental change. From this literature, we review three methodological designs. The first tests whether more strongly coupled families experience worse adjustment with one burst of intensive assessment. The second asks whether coupling and adjustment change together across multiple bursts. The third tests whether unusually strong coupling within a family precedes worsening adjustment. We end by discussing how considering the context where the coupling unfolds and causal inference strengthens these designs, eventually informing family-based treatment.

[div-01]

Psychological research has increasingly paid attention to the intra-and inter-individual dynamics in daily life. Studies using daily diaries and ecological momentary assessment (EMA) have grown rapidly (Laurenceau et al., 2026), including research on adolescents and families (Boele, 2026; Keijsers et al., 2022 Keijsers et al., , 2026)). These studies identify short-term (mostly daily) associations at the within-family level: links between parents' and adolescents' deviations from their own usual levels in outcomes of interest. For example, on days when a parent reports more distress than usual, the adolescent may also report more distress than usual, either during the same day or the next. One key motivation for collecting such data rests on a central yet rarely tested premise: that everyday interactions, repeated across hundreds or thousands of days, accumulate to the developmental outcomes that families and clinicians care about (Lougheed & Keskin, 2021; Ram & Gerstorf, 2009; Wichers, 2014). A typical intensive longitudinal study spans days to weeks, too short a window to test this premise.

In this review, we use intergenerational transmission of psychopathology as a case study because it has direct implications for intervention. We focus on adolescence, a period when depressive symptoms rise sharply (Thapar et al., 2012) and family relationships reorganize.

Growing autonomy and declining time with family may alter how strongly adolescents respond to parental distress, so coupling itself is likely to change during this period (Larson et al., 1996).

In the mostly cross-sectional literature, parental and adolescent psychopathology are reliably associated between families (Goodman et al., 2011). Meanwhile, whether changes in parental symptoms lead to changes in adolescent symptoms within the same family remains an open question (Griffith et al., 2021). Across 10 treatment-control comparisons from eight trials, psychological treatment for maternal depression showed a small average benefit for child mental health (g = 0.29, 95% CI [0.12, 0.45]). Only one contributing trial included adolescents, and the pooled estimate varied across sensitivity analyses. Too few trials reported longer-term outcomes to assess whether these gains persisted (Cuijpers et al., 2026). One possibility is that treatment lowers parents' usual symptom levels while leaving intact the daily processes that link parents' and adolescents' distress. The practical challenge is then to identify such daily processes and rigorously test whether altering them improves adolescents' longer-term adjustment.

In this review, we use parent-adolescent coupling to describe associations between parents' and adolescents' deviations from their own usual levels of affect or behavior. Sameoccasion coupling describes cofluctuation. Lagged coupling relates one member's earlier deviation to the other's later deviation. Both forms have been widely studied within and beyond family research. Family researchers have examined lagged associations under the term emotional transmission (Larson & Almeida, 1999; Larson & Gillman, 1999), and the temporal interpersonal emotion systems (TIES) framework situates dyadic emotion dynamics across timescales (Butler, 2011; Lougheed, 2020). In psychophysiological research on affect contagion, West and Mendes (2023) drew a parallel distinction between concurrent covariation and lagged linkage. In laboratory experiments, mothers' and infants' cardiac responses covaried after they reunited following a stressor that mothers experienced apart from their infants (Waters et al., 2014 (Waters et al., , 2017)). Infants who sat on their mothers' laps showed stronger sympathetic covariation than infants who sat in a nearby high chair (Waters et al., 2017). As West and Mendes (2023) note, this work has focused on antecedents and moderators rather than consequences, so the developmental question is open across fields. Our review emphasizes lagged coupling in both directions and considers same-occasion coupling where relevant.

Connecting family dynamics to developmental change requires linking periods of intensive assessment, or bursts, to adjustment over months or years (Ram & Gerstorf, 2009; Sliwinski, 2008). Our cross-timescale framework (Figure 1) separates stable differences between families from fluctuations within a family (Panel A) and places those fluctuations on a timeline from moments to years (Panel B). The lower panels show three ways to connect these timescales.

The single-burst design (Design 1) tests whether families with stronger coupling have worse later adjustment. The concurrent-change design (Design 2) asks whether coupling and symptoms change together across bursts. The within-family precedence design (Design 3) tests whether unusually strong coupling precedes higher symptoms in the same family. For each, we specify the data and estimation approach, planning trade-offs, supported inferences, and alternative explanations (Table 1). We first clarify how associations differ between and within families and across timescales, then review evidence linking short-term processes to long-term adjustment.

We close by considering context, causal inference (e.g., confounding), and how these designs could help explain, and eventually improve, the modest child benefits of treating parental depression.

Stable Family Differences versus Within-Family Change

Before turning to the designs, we discuss the levels (i.e., between-and within-family) at which parent-adolescent psychopathology associations may arise. One reason psychopathology is transmitted across generations is that certain risk factors, such as genetic liability, chronic family adversity, and socioeconomic disadvantage, are stable across years and shared by parents and children (Goodman & Gotlib, 1999; McAdams et al., 2014; Morneau-Vaillancourt et al., 2025). The association may also arise because changes in one family member's symptoms track changes in another member's symptoms within the same family (i.e., a within-family effect; Bi et al., 2024). Between-family and within-family results can therefore tell different stories. An association may appear only between families, only within families over time, or at both levels with opposite signs (Curran & Bauer, 2011; Hamaker et al., 2015; Wang & Maxwell, 2015; Yip et al., 2026). In research with two Chinese mother-father-adolescent samples, for example, there were stable between-family associations and same-wave cofluctuation within families, yet a temporary symptom increase in one family member did not predict another member's symptoms two years later (Bi et al., 2024).

Why does the distinction of between-and within-family associations matter? A betweenfamily association alone does not establish that reducing a parent's depressive symptoms will improve the adolescent's symptoms. In contrast, within-family associations capture the process an intervention aims to change: whether a parent's departure from their own usual level is followed by a corresponding departure in the adolescent (Hamaker et al., 2015). Separating the levels clarifies what an association can imply about mechanisms and intervention targets (Hamaker & Wichers, 2017).

Multilevel models differentiate between each person's usual level and their momentary or daily deviations from this usual level (Curran & Bauer, 2011; Wang & Maxwell, 2015). A coupling slope relates the adolescent's deviation to the parent's deviation at the same or preceding occasion. A fixed slope describes average coupling; a random slope allows families to differ around that average (Sliwinski, 2008). These differences turn coupling into a family-level characteristic that may predict later symptoms (Ram & Gerstorf, 2009). Coupling is estimated from fluctuations within a family. Yet comparing coupling slopes across families, as in Design 1, is a between-family comparison. Repeated bursts then allow the slope itself to change within the same family.

Within-Family Associations Across Timescales

Within-family processes may operate over seconds to years. Theory should specify the timescale at which an effect is expected to emerge (Keijsers et al., 2022; Lougheed & Keskin, 2021). In laboratory studies of mothers and infants, autonomic responses covaried across paired 30-second segments recorded after reunion (Waters et al., 2014 (Waters et al., , 2017)). In parenting research, associations involving support, conflict, and affect differ across same-day, next-day, biweekly, monthly, and longer intervals (Boele, Nelemans, et al., 2023; Bülow et al., 2025). An association observed across days but absent across six months is therefore not necessarily contradictory.

In a daily diary study of Chinese families, mothers' higher-than-usual negative affect and anxiety symptoms predicted adolescents' higher-than-usual negative affect and anxiety symptoms, respectively, the next day. There were no comparable father-to-adolescent associations on average (Bi, Chen, et al., 2025). Daily parent-adolescent affect synchrony (i.e., same-day covariation) has also been observed in Dutch families (Bülow et al., 2026). By contrast, over 3-month (two U.S. samples; Griffith et al., 2021) and 2-year (two Chinese samples; Bi et al., 2024) intervals, there was no evidence of lagged within-family associations between parent and adolescent depressive symptoms, only same-wave cofluctuation. Thus, lagged parent-adolescent coupling has been observed across days but not across months or years.

When assessments are spaced much more widely than the interval over which a process unfolds, lagged effects can accumulate within a single interval and appear as same-wave cofluctuation (Dormann & Griffin, 2015). That is, the cofluctuation observed across months and years may partly reflect daily coupling summed over time. It remains unknown, however, whether daily coupling matters for developmental change over months or years.

A Research Agenda: Linking Daily Family Dynamics to Long-Term Adjustment

Consider an adolescent who responds strongly to a parent's distress. This adolescent may return to their usual level within days. The developmental question is whether repeated responses contribute to lasting symptom changes. One possibility is that recurrent distress disrupts sleep (Alvaro et al., 2013; Palmer et al., 2022) or reinforces rumination (Michl et al., 2013), gradually shifting the adolescent's usual functioning. We therefore focus on the coupling of negative affect and distress as a candidate risk process. Standard models of a single burst (Design 1, Figure 1) describe fluctuations around a mean assumed stable within that burst; later assessments are needed to test whether coupling predicts longer-term change.

Lessons From Adult Research

Adult stress research has linked short-term dynamics to long-term outcomes most systematically. It typically estimates a person-level parameter from a single diary burst and uses it to predict outcomes years later, adjusting for baseline levels. In the National Study of Daily Experiences, adults whose negative affect rose more on stressful days than on non-stressful days were more likely to report an affective disorder about a decade later (Charles et al., 2013). Over the same interval, greater reactivity predicted reporting a chronic physical health condition (Piazza et al., 2013). Negative affect that lingered after stressors also predicted later chronic conditions and functional limitations (Leger et al., 2018). Similarly, stronger sleep-affect spillover (i.e., higher negative affect after nights of greater sleep-onset difficulty) predicted more anxiety symptoms later among adults with more adverse childhood experiences (Bi et al., 2026).

These studies also point to two methodological considerations. First, each person's slope is estimated with uncertainty. Modeling the slope and its association with later outcomes together, as a latent random effect, carries that uncertainty into the analysis (Zhaoyang et al., 2020).

Second, because dynamic indices overlap with mean levels of affect, their predictive value must be shown beyond those means (Dejonckheere et al., 2019). More recently, repeated bursts have shown that the daily stressor-affect association itself changes over adulthood and that these changes relate to physical health (Rush et al., 2025).

The Gap in Adolescent Research

Child and adolescent research has begun to link short-term dynamics to later adjustment, but rarely through parent-adolescent coupling. Rigid parent-child interactions observed in kindergarten predicted growth in externalizing problems (Hollenstein et al., 2004), consistent with dynamic-systems accounts of coercive family cycles (Granic & Patterson, 2006).

Adolescents' emotional inertia during interactions with a parent predicted the onset of depression 2.5 years later (Kuppens et al., 2012). Children's mood reactivity to daily school problems predicted internalizing problems three years later (Bai et al., 2020), and Latinx adolescents' affective reactivity to momentary stress in high school predicted depressive symptoms in college (Anderson et al., 2021). Day-to-day mood variability (Maciejewski et al., 2014) and parents' daily dampening of adolescents' positive affect (Griffith & Hankin, 2025) also predicted later symptoms, but as observed person-level scores across intensive assessments (e.g., standard deviations or mean of multiple assessments of negative affect) rather than random effects. These studies concern interaction patterns, individual emotional dynamics, or parenting behaviors.

Evidence about parent-adolescent coupling itself is more limited. Recent reviews of physiological and behavioral synchrony highlight the limited longitudinal evidence and the role of context in links with adjustment (Birk et al., 2022; Sfeir et al., 2026). In 540 Dutch dyads, daily affect synchrony was associated with more adolescent depressive symptoms, also beyond mean affect, whereas hourly synchrony was associated with fewer symptoms only before adjusting for mean affect. In exploratory six-month analyses, neither daily nor hourly synchrony predicted adolescents' symptoms beyond baseline levels. Stronger daily synchrony did, however, predict higher parent depressive symptoms beyond baseline symptoms and mean affect (β = .09, p = .018; Bülow et al., 2026). As a contemporaneous correlation from one assessment period, synchrony could not reveal the direction of influence. The key gap is whether changes in a family's coupling precede later changes in adolescent symptoms. Addressing it rigorously requires estimating daily parameters with uncertainty, testing prediction beyond mean levels and baseline symptoms, and repeating intensive assessments.

Three Designs for Linking Daily Coupling to Long-Term Adjustment

Design 1: Single burst. Consider a hypothetical 28-day diary of maternal and adolescent negative affect, with adolescent depressive symptoms assessed before the diary and six months later. Researchers first ask whether unusually high maternal negative affect predicts unusually high adolescent negative affect the next day, accounting for the adolescent's preceding affect. A random slope captures each family's coupling; each slope rests on that family's own days, so a longer diary yields more reliable slopes (Schultzberg & Muthén, 2018). Because adolescents also influence parents (Boele, Nelemans, et al., 2023; Bülow et al., 2025), both directions should be estimated alongside same-occasion covariation. Dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM; Asparouhov et al., 2018; McNeish & Hamaker, 2020) can then relate the latent coupling slope to later depressive symptoms, adjusting for baseline symptoms and mean maternal and adolescent affect. If stronger coupling predicts worse outcomes, it may be a prospective risk marker. This is consistent with accumulation but does not demonstrate it: the comparison is between families, so stable characteristics could explain both stronger coupling and later symptoms (Bi, Merrin, et al., 2025; Curran & Bauer, 2011).

Design 2: Concurrent change.

Repeating the diary and symptom assessment, for example every six months, opens up two questions. First, do families whose coupling increases more also show larger symptom increases? Two bursts permit a comparison of change scores; additional bursts permit estimation of trajectories. In an adult analogue, increases in stress reactivity across three bursts over 18 years accompanied increases in functional limitations and chronic conditions (Rush et al., 2025). Second, is an adolescent more symptomatic during bursts when their family's coupling is unusually strong? This compares deviations from the same family's typical coupling and symptom levels. An adult analogue found stronger daily stress reactivity during bursts of unusually high perceived stress (Sliwinski et al., 2009). Both analyses describe changes over the same period, so neither establishes which comes first or excludes common causes. Bursts spanning developmental transitions can also test age differences (Chen et al., 2024).

Design 3: Within-family precedence. With at least three paired diary bursts and symptom assessments, statistical models can separate each family's typical coupling and symptom level from burst-specific deviations (Hamaker et al., 2015). Autoregressive paths account for persistence from one burst to the next. Cross-lagged paths ask whether unusually strong coupling predicts higher-than-usual symptoms at the next burst, accounting for symptoms at the preceding burst. Reverse paths test whether elevated symptoms precede stronger coupling. Dynamic measurement-burst models, which integrate multilevel autoregressive models of intensive longitudinal data with the random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM), estimate the daily coupling and across-burst paths jointly, carrying uncertainty in coupling into the developmental analysis (Andresen et al., 2025; Table 1). Three bursts are a minimum, not a guarantee of adequate precision; simulation should guide planning. This design tests whether changes in daily dynamics precede changes in longer-term adjustment, consistent with the TIES view that short-term emotional exchanges within dyads accumulate into longer-term relational and developmental patterns (Butler, 2011; Lougheed & Keskin, 2021).

Extensions. The three designs can also use physiological measures. Repeating a laboratory interaction task at several ages, for example, would let researchers ask whether changes in physiological linkage precede changes in adjustment. Measuring physiology and selfreported affect in the same families would help establish whether coupling in the two measures predicts similar outcomes. Fathers, siblings, and triadic processes can be added as further coupled series. These can test whether father-to-adolescent coupling (Bi, Chen, et al., 2025) carries distinct long-term consequences and how dyadic coupling depends on the wider family, extending prior mother-father-adolescent analyses (Bi et al., 2024). The same designs can test whether adolescent-to-parent coupling predicts parents' later symptoms (Griffith & Hankin, 2025), and cross-cultural comparisons can test whether coupling and its consequences vary with norms of family interdependence. The designs also apply to other family processes. For example, because the direction of daily associations between parenting and adolescent affect differs across families (Boele, Bülow, et al., 2023; Janssen et al., 2021), adolescents' responsiveness to everyday parental support may predict their later well-being.

Considering the Context of Coupling

The same coupling may carry more weight in some contexts than others. Contexts can change quickly or slowly. Among fast-changing contexts, coupling during negative interactions, such as conflict or harsh criticism, may be especially predictive of later symptoms. Among slowchanging contexts, coupling may matter most in families with a chronically hostile climate, persistent economic strain, or a history of adversity. Reviews of parent-child synchrony likewise conclude that its meaning depends on context, including family risk (Davis et al., 2018; Sfeir et al., 2026). In adults, sleep-affect spillover is, like stress reactivity, an individual difference captured by a random slope: how much next-day negative affect rises after nights of greater sleep-onset difficulty than usual. Adverse childhood experiences did not predict later anxiety through spillover. Instead, they amplified its link with later anxiety (Bi et al., 2026). Context may thus sharpen the developmental signal of coupling. Context-specific estimates can reveal links that averages would understate. Each design can test this. The coupling slope can vary with daily conflict within families and with family climate between families. Context-specific coupling can then be tested as a predictor of adjustment beyond overall coupling.

Strengthening Causal Inference

Within-family analysis does not resolve all confounding (Mulder et al., 2024; Rohrer & Murayama, 2023). Separating stable differences can address additive confounding by timeinvariant factors (e.g., stable aspects of neighborhood), but changing circumstances remain possible explanations. A parent's job loss before a burst could strengthen coupling and worsen the adolescent's later symptoms. Researchers should specify the exposure, outcome, and assumed causal relationships to identify confounders requiring repeated assessment. Sensitivity analyses can assess how strongly an unmeasured factor would need to be associated with both coupling and later adjustment, after accounting for measured covariates, to meaningfully weaken or explain away the observed association (Bi, Merrin, et al., 2025; Hernán & Robins, 2020; Mulder et al., 2024).

Conclusion

Daily diaries, EMA, and laboratory psychophysiology document short-term coupling between family members. Establishing its developmental consequences requires following families over longer periods. Our three designs distinguish prospective differences between families, concurrent changes, and within-family temporal precedence. They also sharpen the intervention question. Small child benefits from treating parental depression (Cuijpers et al., 2026) could reflect stable shared risks, modest improvements in parental symptoms, or daily processes that treatment leaves unchanged. Lowering a parent's usual symptoms may help the child while leaving daily fluctuations or the child's responsiveness largely intact. Whether targeting those remaining processes adds benefit is a testable hypothesis. Because treatment trials have mostly involved perinatal and early-childhood samples, whether coupling and its treatmentrelated change differ in adolescence remains to be tested. Repeated assessments across development can clarify which daily family processes predict lasting adjustment and, eventually, help loosen the ties that carry distress from one generation to the next.
