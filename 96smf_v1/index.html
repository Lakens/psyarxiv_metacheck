Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Trust in institutional authority is central to social cohesion and public health engagement, yet declining trust has been increasingly linked to conspiratorial thinking. Despite rapid recreational and clinical interest in psychedelics, little research has examined their interactions with attitudes towards trust and authority.

This manuscript addresses several questions: What is known about the associations between recreational psychedelic use and trust in governmental and medical institutions? How have historical and contemporary cultural narratives shaped the relationship between psychedelics and institutional authority? How might mistrust of medical and governmental institutions among psychedelic users affect the integration of psychedelic-assisted therapies into mainstream healthcare? How might conspiratorial thinking affect linking psychedelic use to institutional mistrust?

We synthesise interdisciplinary literature across empirical studies, theoretical models, and historical analyses, with evidence integrated across three domains: historical and sociocultural context, psychological and mechanistic models of belief change, and contemporary institutional dynamics. Findings indicate that while psychedelic experiences may alter belief processes, there is insufficient empirical evidence to determine how the alteration of these processes, in turn, alters trust in institutions or conspiratorial thinking. These relationships appear context-dependent and influenced by pre-existing social, cultural, and political influences, as well as competing narratives surrounding psychedelics. We suggest that current mechanistic accounts may be insufficient in isolation, and the effects of psychedelics on belief systems cannot be understood without situating them within broader contexts. Future research is therefore necessary to explore and clarify these relationships in a multidisciplinary manner that merges empirical data collection with historic context. Such knowledge would be essential for the responsible integration of psychedelics into clinical and societal settings.

1. Introduction

Trust denotes a willingness to depend on another party's competence, goodwill and integrity when the outcome of doing so cannot be guaranteed in advance (McLeod, 2023). Applied to institutions, this willingness rests on a belief that the institution has both the moral authority and the practical capability to carry out its remit, allowing citizens to hand over authority and accept institutional decisions as legitimate (Baier, 1986). In contrast to compliance, trust entails deliberate vulnerability as opposed to behavioural conformity (Domenicucci, 2018). At the same time, increased governmental trust can incentivise citizens' conformity, which in turn can contribute to policy effectiveness (Cairney & Wellstead, 2021). Social cohesiveness, seeking medical and mental health assistance, and adhering to health recommendations and vaccinations are all linked to trust in governmental and scientific institutions (Birkhäuer et al., 2017; Murphy et al., 2021). Where institutional trust is weak, patterns of political withdrawal emerge, along with a turn toward alternative explanatory systems, including conspiracy theories (Oliver & Rahn, 2016).

Conspiratorial thinking stems from an attempt to explain significant events and circumstances that have caused collective harm by attributing them to the premeditated and malicious acts of powerful groups (Douglas et al., 2017; Greenburgh et al., 2022). These beliefs are widespread in the general population and vary with social context. For instance, belief in US election-related conspiracies fluctuate depending on which party wins (Oliver & Wood, 2014; Uscinski et al., 2016; Uscinski & Parent, 2014; Van Proojien & Douglas, 2018). Stigmatised minority groups also show especially high rates of conspiracy belief (Davis, Wetherell & Henry, 2018; Van Prooijen, Staman & Krouwel, 2018). Illicit drug users are frequently classified as one such stigmatised group (Ahern, 2007; Kallen; 1989), though whether they show a similar pattern of elevated conspiracy belief has not been directly tested.

The prevalence of conspiratorial thinking increases during political conflict (van Prooijen & van Dijk, 2014), and other societal crises, such as natural disasters (Van Prooijen & Douglas, 2018) and disease outbreaks (Romer & Jamieson, 2020). Its societal impacts include resistance to public health measures and vaccine hesitancy (Imhoff & Lamberty, 2020; Freeman et al., 2020; Hornsey et al., 2018). Exposure to conspiracy theories can also decrease trust in governments and institutions, even when unrelated to the theories (Einstein & Glick, 2015).

Institutional mistrust, distrust and conspiratorial thinking can be understood as related but distinct orientations towards institutional authority. While mistrust may involve uncertainty or scepticism towards institutional claims, distrust implies a more negative expectation that institutions are deceitful or acting against the public interest (Griffith et al., 2022). Conspiratorial thinking represents a more extreme form of suspicion, involving the belief that powerful actors are intentionally concealing information or coordinating harmful actions. Consistent with this distinction, Pierre (2020) conceptualises epistemic mistrust as a central component of conspiracist ideation and argues that such beliefs exist on a continuum ranging from generic scepticism to a specific and potentially pathological focus on institutions of epistemic authority, including governments, scientific organisations and corporations (Pierre, 2020).

Unlike paranoid beliefs, which are usually held in isolation and involve perceptions of harm to the self, conspiracy beliefs are shared with others and involve the perception of collective rather than personal harm (Greenburgh et al., 2022). There is, however, a connection between individuals displaying paranoia and those endorsing conspiratorial explanations (Lebedev et al., 2023). Substanceuse patterns may also influence alternative beliefs, but empirical research directly linking substance use to conspiracy or paranoid beliefs remains sparse. One study found psychedelic use associated with higher scores on the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire and greater endorsement of alternative facts (Lebedev et al., 2023). However, the authors suggested that these findings might reflect non-conformist thinking or scepticism towards mainstream information rather than conspiratorial ideation specifically (Lebedev et al., 2023; Bruder et al., 2013).

Classic psychedelics such as LSD, psilocybin, mescaline and ayahuasca act mainly on 5-HT2A receptors (Rucker et al., 2018). Use tends to cluster within particular groups, most notably young adults aged 18-35, festival and rave scenes and those seeking spiritual or personal-growth experiences (Krebs & Johansen, 2013; Palamar, 2020; Baker, 2012). Access has broadened considerably in recent years, driven by more favourable media coverage and the growth of online drug markets, including darknet vendors (Sessa, 2018; Barratt et al., 2016). In North America, this has translated into a steady rise in psychedelic use over the past decade, with growing interest since 2010 and a further increase into the 2020s amid renewed attention to psychedelics for mental health (United Nations, 2024). Europe, meanwhile, exhibits low but persistent prevalence of psychedelic use among young adults (1%), with a few notable exceptions, including the Netherlands, Estonia, Finland and France ranging 1.5% to 4% in the 2020s (European Drug Report, 2026). In England and Wales, self-reported hallucinogen use among 16-24 year olds has declined from 2.9% to 1.5%, consistent with a broader downward trajectory in youth drug use in the region since the late 1990s (Office for National Statistics [ONS], 2025). The ONS itself, however, acknowledges that disclosure issues persist even under the survey's self-completion methodology, meaning drug use is likely underestimated (ONS, 2025). In Australia, psychedelic use has noticeably increased between 2019 and 2023, with past-year psilocybin use doubling from 0.9% to 1.8% of the population (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2024). It has also become one of the fastest-moving jurisdictions in the medicalisation of psychedelics, becoming one of the first countries to permit authorised psychiatric prescribing of MDMA for post-traumatic stress disorder and psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression in 2023 (Therapeutic Goods Administration, 2023). In South America, Columbia and Brazil have some of the highest lifetime prevalence rates for ayahuasca use (0.80% and 0.37% respectively) due to the cultural embedding of ayahuasca (ICEERS, 2023).

This widespread use raises questions about the population-level impact of recreational psychedelic use on trust in institutions, mental health, susceptibility to conspiratorial thinking and social cohesion. Despite the gap in research directly linking psychedelic use to conspiratorial thinking, evidence from adjacent fields may suggest plausible pathways connecting psychedelic use to institutional trust. As previously mentioned, conspiratorial thinking has been robustly associated with paranoia, and paranoid traits have in turn been linked to substance abuse, especially in populations engaging in psychoactive drug use (Lebedev et al., 2023; Murri et al., 2025). Although classic psychedelics alone do not produce dependence or withdrawal (Nichols, 2016), psychedelic use has been shown to co-occur with polysubstance use in some populations (Bălăeț et al., 2025a (Bălăeț et al., , 2025b (Bălăeț et al., , 2023)(REFERENCES), some of which show elevated rates of substance use disorder, and, in turn, paranoid ideation (Mendes et al., 2022; Palamar & Acosta, 2021; Crummy et al., 2020).

There are also plausible cognitive pathways linking psychedelic use to belief change more directly. Psychedelics have been shown to heighten suggestibility and cognitive flexibility, opening space for new patterns of thought and behaviour to take hold (Carhart-Harris et al., 2015; Carhart-Harris et al., 2019). Mystical experiences brought on by psychedelics predict long-lasting shifts in worldview and belief systems (Griffiths et al., 2008). Shifts in belief in conspiracy theories represents one such shift with direct consequences for institutional trust, as reduced trust in political, scientific, and medical authority has been repeatedly linked to exposure to conspiracy theories (Einstein & Glick, 2015). Psychedelic culture has historically coexisted with countercultural and anti-establishment movements, especially in the 1960s and 1970s (Friesen, 2022). Conspiracy theories have significantly increased in the wake of the COVID-19 epidemic, particularly in alternative wellness and spiritual communities, some of which intersect with modern psychedelic subcultures (van Prooijen & van Vugt, 2018; Peters, 2023). Taken together, these historical, psychological, and sociocultural findings point toward a convincing but under-examined relationship between psychedelic use, conspiratorial thinking, and trust in political and medical institutions.

This inquiry is highly relevant in the context of the current "psychedelic renaissance", characterised by renewed scientific and public interest in psychedelic-assisted therapy (Rucker et al., 2018). Trust is central to effective mental health care, particularly in building therapeutic alliances, which have been shown to predict treatment outcomes. In psychedelic-assisted interventions, relational safety and trust in practitioners are critical determinants of benefit (Murphy, 2022). These considerations are becoming increasingly relevant as regulatory access expands, with Australia now permitting authorised psychiatric prescribing of MDMA and psilocybin and Canada expanding access through Health Canada's Special Access Program (Therapeutic Goods Administration, 2023; Richard, 2025). Understanding how psychedelic use relates to trust is thus of growing importance. Indeed, trust in science and government is declining globally, and the documented public health risks of misinformation and conspiratorial beliefs raise concerns about help-seeking behaviours and engagement with evidence-based care (Edelman Trust Barometer, 2022; Lewandowsky et al., 2017). These concerns cannot be addressed through clinical research alone, however, as most psychedelic use continues to occur outside formal therapeutic settings, meaning that its psychological and social effects extend beyond clinical contexts. Even so, no integrated review to date has examined how recreational psychedelic use, conspiratorial thinking, and trust in institutions interact across historical, psychological, and sociocultural domains. This represents a significant gap that this review seeks to address, with relevance for psychologists, psychotherapists, substance use researchers, policymakers, and public health authorities.

We address the following questions: • What is known about the associations between recreational psychedelic use and trust in governmental, and medical institutions?

• How have historical and contemporary cultural narratives shaped the relationship between psychedelics and institutional authority? • How might mistrust of medical and governmental institutions among psychedelic users affect the integration of psychedelic-assisted therapies into mainstream healthcare? • How might conspiratorial thinking affect linking psychedelic use to institutional mistrust?

>Methods

2.1. Scoping Review

This study used a scoping review design. Given the conceptual range of the study issues and the heterogeneity of the evidence base, which includes theoretical frameworks, empirical investigations, historical analyses, and policy-oriented literature, a scoping approach seemed required. The goal of this review was to map important concepts, establish thematic patterns, and integrate findings across disciplinary boundaries due to the lack of a single body of research directly addressing all three constructs together.

The search drew on PubMed, PsycINFO and Google Scholar, supplemented by manual searches of reference lists, grey literature such as government documents, and texts of historical significance to the field. The search process was iterative, which allowed terms to be refined as relevant themes emerged. Search strings were developed to capture the primary conceptual domains of psychedelics, trust, conspiratorial thinking, belief formation and policy context. Terms relating to psychedelics included "psychedelic*", "psilocybin", "LSD", "mescaline", "ayahuasca" and "plant medicine*". These were combined with terms relating to trust, including "trust", "mistrust", "institutional trust", and "medical mistrust", as well as terms relating to conspiratorial thinking such as "conspiracy", "conspiratorial thinking", and "paranoia". Additional terms regarding belief processes included "suggestibility", "belief" and "belief updating", alongside terms related to social and clinical interaction such as "therapeutic alliance" or "rapport" and broader factors "policy" and "regulation".

Targeted searches combined these terms using Boolean operators to maximise coverage. Searches were limited to English-language publications, primarily from 1940 to 2026, to reflect the historical scope of the review.

2.2. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria covered peer-reviewed articles and authoritative reports, such as government publications and policy documents, that addressed at least one of the core domains of interest: psychedelic use, institutional trust or mistrust, conspiratorial thinking, belief formation, or relevant sociocultural and policy contexts. Empirical and theoretical, and historical sources were all included in order to capture the interdisciplinary nature of the topic.

The exclusion criteria included studies focusing exclusively on non-psychedelic substances unless used for comparative purposes, as well as opinion pieces or commentaries that lacked references or substantive analytical content. Titles, abstracts and full-texts were reviewed for relevance to the research question, with inclusion guided by conceptual contributions rather than methodological hierarchy.

2.3. Methodological Considerations

Considering the topic is multidisciplinary and the lack of studies that explicitly connect psychedelics to conspiracy theories, a scoping study was used rather than a systematic review. This format is inherently more interpretive, which brings a corresponding risk of subjectivity in how sources were selected and synthesised. That risk was addressed, though not eliminated, by reporting the search strategy and eligibility criteria transparently and by deliberately seeking out and engaging with perspectives that cut against the review's emerging conclusions.

2.4. Ethical Considerations

Formal ethical approval is not necessary because this study is a scoping review and does not involve the acquisition of primary data or human subjects. However, in accordance with rules set forth by the British Psychological Society and Medical Research Council, the review upholds the ideals of academic integrity, honest depiction of findings, and appropriate discussion of delicate subjects including drug use and mental health. The constraints will specifically address potential biases in the review process (e.g., search strategy, selection, and synthesis judgments) as well as in the body of existing literature (e.g., publishing and advocacy bias, sample homogeneity).

3. Historical Background

Understanding the relationship between psychedelic use, conspiratorial thinking and trust in institutional authority requires situating each within its historical and sociopolitical context. The meanings, uses and perceived legitimacy of psychedelic substances have been shaped by shifting cultural narratives, regulatory regimes and institutional practices. These developments have in turn influenced how individuals relate to authority, expertise and alternative belief systems.

3.1. Post-war Expansion and Early Scientific Optimism (40s-50s)

The discovery of lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) by Albert Hofmann in 1943 marked the beginning of Western psychedelic research (Hoffman, 1980). During the 1950s, psychedelics were primarily viewed through a scientific and therapeutic lens, with considerable optimism regarding their potential applications in psychiatry (Nutt, 2019). Substances such as LSD and mescaline were investigated as treatments for conditions including depression, addiction and anxiety, as well as tools for modelling psychosis (Belouin & Henningfield, 2018). This period was characterised by relatively high levels of institutional trust, particularly in scientific and medical authorities (Pew Research Center, 2025). Government bodies actively supported research efforts, funding a large number of clinical studies (Nutt, 2019). Psychedelics emerged within a broader post-war context characterised by confidence in scientific advancement, technological innovation and the perceived capacity of institutional systems to drive improvements in public health and societal wellbeing (Gannon, 2008). However, even during this early phase, tensions were emerging. Covert programmes such as MK-Ultra, which involved the use of psychedelics in non-consensual experiments, introduced an early association between psychedelics, state secrecy and abuse of institutional power (Meier, 2025). Although largely concealed at the time, subsequent exposure of covert government programmes formed part of a wider pattern of institutional disillusionment. This contributed to the marked decline in public trust observed from the 1960s onwards, as confidence in governmental authority eroded in response to perceived misconduct and opacity (Lipset & Schneider, 1983).

3.2. Counterculture, Prohibition and the Collapse of Institutional Trust (60s-70s)

The 1960s marked a dramatic shift in the social and political meaning of psychedelics. Increasing recreational use, particularly among youth and countercultural groups, led to their association with anti-war activism, spiritual exploration and resistance to authority (Wesson, 2011). Psychedelics became embedded within anti-establishment movements, particularly in opposition to U.S. involvement in Vietnam and broader geopolitical tensions in the Cold War (Wesson, 2011; Koning et al., 2024). In response, governments introduced increasingly restrictive policies. Psychedelics were classified as Schedule I substances in the United States in the late 1960s, culminating in the Controlled Substances Act of 1971 (Bonson, 2018). These legal changes effectively halted clinical research and drove psychedelic use underground (Nutt et al., 2019). This period also coincided with a broader collapse in institutional trust, fuelled by political scandals, war-related disillusionment and the exposure of covert government activities such as MK-Ultra (Meier, 2025; Lipset & Schneider, 1983). Psychedelics, once associated with scientific promise, became linked to narratives of social danger, moral decline and psychological instability (Koning et al., 2024).

The criminalisation of psychedelics cannot be understood solely as a neutral public health response. The War on Drugs has been critically described as a system of structural racism, in which drug laws disproportionately targeted Black, Indigenous and other marginalised communities as part of broader mechanisms of social control (Rosino & Hughey, 2018; Trueblood, 1999). This contributed to enduring mistrust in governmental and legal institutions, particularly among communities disproportionately targeted by enforcement practices, as systemic inequalities and racial disparities in policing have been shown to undermine perceived legitimacy, reinforce marginalisation, and erode trust in law enforcement and judicial systems over time (Fry Sandoval, 2024; Ghandnoosh, 2023).

3.3. Stigma, underground cultures, and emerging alternative epistemologies (80s-90s)

Following prohibition, psychedelic use persisted within underground networks and emerging subcultures: the 1980s and 1990s saw the rise of rave and electronic music scenes, within which substances such as MDMA played a central role (Palamar, 2020). Despite early therapeutic interest, MDMA was classified as a Schedule I substance in 1985, further reinforcing the categorisation of psychedelics alongside more heavily stigmatised drugs (Passie, 2018). During this period, public discourse around drugs was dominated by the intensification of the War on Drugs, particularly under the Reagan administration, as drug use was framed as a moral failing and social threat, contributing to widespread stigma (National Archives, 1986). Psychedelics were increasingly subsumed within broader drug discourses that failed to distinguish between different classes of substances, as anti-drug policies and media representations reduced the complexity of drug use into a singular problem and collectively framed substances as inherently dangerous, deviant and criminogenic (Ceja, 2022). At the same time, emerging media and communication technologies facilitated the development of alternative epistemic communities. For instance, early online forums and communities allowed users to share experiential knowledge about drugs outside formal institutional frameworks (Wax, 2002). These spaces often prioritised peer-based knowledge and subjective experience over scientific or medical authority (Wax, 2002; Boyer et al., 2001). The shift toward decentralised knowledge production marked an important development: individuals increasingly relied on non-institutional sources of information, a pattern that would later become central to both psychedelic subcultures and the spread of conspiratorial thinking (Duxbury, 2018).

3.4. Resurgence of research and the digital transformation of mistrust (2000s-2010s)

The early 2000s saw a gradual revival of scientific research into psychedelics, led by institutions such as Johns Hopkins University (Griffiths et al., 2006; Yaden et al., 2022). This "psychedelic renaissance" repositioned substances such as psilocybin and MDMA within a clinical and therapeutic framework. However, this resurgence occurred within a broader sociopolitical environment characterised by increasing mistrust in institutions: events such as the September 11 attacks, subsequent wars, and revelations of government surveillance (e.g. WikiLeaks disclosures) contributed to the growth of conspiracy narratives and scepticism toward authority (Chanley, 2002; Munro & Kenny, 2024). Simultaneously, the expansion of the internet transformed the dissemination of information. Online platforms and forums enabled rapid circulation of both scientific findings and alternative interpretations, often without clear differentiation between evidence-based knowledge and speculation, and psychedelic communities frequently drew on collective experience and peer narratives, reinforcing epistemic practices outside institutional authority (Wax, 2002; Duxbury, 2018). While media representations of psychedelics began to shift toward therapeutic potential, the new narratives often coexisted with ongoing scepticism toward pharmaceutical companies, regulatory bodies and mainstream medicine. These reflected a complex and sometimes contradictory relationship with authority in the form of trust in psychedelic science alongside mistrust of broader medical, governmental and regulatory institutions (Bender et al., 2026; Esty, 2025).

3.5. The "Psychedelic Renaissance", COVID-19 and Contemporary Trust Dynamics (2020spresent)

The 2020s have been marked by a significant expansion in psychedelic research, including large clinical trials such as COMPASS Pathways' phase 2b study on psilocybin therapy (Goodwin et al., 2022). Regulatory developments such as Australia's 2023 rescheduling of psilocybin and MDMA for psychiatric use, as well as Canada's parallel expansion of access pathways, signal increasing institutional acceptance (Therapeutic Goods Administration, 2023). However, this institutionalisation is occurring against a backdrop of declining global trust in governments and scientific authorities (Edelman Trust Barometer, 2022). The COVID-19 pandemic intensified these trends, with widespread debates over public health measures, vaccine safety and the role of scientific expertise (Abi-Rizk, 2025).

Conspiratorial thinking increased significantly during this period, particularly within online and wellness communities, some of which overlapped with contemporary psychedelic subcultures (van Prooijen & van Vugt, 2018; Peters, 2023). This has given rise to a notable tension in how psychedelics are interpreted. On one hand, they are increasingly framed as clinically legitimate and scientifically validated treatments (Andersen et al., 2021). On the other, they are sometimes understood within alternative frameworks as tools for accessing hidden truths or resisting institutional control (Griffiths et al., 2008). These contrasting narratives reflect broader divisions in epistemic trust, where individuals selectively engage with certain forms of authority while rejecting others.

At the same time, the contemporary "psychedelic renaissance" raises concerns about how legitimacy is constructed. Psychedelics historically associated with Indigenous practices, spiritual communities and counterculture were criminalised and excluded from legitimate medical knowledge, yet many of the same substances are now accepted when placed within regulated medical frameworks. The substances have not fundamentally changed, but their institutional framing has. This raises an important question: if psychedelics become legitimate because they are sanctioned by medical and scientific authorities, what happens when the people using them do not trust those authorities? As psychedelics move from counterculture into mainstream healthcare, this tension may become increasingly important.

3.6. Interpretive Context of History

Beliefs about psychedelics and institutional authority are culturally and historically situated, which limits cross-population generalisability. Cross-cultural evidence illustrates this clearly. During COVID-19, psychedelic use was associated with poorer mental health in some Western samples but not in Spain and Brazil, a difference that could reflect variation in legal status, historical familiarity and social integration (Bălăeț et al., 2023; Révész et al., 2021). Where psychedelics are more culturally embedded and less criminalised, they may carry fewer associations with deviance, stigma and antiestablishment identity. Findings derived from Western populations shaped by the legacy of prohibition should not, therefore, be assumed to generalise across sociocultural contexts.

Historical context also influences how psychedelics are perceived and how their effects are interpreted. Public narratives have shifted from criminalisation and moral panic towards therapeutic optimism and clinical legitimacy (Soylemez et al., 2025). Since expectations shape both the subjective experience of psychedelics and how it is later reported, studies conducted during periods of heavy stigma may reflect participants' mindsets at the time more than the drug's actual effects (Butler et al., 2022). This limits their comparability to findings from the contemporary "psychedelic renaissance" (Bălăeț, 2024; Butler et al., 2022). Prevailing narratives may also shape the experience itself, as expectations formed through public discourse have been proposed to influence how psychedelic experiences are perceived and integrated (Bălăeț, 2024). Online media environments raise a further concern, as interpretations often circulate and gain social legitimacy faster than the evidence required to evaluate them (Soylemez et al., 2025).

This changing landscape carries important implications for institutional trust. Perceptions of psychedelics and their medicalisation may be shaped by whether scientific and medical institutions are viewed as legitimate sources of expertise or as mechanisms of control (Pierre et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Trust in psychedelic medicine therefore reflects broader attitudes towards scientific, medical and regulatory authority, making it difficult to separate psychedelic effects from the contexts through which they are interpreted.

4. Individual Differences in Trust and Belief Related Outcomes

Psychedelic experiences occur within individuals who differ in demographic characteristics, social environments, psychological traits and biological predispositions. These individual differences may help explain in part why identical substances are associated with divergent trust and belief related outcomes and why accounts explaining psychedelic effects purely in terms of drug action are unlikely to capture the full picture. Before considering theoretical accounts of psychedelic-related belief change, it might be necessary to consider these tangible factors that may shape how these experiences are perceived and interpreted in the first place.

4.1. Demographic and Social Differences

Demographic predictors of psychedelic-related major life change show inconsistent associations across studies. Carlisle et al. (2026) found that age and gender initially predicted change in domains such as relationships and substance use; however, neither age nor gender remained a significant predictor once psychological and contextual variables were statistically controlled (see also Perkins et al., 2023). In contrast, Aday et al. (2026) reported the opposite pattern: even after adjustment for other variables, women were 21% more likely than men to report major life change, and both younger age and lower educational attainment remained significant predictors of a greater number of changes. These discrepancies make it difficult to determine whether demographic characteristics contribute independently to psychedelic-related change or primarily reflect broader psychological and contextual factors. The extent to which pre-existing orientations toward belief and authority shape such changes remains unknown.

Religiosity may provide a relevant pre-existing orientation through which authority and belief are interpreted. Carlisle et al. (2026) found higher religiosity among the strongest predictors of reporting major life change following psychedelic use, a relationship that persisted even after excluding participants who reported a change in religious belief itself (Carlisle et al., 2026). The same study found lower educational attainment associated with a greater likelihood of reporting change; the direction of both relationships remains unclear, consistent with psychedelics interacting with existing belief systems rather than producing uniform change (Carlisle et al., 2026).

Direct evidence on institutional trust remains limited to engagement rather than trust itself. Pierre et al. (2024) reported that willingness to disclose psychedelic use to a physician was tied to age and clinical history, but not gender or ethnicity. Separately, in the same sample, mistrust in physicians' judgment was the second most commonly cited reason for non-disclosure, after stigma, suggesting that disclosure may function, at least for some patients, as a behavioural proxy for trust (Pierre et al., 2024). Whether this relationship holds true for the demographic factors found to predict disclosure (age and clinical history), however, was not tested by the study directly.

Social factors may also bear on institutional trust through group mechanisms. People's levels of group attachment and identity fusion differ substantially. These differences may shape whose clinical, communal or spiritual authority a person trusts following a psychedelic experience. Identity fusion describes a strong alignment between personal and group identity, such that commitment to group beliefs becomes highly consequential for the individual (Swann et al., 2009). Pre-existing identity fusion with a ceremony group is associated with subsequent shifts in metaphysical beliefs, mediated by perceived emotional synchrony (shared attention, behavioural coordination and affective resonance) (Timmermann et al., 2021; Apud et al., 2023; Páez et al., 2015; Weiss et al., 2021). This association was strongest among individuals prone to conforming to group norms (Timmermann et al., 2021). Such findings suggest psychedelic-related trust may be redistributed and shifts toward the groups and communities that provide meaning, validation and interpretation following the experience.

4.2. Trait Differences Relevant to Psychedelic Experiences

Beyond demographic and social differences, individuals vary in psychological traits that may influence how psychedelic experiences are interpreted and integrated.

Openness functions both as a pre-existing disposition and as a trait that shifts following psychedelic use. Openness, alongside agreeableness, conscientiousness and meaning in life, was associated with more favourable pre-existing attitudes toward psychedelics (Holas et al., 2025). MacLean et al. (2011) found a high-dose psilocybin session associated with a subsequent increase in openness, persisting at 14-month follow-up and correlated with the intensity of the mystical experience (MacLean et al., 2011). If openness both predicts engagement with psychedelics and increases afterward, the same disposition could plausibly heighten receptivity to legitimate new information and institutions, or equally to alternative and conspiratorial narratives.

Neuroticism presents a similar ambiguity. Godfrey et al. (2024) found high-dose psilocybin associated with a reduction in neuroticism one month later, moderated by the subjective meaningfulness of the experience. Given the association between paranoid traits and conspiratorial thinking (Lebedev et al., 2023; Godfrey et al., 2024), reduced neuroticism could plausibly lower susceptibility to conspiratorial explanations by lowering paranoid vigilance . Equally, however, neuroticism captures general anxious caution as well as paranoia, and its reduction could instead increase susceptibility to unconventional claims of any kind, conspiratorial or otherwise, by removing the hesitancy that would normally prompt scrutiny.

Absorption, a tendency toward immersive attentional engagement, vivid imagery and altered states of consciousness, is one of the most consistently implicated traits in psychedelic response (Lifshitz et al., 2019). Absorption is strongly associated with acute responses after drug dose and predicts the intensity of mystical-type experiences, visual effects and overall altered states of consciousness under psychedelics (Studerus et al., 2012).

Psychedelics themselves appear to act on many of the processes engaged by absorption. LSD has been shown to increase suggestibility and mental imagery, while high-dose psilocybin has been associated with long-term increases in openness to experience, a trait closely related to absorption (Lifshitz et al., 2019). Individuals high in absorption may therefore be more susceptible to psychedelicrelated shifts in meaning, interpretation, and perhaps the sources and authorities they trust afterward.

4.3. Biological Contributions to Trait Differences

Twin studies suggest that around 50% of the variance in absorption is attributable to genetic factors (Lifshitz et al., 2019). Consistent with this, Ott et al. (2005) found that individuals carrying the T/T variant of the 5-HT2A receptor gene exhibited significantly higher absorption scores than other participants. Given that classic psychedelics exert their effects primarily through 5-HT2A receptors, genetic variation at this site may contribute to differences in how psychedelic experiences are perceived and interpreted (Ott et al., 2005).

These trait differences are also associated with variation in psychedelic phenomenology. Absorption predicts the intensity of synaesthesia-like experiences induced by classic psychedelic as well as near-death-like experiences following DMT administration (Lifshitz et al., 2019). Genetic variation may therefore contribute indirectly to outcomes on trust and belief through its influence on psychedelic phenomenology and individual susceptibility to psychedelic experiences.

Based on these natural personality and biological variations, we can expect different individuals to show varying propensities for belief moderation or reinforcement under psychedelic states. Moreover, a proportion of such individuals will choose to take psychedelics, while others may never do so, but would nonetheless possess this latent predisposition were they to engage with these substances. This raises the question: given these pre-existing traits and biological differences, what do psychedelics themselves appear to do to belief formation and trust-related mechanisms?

5. Frameworks for Understanding Psychedelic-related Belief Change

Two prominent models have attempted to explain how psychedelics alter beliefs once the drug has been administered. The REBUS model (Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics) posits that 5-HT2A receptor agonism on deep-layer pyramidal neurons increases their excitability, leading to a desynchronisation of high-level brain rhythms. This causes a relaxation of the precision weighting of high-level priors, or beliefs. It is claimed that in this "anarchic brain" state, the ego's habitual compressive influence is impaired, allowing bottom-up prediction errors (raw sensory or limbic signals) to travel up the hierarchy with unprecedented latitude. As a result, psychedelics may temporarily weaken rigid assumptions about the world, allowing sensory information, emotions and environmental influences to exert a greater influence on perception and interpretation (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).

The second model, SEBUS (Strengthened Beliefs Under Psychedelics), proposes an almost opposite mechanism. It suggests that 5-HT2A agonism can actually strengthen synchronous neural activity, imbuing prior expectations with greater precision (Safron et al., 2025). Here, psychedelics do not necessarily promote openness to alternative perspectives, but may rather reinforce and increase confidence in previously held assumptions. Both models are frequently used to explain psychedelicrelated changes in worldview, meaning-making and belief, yet they imply fundamentally different mechanisms and predictions. Despite these opposing assumptions, both models are frequently used to explain the same subjective experiences and are often presented as complementary rather than contradictory. Contradictions become particularly apparent when considering whether psychedelics increase belief flexibility or belief reinforcement, whether trust-related outcomes are shaped primarily by social context or pre-existing beliefs, and how phenomena such as hallucinations, delusional thinking, apophenia and pareidolia arise under psychedelic states.

The first point of tension concerns the stability of beliefs, where the models offer contradictory explanations for whether psychedelics promote psychological flexibility or ideological entrenchment. REBUS predicts that psychedelics increase flexibility by weakening existing assumptions and increasing openness to revision, whereas SEBUS predicts that psychedelics increase conviction by strengthening existing assumptions. These models therefore imply opposing outcomes for belief change itself: under one account, psychedelic experiences should facilitate the modification of existing beliefs; under the other, they should reinforce them.

The second contradiction concerns the source of trust-related outcomes, where one account (REBUS) locates the source of change in the external environment, whereas the other (SEBUS) locates it in the individual's pre-existing worldview. According to REBUS, an individual immersed in a supportive therapeutic environment may become more trusting of medical or institutional authorities, whereas an individual embedded within anti-establishment or conspiratorial communities may become more receptive to those perspectives. Under this framework, the direction of belief change is largely determined by the informational environment encountered during the psychedelic experience. On the other hand, SEBUS could suggest that individuals who are already trusting of institutions may become more convinced of those views, whereas individuals who are sceptical of institutional authority may leave the experience with those beliefs reinforced. Trust-related outcomes in this case should depend primarily on the beliefs individuals bring into the experience rather than the social environment encountered during it. When applied to institutional trust and conspiratorial thinking, the models generate fundamentally different predictions regarding whether context or prior beliefs should exert the greater influence.

The third conflict concerns both REBUS and SEBUS claiming to account for the same subjective phenomena through opposing means. For instance, both models claim to explain delusional thinking and hallucinations. REBUS argues these arise because high-level priors are too weak to constrain lower-level noise, while SEBUS argues these same phenomena occur because priors are too strong, forcing reality to match internal expectations (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Safron et al., 2025). Similarly, SEBUS attributes pareidolia (discerning patterns in noise) and apophenia (perceiving meaningfulness in random data) to strengthened beliefs, yet these could just as easily be explained under REBUS as a desperate attempt by a prior-stripped brain to explain an influx of anarchic prediction errors by grasping at any available pattern (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Safron et al., 2025). Safron et al.'s (2025) own ALBUS (Altered Beliefs Under Psychedelics) framework offers a partial resolution, where REBUS and SEBUS may describe different regimes of the same dose-response curve of 5-HT2A receptor agonism, with relaxation dominating at very high agonism and strengthening dominating across much of the remaining range. While this might account for why both models can claim the same acute phenomena, it does not resolve the directional question of whether trust-related change is shaped primarily by the immediate social environment or the user's prior internal state. These models imply that psychedelics act as context-dependent amplifiers of pre-existing orientations, with the source of those orientations remaining an open question.

6. Critical Periods of Social Learning as a Potential Mechanism

The social-reward learning model proposed by Nardou et al. (2023) maintains that psychedelics reopen an adult "critical period" for social reward learning and metaplasticity. It is a temporary window, following the acute drug state, of heightened sensitivity to social and environmental input. This predicts that whoever or whatever a person encounters in the days or weeks after a psychedelic experience should carry disproportionate weight in shaping what they come to believe (Nardou et al., 2023). It is assumed that a political opinion offered by a trusted figure during this window, for instance, could plausibly shift the listener's views more than the identical opinion offered a month earlier or a month later.

This has direct relevance to institutional trust and conspiratorial thinking, both of which are acquired and revised socially. If sensitivity to social input is genuinely heightened post-experience, then the environment a person returns to, such as a therapeutic setting, a spiritual community or an antiestablishment network, may do more to determine subsequent attitudes toward authority than the pharmacological experience itself.

7. Implications of the Findings

The uncertainty surrounding psychedelic-related belief change has implications far beyond academic theory. Questions of trust, belief formation and authority permeate almost every domain of the contemporary "psychedelic renaissance", including psychedelic-assisted therapy, healthcare implementation, harm reduction, public communication, policy development and the medicalisation of psychedelic substances. Yet despite the growing importance of these issues, the available evidence remains insufficient to determine whether psychedelics reliably increase or decrease institutional trust, reduce or reinforce conspiratorial thinking, or produce broader changes in belief systems.

Where attitudes towards institutional authority fall, ranging from scepticism to more conspiratorial forms of institutional suspicion, matters for healthcare, public communication and drug policy. Scepticism towards prohibition, commercial interests, regulatory decisions or clinical evidence can constitute legitimate scrutiny, particularly given past institutional failures. At the more conspiratorial end, explanations based on concealed intent may extend to scientific evidence, publichealth guidance and institutional expertise more broadly. If psychedelic experiences heighten sensitivity to social and informational environments, establishing where subsequent attitudes towards authority fall along this spectrum becomes an important question.

At present, however, the evidence base remains unable to answer this question. Scientific and cultural narratives frequently imply a level of understanding that the evidence does not yet support. Psychedelics are increasingly framed as agents of openness, connection, psychological growth and social transformation, yet no consistent directional relationship between psychedelic use and trust or belief has been established (Yaden et al., 2021; Soylemez et al., 2025). The same mechanisms proposed to facilitate flexibility, meaning-making and belief revision (Murphy et al., 2022; Timmermann et al., 2021) could plausibly produce divergent change depending on context.

Clinical inference requires caution, because the evidence derives predominantly from recreational and naturalistic use. Patients are likely to differ from recreational users in psychiatric history, treatment goals, healthcare engagement and relationship to institutional authority. Observations on trust and conspiratorial thinking cannot be assumed to generalise to them.

A concrete mismatch between psychedelic users and institutional actors already exists. Users frequently report mistrust of healthcare professionals, citing stigma and perceived gaps in clinician knowledge, while clinicians themselves report uncertainty about implementation and training, with discrepancies between self-rated and actual knowledge of these substances (Pierre et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Trust is fragmented on both sides of the clinical relationship, with direct consequences for help-seeking and treatment engagement.

This mismatch may play out differently across the access pathways through which psychedelics reach users: either through underground and retreat settings, private clinical models, and public healthcare systems such as the NHS (Simon et al., 2026; Chapman & Naulls, 2025). Underground and retreat settings may offer peer legitimacy without institutional oversight; whereas private models offer scientific credibility alongside concerns about profit and unequal access; public healthcare offers regulations and equity, but inherits existing dissatisfaction with bureaucratic and medical institutions (Noorani, 2020; Chapman & Naulls, 2025; Simon, 2024; McGuire et al., 2026). Substances often framed as personal or emancipatory are consequently being incorporated into institutions many users already mistrust (Simon, 2024; Sanchez Petrement, 2023).

Dialogue between clinical, community-based and Indigenous approaches bears directly on this trust deficit. Contemporary psychedelic use protocols draw in part on practices rooted in Indigenous tradition, yet tensions remain in how these systems of knowledge are represented within Western medical frameworks (Gaughan & James, 2025). Addressing this may improve legitimacy and inclusion as much as any clinical refinement.

Trust may nevertheless be central within treatment settings. Therapeutic alliance influences responses to psilocybin-assisted therapy (Murphy et al., 2022) and trust in healthcare professionals is associated with health outcomes more broadly (Birkhäuer et al., 2017). Whether psychedelic-assisted treatment alters trust in clinicians, healthcare institutions or scientific authority remains unknown. Likewise, little research has examined institutional trust among patients with treatment-resistant depression, despite evidence that many individuals with treatment-resistant depression reach such interventions following repeated treatment failures and frustrations with existing treatment pathways (Breeskema et al., 2022). These experiences may have implications for how novel treatments are evaluated, interpreted and integrated. Addressing these questions will require studies conducted in clinical populations.

8. Interpretation

Contemporary psychedelic research may be attempting to explain belief change before adequately understanding belief formation itself. As previously illustrated, mechanistic models increasingly attempt to explain how psychedelics alter belief, trust and worldview, yet remain unable to establish whether observed changes originate from the psychedelic experience itself, or people's preexisting dispositions, social environments, historical context, or some combination of these.

The treatment of context reveals a parallel pattern. Across clinical, sociocultural and theoretical literatures, factors such as set and setting, therapeutic alliance, group identity, social learning and historical narrative are routinely described as central to psychedelic-related change. However, they are rarely tested directly, with researchers assuming these factors matter without designing studies that isolate or measure these effects. This extends to trust specifically. A substantial body of theory already explains how institutional trust is built and eroded, but this theory has developed almost entirely separately from psychedelic research, leaving the two literatures unconnected rather than genuinely in tension. Context, individual difference and trust formation are each treated as indispensable, yet each remain underexamined, leaving the evidence base better suited to generating hypotheses than to informing social, clinical or policy decisions at scale.

A further asymmetry concerns pace. Psychedelics are moving through clinical trials, regulatory change and medical integration faster than research has examined what that does to institutional trust, healthcare engagement or social cohesion. This gap reflects what the underlying trials were built to measure: symptom reduction and safety, without considering how these could be affected by trust, authority and belief. This raises a question of legitimacy that emerges from the historical record. For individuals who already mistrust these institutions, it remains unclear whether institutional endorsement increases trust in those institutions or merely changes perceptions of the substances themselves.

The literature rarely distinguishes between mistrust arising from erroneous beliefs and mistrust grounded in documented institutional failings. Accounts of non-consensual experimentation and discriminatory drug enforcement provide reasons for scepticism that cannot be reduced to misinformation alone. Interpreting all mistrust as a deficit in communication therefore risks overlooking the historical experiences from which it may arise.

None of these questions can be resolved through clinical research alone. Most psychedelic use continues to occur outside formal therapeutic settings, shaped by legal environments, stigma, local communities and informal knowledge networks rather than clinical protocols. Evidence derived primarily from clinical trials and organised ceremonial settings therefore provide only a partial account of how trust and belief operate across the wider population of psychedelic users.

Finally, theory may currently be advancing more rapidly than measurement. Existing models offer increasingly sophisticated explanations of how psychedelics might reshape trust, belief and worldview, yet direct measures of institutional trust remain rare. As seen throughout the previously described demographic, social and trait literature, individuals differ substantially in characteristics such as religiosity, openness, absorption and identity fusion, yet these factors have seldom been examined alongside baseline trust or conspiratorial thinking. In this sense, psychedelic research may be moving more quickly in explaining trust-related change than in demonstrating that such change has occurred.

9. Limitations

No existing study directly examines the relationship between recreational psychedelic use, conspiratorial thinking and institutional trust together. This review instead synthesises adjacent literatures, and the conclusions drawn are correspondingly indirect. Most available evidence on psychedelics and belief-related change is observational, cross-sectional or drawn from self-selected samples. This limits causal inference and leaves it unclear whether psychedelic use itself shapes trust and belief, or whether individuals with particular pre-existing predispositions are more likely to use these substances and to report such change. Sample bias compounds this problem: Timmermann et al. (2021), for instance, drew on a sample unlikely to represent psychedelic users more broadly, since people who opt into ceremony-based or communal use may differ from causal or first-time recreational users. This limitation is shared by much of the literature this review relies on. Retrospective selfreporting further raises concerns about recall bias, particularly given the intensity of the experiences being recalled.

The relevant evidence base remains sparse, with several findings discussed resting on single studies that have not been independently replicated. St Pierre et al.'s (2024) cross-sectional survey of 791 psychedelic users, for instance, remains the only study identified that directly examines demographic predictors of disclosure to physicians. This matters because patient disclosure to physicians can directly affect the safety and quality of care they receive. As only one study has examined this, the findings have not been replicated and may not generalise beyond that sample. Constructs such as trust, belief, change, and conspiratorial thinking are also operationalised differently across the studies reviewed, as seen in Carlisle et al. (2026) and Aday et al. (2026), which also limit how directly comparable these findings are.

This review carries its own methodological limitations. Sources were selected for conceptual relevance rather than through formal systematic screening, introducing subjectivity into selection and synthesis and creating some risk of confirmation bias. This risk may be even further compounded by the broader research context, as integrating empirical, theoretical and historical literature of uneven rigour risks uneven weighing of evidence. As such, the relationships this review identifies should be read as theoretically grounded and contextually informed, not empirically established.

10. Future Directions

Future research should focus on resolving whether associations between psychedelic use, institutional trust and conspiratorial thinking reflect pharmacological effects, pre-existing disposition, or context. A prospective longitudinal design, profiling institutional trust, conspiratorial thinking, religiosity, metaphysical beliefs and psychedelic expectancy before use, then tracking them immediately after and at follow-up, would establish the baseline the literature currently lacks.

Conspiratorial thinking specifically should be measured with validated instruments, for instance the Generic Conspiracist Beliefs Scale (Brotherton et al., 2013), the Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire (Bruder et al., 2013), or the Belief in Conspiracy Theories Inventory (Swami et al., 2010), embedded within clinical trials, retreat studies and population surveys rather than left to a single measure. Longitudinal administration would establish whether psychedelic experiences increase conspiratorial thinking, reduce it, or simply reveal a pre-existing disposition that use itself does not create.

Combining that profiling with experimental manipulation of context would sharpen the test; a standardised dose delivered in contrasting settings, clinical versus community-based, would show whether trust-related outcomes track the environment or the beliefs brought into it. Varying the timing of identical information relative to the proposed critical learning period would similarly test whether psychedelic experiences increase sensitivity to social influence during integration (Nardou et al., 2023).

The strongest causal test would involve deliberately attempting to modify a specific institutional or political belief during a psychedelic experience. However, such a design would raise serious ethical concerns, as manipulating belief without the participant's consent compromises autonomy.

Better measurement of mistrust itself is also needed; existing research rarely distinguishes misinformed mistrust from mistrust grounded in documented institutional harm. Large-scale qualitative interviews, analysed with natural language processing, could recover how users explain their own trust and mistrust, and whether that explanation differs systematically across groups.

Cross-cultural replication is a related priority. Much of the evidence reviewed comes from Western and English-speaking samples, and Section 3.6 outlined reasons to expect that beliefs about psychedelics and institutional authority are shaped by local legal status, historical familiarity, and stigma. For example, ayahuasca's religious use is legally protected in Brazil, with the National Council on Drug Policy grounding this protection in constitutional guarantees of religious freedom and cultural heritage (ICEERS, 2023). Furthermore, religious ayahuasca groups have pursued formal recognition of the practice as national cultural heritage (MAPS, 2024). Whether the trust-related associations reported in this review hold in contexts where psychedelics are more culturally embedded and less criminalised remains untested, since no study identified has measured institutional trust directly in a setting where psychedelics are culturally and legally embedded.

Existing findings also need replication. Timmermann et al. (2021) identity-fusion findings, for instance, came from a sample that was predominantly Western, well-educated and white, with no assessment of participants' religious background or the views of ceremony staff and peers who may have shaped the reported belief shifts. Recruitment through harm-reduction services, festival outreach and online drug-user networks would show whether such findings reflect population-level trends or simply who is most willing to take part in psychedelic research.

11. Conclusion

The relationship between psychedelic use, trust in authority and institutional trust remains poorly understood, and the "psychedelic renaissance" is advancing more rapidly than the evidence required to support many of its broader claims. While theories of psychedelic-related belief change continue to proliferate, the empirical evidence linking psychedelics to trust and conspiratorial thinking remains limited, indirect and often contradictory.

Evidence on the recreational use and institutional trust is sparse and suggests that any relationship depends on the individual, social and historical context rather than the drug alone. Covert state experimentation, criminalisation and medicalisation have repeatedly repositioned psychedelics relative to institutional authority. Users' mistrust of clinicians, set against clinicians' own uncertainty, may be inherited by clinical integration, but its effect is unclear as patients' trust has not been measured. Institutional attitudes may shift towards greater trust, scepticism, mistrust or conspiratorial interpretations depending on the interaction between psychedelic experiences and their surrounding social contexts. While conspiratorial thinking represents one of the more consequential manifestations of institutional suspicion, direct evidence linking psychedelic use to changes in institutional attitudes remains scarce.

Until these uncertainties are resolved, the "psychedelic renaissance" will remain a promising project whose foundations are still work in progress.
