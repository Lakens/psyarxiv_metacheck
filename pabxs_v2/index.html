Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Ethics Approval Check

Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, and it was present:

For injection trials, participants were informed whether the target could feel pain (pain-sensitive) or not (pain-insensitive, due to local anesthesia), allowing assessment of how cognitive appraisal of the target’s pain sensitivity contributes to the empathic responses and their context-dependent modulation.

Participants were informed whether the target was actually able to feel the pain of the injections, or not, because the injection had been performed under local anesthesia.

In particular, the pain-insensitive condition was expected to place higher demands on affective self-other distinction, as participants had to disambiguate their own aversive response to the sight of the injection from the actual, non-painful state of the target.

We recruited 20 autistic adults (ASD) and 21 CON adults.

Autistic participants were recruited through Austrian autism programs and online advertisements and had received a clinical diagnosis from a qualified psychiatrist or psychologist.

Per ICD-10, 18 participants were diagnosed with Asperger’s syndrome and two with Autistic disorder, both classified as ASD in DSM-5 and ICD-11.

CON were recruited through online advertisements and reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders.

All participants provided written informed consent and received financial compensation.

Thereafter, the EEG recording was set up and participants were seated in an EEG recording chamber (Desone E:BOX, Desone Modulare Akustik) in front of a computer monitor.

After the EEG recording, the participants completed questionnaires assessing empathic and autistic traits.

Participants completed standardized measures of autistic traits (the Autism-Spectrum Quotient questionnaire, AQ, Baron-Cohen et al., 2001; German adaptation by Freitag et al., 2007), empathy/interpersonal sensitivity (Interpersonal Reactivity Index, IRI, Davis, 1980 Davis,, 1983; German adaptation by Paulus, 2009 ), alexithymia (the Bermond-Vorst Alexithymia Questionnaire, version B, BVAQ-B, Vorst & Bermond, 2001), and general intelligence (the Standard Progressive Matrices, Raven et al., 1988; see supplementary materials S1 for further details).

Participants were instructed to attend to the stimuli and empathize with the depicted individual.

At the beginning of the injection block (block 2), participants were informed about the meaning of the background color, indicating whether the target could feel pain; color assignment was counterbalanced across participants.

Although the pain-insensitive condition placed higher demands on affective regulation and appraisal, as participants had to disambiguate their own aversive response from the actual, non-painful state of the target, the pain ratings indicate that participants of both groups showed preserved explicit appraisal of the target’s pain.

Ethical and participant consent statement: All participants provided written informed consent and received financial compensation.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

Empathy for pain-sharing another person's affective state while maintaining self-other distinctiontriggers sensorimotor and cognitive-emotional responses in the observer. We investigated whether these processes differ in autistic adults (ASD) and non-autistic controls (CON). Twenty ASD and 21 matched CON viewed images of a hand being either gently touched with a cotton bud or injected with a needle.

For injection trials, participants were informed whether the target could feel pain (pain-sensitive) or not (pain-insensitive, due to local anesthesia), allowing assessment of how cognitive appraisal of the target's pain sensitivity contributes to the empathic responses and their context-dependent modulation. EEG recorded sensorimotor engagement via event-related desynchronization (ERD) of central mu (7-13 Hz) and beta (14-25 Hz) rhythms. Participants also rated the target's pain and their own unpleasantness.

Both groups rated pain-sensitive injections as more painful than pain-insensitive injections, with no group differences, indicating comparable explicit appraisal of others' pain in ASD and CON. In contrast, self-reported unpleasantness and ERD were modulated by target pain sensitivity only in CON, who reported greater unpleasantness and stronger mu ERD when the target could feel pain vs. not; this modulation was absent in ASD. No group differences emerged for touch or pain-insensitive injections, and viewing perspective (egocentric vs. altercentric) did not differentially affect responses. These findings suggest preserved explicit appraisal of others' pain in autistic adults but reduced contextdependent modulation of affective and sensorimotor responses, pointing to a selective alteration in affective and sensorimotor sharing rather than a global empathy impairment in ASD.

1. INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent difficulties in social interaction and communication, as well as restricted interests and repetitive stereotyped or unusual motor behaviors (Lord et al., 2018; Persico et al., 2020). It has been suggested that reduced empathy is at the core of the social difficulties in ASD (Gillberg, 1992), with empathy broadly defined as the capacity to understand and share emotions of other people (for a recent review see e.g., Rütgen & Lamm, 2024). Yet, the concept of empathy refers to a wide range of complex and mutually interacting cognitive and affective processes, posing a challenge for research aimed at disclosing the nature of alterations of empathy in ASD (Bollen, 2023; Coll et al., 2017). While numerous studies have shown that autistic adults show reduced performance in cognitive empathy-related capacities (such as paying attention to social cues, recognizing emotions from facial expression, or the ability to take the perspective of other individuals), it is less clear whether affective functions (such as sharing and emotionally responding to the affective states of others) are also compromised in this group (for recent reviews and meta-analytic evidence see Cusson et al., 2025; Fatima & Babu, 2023; Van Der Zee & Derksen, 2020). Moreover, there is an ongoing debate as to how self-other distinction, i.e., differentiating between perspectives of self and other, which is a key requirement of empathy, is altered in autistic adults, and how this may impact affect sharing (Bird et al., 2010; Bird & Viding, 2014; Lamm et al., 2016; Simantov et al., 2021).

One commonly used approach in research into the psychological and neural basis of empathy is to study the responses to pain of other individuals, using so-called empathy for pain paradigms (Jackson et al., 2005; e.g., Lamm et al., 2007; Singer et al., 2004). Pain is a salient sensory-emotional experience and serves an important social signaling and behavioral trigger function. It has repeatedly been demonstrated that seeing other individuals in pain evokes in the observer a matching sensorimotor and affective state, and that this is accompanied by activations in several brain regions implicated in the processing of first-hand pain (recently reviewed e.g., by Jauniaux et al., 2019; Rütgen & Lamm, 2024; Zhang & Chen, 2025). Of particular relevance in the present context, seeing images of painful interventions and injuries inflicted on the bodies of others elicits significant activations of the sensorimotor system (Avenanti et al., 2005; Bufalari et al., 2007). These activations can be tracked using electroencephalography (EEG), and they are similar to those when the observers are themselves exposed to noxious stimuli (for review see Riečanský & Lamm, 2019). There is an ongoing debate though regarding the precise functional processes and possible equivalence of such sensorimotor resonance with those engaged by first-hand pain (Hartmann, Riva, et al., 2021; Hartmann, Rütgen, et al., 2021). This debate notwithstanding, it is widely accepted that sensorimotor resonance is an important component of the empathy for pain response, and that empathic pain sharing has strong implications for prosocial behavior (Fan et al., 2014; e.g., Gallo et al., 2018; Hartmann et al., 2022; Krishnan et al., 2016; Zaki et al., 2016).

There is, however, considerable variation in research findings regarding whether sensorimotor resonance to others' pain is altered in autistic adults. Studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which had compared brain responses to images and videos of painful bodily events in autistic adults to those of non-autistic individuals, reported either increased activity in the somatosensory cortex in ASD (Fan et al., 2014), reduced activity in the motor cortex (Lassalle et al., 2019), or no activity difference of sensorimotor areas (Gu et al., 2015; Krach et al., 2015). Assessing corticospinal excitability by applying single pulses of transcranial magnetic stimulation over the primary motor cortex and measuring the amplitude of the elicited motor evoked potentials of the corresponding skeletal muscle revealed corticospinal excitability to be decreased in controls compared to autistic individuals (Minio-Paluello, Baron-Cohen, et al., 2009). This has been interpreted as reduced or absent embodied pain empathy in ASD.

Another method to assess activations of the sensorimotor system is to use EEG (or MEG) measurements of modulations of oscillatory power of the central (Rolandic) mu and beta rhythms.

Numerous studies have shown that observing pain-eliciting, compared with innocuous, bodily events is accompanied by a suppression of mu and beta oscillations, the so-called event-related desynchronization (ERD). In several studies, the magnitude of mu and beta ERD was associated with empathic abilities (for review see Riečanský & Lamm, 2019). In ASD, however, this index of sensorimotor activation has not yet been rigorously explored with respect to empathy for pain. Of note, an earlier study (Fan et al., 2014) reported that reduction of mu (~10 Hz) power was comparable in autistic and non-autistic (control) individuals when observing images of painful events. This study, however, only analyzed averaged EEG and thus has not captured spectral power modulations across single EEG epochs, which are much more directly coupled with ongoing neural processing than averaged EEG. In addition, the stimuli in the study by Fan et al. (2014) were only presented from an egocentric viewing perspective, blurring the observer's distinction between the self and the other as the target of the depicted intervention.

Therefore, the main aim of the current study was to shed more light on sensorimotor and affective resonance to pain of others in ASD. We recorded EEG in a group of autistic (ASD) and nonautistic control individuals (CON) and compared mu (7-13 Hz) and beta (14-25 Hz) ERD elicited by observing pictures of hands experiencing painful needle injections or gentle, non-painful contact by a cotton bud. The painful injections were our condition of main interest, as they allowed us to tap into sensorimotor responses to another person's pain and their putative group differences. The cotton bud stimuli served as a control condition to assess whether the hypothesized differences in responses to the painful injections can be attributed to empathy for pain specifically, rather than being related to nonspecific effects, such as differences in the domain-general processing of actions targeting the body of another person. Two additional experimental manipulations were introduced. The first targeted self-other distinction, a key requirement for accurate empathy. Self-other distinction allows the empathizer to track and disambiguate whether they indeed share what the other person is feeling, or whether (some of) their feelings rather originated in themselves. This capacity has been suggested to be affected in a range of clinical conditions (Eddy, 2022; Pronizius et al., 2026), including ASD (for review see Lamm et al., 2016; Simantov et al., 2021). The present study builds on evidence that sensorimotor activations in response to others' pain serve as sensitive markers of self-other distinction (De Guzman et al., 2016; Riečanský et al., 2020). To manipulate demands on self-other distinction, the hand was depicted in either an ego-or an altercentric (first-person vs. third-person) perspective, with the egocentric perspective carrying higher demands on self-other distinction compared to the altercentric view (see Bucchioni et al., 2016; Lisi et al., 2024 on the effects of viewer perspective on sensorimotor activations).

The second manipulation targeted cognitive appraisal. Participants were informed whether the target was actually able to feel the pain of the injections, or not, because the injection had been performed under local anesthesia. The rationale of this manipulation was that the sight of a needle injection is expected to trigger an automatic aversive response in the observer, which needs to be modulated in light of the knowledge that the injection is not painful for the target. Such appraisal-based modulation has been proposed to involve emotion regulation and valence evaluation, as well as self-other distinction in the affective domain (Lamm et al., 2007). In particular, the pain-insensitive condition was expected to place higher demands on affective self-other distinction, as participants had to disambiguate their own aversive response to the sight of the injection from the actual, non-painful state of the target. This aspect of our design builds up on earlier work by Perry et al. (2010). In their study, mu ERD was sensitive to such a manipulation in non-autistic individuals, implying sensorimotor resonance to be weaker when the observer knew that the injection was not painful to the target (see also Lamm et al., 2010; Tomova et al., 2017 for a similar approach used in fMRI studies).

Concurrently to capturing brain responses by means of EEG and frequency-based topographic analyses, we assessed participants' affective experiences and their subjective responses to the stimuli by using two types of self-report measures, using 7-point rating scales. One such scale required participants to estimate the amount of pain the target shown on a stimulus was supposedly experiencing, while the other required reporting to what extent seeing a certain stimulus resulted in aversive feelings in the participant. Similar rating scales have been widely used in previous empathy research (see e.g., Rütgen et al., 2015; Batson et al., 1987 for review). Here, ratings of the target's pain index the explicit appraisal of the target's pain, and thus also allow us to verify that participants understood and applied the information about the target's pain sensitivity. Ratings of one's own unpleasantness, in contrast, index the observer's affective response, and thus captures affect sharing and its context-dependent modulation.

Together with mu and beta ERD, these measures allowed us to assess the context-dependent modulation of the observer's cognitive, affective, and sensorimotor responses by the target's pain sensitivity.

In sum, the present study addressed whether autistic adults differ from non-autistic adults in how cognitive appraisal of another person's pain modulates their own affective and sensorimotor responses. To our knowledge, sensorimotor oscillatory responses to others' pain have not yet been examined in ASD under such a manipulation. Because the pain-sensitive and pain-insensitive images differ only in the background color, i.e., the information about the target's pain sensitivity, our design allows us to dissociate the explicit appraisal of the target's pain (target pain ratings) from the contextdependent modulation of the observer's own responses (unpleasantness ratings and mu and beta ERD).

While the study was not pre-registered, as its planning and data collection had taken place during a time (2013) (2014) when open-science practices had not yet become a standard in our lab, our major hypotheses were as follows: First, we predicted that autistic individuals and CON would differ in the overall strength of their EEG and behavioral responses to the injection images, i.e., a main effect of Group (H1). As previous studies had reported increased, reduced, or unchanged sensorimotor responses to others' pain in ASD (Fan et al., 2014; Gu et al., 2015; Krach et al., 2015; Lassalle et al., 2019; Minio-Paluello, Baron-Cohen, et al., 2009), and as findings on affective empathy in ASD have been inconsistent, depending on the measures used and on co-occurring alexithymia (Bird et al., 2010; Van Der Zee & Derksen, 2020; for review, Cusson et al., 2025), we did not specify the direction of group differences a priori. Therefore, two possibilities existed: either autistic individuals would show weaker responses, in line with purported reductions in empathic resonance, or stronger responses, aligning with generally elevated distress associated with social stimuli, but in particular affectively loaded ones.

Second, as the egocentric view was expected to place higher demands on self-other distinction, we predicted these group differences, irrespective of their direction, to be more pronounced in the egocentric viewing condition, i.e., a Group × Perspective interaction (H2).

Third, as the pain-insensitive condition was expected to place higher demands on self-other distinction, among other appraisal-related processes, we predicted that the groups would differ in the extent to which their neural and affective responses were modulated by cognitive appraisal, i.e., by the information whether the target could feel pain, again without specific assumptions regarding the direction of this difference (Group × Sensitivity interaction, H3). Irrespective of group, we expected higher target-related pain ratings for pain-sensitive than for pain-insensitive injections, and stronger mu and beta ERD to injection than to touch images, both serving as manipulation checks. Finally, if group differences reflected empathy for pain specifically, rather than domain-general processing of actions targeting another person's body, they should not be present for the touch stimuli (control condition, H4).

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

>MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1 Participants

Throughout the manuscript, we use identity-first language ("autistic adults"), which most autistic adults prefer ( Kenny et al., 2016; see also Autism, 2021). We recruited 20 autistic adults (ASD) and 21 CON adults. Sample size was determined by practical constraints and was comparable to previous EEG studies of motor resonance and mu rhythm desynchronization in ASD (e.g., Fan et al., 2014; Minio-Paluello, Baron-Cohen, et al., 2009; also see Lockhart et al., 2024). Autistic participants were recruited through Austrian autism programs and online advertisements and had received a clinical diagnosis from a qualified psychiatrist or psychologist. Per ICD-10, 18 participants were diagnosed with Asperger's syndrome and two with Autistic disorder, both classified as ASD in DSM-5 and ICD-11. Autistic individuals with comorbid psychiatric or neurological conditions or psychopharmacological medication were excluded from study participation. CON were recruited through online advertisements and reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. The groups were matched for age and sex (Table 1), and each included two left-handed participants. All participants provided written informed consent and received financial compensation. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Vienna and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.2 Procedure

The study was carried out in the EEG lab of the University of Vienna, Austria. The session started with a brief interview, followed by the administration of a test of general intelligence and a questionnaire assessing alexithymia traits (see section 2.3), which were adopted to assess group differences in these psychological variables and treat them as possible confounds. Thereafter, the EEG recording was set up and participants were seated in an EEG recording chamber (Desone E:BOX, Desone Modulare Akustik) in front of a computer monitor. They were instructed to relax, look straight ahead, attentively watch the presented visual stimuli, and avoid movements. The well-being of participants was monitored with a video camera. Regular breaks in data recording were introduced in order to prevent fatigue and attention deterioration. Stimuli-related ratings were carried out within the EEG data recording block. After the EEG recording, the participants completed questionnaires assessing empathic and autistic traits.

2.3 Trait measures

Participants completed standardized measures of autistic traits (the Autism-Spectrum Quotient questionnaire, AQ, Baron-Cohen et al., 2001; German adaptation by Freitag et al., 2007), empathy/interpersonal sensitivity (Interpersonal Reactivity Index, IRI, Davis, 1980 Davis, , 1983; German adaptation by Paulus, 2009 ), alexithymia (the Bermond-Vorst Alexithymia Questionnaire, version B, BVAQ-B, Vorst & Bermond, 2001), and general intelligence (the Standard Progressive Matrices, Raven et al., 1988; see supplementary materials S1 for further details).

2.4 Stimuli and stimuli-related ratings

Stimuli depicted a male right hand either being touched with a cotton bud or injected with a syringe (without visibility of the agent's hand, see Fig. 1). Images varied in perspective (egocentric vs. altercentric), and background color indicated the target's pain sensitivity (pain-sensitive vs. paininsensitive). Participants were instructed to attend to the stimuli and empathize with the depicted individual. Stimuli were presented in two separate blocks ("touch" in block 1 followed by "injection" in block 2) to avoid contextual carryover effects. At the beginning of the injection block (block 2), participants were informed about the meaning of the background color, indicating whether the target could feel pain; color assignment was counterbalanced across participants. During EEG recording, participants intermittently rated perceived pain in the target and their own unpleasantness on 7-point scales. Further details on stimulus timing, trial structure, and presentation parameters are provided in supplementary materials S2.

2.5 EEG recording and analysis

EEG was recorded from 59 equidistant scalp sites using Ag/AgCl ring electrodes mounted on a flexible EEG cap (montage M10, EASYCAP, GmbH, Herrsching, Germany). Additional electrodes were placed above and below the left eye and at the lateral eye canthi to register eye movements. Underneath the electrodes, the skin was gently scratched and electrodes were filled with a degassed electrolyte gel.

Electrode impedances were measured using an impedance meter (Ing. Kurt Zickler GmbH, Pfaffstätten, Austria) and kept below 3 kΩ. EEG was recorded with a DC-EEG amplifier Neuro Prax (neuroConn GmbH, Ilmenau, Germany). The reference electrode was placed on the right mastoid and the ground electrode at the nasion. Signals were analog filtered in the range of 0-250 Hz and digitally stored with a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Bottom image: A photograph of a male right hand in an egocentric perspective being injected with a needle syringe. This is an example trial from block 2, which comprised 240 injection images, arranged in a 2 x 2 manner based on perspective (altercentric or egocentric) and pain sensitivity (pain-sensitive or pain-insensitive). The background color of each image (either blue or green, counterbalanced between participants) indicated whether the target could feel pain or not (because their hand was under local anesthesia).

After an average of 12 images, participants rated (i) the pain experienced by the target in the last image and (ii) how unpleasant viewing the image was for themselves, using a 7-point scale from "not at all" to "extremely strong". ISI -inter-stimulus interval, ms -milliseconds.

EEG was processed using MATLAB (TheMathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA) and the EEGLAB toolbox (Delorme & Makeig, 2004). EEG data were re-referenced to average mastoid reference and digitally filtered in the range from 0.1 to 45 Hz. Data epochs were extracted in the time range from -1000 to 3000 ms with respect to stimulus onset. In each epoch, the mean signal within 200 ms prior to the stimulus onset was subtracted. All epochs were inspected and those containing gross artifacts as well as epochs with voltage values exceeding ±75 μV or signal drifts larger than 50 μV were removed. The number of retained epochs was comparable between groups (median 54-58 of 60 epochs per participant and condition in block 2; minimum 42). Thereafter, independent component analysis (ICA) was calculated and the ICs with artifactual signals (such as blinks, eye movements, muscle and heart activity) were subtracted. Channels containing no reliable EEG signals (which occurred occasionally) were not included in ICA and were interpolated after subtracting the artifactual ICs. EEG signals were then transformed to reference-free scalp current source density in order to eliminate volume-conducted contributions from distant regions and hence signals likely not originating in sensorimotor cortex (Kayser, 2009).

Four sensors over each hemisphere, overlying the left and right sensorimotor cortex (around C3 and C4), were selected a priori for further analyses (see insert in Fig. 3; electrode labels are listed in supplementary materials S3). For each of these sensors, event-related spectral power modulation (also termed event-related synchronization/desynchronization, ERSD) was computed using the newtimef function of EEGLAB 14 (Delorme & Makeig, 2004). Data were resampled to 128 Hz and fast Fourier transform (FFT) was applied to a sliding signal window of 32 samples (250 ms) with Hanning tapering and 4 times zero padding. The window was systematically shifted by one data point over the epoch (from -500 ms to 2500 ms). For each individual and experimental condition, ERSD was expressed in decibels relative to the mean power of the 500 ms prestimulus baseline. Throughout this paper, we use ERSD to refer to spectral power modulation in general, and event-related desynchronization (ERD) and event-related synchronization (ERS) to refer specifically to decreases and increases in oscillatory power, respectively.

2.6 Statistical analysis

The data were processed and analyzed in JASP (JASP Team, 2023, version 0.18.1 ) and R (R Core Team, 2025; RStudio Team, 2020). Group differences in continuous demographic characteristics, trait measures and stimuli ratings of the touch images in block 1 (pooled across perspectives) were tested using a Mann-Whitney test for independent samples. Descriptive analysis of the self-report stimulirelated ratings in block 2 and ERSD indicated the presence of a few outlying observations (values 1.5 interquartile range below Q1 or above Q3), and therefore the data were winsorized using two-sided 10% trimming (separately for each experimental condition and group) prior to statistical modeling. Subsequently, linear mixed effect models (LMEM) were calculated.

For the ratings of the injection images (block 2), the LMEM included a fixed between-subject factor Group (CON vs. ASD) and fixed within-subject factors Sensitivity (sensitive vs. insensitive to pain) and Perspective (egocentric vs. altercentric view).

For ERSD to touch images (block 1), the LMEM included a fixed between-subject factor Group and a fixed within-subjects factor Hemisphere (left vs. right). For ERSD to pain images (block 2), the LMEM included fixed between-subjects factor Group, and fixed within-subject factors Sensitivity, Perspective, and Hemisphere.

All LMEMs included participant-specific random intercepts, random slopes by participant for within-subject factors, and correlations among random effects, where estimable (i.e., the maximal random-effects structure supported by the data). All LMEMs were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood and omnibus fixed effects were evaluated using Type III with p-values derived from the Satterthwaite approximation for degrees of freedom, as these were shown to produce optimal estimates even for smaller samples (Luke, 2017). Semi-partial R² was computed to estimate effects sizes for the LMEM analyses. Significant interactions involving the factor Group were further analyzed by calculating estimated marginal means and simple contrasts, with p values adjusted using Holm method.

To quantify evidence for the absence of group differences, non-significant between-group comparisons were supplemented with Bayesian analysis. A Bayes factor in favor of the null hypothesis (BF01) > 3 was interpreted as moderate evidence for the null hypothesis.

Finally, considering that autistic participants in our sample scored high on cognitive alexithymia, and previous work has shown that alexithymia, rather than autism per se, may account for reduced empathic brain responses to others' pain (Bird et al., 2010), we conducted an additional exploratory analysis, examining whether cognitive alexithymia accounted for the observed Group × Sensitivity interactions. For this purpose, participant-level sensitivity effects for unpleasantness ratings and mu ERD were entered as dependent variables in exploratory linear regressions with Group and cognitive alexithymia (BVAQ cognitive subscale) as predictors.

3.2 Stimuli-evoked psychological effects (pain and affect ratings)

In block 1 ("touch"), no statistically significant group differences were found, neither for target-related painfulness [median (IQR): CON = 1.00 (0.25), ASD = 1.00 (0.35), U = 208, p = .964] nor for self-related unpleasantness [median (IQR): CON = 1.00 (0.07), ASD = 1.00 (0.94), U = 179.5, p = .330], with most participants giving the lowest possible ratings in both groups.

In block 2 ("injection"), ratings of target-related painfulness were overall higher in the painsensitive condition (i.e., the injection into a non-anesthetized hand) than in the pain-insensitive condition (i.e., the injection into an anesthetized hand, Fig. 2, Sensitivity: p < 0.001, R² = 0.807; supplementary materials S4, Table S1), confirming the effectiveness of the cognitive appraisal manipulation. The main effect of perspective (egocentric vs. altercentric) was not significant (p = 0.398). Ratings of target-related painfulness were higher in the pain-sensitive (red dots) than in the pain-insensitive (green dots) condition and did not differ between groups. For unpleasantness, CON reported greater self-perceived negative affect in the pain-sensitive compared to the pain-insensitive condition, whereas this modulation was absent in the ASD group. In addition, CON reported higher unpleasantness than ASD in the pain-sensitive condition. CON -non-autistic individuals (control group), ASD -autistic individuals (ASD group).

Contrary to H1 and H2, neither the main effect of Group (p = 0.678) nor the Group × Perspective interaction (p = 0.92) was significant. The Group × Sensitivity interaction was not significant either (p = 0.838; supplementary materials S4, Table S1). As the absence of a significant effect does not constitute evidence for its absence (Keysers et al., 2020), we additionally computed Bayesian factors, which provided moderate evidence for the absence of group differences in painfulness ratings in both the painsensitive (BF01 = 3.11) and the pain-insensitive condition (BF01 = 3.17). As the pain-sensitive and paininsensitive images differed only in background color, these results indicate that participants of both groups used the information about the target's pain sensitivity when judging the target's pain, consistent with preserved explicit appraisal of the target's pain in this task.

In block 2, the intensity of negative affect (i.e., self-perceived unpleasantness) elicited in the observers when viewing the injection stimuli was stronger in the pain-sensitive condition than in the pain-insensitive condition (Sensitivity: p < 0.001, R² = 0.387; supplementary materials S4, Table S2), confirming the effectiveness of the cognitive appraisal manipulation. The main effect of perspective (egocentric vs. altercentric) was not significant (p = 0.714).

Contrary to H1 and H2, neither the main effect of Group (p = 0.159) nor the Group × Perspective interaction (p = 0.445) was significant. In line with H3, the intensity of negative affect differed between the groups as expressed by a significant interaction effect (Group × Sensitivity: p < 0.001, R² = 0.296, condition than in the pain-insensitive condition (rating mean difference = 1.042, SE = 0.161, p < 0.001).

In contrast, the unpleasantness ratings of autistic individuals were not affected by the target's pain sensitivity (rating mean difference = 0.105, SE = 0.165, p = 1.000). After accounting for Group, cognitive alexithymia (BVAQ cognitive subscale) was not significantly associated with unpleasantness differences (b = -0.002, p = .799). This result does not support cognitive alexithymia as a potential explanation for the observed Group x Sensitivity interactions (see supplementary materials S5 for more details).

Furthermore, CON reported higher unpleasantness in the pain-sensitive condition compared to autistic individuals (rating mean difference = 1.057, SE = 0.439, p = 0.048). In the pain-insensitive condition, the groups did not differ in unpleasantness ratings, with Bayesian analysis providing moderate evidence for the null hypothesis (BF01 = 3.16).

The findings indicate a dissociation between cognitive-evaluative and affective components of empathy for pain. While both groups show preserved explicit appraisal of others' pain, only CON exhibit context-dependent modulation of affective responses. Thus, for the ratings, H1 and H2 were not supported, whereas H3 was supported for unpleasantness ratings.

3.3 EEG data

The basic pattern of changes in EEG oscillations over the sensorimotor cortex was similar for all stimuli and conditions (Fig. 3; group mean ERSD across the experimental conditions and hemispheres are depicted in supplementary materials S6).

Rapidly after the onset of a touch or injection image, transient ERS occurred in the delta/theta band (1-7 Hz). Thereafter, with a latency of about 200 ms following stimulus onset, ERD occurred in the frequency range of 7-25 Hz, peaked within the first second, but persisted during the whole period of image presentation. Based on the pattern of ERSD, time-frequency windows of analysis were selected (time interval: 200-1200 ms, mu ERSD: 7-13 Hz, beta ERSD: 14-25 Hz). Given the lack of consensus on EEG frequency band boundaries across studies (Newson & Thiagarajan, 2019) and recommendations (Babiloni et al., 2020), we adopted a mu range of 7-13 Hz to accommodate interindividual variability in peak frequency, including lower alpha/mu peaks associated with age and autistic traits (Dickinson et al., 2018; Strang et al., 2022). The upper cut-off for the beta band was set at 25 Hz to capture the observed ERD at higher frequencies and align with previous studies of sensorimotor processing (Avanzini et al., 2012; Cheyne et al., 2003).

In each window, peak ERD (i.e., minimum ERSD) was identified for each sensor, condition, and individual, and entered into the statistical analysis to evaluate the experimental effects. Peak rather than mean ERD was used to reduce the influence of interindividual and between-group variability in peak frequency and response timing (Dickinson et al., 2018; Haegens et al., 2014; Peter et al., 2022). First, as a manipulation check, we compared the overall magnitude of ERSD between block 1 (touch images) and block 2 (injection images). To do so, we calculated, for each block and group, mean mu and beta ERSD across hemispheres and conditions. Viewing injection images elicited a stronger ERD in the mu (p = 0.004) and the beta band (p = 0.040) than viewing touch images, and this effect was comparable in both ASD and CON group (Block × Group interaction, ps > 0.456, see supplementary materials S4, Tables S3 and S4). Thus, the manipulation was successful.

Next, we analyzed mu and beta power modulation in each block separately, with a focus on the effects involving the factor Group. Consistent with the expectation for the control condition, no reliable group differences emerged for touch images (block 1, main effect of Group, p = 0.417, supplementary materials S4, Table S5). For beta ERSD, we found a significant Group × Hemisphere interaction for beta ERSD (p = 0.041, see supplementary materials S4, Table S6), descriptively reflecting opposite hemispheric asymmetries in the two groups.

For injection images (block 2), in line with H3, we found a significant Group × Sensitivity interaction in mu ERSD (p < 0.001, R² = 0.056). As depicted in Fig. 4, CON showed stronger ERD in the pain-sensitive condition than in the pain-insensitive condition (mean ΔERSD = -0.258 dB, SE = 0.082, p = 0.007), a difference that was not present in autistic participants (mean ΔERSD = 0.143 dB, SE = 0.084, p = 0.271). Contrary to H1 and H2, neither the main effect of Group (p = .328) nor Group x Perspective interaction was significant for mu ERSD (p = 0.303, supplementary materials S4, Table S7), and no significant effects involving the factor Group were found for beta ERSD (Table S8).

A simulation-based sensitivity analysis indicated that our study had approximately 90% power to detect a Group × Sensitivity interaction on mu ERD of the observed magnitude (0.40 dB) at α = 0.05 (for details see supplementary materials S7).

An exploratory sensor-wise analysis showed that, in CON, the tendency toward stronger ERD in the pain-sensitive than in the pain-insensitive condition extended beyond the selected sensors, whereas no such pattern was evident in ASD; however, no individual sensor reached statistical significance (supplementary materials S8). Further, an exploratory analysis with alexithymia scores did not support cognitive alexithymia as a potential explanation for the observed Group x Sensitivity interactions (mu ERD differences, b = -0.010, p = .151; see supplementary information S5 for more details). Thus, for the EEG data, H1 and H2 were not supported, whereas H3 was supported for mu ERD, but not for beta ERD.

4. DISCUSSION

The present study investigated sensorimotor resonance and pain empathy in autistic compared to nonautistic individuals (CON). While recording EEG, participants observed a target hand being touched by a cotton bud (block 1) or injected with a needle syringe (block 2). In block 2, the background color indicated to the participants whether the target hand was sensitive or insensitive to pain as a result of local anesthesia. Irrespective of group, the EEG and rating analyses indicated effects that aligned well with previous work. This included consistent ERD patterns over the central scalp in response to all stimuli, and pain ratings that largely matched the experimental conditions (i.e., higher ratings of targetrelated painfulness in the painful conditions, lower ratings where the target was not in pain). This pattern of findings suggests that our experimental design worked as intended and triggered the expected psychological and neural responses.

Autistic participants correctly differentiated the target's pain between the pain-sensitive and pain-insensitive conditions, but, unlike CON, their own unpleasantness was not modulated by the target's pain sensitivity and was lower than that of CON in the pain-sensitive condition. The effect of target pain sensitivity on mu ERD was evident in controls but not in autistic adults. Contrary to our hypothesis, these psychological and EEG effects were not moderated by viewing perspective of the depicted hands. Thus, the groups did not differ in the overall strength of their responses (H1) or in the effect of viewing perspective (H2), but in how their own affective and sensorimotor responses were modulated by the information about the target's pain sensitivity (H3). The control condition (H4) confirmed our hypothesis of no consistent group differences for the touch stimuli, indicating that the group differences for injections cannot be attributed to domain-general differences in response to the stimuli.

The central finding of this study is a conceptual dissociation: autistic adults showed preserved explicit appraisal of the target's pain, but no context-dependent modulation of their own unpleasantness and mu ERD by the target's pain sensitivity, while the overall strength of their responses did not differ from that of controls. In our view, the most parsimonious interpretation is that autistic adults use information about another person's pain when judging it, but that this information is less translated into a corresponding adjustment of their own affective and sensorimotor responses. Below, we first consider alternative explanations that the present data allow us to address, namely a failure to understand the cue, alexithymia, and domain-general differences in stimulus processing or attention. We then discuss mechanisms that the present data cannot adjudicate and that require future research, namely altered motor prediction and first-hand pain processing.

The ratings of target-related painfulness show that autistic adults, like CON, correctly identified the conditions of present or absent pain sensitivity, i.e., the situations in which injections to the hand would or would not evoke pain in the target. Yet, unlike controls, they showed no context-dependent modulation of their own unpleasantness. This pattern is consistent with reduced (or less accurate) affect sharing, but it may also reflect differences in the awareness of one's own emotional states.

However, cognitive alexithymia did not account for the group differences in the context-dependent modulation of unpleasantness or mu ERD, which remained significant when alexithymia was taken into account. In addition, autistic participants did not report lower unpleasantness in the pain-insensitive condition, making a general under-reporting of negative affect unlikely. Nevertheless, as self-reported unpleasantness relies on introspection, more subtle differences in the awareness of one's own emotional states cannot be fully excluded.

Reduced sharing of others' emotions in autistic individuals has been reported in numerous previous studies (for review see Fatima & Babu, 2023; Minio-Paluello, Lombardo, et al., 2009; Van Der Zee & Derksen, 2020) and linked with decreased emotional reactivity and emotional awareness (for review see Bird & Cook, 2013; Huggins et al., 2021; Kinnaird et al., 2019). In accordance with this research, the scores of cognitive alexithymia indicate that identification of emotions in our sample of autistic individuals was lower than in the control participants. It has been found that decreased emotional awareness is associated with increased self-reported trait personal distress (Guttman & Laporte, 2002; Moriguchi et al., 2007). In line with these findings and in agreement with the majority of previous reports (for review see Fatima & Babu, 2023; Song et al., 2019), autistic individuals in our sample scored higher than controls on the personal distress scale of the IRI questionnaire. Results of the behavioral assessments are thus generally congruent with previous research on ASD.

The lack of group differences in the EEG and ratings in the insensitive condition suggests that the groups did not differ in what would have to be automatic and largely bottom-up driven neural responses to displays of aversive and potentially harmful objects (i.e., the needle), even when the object was directed at the body of another person. Although the pain-insensitive condition placed higher demands on affective regulation and appraisal, as participants had to disambiguate their own aversive response from the actual, non-painful state of the target, the pain ratings indicate that participants of both groups showed preserved explicit appraisal of the target's pain. Also, groups did not differ in their responses to innocuous touch by a cotton bud. The absence of these differences allows us to better contextualize the existing group differences in response to the painful injections. It suggests they cannot simply be attributed to domain-general factors that are not specific for pain or empathy. However, as cognitive appraisal of the target's pain sensitivity encompasses several processes besides self-other distinction specifically -such as emotion regulation, valence evaluation and cognitive categorizationneither the preserved explicit appraisal nor the reduced context-dependent modulation in autistic participants can be taken as specific evidence of differences in self-other distinction. This contrasts with the perspective manipulation, which targeted self-other distinction more directly, but contrary to our predictions did not reveal any group differences. Thus, it seems our data do not fully confirm our own proposition and theoretical arguments that autistic participants may be affected in the domain of selfother distinction (Lamm et al., 2016).

Since sensorimotor rhythms are sensitive to attentional modulation (Schuch et al., 2010; for review see Kemmerer, 2021) deployment of attention must also be considered as a mechanism potentially playing a role in the observed group effects. It has often been reported that autistic individuals pay less attention to social stimuli and increasingly avoid negative stimuli (for review see Chita-Tegmark, 2016; Stuart et al., 2023). Diverting attention away from distressing stimuli may represent a coping strategy to prevent overarousal and discomfort from social situations (for review see Yi et al., 2022). It has been recently reported (Li et al., 2024) that autistic children, compared to non-autistic controls, spent shorter time looking at images of limbs in painful situations. Furthermore, Galang and Obhi (2020) have demonstrated that stronger attentional engagement to others' physical injuries enhances motor facilitation in the observer over the baseline level. We did not collect eye gaze data, so differences in overt attention cannot be excluded. However, painfulness ratings, which required flexible instruction following and accurate stimulus recognition, suggest that autistic participants attended adequately to the images. Moreover, the absence of consistent ERD differences in the touch control condition argues against a simple domain-general explanation such as general attentional differences.

As expected based on numerous previous observations, viewing images of painful vs. nonpainful bodily events was associated with power reduction of mu and beta band EEG oscillations over the central scalp region (for review see Riečanský & Lamm, 2019). When considering the effect of block (touch vs. injection images), this effect was comparable in autistic and non-autistic participants and resembles the results of the study by Fan et al. (2014). On the other hand, since Fan and co-authors (2014) adopted a very different method to assess the power in the mu band, our and their results cannot be directly compared. It is well established that viewing images of painful bodily events activates cortical areas engaged in sensorimotor processing and elicits facilitatory effects on motor behavior (Fabi & Leuthold, 2016; Galang et al., 2017 Galang et al., , 2021;; Galang & Obhi, 2020 , 2021; Morrison et al., 2007). Yet, in autistic individuals, the engagement of sensorimotor processes in response to others' painful injuries showed no context-dependent modulation by the target's pain sensitivity, as reflected by the absence of the stronger mu ERD to pain-sensitive compared with pain-insensitive injections that was observed in CON. Previous EEG studies of ASD, which assessed modulation of sensorimotor rhythms to visual stimuli, had mainly focused on action observation. The majority of them reported that power reduction of the mu rhythm during observation of human movements was weaker in autistic in comparison with non-autistic individuals, which has been generally interpreted as an index of reduced activation of the mirror neuron system in ASD (for review see Lockhart et al., 2024). Motor differences are among the core signs of ASD and analyses show that ASD is associated with a wide range of motor difficulties (for review see Coll et al., 2020; Zampella et al., 2021). Several motor differences are particularly linked with the social domain such as reduced facial and body mimicry to other's emotions, reduced motor contagion from other's actions and gaze, difficulties in imitating other's movements or coordinating actions with other individuals (reviewed e.g., in Becchio & Castiello, 2012; Casartelli et al., 2016; Cerullo et al., 2021; Lidstone & Mostofsky, 2021; Minio-Paluello, Lombardo, et al., 2009; Moseley & Pulvermüller, 2018). Our findings are compatible with the view that altered visuo-motor integration may contribute to social difficulties in ASD.

A mechanism that the present data cannot test directly, but that is compatible with the mu/beta dissociation, is altered motor prediction. While observing pain vs. no-pain images elicited a stronger ERD in both mu and beta bands, the Group × Sensitivity interaction was only present in the mu band.

ERD responses of mu and beta rhythms to experimental manipulations are usually very similar and strongly coupled, but research shows that Rolandic mu and beta oscillations reflect different neurophysiological processes (Brinkman et al., 2014; Stolk et al., 2019). In particular, a recent study by Ghilardi et al. (2023) suggests that mu and beta power reductions over the sensorimotor cortex are related to specific types of preparatory motor processes. While mu ERD was related to anticipation of actions, beta ERD rather reflected action probability. The absence of sensitivity-dependent mu ERD in autistic individuals could thus indicate decreased expectations of these participants that the painful event will elicit motor behavior as a consequence (such as protective movement or reactive stiffening of the limb in response to the pain inflicted). This explanation would be in line with the accumulating evidence that ASD is linked with differences in predicting others' upcoming actions (Schuwerk et al., 2016; Von Der Lühe et al., 2016; for review see Cannon et al., 2021; Keysers et al., 2024). We can speculate that when exposed to others' painful events and experiences, autistic individuals would demonstrate a complex of mutually interrelated differences pertaining to own emotional experience and the propensity to act, as well as to the expectation of the other person's subsequent behavioral actions, despite a preserved explicit appraisal of the other person's pain.

Another possible mechanism, which the present study did not assess, concerns first-hand pain processing in ASD. Several studies, including those from our group (recently reviewed by Hartmann et al., 2026), have demonstrated that observers' own pain sensitivity and neural processing play a role in how they perceive and respond to other individuals in pain. Furthermore, aberrant processing of pain has been linked with altered brain oscillatory activity assessed by EEG or MEG (Ploner et al., 2017).

There are numerous reports of diminished pain perception in ASD (for review see Bogdanova et al., 2022; Moore, 2015). An "indifference to pain" is listed in the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria of ASD as an example of decreased reactivity to sensory stimuli often present in these individuals. However, the research outcomes are considerably heterogeneous, with some studies indicating a decreased pain perception, other studies the opposite, and yet other studies documenting atypical, qualitatively different pain sensations in ASD. Nevertheless, there is evidence that decreased affect sharing to pain of others is associated with decreased first-hand pain sensitivity in autistic adults or high autistic traits (Chen et al., 2017; W. Zhang et al., 2023). Furthermore, studies involving individuals with high alexithymia (a trait prevalent in our sample and ASD in general) show that reduced ability to perceive emotional states is associated with a complex pattern of alterations in somatosensation and the processing of pain (Borhani et al., 2017; Katz et al., 2009; Nyklíček & Vingerhoets, 2000; Pollatos et al., 2015). Therefore, it would be useful to explore in more detail in future studies how different aspects of pain processing (sensory vs. affective) are related to affective resonance to pain of others in autistic individuals.

Despite several methodological strengths, the study has limitations. We did not assess gaze fixation or participants' own pain perception, both of which may have contributed to the observed effects. The sample size and its composition also limited our ability to examine sources of variability within the autistic group. Nevertheless, our sample size was comparable to those of previous mu-rhythm studies in ASD (see Lockhart et al., 2024) and the main finding of our study is backed by a sensitivity analysis indicating adequate statistical power. Furthermore, the perspective manipulation did not produce detectable effects in either group, despite rather strong predictions to do so; whether this reflects an insufficiently strong manipulation of visuo-motor demands on self-other distinction remains an open question. Moreover, although current source density transformation reduces volume-conducted activity, the spatial resolution of scalp EEG does not allow us to attribute the observed effects to specific cortical areas. In addition, the use of static images may reduce ecological validity compared with dynamic social stimuli. Finally, autistic participants viewed stimuli depicting non-autistic individuals, who may have been perceived as less relatable than other neurodivergent persons, consistent with the "double empathy problem" (Milton, 2012). Future research should address these issues.

5. CONCLUSION

Our study provides evidence that autistic individuals show reduced context-dependent modulation of affective and sensorimotor responses when observing other's painful bodily events. Knowing that the target could feel pain increased mu ERD and self-reported unpleasantness in non-autistic controls, but not in autistic participants, despite comparable overall response strength between groups. At the same time, autistic participants correctly judged whether the injections were painful for the target, indicating preserved explicit appraisal of the target's pain in this task. Together, this points to a selective alteration in how affective and sensorimotor sharing is adjusted to the context, rather than a global empathy impairment in autistic adults. Further neuroscientific research is needed to clarify how these alterations are related to the processing of first-hand pain, the awareness of self and other, and behavior toward others in pain and distress.
