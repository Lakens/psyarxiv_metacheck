Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Combat veterans experience higher incidences of depression, anxiety, and PTSD, along with challenges adjusting to life post-discharge, but have been under-served by traditional mental health treatments. Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu has emerged as potentially therapeutic for combat veterans, although empirical support is lacking. A single-arm pre-post pilot study was used to evaluate seven psychosocial outcomes among participants from the We Defy Foundation program (N = 40; 87.5% male; Mage = 41.1, SD = 5.8). Primary outcomes were self-reported post-traumatic stress severity (PTSD Checklist for DSM-5; PCL-5), depressive symptoms (Patient Health Questionnaire-9; PHQ-9), anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7; GAD-7), and military-to-civilian transition difficulty (Military to Civilian Questionnaire; M2CQ). Secondary outcomes included Self-Efficacy (General Self-Efficacy Scale; GSE), Functional Disability (World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule; WHODAS 2.0), and Life Satisfaction (Satisfaction with Life Scale; SWLS). Assessments were completed at baseline (T0; n = 40), 3 months (T3; n = 34), and 6 months (T6; n = 23). Results revealed high symptom scores at baseline, with significant improvements in the four primary outcomes at both T3 (Cohen's dz = 0.52-0.63) and T6 (dz = 0.84-1.04) and seven of eight remained significant under Bonferroni correction. GSE and SWLS improved significantly at T3, and SWLS and WHODAS at T6. These preliminary findings suggest that BJJ participation may be associated with positive mental health improvements for the veteran population and provide a basis for future controlled studies investigating similar, complementary and alternative approaches.

INTRODUCTION

After military service, many Americans carry at least one mental health diagnosis, with the most common being Post-traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), other anxiety disorder, and alcohol use disorder, with estimated prevalence rates of 28%, 22%, 12%, and 6%, respectively (Brancu et al., 2017; Department of Veterans Affairs & Department of Defense, 2016). Related is the prevalence of suicidal ideation and PTSD among veterans post-discharge which has risen sharply at a population level over the past fifteen years going from 8% in 2011 to 14% in 2025 (Fischer et al., 2026; Pietrzak et al., 2026). Veterans suffer from these mental health issues concurrently with physical health concerns as well, particularly musculoskeletal difficulties (e.g., orthopedic injuries and back pain), that are the number one source of service-connected disabilities among veterans receiving VA compensation, exceeding the individual contributions of both mood disorders and PTSD (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, 2023) resulting in considerable distress for the veteran, and additional strain on the Veterans Health Administration (Maynard et al., 2018).

Current best practice for the above mental health conditions emphasizes evidence-based treatments (EBTs) including psychotherapy and pharmacological intervention (Schnurr et al., 2024). Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) and Prolonged Exposure (PE) have the best evidence of effectiveness in treating PTSD (Schnurr et al., 2022), while treatment of MDD appears more agnostic to the specific modality (VA/DoD Clinical Practice Department of Veterans Affairs & Department of Defense, 2022). Despite the need for better interventions (Steenkamp et al., 2015), challenges remain in terms of access, utilization, and the narrow scope of a treatment paradigm relative to one of wellbeing and thriving.

There is a known inequity in treatment access for former service members related to several issues: geographic distribution of care (Doyle & Streeter, 2017), difficulty navigating VHA eligibility and enrollment (Bovin et al., 2019), longer and unequal wait time for specialty care (Gurewich et al., 2023), and access to the VA systems in general due to discharge status (Elbogen et al., 2018). Several barriers exist for veteran care including stigma (e.g., the sentiment that therapy is for the weak), fear of adverse effects to employment/career advancement opportunities, distrust of civilian mental health systems, and a perceived cultural mismatch between the "patient" role and military identity. These are largely reflected in lower initiation rates and dropout rates near 50% (Edwards-Stewart et al., 2021; Penix-Smith & Swift, 2025).

Collectively, these challenges point to the need for and value in developing interventions that carry the following characteristics: empirically supported, culturally relevant, accessible, and able to foster engagement for those veterans who either decline or fail to pursue traditional clinical mental health services (Seal et al., 2010). One option is the integration of physical activity and complementary approaches within the VHA's Whole Health framework which views wellness as multidimensional and person-driven, going beyond symptom reduction to recognize purpose, community, and daily functioning (Bokhour et al., 2022; Krejci et al., 2014). The evidence basis for physical activity as a mental health intervention has grown substantially (Rebar et al., 2015; Rosenbaum et al., 2015), however, generic formats for resistance or aerobic training constitute the overwhelming majority of exercise programs in the literature. These may be sound physiologically but miss opportunities to leverage related factors such as group cohesion, discipline, and a shared mission/challenge. A robust complementary intervention for this group should offer not only physical activity but a framework consistent with military identity, a community emulating the kinship of service and a structure rewarding dedication. The focus of this research is on one such possibility: Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu (BJJ). BJJ is a sport and grappling-based martial arts system with roots in Japanese Judo, which focuses on positional control (e.g., having an opponent pinned) and submission techniques (e.g., joint manipulation resulting in an opponent "tapping out" to signal defeat) with emphasis on live sparring. The infrastructure and ethos of modern BJJ practice maps onto much of military culture, containing: (1) a belt-rank hierarchy mirroring military rank structure, (2) physical rigor and shared hardship mirroring unit cohesion, and (3) technical mastery mirroring mission focus. The training gym and community provide a "new unit" in the civilian world (Collura, 2018). This has long been recognized outside of academia by organizations like the We Defy Foundation (WDF).

Following the biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1980), we suggest several pathways through which BJJ may confer benefits beyond the well-documented benefits from vigorous physical exercise (Rebar et al., 2015), though testing these mechanisms was beyond the scope of this study. First, BJJ carries a distinct culture of discipline, perseverance, and mutual respect mapping onto military values, and shared physical struggle may serve as a form of bonding that many veterans report losing upon separation from service. Second, the technical complexity of BJJ demands problem solving under pressure, thereby building and relying on executive function, attentional control, and self-efficacy through the gradual acquisition of skill. Third, training demands present-moment attention and graded exposure to physical closeness, while participants retain autonomy, which may interrupt ruminative cycles (Hsu et al., 2015) and offer a structured rehearsal of distress tolerance (Craske et al., 2014). Fourth, the social membership inherent to the practice and shared experiences with fellow training partners promote connection and combat isolation. Taken together, these pathways could support the (re)construction of a coherent identity after separation, which many veterans experience as an identity transition as much as an occupational one (Orazem et al., 2017). Veterans' own treatment goals often extend beyond symptom relief to restored social roles and a sense of progress and purpose (Rattray et al., 2023; Wells et al., 2025).

The peer-reviewed research on this topic is sparse. To date, only five articles in academic journals study mental health outcomes of BJJ in a veteran population, only three of which are empirical. One review by Blomqvist Mickelsson (2021) included 12 articles that conceptualize BJJ as largely a psychosocial therapy and found a trend of a negative association between training experience and aggression. The review by Segovia et al. (2024) highlighted that there is very little systematic research on the subject, and the studies that exist are limited by small, cross-sectional samples. Of the three research studies that used longitudinal designs, Willing et al. (2019) investigated a 5month course of BJJ for 29 male US service members, but only 9 participants completed the study, showing improvements in symptoms of PTSD, depression, anxiety, and substance-use. Second, Weinberger and Burraston (2022) examined the trajectory of PTSD symptoms and general factors of well-being after 20 hours of BJJ (spread across multiple weeks) resulting in reductions in trauma-related symptoms and improvements in areas like self-confidence, patience, and empathy, but design flaws and missing data issues cloud a clear interpretation. Most recently, Channon et al. (2025) conducted the first study with a comparator group, comparing BJJ with CrossFit training among military veterans and emergency services personnel in the United Kingdom. Both modalities showed improvements in anxiety, depression, PTSD, and sleep over a two-month time frame. They also produced some unique insights based on qualitative interviews, which suggested BJJ participants more frequently connected their identities to the benefits of "constructive hardship" and community support.

The current pilot study attempts to fill this void using a single-arm pre-post longitudinal study design to evaluate changes in the following four primary outcome variables from Baseline (T0) to 3-Month (T3) and 6-Month (T6) follow-up in a group of veterans participating in a We Defy Foundation BJJ Program: PTSD symptom severity (PTSD Checklist for DSM-5; PCL-5), depressive symptoms (Patient Health Questionnaire-9; PHQ-9), anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7; GAD-7), and transition from military-to-civilian life (Military to Civilian Questionnaire; M2CQ). Secondary outcomes assessed were self-efficacy (General Self-Efficacy Scale; GSE), functional disability (World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule; WHODAS 2.0), and satisfaction with life (Satisfaction with Life Scale; SWLS). We had two main aims: first, to evaluate the change in the primary outcomes hypothesizing decreases in symptoms and difficulties at both follow-ups, and second, to evaluate secondary outcomes which we expected to improve similarly. Linear mixed models using all enrolled participants supplement these analyses. Exploratory aims included a direct comparison of 3-and 6-month outcomes, associations between training sessions and response, and sensitivity analyses addressing missing data and outlier influence. We also describe feasibility based on retention and training attendance. This study is framed as a single-arm pilot with the goal of providing preliminary estimates to inform a controlled trial and not to establish efficacy.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were veterans recruited through the We Defy Foundation, a national nonprofit organization providing BJJ training scholarships and resources to combat veterans with serviceconnected disabilities. This process involves a twice-yearly application cycle where veterans apply for the scholarship and We Defy assesses fit with the program and determines eligibility (which requires a VA disability rating of 80% or higher, having served in a combat zone, having an honorable discharge from the military, being separated or retired from active duty, being willing to train at least twice a week for one year, and being new to Jiu-Jitsu or a current white belt). The study team did not independently verify all We Defy criteria. Additional exclusions for the current study were inability to understand English and lack of access to a computer or phone with internet capability and videoconferencing software. Participants enrolled between June and December 2025.

The study was approved by the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki; all participants provided informed consent via videoconference and electronic signature. Data were stored as coded study IDs on HIPAA-compliant platforms. Procedure BJJ training was administered through We Defy Foundation "Approved Training Facilities" (currently numbering 1,209 across 50 States, 2 US Territories, Guam, and Japan), a classification awarded by the organization that recognizes gyms after having demonstrated safe and quality instruction (with teachers at the highest belt rank (black) and positive track records). Sessions themselves were not standardized, but typically consisted of a structured warm-up, technical instruction and drilling, and live sparring (rolling) for 60-90 minutes.

Assessments took place at baseline and after 3 and 6 months (median of 96 and 187 days after baseline, respectively). This first assessment window was chosen to mirror approximate length of care seen both in exercise-based interventions (Jadhakhan et al., 2022) as well as trauma-focused psychotherapy protocols (e.g., CPT or PE; (Schnurr et al., 2022) and the second was used to examine if changes continued with more training. Participants were encouraged to keep a training log to support their reporting of average session length and number of sessions attended.

Study data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, tools hosted at UT Southwestern (Harris et al., 2019).

Measures

Seven outcomes were assessed via self-report by participants at T0, T3, and T6. All instruments have established psychometric properties, most of them in veteran populations. Internal consistency at 3-and 6-month follow-ups was in the acceptable to excellent range (Cronbach's α = .76-.97) Primary Outcomes PHQ-9 (Kroenke et al., 2001). The Patient Health Questionnaire is a 9-item depression severity screener. Items are rated from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), and total scores span 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater severity (α = .89 in this sample). Common practice suggests cut-off scores between 5 and 9 be classified as mild, and ≥ 10 as moderate-to-severe, which holds true in the veteran population as recommended by the VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guidelines (2022). GAD-7 (Spitzer et al., 2006). The Generalized Anxiety Disorder screener gauges anxiety severity with 7 items rated on the same 0 to 3 scale as the PHQ-9 and total scores ranging from 0 to 21 (α = .93 in this sample). It shares the same thresholds as the PHQ-9 of between 5 and9 = mild, and ≥10 = moderate-to-severe. PCL-5 (Blevins et al., 2015). The posttraumatic stress disorder checklist for DSM-5 is a 20-item DSM-5 PTSD severity measure (α = .97 in this sample) with items rated from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). Scores range from 0 to 80, with scores ≥ 33 (the upper end of the 31-33 range validated) being flagged for probable PTSD (Bovin et al., 2016). M2CQ (Sayer et al., 2011). The Military to Civilian Questionnaire is a measure of transition difficulties reintegrating after service. Its 16 items (e.g., "Dealing with people you do not know well") are rated from 0 (no difficulty) to 4 (extreme difficulty). Totals range 0 to 64 (items marked N/A were excluded and totals prorated), with higher scores indicating greater difficulty (α = .93 in this sample).

Secondary Outcomes GSE (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995). The General Self-Efficacy Scale is a 10-item global measure of self-efficacy with scores ranging from 10-40; higher scores indicate greater self-efficacy (α = .92 in this sample). WHODAS 2.0 (Üstün, 2010). The World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 is a functional disability measure -probing the past 30 days -with summed scores ranging 0-144. The 36-item version was used in this study, which has been used extensively in the veteran population (Marx et al., 2015). Higher scores indicating greater disability (α = .96 in this sample). SWLS (Diener et al., 1985). The Satisfaction with Life Scale consists of five items rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), measuring its namesake with scores ranging 5 to 35; scores below 20 fall in the dissatisfied range (Pavot & Diener, 2008; α = .81 in this sample).

Data Analysis

An a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 was used to help determine sample size before enrollment (Faul et al., 2007), resulting in N = 34 for a matched-pairs t test (two-tailed, dz = 0.50, α = .05, power = .80). We targeted 40 individuals anticipating a 15% attrition rate.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

Paired-samples t tests were computed to compare T0 to T3 for all completers (n = 34) as well as T0 to T6 (n = 23). The primary effect size used was Cohen's dz (M change / SD of difference score; Lakens, 2013) to match the paired test with 95% confidence intervals computed from the noncentral t distribution. Cohen's d values (computed using baseline SD; Cohen, 1992) are also reported to enable smoother comparison to other study results in the literature that published benchmarks for cohorts, cross-sectional analyses or other non-paired samples (Table 3a and 3b). The PHQ-9, GAD-7, PCL-5, and M2CQ were designated primary outcomes, and the GSE, WHODAS 2.0, and SWLS secondary outcomes.

To correct for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied separately within the primary (four) and secondary (three) test outcomes at each follow-up (uncorrected p values also reported). As a stricter check, we also applied Bonferroni correction across all 14 comparisons with baseline.

To estimate change across all observations, linear mixed models were fit to the full sample at all time points (T0, T3, T6) with T0 as reference (time treated as a categorical fixed effect). A random intercept was used for each participant to account for within-person correlations. A sensitivity model is reported in the supplement (Table S2) that adds reported training sessions as a covariate. Models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The model retains all 40 participants under the missing at random (MAR) assumption (no data were imputed).

Exploratory Analyses

Pearson and Spearman correlations were calculated for training sessions in the first 3-month assessment window and T0 to T3 change for all seven outcomes to gauge possible dose-response effects, with FDR correction across the seven outcomes. We also compared T3 and T6 directly among participants who completed both (n = 23). To describe clinical significance, we counted participants who improved or worsened by an established threshold and who moved below the clinical cutoffs described above. Thresholds were PCL-5 ≥ 15 points (Marx et al., 2022), PHQ-9 ≥ 5 (Löwe et al., 2004), and GAD-7 ≥ 4 (Toussaint et al., 2020). Lastly, a sensitivity analysis was conducted with and without participants with extreme change scores (|z| > 3) to assess if results were driven by single cases at a given time point.

Missing not at random (MNAR) tipping-point sensitivity was used to assess robustness of primary and secondary outcomes for participants missing T6 data (Cro et al., 2020). For missing values at T6, T0 scores plus a penalty delta (δ) were used for imputation; the tipping point was the smallest δ that yield a nonsignificant result at α = .05.

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.6.0; R Core Team, 2026) with the following packages: psych (Revelle, 2015) and nlme (Pinheiro et al., 2026). Scripts used for data analyses can be made available upon request by the corresponding author.

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics

Forty veterans enrolled at baseline. The sample was predominantly male (n = 35 [87.5%]), white (77.5%), well-educated (65% had a bachelor's degree or higher) and had a mean age of 41.1 years (SD = 5.8, range = 25-53). Demographic and military service characteristics are presented in Tables 1a and 1b (and see Figures S1-S2 for expanded visualizations). Occupation at discharge varied (n = 38), with the largest fields in combat arms and maintenance and logistics (21.1% each), followed by security and emergency services (18.4%), aviation (18.4%), intelligence and communications (15.8%), and medical (5.3%; Table 1b).

Baseline results showed that 80% reported at least mild depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥ 5) and 52.5% moderate to severe (PHQ-9 ≥ 10; outcome intercorrelations in Table S1).). For anxiety, 72.5% reported at least mild anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 5), and 57.5% moderate to severe (GAD-7 ≥ 10). In the full sample, 45% (18/40) were above the probable PTSD threshold (PCL-5 ≥ 33). On average, participants scored 21.40 (SD = 6.63) on the SWLS, with 32.5% (13/40) scoring in the dissatisfied range (< 20). Statistics for each variable by time point can be found in Table 2 (See Figure S3 for more details).

Of the 40 original participants enrolled, 85.0% (n = 34) completed T3. Seventeen participants missed the 6-month assessment window, leaving a sample of 23 (57.5% of baseline); of these 17, 11 had completed T3 and six had completed neither follow-up. Thus, the power analysis held for T3, but not for T6 where the 23 remaining participants allow for 80% power to detect effects of dz = 0.61 or larger. To test if there were significant differences in any of our outcome measures at T0, Welch's independent-samples t tests were run comparing T3 completers to non-completers on all seven clinical scales. These revealed no significant differences (all ps > .45). The same comparisons at 6 months showed no significant baseline differences (all ps > .06), but completers had higher baseline depression scores (PHQ-9; M = 11.87 vs. 8.24, d = 0.59, p = .065), PTSD severity (PCL-5; M = 39.96 vs. 28.00, d = 0.56, p = .075), anxiety (GAD-7; d = 0.42) and lower life satisfaction (SWLS; d = -0.51) than non-completers. Among participants who completed the 3-month assessment, those lost before 6 months had smaller 3-month improvements than those retained (PCL-5 of -5.2 vs. -13.0; PHQ-9 of -1.8 vs. -3.4) and fewer training sessions by 3 months (M = 23.1 vs. 32.0), although M2CQ change was similar (-5.6 in both groups).

Primary Outcomes

All four primary outcomes demonstrated statistically significant improvements at T3 (n = 34) after BH-FDR correction, with medium effect sizes (dz ranged from 0.52 to 0.63; Cohen, 1992). Full results are available in Table 3a and depicted in Figures 1 and 2.

At T6 (n = 23), the four primary outcomes showed statistically significant improvement after BH-FDR correction, with large effect sizes (dz = 0.84-1.04; Cohen, 1992). With Bonferroni correction across all 14 comparisons, seven of eight primary comparisons remained significant (3-month M2CQ adjusted p = .071), and no secondary comparison did. For full results, see Table 3b and Figures 1 and 2.

Lastly, to address potential differential attrition and to offer a stronger evaluation of our outcomes, linear mixed models were fit using all 40 enrolled. The results were consistent with completeronly analyses, where all four primary outcomes showed significant improvements from baseline to T3 and T6 (all p ≤ .003). For secondary outcomes, WHODAS and SWLS improved at both time points though GSE did not change significantly. Estimates were smaller when training sessions were added as a covariate in the model, which was expected due to sessions accumulating with time (see Table S2 for full models).

Secondary Outcomes

Two of the three secondary outcomes showed statistically significant differences at T3 compared to baseline (GSE; dz = 0.45, pFDR = .045, SWLS; dz = 0.39, pFDR = .047). WHODAS did not reach FDR-corrected significance (Table 3a).

At T6, SWLS was significantly higher (dz = 0.60, pFDR = .020) and WHODAS was significantly lower (dz = 0.60, pFDR = .020) versus baseline, both indicating improvement. Though the GSE was not significantly higher, excluding onc extreme change score made the GSE improvement significant (Exploratory Analyses; Table S3).

Exploratory Analyses

Across the follow-up 3-month assessment window, participants reported attending a mean of 29.1 sessions (SD = 14.0), with 20 of 34 (59%) reaching 26 sessions or more (roughly, the two sessions per week expected). More training sessions were associated with greater improvements from T0 to T3 for PCL-5 (r = .51, 95% CI [0.20, 0.72], p = .002), GAD-7 (r = .49, 95% CI [0.18, 0.71], p = .004), and PHQ-9 scores (r = .39, 95% CI [0.07, 0.65], p = .021), and remained significant after FDR correction (pFDR ≤ .049; Spearman ρ = .39-.45). Associations with other outcomes were nonsignificant (r ≤ .28). Participants improving more may have gone more, so these associations do not prove an effect of dose (Figure S4). Excluding one participant with the largest PCL-5 improvement who also reported 75 sessions (z = 3.28), Pearson correlations were smaller and nonsignificant (r = .25-.31), Spearman correlations changed little (ρ = .33-.40).

Among the 23 participants with both follow-ups, PHQ, GAD and PCL scores continued to improve from 3 month to 6 month follow-up (dz = 0.55-0.60, 95% CIs from [0.10, 0.98] to [0.15, 1.04], all pFDR = .021), whereas M2CQ scores did not change significantly. By T6, approximately half of the completers showed meaningful improvements on each of the three symptom measures (PCL-5 ≥ 15 points: 11/23, 48%; PHQ-9 ≥ 5: 11/23, 48%; GAD-7 ≥ 4: 13/23, 57%). By T3, corresponding figures were slightly lower: 35% to 41% of 34 completers. Worsening by the same margin was comparatively rare (only one at T6 and two to four at T3). Finally, for those at baseline with symptoms above the clinical cutoff, most were below it by T6 (PCL-5: 8 of 13; PHQ-9: 11 of 15; GAD-7: 11 of 15; Table S5).

Outlier detection flagged six extreme change scores (|z| > 3), that if excluded, did not change any primary results. Excluding one PCL-5 reduction of 66 points (z = -3.32) at T3 did not remove the effect (dz = 0.64, p < .001). Excluding one GSE score (z = -3.50) at T6 made the improvement significant (dz = 0.57, p = .014; Table S3). In the tipping point analysis, T6 results only became nonsignificant if missing data is assumed worse than baseline by 3.0 to 3.5 points on the PHQ-9, GAD-7, M2CQ, and WHODAS, 10.5 points on the PCL-5, or 1.0 point on the SWLS (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this preliminary investigation, combat veterans with service-connected disabilities who engaged in BJJ reported improvements in psychiatric symptomatology and indices of wellbeing with six of seven outcomes improving by three months. At six months, six of seven outcomes were also improved, and among participants who completed both follow-ups, PTSD, depression and anxiety symptoms continued to improve between T3 and T6. Importantly, these results were found across not only the domains of psychiatric symptoms (depression, anxiety, PTSD), but also functional wellbeing (life satisfaction, disability) and veteran-specific constructs (military-to-civilian transition). Further, mixed models using all 40 enrolled participants showed the same pattern for the four primary outcomes.

Two factors distinguish our study over existing work. First, positive findings on the M2CQ speak directly to transition-specific challenges in this population like relating to others, feeling belonging, finding meaning (Sayer et al., 2010), suggesting BJJ research may add to the literature on adjustment-related difficulties that generic exercise programs do not often measure. Second, the sample endorsed symptom severity near and above the clinical cut-offs for several measures at baseline indicating that change was found in a sample more impaired than typical in this literature (Bellón et al., 2021; Hegberg et al., 2019). PCL-5 scores are particularly relevant to this population and at both time points showed meaningful change. By T6, the effect size observed (d = 0.82, using baseline SD) was a similar magnitude to within-group change of self-reported PTSD severity after PE and CPT (d = 0.64-0.74; Schnurr et al., 2022). These estimates are not directly comparable due to the uncontrolled sample in this study, but they make a case for a controlled trial. Improvements may stem from several pathways including, repeated exposure to reduce avoidance and hyperarousal, a bodyfocused modality that addresses somatic symptoms, and a training environment structured to counter hypervigilance, but further research is needed to evaluate these possible mechanisms because they were not directly studied here.

The continued improvement in PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms (though not M2CQ scores) from T3 to T6 in our participants assessed at both follow-ups, rather than the plateau or reversal often seen in the exercise literature on depression, for example (e.g., Krogh et al., 2011), is a noticeable feature of these findings. This pattern may reflect the framework proposed in the Introduction, though its mechanisms were not tested in this study: continued improvement may stem from processes seen in BJJ over time, such as technical skill accumulation, deeper social integration (e.g., relationships with training partners), and the solidifying of a reconstructed identity.

The M2CQ -without published and validated clinical cut-offs -is worth noting given the substantial effect sizes (T3 dz = 0.52; T6 dz = 0.84), but interpretation of change at the individual level is difficult. Separation from service comes as a significant adjustment for many veterans, often distinct from psychiatric burden alone (Orazem et al., 2017; Zogas, 2017): a sense of social belonging may be lost alongside their rank and role within their unit, so approaches suited to address this gap merit further investigation. One untested hypothesis is that a BJJ gym may be a viable option with its familiar rank structure, shared goals, and community based on closeness with training partners. These processes may also make conventional care easier to approach or stay in, which is crucial because veterans have been shown to seek care less often, a pattern that hyperarousal, avoidance, general distrust may help explain (Bovin et al., 2019; Seal et al., 2010), and respond less well to first-line PTSD therapies (Milligan et al., 2025; Straud et al., 2019). A veteran who is less reactive, with increased self-efficacy, and more connected with peers may find seeking or staying in treatment less costly. This possibility was not examined here and would require measuring treatment engagement.

Limitations

First and foremost, causal inference is impossible due to the lack of a control group (e.g., we cannot rule out natural recovery or non-specific effects). Because concurrent treatment was not assessed, we cannot rule out possible contributions to change from psychotherapy, medications, peer support, or other exercise. Regression to the mean is also always a possible contributor to results such as these, as participants with the highest baseline scores tended to improve the most. As well, the small sample is a real limitation with wide confidence intervals around effect sizes requiring replication in a larger sample. Next, the study recruitment pipeline relied on self-selection for participation in BJJ and likely reflects a positively-biased view towards the practice. Participants with an aversion to contact sports were unlikely to enroll, as were the most severely or functionally impaired. Also, the sample was predominantly male (though only slightly more proportionally when compared to the U.S. military), white, and well-educated, so results may not extend to other groups.

Retention at T6 was 57.5%, and there were no statistically significant differences between completers and non-completers on baseline clinical measures, but 6-month completers had moderately higher baseline depression and PTSD scores (d = 0.59 and 0.56). Thus, T6 estimates may overstate change and futher investigation for dropout reasoning is warranted; the study team did not track program participation and could not distinguish discontinuation of training from loss to research follow-up. Next, all variables were measured in a self-report fashion. While this is a strength for understanding one's personal emotional experiences and appreciating their perspective on functioning, it has limitations, including susceptibility to demand characteristics of the study. Finally, intervention delivery was heterogeneous; number of training sessions attended varied widely, instructors and facilities differed (though all were vetted), and attendance was measured by participant report.

Implications & Future Direction

This study represents a step towards understanding BJJ as a veteran mental health intervention by documenting multidimensional changes across symptoms, functioning, and transition outcomes in combat veterans with service-connected disabilities who trained, and by providing effect size estimates to plan a controlled trial.

Future studies should focus on (1) including an active control group, (2) longer follow-up intervals to examine longer-term trajectories, (3) inclusion of more diverse groups, (4) deploying physiological measures to track changes more directly (e.g., wearable technology to probe heartrate variability), (5) determining psychological and personality profiles to help identify bestresponders, as this martial art will not be appropriate for many, and (6) directly measuring the proposed mechanisms, such as social connectedness, military identity and treatment engagement. To this end, the research team is examining longer-term outcomes and a future phase II controlled trial using physiological measurements.

While more empirical work is needed, service organizations and community partners should be encouraged by these results and continue reducing barriers to this and similar activities while controlled studies proceed. BJJ may offer a community-based and culturally congruent option for reaching veterans who do not always engage with or benefit sufficiently from conventional mental health care. For veterans who are physically able and psychologically willing to engage, BJJ may help reduce psychiatric symptom burden, and combat isolation and disconnection from purpose.
