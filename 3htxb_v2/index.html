Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Loneliness, the subjective experience of a perceived discrepancy between desired and actual social connection, is robustly associated with poor mental and physical health across the lifespan. Although not uniformly increasing in prevalence, loneliness is rising among adolescents and young adults, making it an important public health concern. Neuroimaging offers a means to examine the neural pathways through which loneliness emerges and affects health. A growing body of structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging research implicates differences in regions and networks central to social cognition, internally directed thought, episodic memory, and executive control, most consistently the default mode network, medial temporal structures, and prefrontal cortex. In this Perspective, we review this literature, critically assess its limitations, and outline priorities for future research, including the need for refined measurement, longitudinal developmental and experimental designs, and integration with genetic and contextual factors.

1. Loneliness: Definition, Prevalence, and Health Relevance

Humans are inherently social, and the need to belong constitutes a fundamental motivational drive 1 . Loneliness refers to the subjective experience arising from a perceived discrepancy between the desired and actual quantity or quality of one's social relationships 2, 3 . It is associated with, but conceptually distinct from, social isolation, which refers to the objective degree of social contact a person has 4, 5 . Although transient loneliness is a normative part of human existence and may serve adaptive functions 6, 7 , persistent loneliness is distressing, linked to adverse long-term outcomes, and increasingly recognized as a public health challenge 8, 9.

Loneliness is frequently described in public discourse as an "epidemic", implying a marked and ongoing rise in prevalence. Empirical evidence does not support a major overall increase, however 10, 11 , though studies consistently suggest rising loneliness among adolescents and emerging adults in particular 12, 13 . These patterns may have been exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic for certain subgroups 14 , and may be shaped by broader societal shifts, including changes in digital technology use and the rise of remote work, though the direction, magnitude, and durability of these associations remain debated [15] [16] [17] . Regardless, loneliness is robustly associated with poorer mental and physical health [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] , less favorable educational and occupational outcomes 23, 24 , and differences in cognitive functions in older adults 25.

Loneliness levels vary systematically across the lifespan, peaking in adolescence and emerging adulthood and again in older age [26] [27] [28] . Yet research has historically concentrated on older populations, leaving important gaps in understanding loneliness earlier in life and across key developmental transitions. Because loneliness is shaped by a complex interplay of genetic, neural, psychological, and environmental factors 29 , understanding its origins and consequences requires examining each of these levels 30 . The neural level is especially informative, as it offers a window into the mechanisms through which loneliness develops and affects cognition, emotion, and ultimately mental and physical health.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is particularly well-suited to this purpose, offering whole-brain coverage of brain structure and function with sufficient resolution to characterize both regional and network-level properties. This enables researchers to ask whether loneliness is reflected in measurable neural signatures, whether such signatures precede or follow its onset, and whether they can be modified through intervention. Unlike animal models of social isolation, which cannot operationalize the subjective dimension of loneliness, human MRI studies are uniquely positioned to address these questions. Several earlier reviews have examined aspects of the neuroscience of loneliness [31] [32] [33] , but the MRI literature has grown substantially in recent years, structural and functional findings have rarely been considered together, and integration with developmental and genetic perspectives remains limited.

In this Perspective, we review structural and functional MRI studies of loneliness, identified through PubMed and Scopus searches combining "loneliness" with MRI terms and supplemented by reference lists and forward citation searches of key studies. We included only studies that measured loneliness specifically, rather than related constructs such as social isolation or social support. The included studies are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Across modalities, loneliness has been associated with differences in regions and networks involved in social cognition, internally directed thought, episodic memory, and executive control, most consistently the default mode network, medial temporal structures, and prefrontal cortex. Structural studies, largely of middle-aged and older adults, mainly link loneliness to lower brain volumes, particularly in medial temporal structures, whereas functional studies more often implicate differences in processing of social information and stronger connectivity within the default mode network. These associations are, however, small and often inconsistent across studies, much of the functional evidence comes from modest samples, and nearly all studies are cross-sectional and restricted to specific age groups. We argue that progress will depend on refining how loneliness is measured, on longitudinal and experimental designs that can establish the direction of loneliness-brain associations, particularly in adolescence and emerging adulthood, and on integrating neuroimaging with genetic and contextual information.

2. MRI Correlates of Loneliness

>MRI Correlates of Loneliness

2.1 Structural MRI

Early efforts to identify structural brain correlates of loneliness largely relied on whole-brain voxelbased morphometry (VBM), which quantifies regional gray matter (GM) volume across the brain, in relatively modest samples of either younger or older adults 33 . One of the earliest such studies, conducted in young adults, found that loneliness was associated with lower GM volume in the left posterior superior temporal sulcus 34 . Subsequent work in young adults found a positive association between loneliness and GM volume in a small region in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), with neuroticism and extraversion partially mediating this relationship 35 . A later study similarly observed higher right DLPFC volume in lonelier young adults 36 . In older adults, VBM studies have more consistently implicated medial temporal and subcortical structures. Here, loneliness has been linked to lower GM volume in the amygdala, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, cerebellum, and thalamus, with some evidence that associations differ by cognitive status [37] [38] [39].

More recent studies have substantially increased statistical power but have also introduced additional complexities. In approximately 40,000 older adults from the UK Biobank, Spreng et al. 40 found that loneliness was associated with volume variation within default mode network (DMN) regions, with greater volume in some regions and smaller volume in others. Other large studies in middle-aged and older adults suggest more uniform patterns, linking loneliness to smaller total cerebral volume and larger white matter hyperintensity volume 41 , as well as to lower temporal, hippocampal, and frontal volumes and larger lateral ventricles 42 . This is consistent with loneliness co-occurring with broader markers of brain aging and neurodegeneration, though cross-sectional data cannot establish the direction of this relationship.

Other UK Biobank studies point to heterogeneity, suggesting that associations vary by sex and clinical or social context. For example, loneliness was negatively associated with bilateral anterior cingulate and right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex volume in males with recurrent depression, but positively associated with right anterior cingulate cortex volume in healthy male controls, with no associations in either female group 43 . Another study found a higher brain age gap, indicating an older-appearing brain, only among individuals experiencing both loneliness and social isolation, with loneliness alone showing no association with brain age 44 . Null findings have also been reported for total, GM, and hippocampal volumes in an independent cohort of older adults 45 . Together, these results indicate that loneliness-related GM volume variation is subtle, context-dependent, and not consistently detectable even in large samples.

Emerging work has examined more specific morphometric GM measures, but studies are sparse and available evidence has not revealed robust associations. Loneliness was not associated with global cortical thickness in older adults, although subcortical volume differences in the putamen and globus pallidus were observed 46 . A multisite study including a small adolescent sample and two older adult cohorts similarly found no significant associations between loneliness and vertex-wise cortical thickness 47 , although statistical power, particularly in the adolescent group, may have been limited. To our knowledge, neither cortical surface area, a component of cortical structure anatomically and genetically distinct from cortical thickness 48 , nor measures of cortical folding such as curvature and gyrification have been examined in relation to loneliness, representing notable gaps.

White matter (WM) has received less attention than cortical and subcortical GM. Available evidence suggests that loneliness is associated with lower WM volume in temporo-parietal, insular, and prefrontal regions broadly overlapping with the DMN, salience, and frontoparietal networks in younger adults 49 , and with smaller global and frontal WM volumes in older adults 45, 46 . Diffusion MRI (dMRI), which probes WM microstructure through measures such as fractional anisotropy (FA; reflecting directional coherence of water diffusion) and mean diffusivity (MD; reflecting overall diffusion magnitude), has yielded inconsistent findings. An early small study reported lower FA within the ventral attention network in lonelier young men 50 , but subsequent larger studies found limited or directionally varying associations 40, 45, 49 . A tractography analysis did link loneliness to higher MD in the superior thalamic radiation and parahippocampal cingulum 51 , but the overall picture for WM microstructure remains inconclusive.

In sum, structural MRI studies point to subtle and heterogeneous associations between loneliness and brain morphology. Across studies, loneliness has most consistently been linked to lower GM and WM volumes, either globally or within regions of the default mode, medial temporal, prefrontal, and thalamic systems implicated in internally directed thought, memory, and executive control. Evidence for associations with more specific cortical morphometric measures and WM microstructure remains sparse and inconsistent. Most large-scale structural studies rely on single-item loneliness measures and are restricted to middle-aged and older adults, limiting both phenotypic precision and developmental scope.

2.2 Functional MRI

Functional MRI (fMRI) studies of loneliness largely adopt one of two approaches: task-based fMRI, which measures neural activity or connectivity during structured tasks, and resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), which characterizes functional connectivity in the absence of explicit task demands. Most studies dichotomize participants into high and low loneliness groups, though some treat loneliness as a continuous variable. A recent review summarized much of this literature 31 but deliberately excluded some fMRI studies, drew on broader social connection measures such as social support in addition to loneliness, and intermixed neural and behavioral results. Here, we focus specifically on fMRI correlates of loneliness as a subjective construct.

The majority of task-based fMRI studies focus on social information processing. Lonely compared to non-lonely individuals show greater visual cortex activity while viewing unpleasant social images relative to unpleasant non-social objects 52 , and loneliness was associated with higher activation in insula, basal ganglia, and orbitofrontal cortex when viewing negative compared to neutral pictures 53 , and, in men but not women, diminished amygdala habituation to repeated fearful faces 54 . Differences have also been observed for positive social stimuli: lonely individuals show lower orbitofrontal cortex activation during an online interaction game 55 , lower activation during an interpersonal trust game with a stranger in anterior insula, ventral striatum, and amygdala 56 , and lower ventral striatum activity in response to positive social images 52 (although this finding failed to replicate using a similar task 57 ). These findings are broadly consistent with heightened neural responses to negative social stimuli and attenuated responses to positive social stimuli, though the direction of associations is not always consistent across studies. For example, one study that used images of close others rather than strangers showed increased ventral striatum activity in lonely individuals 58 , and another found a negative correlation between loneliness and anterior insula activation to fearful faces 59 . In addition, the specific stimuli and contrasts used vary considerably, challenging direct comparison and interpretation.

Task-based fMRI studies of functional connectivity have likewise focused predominantly on social processing. Using machine learning to predict individual loneliness scores from task-induced connectivity patterns during a theory of mind task, one study identified connectivity among social, default mode, and somatomotor networks as key predictive features 60 . During a variation of the Cyberball paradigm designed to measure motivation for social inclusion, lonely individuals displayed greater urgency to be included, specifically during fair play, and stronger thalamo-cortical connectivity, specifically between the thalamus and the temporoparietal junction, putamen, and insula 61 . A further study, in which participants were asked to cultivate neutral, pleasant, and unpleasant feelings towards social acquaintances, found that loneliness amplified age-related differences in the integration of the allostatic-interoceptive system with the rest of the brain 62 , suggesting that loneliness may exacerbate network-level changes that otherwise emerge gradually with age. Whether connectivity differences extend beyond the social cognition domain remains largely unexplored; one study reported that loneliness was associated with stronger connectivity between inferior parietal cortex and rostral dorsomedial prefrontal cortex during a working memory task 63 , but this finding awaits replication and broader investigation. Finally, another study found that while viewing short videos, lonely participants showed less similar neural responses to others (lonely and non-lonely alike), particularly in default mode network regions 64 . The authors interpreted this as reflecting an idiosyncratic way of processing the world, an intriguing possibility that remains to be confirmed in independent samples. Rs-fMRI studies offer a complementary window into how loneliness relates to intrinsic brain connectivity. Notably, loneliness is associated with stronger within-DMN connectivity and denser connectivity between the DMN and other networks 40, 65 . Additional studies report loneliness-related increases in connectivity between the anterior insula, posterior cingulate cortex, and DLPFC 66 , the lingual gyrus 67 , and within the cingulo-opercular network alongside reduced functional connectivity between this network and frontal regions 68 . Lower connectivity has also been observed between attention networks and the affective and visual network 69 , and between amygdala and insula and frontal regions 70 . More recent work reports further positive and negative associations involving prefrontal and posterior cortical regions 71 , though the direction and regional specificity of these associations remain difficult to interpret. One recent study of adults found no overall association between loneliness and network organization, but an interaction between loneliness and age for local interconnectivity in two nodes assigned to the DMN and frontoparietal network, with positive associations in younger and negative associations in older participants 72 . Finally, one study showed that individual loneliness scores could be predicted from whole-brain resting-state functional connectivity patterns 73 , pointing to a distributed neural signature that may have predictive utility. Interpreting specific network-level findings from rs-fMRI without relying on reverse inference is, however, challenging. Resting-state paradigms lack concurrent behavioral measures, and most implicated regions and networks subserve many cognitive and affective functions, making it difficult to draw meaningful inferences about the involved psychological processes. In sum, functional MRI studies consistently implicate altered neural processing of social information in lonelier individuals, alongside differences in connectivity patterns that extend beyond task-evoked responses. Across task-based and resting-state approaches, the DMN, prefrontal regions, insula, and subcortical reward-related structures emerge most frequently, broadly overlapping with systems also identified in structural MRI research. At the same time, effect sizes appear small, results are not consistently replicated across studies, and the functional literature shares several key limitations with the structural one: most studies include modest samples, are restricted to specific age groups, and rely on simple loneliness measures and grouping approaches. The predominant focus on social cognition tasks, while theoretically motivated, leaves open the question of whether lonelinessrelated functional activation and connectivity differences are specific to social processing or reflect more general differences in affective and cognitive function.

2.3 Converging findings and shared limitations

Across structural and functional MRI, the DMN, medial temporal structures, and prefrontal cortex emerge as the most consistent sites of loneliness-related variation (Fig. 1), regions jointly implicated in social cognition, internally directed thought, episodic memory, and executive control. This crossmodal convergence strengthens the inference that these systems play a role in loneliness, whether as neural substrates that shape its emergence or as targets of its effects. That said, effect sizes are small and findings across studies are inconsistent, underscoring that this inference remains provisional. Moreover, this convergence has rarely been tested directly with multimodal MRI data within the same individuals; Spreng et al. 40 is a notable exception. A recent large multimodal study using data from the ABCD Study similarly examined structural and functional MRI data in children aged 9-10, comparing those whose parents reported loneliness at a later follow-up (ages 10-14) with those who did not, finding differences in superior frontal, entorhinal, and isthmus cingulate GM volume, superior longitudinal fasciculus FA, and broadly distributed resting-state functional connectivity 74 . Patterns of differences were not always consistent with prior findings, and the loneliness measure was limited to a single dichotomized, parent-reported item. Methods that jointly decompose covariation across modalities, such as linked independent component analysis 75 and canonical correlation analysis 76 , or methods that integrate structural and functional features within predictive frameworks 77, 78 , might offer more complete and statistically powerful characterizations of the neural substrates of loneliness than single-modality approaches alone.

The inconsistency of findings across studies is partly attributable to limited statistical power. Influential work on brain-wide association studies demonstrated that effect sizes linking brain measures to complex psychological phenotypes are typically small and that samples in the thousands are needed for reliable detection and replication 79 . A subsequent exchange clarified that this does not invalidate neuroimaging research on individual differences, but does underscore the problem of underpowered studies and the importance of measurement and study design 80, 81 . Many MRI studies on loneliness reviewed above involve samples of fewer than 200 participants, and even biobank-scale findings have not replicated consistently. Larger samples improve statistical power and remain a priority, but are not the only path forward 82 . Some questions may be better addressed, for instance, through intensive longitudinal or experimental designs that trade sample size for depth of measurement and stronger control of confounders. Across study designs, key gaps remain in how loneliness is measured, how loneliness and the brain are tracked over time, and how neural correlates are situated within broader genetic and contextual frameworks. We outline these priorities below.

3. Priorities for Future Neuroimaging Research

>Priorities for Future Neuroimaging Research

3.1 Refining loneliness measurement

In the reviewed neuroimaging literature, loneliness has most commonly been measured with a single direct question or with versions of the UCLA Loneliness Scale 83 . The dimensional structure of loneliness remains debated: factor analyses of loneliness scales have yielded one-, two-, and multifactor solutions 84 . Yet, to our knowledge, no MRI studies have examined neural correlates of distinct facets of loneliness, such as emotional versus social loneliness 85 , or loneliness related to specific relationship types 86 . Measurement choices have consequences. Single-item measures show adequate convergent validity with multi-item scales 87 , but may attenuate observed associations, a particular concern in neuroimaging where effect sizes are already small. Whether loneliness is measured directly or indirectly, and whether emotional and social loneliness are distinguished, also yield different associations with age, gender, and psychosocial variables 27, 88 , suggesting that measurement differences may partly explain heterogeneity in reported neural correlates.

A further methodological concern, particularly evident in fMRI studies, is the dichotomization of continuous loneliness scores into lonely and non-lonely groups. Treating loneliness as a binary variable discards individual differences, attenuates statistical power and effect sizes, and introduces misclassification of individuals near the cut-point, which is arbitrary in the absence of evidence for a genuine categorical structure in loneliness scores 89 . Age-sensitive measurement is also needed: existing scales were developed primarily for adults, measurement invariance across age groups cannot be assumed 84 , and studies of adolescent loneliness should ensure measures are valid for this age group 90 . Finally, most loneliness measures, including the widely used UCLA Loneliness Scale, do not specify a time frame, and a single assessment cannot distinguish persistent from transient loneliness. Future studies should incorporate repeated assessments 91 and trajectory-based approaches 24 to distinguish groups with transient, chronic, and increasing or decreasing loneliness, as these are likely to differ in the nature and strength of neural correlates.

3.2 Mapping temporal dynamics

Nearly all existing MRI studies of loneliness are cross-sectional, meaning that the directionality of loneliness-brain associations remains fundamentally unclear. Three interpretations are possible (Fig. 2): structural and functional brain differences may reflect neural vulnerability markers that predispose individuals to loneliness; they may reflect neurobiological consequences of prolonged loneliness; or loneliness and the brain may influence each other reciprocally over time. These are not mutually exclusive but have different implications. The first framing positions loneliness neuroscience within a broader transdiagnostic literature on mental health risk 92 , given likely overlap between neural vulnerability profiles for loneliness and those for depression, anxiety, and related conditions. The second, that brain differences are neurobiological consequences of loneliness, is perhaps the most commonly assumed framing in the field, often implicitly, yet lacks direct support given the near-total reliance on cross-sectional designs. The third framing, reciprocal influence, is consistent with a transactional developmental perspective in which early neural differences shape social experience, which in turn further modifies the brain, but is also the most demanding to test empirically. Distinguishing among these interpretations requires longitudinal designs capable of tracking how loneliness and brain structure and function co-evolve over time.

A few longitudinal neuroimaging studies have recently begun to address this gap, though existing evidence is limited. A large longitudinal study of older adults found no associations between baseline loneliness and subsequent change in brain structure or white matter microstructure 45 . Similarly, a study including one adolescent and two older adult groups found no significant associations between loneliness and hippocampal volume change or regional cortical thinning in any group 47 . These null findings may reflect genuine absence of effect, but may equally reflect insufficient statistical power, insensitive loneliness measures, or follow-up periods too short to capture cumulative neural change.

Developmental timing also matters. Adolescence has been proposed as a sensitive period for social cognitive development 93, 94 , during which the brain undergoes substantial refinements 95, 96 and sensitivity to peer evaluation and influence is heightened 97, 98 . Loneliness during this period may therefore carry particular neurobiological significance, yet neuroimaging studies specifically targeting adolescent loneliness remain scarce. Childhood has received even less attention, with only isolated MRI studies of loneliness in children 74 , despite substantial development of the neural systems supporting social cognition during this period 99 . Beyond developmental sampling, more advanced longitudinal models that partition stable between-person differences from within-person change 100, 101 should also be applied to neuroimaging data to better characterize temporal dynamics and strengthen causal inference.

Experimental paradigms and intervention studies offer complementary approaches to address directionality and mechanisms. Tomova et al. 102 used a within-subject fMRI design to show that 10 hours of acute social isolation selectively activated dopaminergic midbrain regions (substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area) in response to social cues, analogous to responses to food cues after 10 hours of fasting. Importantly, however, participants who reported higher pre-existing chronic loneliness showed a muted neural response to social cues after acute isolation, consistent with dampened social reward sensitivity. Such designs can help disentangle state-dependent neural responses from more stable individual differences and point to dopaminergic social motivation circuitry as one candidate substrate for vulnerability to loneliness. Randomized controlled trials with neuroimaging remain rare, and the only one we identified tested an exercise intervention rather than one designed to target loneliness 103 . An important next step would be neuroimaging before and after interventions designed to reduce loneliness, with later follow-up, testing whether individual change in loneliness tracks change in the structural or functional correlates identified in prior research. If the correlates change along with loneliness, this would suggest that they are reversible and at least partly reflect the current experience of loneliness. If they remain stable despite reduced loneliness, this would be more consistent with pre-existing vulnerability or with lasting consequences. These designs could be extended by ecological momentary assessment, capturing loneliness and social interactions as they unfold in daily life and allowing researchers to test whether neural change translates into change in everyday social experience.

3.3 Integrating genetic and contextual factors

Strengthening causal understanding also requires moving beyond the brain to incorporate upstream genetic and contextual factors that shape who becomes lonely and why. Twin studies document moderate heritability of loneliness 104, 105 . Genomic studies have identified several relevant loci and genetic overlap with mental disorders and cardiovascular risk factors 40, 106, 107 , underscoring the transdiagnostic relevance of loneliness. Beyond genetics, loneliness is also robustly predicted by both contextual and individual factors, including low socioeconomic status, living alone or being unmarried, poor health, psychological attributes, and infrequent socializing 108, 109 . Major family-and work-related life events can also trigger loneliness 110 . At a broader level, cultural values such as individualism-collectivism are linked to national differences in loneliness and may shape social expectations, and thereby how individual-level factors relate to loneliness 11 . Existing neuroimaging studies have largely ignored genetic and contextual factors, limiting their ability to explain why neural correlates of loneliness are heterogeneous across individuals and studies. One exception is a study of young adults showing that associations between loneliness and white matter microstructure differed by BDNF Val66Met genotype 111 , though findings should be interpreted cautiously given limited sample size and limitations of candidate gene approaches. Among contextual factors, objective social isolation is particularly relevant, as measuring it alongside loneliness can clarify whether neural correlates are specific to the subjective experience or differ when the two co-occur or diverge 44.

Sex may also moderate neural correlates of loneliness. Although mean levels of loneliness differ little between males and females 112 , two studies report loneliness-related neural differences in males only 43, 54 , whereas a large UK Biobank study found sex-specific associations in different regions, involving the amygdala in males and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex in females 113 . These findings await replication with formal tests of sex-by-loneliness interactions. More broadly, future neuroimaging studies should integrate genetic data, including polygenic scores for loneliness and related traits, alongside sex and gender, measures of social and cultural context, psychological attributes, and life events, to clarify causal pathways and boundary conditions.

3.4 Outlook

Neuroimaging research has begun to link loneliness to variation in human brain structure and function, but the field remains limited by mostly small, cross-sectional studies and inconsistent findings. Progress will require large-scale longitudinal, experimental, and intervention designs, together with refined measurement of loneliness as a continuous and multidimensional construct. Routine reporting of effect sizes would facilitate future meta-analyses, while harmonized megaanalyses of existing datasets could enable formal tests of moderators such as age, sex, and loneliness measure. A central unresolved question is directionality: whether brain differences are vulnerability markers, neurobiological sequelae, or both. Particular attention should be given to adolescence and emerging adulthood, periods characterized by elevated and increasing loneliness, protracted development of the social brain and executive control networks, and heightened sensitivity to the social environment. Addressing these questions calls for causally informative designs and integration of neuroimaging data with genetic, psychological, and contextual information to understand the factors that jointly give rise to loneliness and its consequences. Loneliness reflects both individual biology and social experience; understanding how the brain shapes individual differences in the perception of social connection and links these to mental and physical health outcomes is central to building the evidence base that effective public health responses to loneliness will require.
