Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

study_design_overview Within-Subjects Design. All participants will complete 2 Breathing Tasks measuring Interoceptive Awareness (Task A & Task B), subjective confidence and arousal questions following each trial, and a MAIA questionnaire. Order of performing Task A and Task B will be counterbalanced across participants.

Task A: In this task participants will be asked to match their breath to a circle on a computer screen that expands and contracts rhythmically. Pulse rate will always start at 5-seconds/pulse. Participants will also be asked to indicate through a key press the beginning of every inhale and exhale. This offers a behavioral measure that indicates their ability to match their breathing rate with the pulsing circle. Each trial will consist of 8- circle pulses. As discovered in a previous pilot study (Farb & Logie, 2018) if the pulse of the circle is changed gradually with each breath, on average participants awareness of respiration change is no greater than chance levels even when the overall change is as large as 65% from initial breathing rate. Awareness of change is determined through a 3-way forced choice question after each trial (“On the previous trial did your breathing rate increase, decrease, or stay constant?”). For the current study, we are interested in calculating a threshold of awareness of change in respiration. To do so, a staircase method will be used, such that the overall change in respiration rate over the full trial will either increase or decrease (using a 1-up, 2-down rule) based upon correct or incorrect response on the previous trial. 2 correct responses in a row will lead to a step down in the staircase while 1 incorrect response will lead to one step easier. Step sizes represent total increase or decrease in respiration rate from the previous position on the staircase. The first level of overall respiration rate change will be 50% over the 8 pulses. Speed-up, Slow-down as well as “no-change” trials will be used [1/3 of total trials for each] so that the pulse rate is not changing every time.

Task B. In this task participants will also match their breath to a circle on a computer screen that expands and contracts rhythmically. Pulse rate will always start at 5-seconds/pulse. Participants will also be asked to indicate through a key press when they begin each inhale and exhale. However in this task participants will be asked to press the spacebar once they notice that their breathing rate has changed. “No change” trials, in which the rate of the pulses stays constant the entire duration of the trial, will be randomly interspersed so that the pulse rate is not changing every time. A three-alternative forced choice task that employs a psychophysics staircase to estimate participant’s ability to detect change in respiration rate.

Training Trials: Participants are presented with trials in which their breathing rate either increases or decreases over four breaths, with changes occurring either gradually or quickly. After each trial, participants are asked to identify whether their breathing rate increased or decreased, rate their confidence in the accuracy of their response, and report their level of wakefulness or arousal. Four distinct conditions are used: high salience (rapid changes) and low salience (gradual changes) for both acceleration and deceleration of breathing.

For high salience trials, the change amount is applied to the circle pulse duration in a single multiplication between pulse 2 and 3: New duration = Old Duration * Change Amount

For low salience trials, the local (pulse specific) change amount is applied to the circle pulse duration between every pulse using the following calculation:
LocalChange= ChangeAmount ^ 1/(numBreaths -1)

Test trials: Following the staircase for the four conditions, the high salience thresholds will be used for 16 test trials. The test trials will evenly split direction type: i.e., they will consist of 8 acceleration and 8 deceleration trials. Within each direction, there will be 4 high salience and 4 low salience trials. However, the threshold will be set consistently at the high salience threshold for that direction across all 8 trials. We would therefore expect accuracy to decrease for low salience trials as generally the high salience threshold will be too difficult to detect in a low salience trial presentation.

Accuracy: participant’s ability to correctly assess whether the breathing rate has increased or decreased after each trial by comparing their response to the actual change that occurred.

Confidence: the self-reported certainty a participant has in their assessment of whether the breathing rate increased or decreased after each trial.

Arousal: self-reported level of alertness after each trial to capture the subjective experience of perceived difficulty or the extent to which the change in breathing rate captured their attention.

Direction: whether a given trial involves acceleration or deceleration of the respiratory rate. A -1 indicates acceleration, and a 1 indicates deceleration

Level: the proportion change in the circle pulse duration from the start to the end of the trial. For example a 30% acceleration or deceleration is a level of .3

Magnitude of Respiratory Change: the degree of variation in the breathing rate, specifically the extent of acceleration and deceleration, observed across trials. This measures how large or significant the change in breathing rate is as determined by the psychophysical staircase method, which adjusts based on the participant’s correct or incorrect assessment of whether their breathing rate has increased or decreased. It is formally calculated as Direction * Level, so an acceleration of 30% = -1 * .3 = -.3, where as a deceleration of 30% = 1 * .3 = .3; this allows for the total change amount to be calculated as 1 + the magnitude of change.

Salience: how easily noticeable the change in breathing rate is after each trial. Either a gradual change in breathing rate (low salience) or marked change in breathing rate (high salience).

Threshold: the minimum change in respiratory acceleration or deceleration required for a participant to reliably detect a shift in breathing rate. This threshold is determined using a psychophysical staircase procedure, where stimulus intensity is adaptively adjusted based on performance. A lower threshold indicates greater sensitivity, indicating that a participant can detect smaller changes in breathing rates, whereas a higher threshold suggests reduced sensitivity, requiring larger changes for detection. This measure serves as an index of interoceptive precision in the context of respiratory perception. The threshold score is calculated as the average of the last 2 trials in the staircase for any given condition.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Abstract

The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA) measures adaptive interoceptive sensibility (attention to the body and how one appraises it) and predicts mental health; how that disposition shows up during a bodily task has rarely been tested. We asked whether MAIA scores predict accuracy, confidence or their coupling in the Breath Change Awareness Task, in which participants breathe along with a visual pacer, report whether its pace changed and rate confidence on every trial. Across five samples (N = 687; in Study 5, a respiratory belt confirmed breathing along in 145 of 170 recorded participants), MAIA scores predicted mean confidence (metacognitive bias) in every sample (pooled r = .27, 95% CI [.17, .37]), including Study 5's preregistered test (r = .26 [.13, .38]; Breath blocks only, r = .26). They were not positively related to perceptual sensitivity (how well changes were detected) or to metacognitive sensitivity, how well confidence tracked accuracy: pooled, positive relations of r = .10 or more were ruled out for both. Study 5's preregistered detection-threshold null, a stricter criterion, was not established (BF₀₁ = 2.2, below the required 3). In Study 5, the association held with self-esteem, self-doubt, alexithymia and affect controlled. It was also present with vision alone (following the pacer by eye without breathing along), which should weaken a body-based link: r = .22 [.01, .41], with no detectable difference from breathing along (.20). During sensory discrimination, adaptive sensibility showed up as confidence, independent of accuracy and present even without breathing along.

1. Introduction

The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA; Mehling et al., 2012 Mehling et al., , 2018) measures interoceptive sensibility (Garfinkel et al., 2015): how often people attend to the body and how they appraise what they notice. It is not a measure of perceptual skill: trait mindfulness is largely unrelated to objective body awareness (Treves et al., 2019), and MAIA subscales were unrelated to cardiac and respiratory task performance but related to confidence on both (Banellis et al., 2026). Yet it predicts mental health and tracks treatment response (Banellis et al., 2026; Eggart et al., 2021; Eggart & Valdés-Stauber, 2021; Khalsa et al., 2018). How that adaptive disposition shows up while a person attends to a bodily signal has rarely been tested: as accuracy, as confidence, or as their coupling.

Which of these does sensibility predict? Average confidence (metacognitive bias) is separable from how well confidence distinguishes correct from incorrect judgments (metacognitive sensitivity; Fleming & Lau, 2014), including in respiratory interoception (Harrison et al., 2021; Nikolova et al., 2022). And is the association about the body at all? Confidence varies between people in a trait-like way (Ais et al., 2016; Rouault et al., 2018), so a sensibility-confidence association could reflect a general disposition.

Five samples completed the Breath Change Awareness Task (BCAT; Farb et al., 2026): participants breathe along with a visual pacer, judge whether its pace changed and rate their confidence. In a vision-alone condition, participants followed the pacer by eye without breathing along, largely removing the bodily signal. If MAIA scores nonetheless reflect insight into bodily signals, they should track accuracy and metacognitive sensitivity, and the MAIA-confidence link should weaken with vision alone. If they reflect a disposition toward confidence, they should track bias with or without breathing along.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Samples

The BCAT paper describes the task, procedures and exclusions (Farb et al., 2026; Table 1). The University of Toronto Research Ethics Board approved all studies (Studies 1-3: Protocol #32100; Studies 4-5: #45079). Participants gave informed consent; those aged 16 or 17 (Studies 3-5) consented for themselves, as the board permits. S13). Study 3: a coding defect altered the delivered pace on every trial and turned 1,421 of the 3,192 trials (gradual trials after a run's first abrupt trial) into one fixed abrupt slowing. Accuracy was therefore scored against the change actually displayed, reconstructed from the task code (Farb et al., 2026). As displayed, 223 of the 3,192 trials had no change (210 of the 3,000 analyzed), although none was designed, and "same" is scored correct on them. Study 5: 204 of the 206 participants have MAIA scores (2 Breath-first participants have none); Block 1 was Breath (n = 93) or Visual (n = 111), and of the 157 who completed Block 2, in which everyone breathed along, 156 have MAIA scores (90 Visual-first, 66 Breath-first), so 183 have a Breath block.

2.2 Task and measures

Participants were asked to breathe along with a circle (the pacer) for four cycles; on most trials its pace changed, abruptly or gradually. They reported whether the pace had slowed, stayed the same or sped up, then rated their confidence (scale: Table 1). A respiratory belt confirmed breathing along in 145 of 170 recorded Study 5 participants (Farb et al., 2026).

Study 4 assigned participants to the Breath or the Visual (vision-alone) condition; in Study 5, Block 1 was Breath or Visual, and everyone breathed along in Block 2 (Table 1). Vision-alone instructions did not mention breathing. In Study 5, belt recordings (n = 34) showed little breathing along (breath-pacer phase gain .12 [.04, .19] vs. .55 [.48, .62] in the Breath condition; chance .03). Participants using vision alone detected changes at least as well as those breathing along (Farb et al., 2026).

Three measures were computed per participant, in the Breath condition unless stated.

Metacognitive bias was mean confidence. Perceptual sensitivity was the detection threshold (Table 1), sign-reversed so that higher means finer detection, or proportion correct in Study 3.

Metacognitive sensitivity was type-2 AUROC, the probability that a correct trial received higher confidence than an incorrect one (ties count half). It needs at least five correct and five incorrect trials, which 53 of 181 participants had in Study 1 and at least 88% elsewhere.

The MAIA total was computed from the item responses with each version's published key (Mehling et al., 2012 (Mehling et al., , 2018;; Rogowska et al., 2023; Supplementary Table S1) and standardized within sample. Study 5 also measured self-esteem and self-doubt (Rentzsch et al., 2022), alexithymia (Preece et al., 2023) and positive and negative affect (Diener et al., 2010).

2.3 Analysis

We correlated the MAIA with each measure, with 95% CIs, and repeated the confidence correlation with proportion correct partialed out. Default two-sided Bayes factors (r scale = 1/3; Ly et al., 2016; BayesFactor, Morey & Rouder, 2024) and one-sided non-superiority tests against r = .10 (Supplement S11) distinguished evidence against a positive relation from absence of evidence. Within each sample, a trial-level model, confidence (z) ~ accuracy × MAIA (z) + |change| (z) + (accuracy | participant), with trial accuracy coded ±0.5 (lme4, lmerTest; Bates et al., 2015; Kuznetsova et al., 2017), separated bias (the MAIA term, averaged over correct and incorrect trials) from metacognitive sensitivity (the interaction).

For the vision-alone test, we compared Breath and Visual correlations between groups in Studies 4 and 5 and within person in Study 5 (Williams' test; Steiger, 1980; Williams, 1959), correcting for order with Breath-first participants' change between their two breathing blocks (bootstrap CI).

Correlations were pooled by random effects on Fisher-z, with Hartung-Knapp CIs under the ad hoc correction and 95% prediction intervals (metafor; Knapp & Hartung, 2003; Viechtbauer, 2010).

2.4 Preregistration

Study 1 (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/53GJM) was preregistered on 6 April 2021, after 170 of its 184 Prolific completers had been tested, although the form states that no data existed; no one had looked at the data. It predicted a negative MAIA-threshold correlation (higher MAIA, finer detection; α = .05/7) that task version would not moderate (S1-H2A-B), and named an exploratory undergraduate wave (n = 85; Supplement S14).

Study 5 (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/R6ZJA) was preregistered on 26 September 2025, after 104 of its 206 participants had been tested but before data access; the preregistration gives July for the start of collection, which began on 20 May. It predicted a positive MAIAconfidence correlation (H3A), within a Benjamini-Hochberg family of seven tests with the BCAT hypotheses, and no MAIA-threshold correlation (H3B), with a Bayes factor of 3 or more for the null; it also planned an exploratory control for self-esteem and self-doubt. As preregistered, H3A-B average both blocks, so for Visual-first participants they include the vision-alone block. 3. Results

>Results

3.1 Confidence, not detection

The MAIA predicted confidence in every sample (Figure 1; r = .19 to .37, BF₁₀ = 3.1 to > 1,000). Pooled, r = .27 [.17, .37] (prediction interval [.15, .39], I² = 7%); with proportion correct partialed out, .28 [.17, .38], and with the threshold, .18 to .37 (Supplement S15). The preregistered confidence prediction (H3A) was supported: r = .26 [.13, .38], p < .001, pBH < .001, BF₁₀ = 158 (completers: r = .31 [.16, .44]; Breath blocks only: r = .26 [.11, .39]).

The MAIA was not positively related to perceptual sensitivity in any sample (r = -.17 to -.01, BF₀₁ = 1.2 to 5.5). Pooled, r = -.09 [-.19, .02], and a positive relation of .10 or more was excluded (one-sided p = .004). Study 1's preregistered prediction of a negative correlation with the threshold (higher = coarser) was not supported: both estimates were positive (Task A: r = .09, BF₀₁ = 2.7; Task B: .01, BF₀₁ = 5.7; one-sided, 12.5 and 6.6), and the versions did not differ detectably (Supplement S3). Study 5's preregistered threshold null was not established: r = .10 [-.03, .24] (higher = coarser), BF₀₁ = 2.2 against a criterion of 3 (completers: .14, BF₀₁ = 1.3; wider priors, Supplement S3).

3.2 Bias, not metacognitive sensitivity

The MAIA was not positively related to metacognitive sensitivity in any sample: BF₀₁ = 3.2 to 4.1 in four samples, and in Study 5 r = -.16 [-.30, -.01]. Pooled, r = -.09 [-.21, .03], and a positive relation of .10 or more was excluded (one-sided p = .006).

In the trial-level models, higher MAIA scores went with higher confidence in all five samples (b = 0.09 to 0.22 SD per SD, all p ≤ .034). The association held with the no-change false-alarm rate partialed out (Supplement S15), so the bias does not reflect a liberal "changed" criterion. The MAIA did not strengthen the accuracy-confidence relation: the interaction was negative in four samples and significant in none (b = -0.07 to 0.02); pooled, b = -0.04 [-0.08, 0.01], p = .10 (sample estimates), and -0.03 [-0.07, -0.002], p = .04 (one model). Other checks agreed (Supplement S15).

3.3 The association with vision alone

The association showed no detectable weakening with vision alone. For Visual-first participants in Study 5 (n = 90), the MAIA predicted confidence about equally with vision alone (r = .22 [.01, .41]) and when breathing along (r = .20 [-.01, .39]; Williams t(87) = -0.33, p = .74); corrected for order with the Breath-first group (n = 66), Breath -Visual = -.04 [-.22, .16].

Their confidence correlated .80 across blocks, and with Visual-block confidence partialed out, the MAIA correlated .04 with Breath-block confidence (Supplement S6). Between groups it was somewhat weaker with vision alone, but not reliably: Study 4, .35 (Breath) vs. .14 (Visual); Study 5 Block 1, .29 vs. .22; combined, Δz = 0.13 [-0.09, 0.35], p = .23. Neither between-group comparison isolates breathing: post hoc, Study 5's groups differed more when both breathed along in Block 2 (Breath-first .40 vs. Visual-first .20), and Study 4's breathing was not recorded.

4. Discussion

Across five samples, people with higher MAIA scores were more confident in their breathing-change judgments, but no better at detecting the changes or telling correct from incorrect.

In Study 5, exploratorily, confidence went with positive affect more than with distress, and affect controls lowered the association, whereas distress controls barely moved it (Tables S18-S19). With the BARQ-R reversed, both body-awareness questionnaires went with less distress and more positive affect (Studies 4-5), but only the MAIA detectably predicted confidence. The MAIA's six general-factor subscales shared the association; none carried it alone (Supplement S9). The total without the Trusting subscale correlated .26 with confidence (Supplement S10). A disposition toward confidence that showed no detectable weakening with vision alone fits evidence that average confidence is stable across tasks and domains (Ais et al., 2016; Kelemen et al., 2000; Mazancieux et al., 2020). MAIA subscales likewise went with confidence on cardiac and respiratory tasks (Banellis et al., 2026).

The samples used three MAIA versions and evolving task designs, in sequence, not as independent replications. Because detection could draw on vision, perceptual sensitivity here is change detection in this task, not necessarily respiratory accuracy. Type-2 AUROC rests on modest trial counts, especially in Study 1. Every pooled estimate holds without Study 3, whose displayed changes were reconstructed (Table 1; Table S4). Pooled, the data rule out observed positive relations of .10 or more with detection and metacognitive sensitivity, but not negative ones, nor a Breath-Visual difference of .10. Both measures are self-report, and in Studies 1-2 agreement style predicted confidence alongside the MAIA's shared content (Supplement S7); the Brief MAIA-2 cannot separate the two. Partialing out arousal, rated like confidence, barely changed the association, and it held in Study 4, where the MAIA came before the task.

The MAIA tracked confidence: present in every sample, intact under Study 5's controls, present with vision alone, and independent of accuracy. Adaptive interoceptive sensibility shows up during sensory discrimination as trust in one's judgments rather than as perceptual skill.

Whether that trust carries part of sensibility's clinical benefits (Khalsa et al., 2018) is the next question.
