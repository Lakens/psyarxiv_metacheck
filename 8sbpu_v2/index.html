How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

As social animals, why do we sometimes desire solitude? Across three ecological momentary assessment studies with an adult sample (N = 110), an adolescent sample (N = 68), and a UK representative sample (N = 235), we found support for an allostatic framework of momentary preference for solitude. Negative emotions, worsened mood, loneliness, and social load predicted higher odds of preferring solitude, even when one was already alone -a pattern inconsistent with social homeostasis, which would predict a desire for sociality to restore equilibrium. When experiencing negative states, participants preferred solitude both to avoid other people and to pursue its own benefits, such as rest, emotion regulation, and self-reflection. Overall, the findings suggest that the everyday desire for solitude to manage external demands serves not just as an escape but also as a regulatory function -highlighting moments when withdrawing, rather than seeking more socialization, may be preferable.

Introduction

The phrase "humans are social animals" is often cited, yet data from the American Time Use Survey (2003 Survey ( -2020) ) show a steady increase in time spent alone over the past two decades (Atalay, 2022). While this trend is U.S.-specific, similar patterns are emerging globally. For example, singleperson households are rising steadily, especially in developed countries (Cohen, 2021). These shifts have raised concerns about declining mental health, as more time alone is associated with heightened loneliness and lower life satisfaction (Chua & Koestner, 2008; Danvers et al., 2023; Weinstein et al., 2023). A stronger inclination toward solitude has also been linked to anxiety, chronic loneliness, and past history of social exclusion (Burger, 1995; Goossens, 2014; Ren et al., 2016; Sakurai et al., 2024).

Together, these findings suggest that withdrawing from social interactions, especially when done excessively, can be harmful to well-being.

In parallel with the literature framing chronic social withdrawal as a maladaptive condition, other research suggests that temporary retreat from social situations can be a way to cope with social threats (Ren et al., 2016 (Ren et al., , 2020;; Wesselmann et al., 2021), or a short-term response to ongoing stress (Repetti, 1992; Story & Repetti, 2006). It has been theorized that staying away from social situations under stress may prevent further social hurt (Wesselmann et al., 2021) and allow one to attend to one's needs (Repetti, 1989). Indeed, being alone has been showed to promote rest (Hammond, 2016; Nguyen et al., 2018; Rodriguez et al., 2025) and offer opportunities for autonomy and freedom (Weinstein et al., 2023). As such, while majority of previous work treats social withdrawal as a chronic and maladaptive tendency (Bowker et al., 2013; Coplan et al., 2013; Goossens, 2014; Rubin et al., 2009), it is important to distinguish chronic withdrawal from temporary withdrawal -the latter of which might not necessarily incur long-term detriments.

Solitude -a state of being detached from all sources of social communication and demands (Campbell & Ross, 2021; Long et al., 2003; Nguyen & Rodriguez, 2024) -may allow an individual to satisfy needs that social interactions leave unmet. On this basis, researchers have proposed that, even as social beings, one needs to balance social time with solitude time to optimize well-being (Coplan et al., 2019; Weinstein et al., 2023). This aligns with the social homeostasis model, which proposes that people regulate social connection much like temperature or hunger: when underconnected, we seek contact to regain equilibrium; the model has mostly been used to explain approach social behavior aimed at restoring connection (Matthews & Tye, 2019). Following the same line of logic, if social contacts begin to deplete the individual, they might scale down and withdraw, and once restored, seek out contact again. As such, under social homeostasis, there is a social equilibrium that must be balanced by the amount of time spent in social contact versus solitude.

However, there are times when staying away from social interactions is not about restoration, but rather about not having resources left to meet the demands of interaction in the first place (Repetti, 1992). In contrast to social homeostasis, which assumes a fixed setpoint of social connection that the individual works to restore, an allostatic view suggests that shifting demands in the environment may drive the organism to change its behaviours to adapt (Ramsay & Woods, 2014). This shift has been showed to be triggered by external stressors, such as social aggression (Carnevali et al., 2020; Ike et al., 2020) or wide-spread illness or infection (Yee & Prendergast, 2010), and serves self-protection or energy conservation. Failure to reduce demands might lead to buildup of allostatic load, which can itself be detrimental in a long term. As such, the allostatic view may explain well why individuals sometimes prefer solitude -to reduce load, preserve emotional energy, and support recovery.

In this presented research, we tested whether allostasis would explain better momentary preference for solitude than social homeostasis. We conducted three ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies with adult, adolescent, and representative samples. We tested whether aversive states (i.e., negative emotions, worsened mood, loneliness, high social load) -which signals unmet needs -predict a desire to be with others versus alone. If social homeostasis holds, the individual should prefer solitude when experiencing negative social interactions -consistent with findings that ostracism triggers preference for solitude (Ren et al., 2016; 2020) -but should prefer social contact if they experience negative solitude, since aversive states should push toward its opposite.

By contrast, building on the allostatic view, we expect that people will prefer solitude in general when experiencing negative states, because being alone helps with downregulation, potentially offering an opportunity to reduce load and minimize further energy costs once load is already high. In that sense, a person will not necessarily swing back to social contacts if they experience negative solitude, as the priority shifts from restoring social equilibrium to managing allostatic load. In Studies 2 and 3, we also further investigated reasons behind solitude preference, distinguishing reasons driven by the anticipation of potential unpleasant social experiences (non-selfdetermined) and those rooted in a desire to gain benefits from solitude (self-determined) (Thomas & Azmitia, 2019).

The use of EMA design allows us to capture individuals' preference for solitude at the moment it arises. Previous research has assessed preference for solitude as a personality disposition, finding that it correlates with negative constructs such as neuroticism and loneliness (Burger, 1995) or anxiety (Thomas & Azmitia, 2018). These findings reflect a more stable, trait-like pattern, suggesting that a chronic preference for solitude is likely a maladaptive tendency. With EMA, we instead wanted to assess solitude preference as a momentary desire, which can fluctuate depending on situational factors (Ren et al., 2016; 2020).

Study 1

Study 1 was our first exploratory study to examine the association between negative states and momentary preference for solitude. In doing so, we also looked at whether this association might depend on whether participants were alone or interacting with other people. There were several possibilities. First, there might only be a main effect of negative states on preference for solitude, regardless of situation; this would be consistent with previous research showing a positive correlation between trait-level preference for solitude and negative traits such as anxiety, neuroticism, or loneliness (Burger, 1995; Thomas & Azmitia, 2019). Second, there might be a crossover interaction, such that negative states during social interactions are associated with a preference for solitude (aligning with Ren et al., 2016 Ren et al., , 2020)), whereas negative states in solitude are associated with a preference for other people (supporting social homeostasis). Finally, the interaction might show that negative states during social interactions are associated with a preference for solitude (again aligning with Ren et al., 2016 Ren et al., , 2020)), but negative states in solitude are associated with a continued preference for solitude (supporting allostasis).

We tested our research question on an age-restricted sample between 18 and 45. Previous research suggests that older adults generally have more positive relationships (Luong et al., 2011) and also enjoy their solitude more (Larson, 1990; Lay et al., 2019; Pauly et al., 2017) compared to younger and middle-aged adults. This means they might have fewer incidents in which they experience negative social interactions or solitude, and yet might generally prefer solitude more, but not as a way to withdraw. To minimize this age-based variability and maximize internal validity in capturing the association of interest (i.e., aversive states linking to preference for solitude), we restricted our sample to adults below the age range (50-85 in Lay et al., 2019) where these qualitatively different patterns of social and solitary experiences may emerge.

Methods

Participants

We recruited Study 1 participants through [masked]'s weekly newsletters sent out to staff and students, and through Prolific Academic. There were 114 participants who signed up for the study; 51 through the newsletters and 63 through Prolific. However, 4 participants did not provide any EMA data, so the final sample included 110 participants aged 18 to 45 (M = 28.08, SD = 7.98). The sample included 57 males, 52 females, and one participant who did not disclose their gender. Of the participants, 91 completed more than 21 surveys (50% of signals), 11 completed between 10 and 20 surveys, and 8 completed fewer than 10 surveys.

Measures

Demographics. Participants were asked to report their age and gender (i.e., male, female, other, or prefer not to say).

Aloneness.

To assess participants' current situation, we first asked whether participants were around other people; if they were, they were asked about the company, whether it was an acquaintance, friend, family, significant other, or whether they were in a crowd or surrounded by strangers. We also asked whether they were engaging in any back-and-forth interaction, either in person or remotely (i.e., on the phone or online). A situation was coded as solitude if all of the following applied: 1) participants were not engaged in back-and-forth interaction, and 2) no one was physically present, or they were only near strangers or in a crowd. We took this operationalization of current situation from Lay et al. (2019), excluding situations where others were nearby but one was not interacting with them. However, we further distinguished whether nearby others were familiar others or strangers, since previous research suggests that being around strangers without interacting is still consistent with solitude (Epley & Schroeder, 2014) whereas being around familiar others entails some levels of social demands even without interaction.

Solitude Preference.

Regardless of whether participants were alone, they were asked which option was most appealing at that moment: "spend time alone," "spend time with people," or "spend time with someone else other than the people I am with." The latter two responses were combined into a single category representing a preference for being with others.

Negative Emotions. Every two hours throughout the day, participants rated their overall experience in the past one hour on 11-point scale, from "Negative" to "Positive". We reverse-coded the emotional valence item so that higher scores mean more negative emotions; possible scores on negative emotions range between 0 and 10.

Worsened Mood.

Every two hours throughout the day, we also asked participants to evaluate how they felt currently compared to the past hour and they were provided five options, including "much more negative", "slightly more negative", "about the same", "slightly more positive", "much more positive". We reverse-coded this item so that higher scores mean worsened mood compared to the past-hour assessment, and possible scores on this item range between 1 and 5.

Loneliness. In the last survey of the day, we included one single question to ask participants how lonely they felt at that moment, rated on 100-point visual analogue scale.

Social Load.

At the end of the day, we also included 6 items to measure the level of social load that participants had experienced that day. These items were adapted from the measures of negative interactions with family and romantic partners taken from Schuster et al. (1990) and Walen & Lachman (2000). They were "Today, has someone (or several people) made too many demands on you", "criticized you", "let you down when you were counting on them", "got on your nerves", "argued with you", and "made you feel tense". Participants rated their experience on 4-point scale from "not at all" to "constantly". The scale showed adequate reliability to detect within-person associations (Rcn = .69) (Nezlek, 2017).

Procedure

After participants consented to participate in the studies, they were sent instructions to download the ExpiWell app on their smartphones. Once the app was downloaded, participants could enter the access code to enroll in our study. Then, they filled out a questionnaire that collects data on participants' demographics. In this questionnaire, we also measured participants' trait Introversion and Neuroticism; however, those measures and the analyses involving them are reported in the Supplementary Materials to keep the main manuscript focused on the key research question: comparing the two theoretical models -social homeostasis and allostasis -in explaining everyday preference for solitude. Filling out this first "intake" survey triggered the EMAs being sent out the following days. We sent out six surveys a day, for seven days; so in total, there were forty two being sent out. Participants were instructed to submit as many surveys as they could; missing surveys did not disqualify them from the study. Each survey took about 2-3 minutes to complete, included the same questions, and was scheduled approximately 2 hours apart.

Compensations were awarded incrementally depending on the number of EMAs that participants submitted. If participants were Psychology students, they received credits counted toward their research methods courses. The rest of the participants received monetary compensations. For those who received credits, they were offered .5 credit for completing the first 14 surveys, 1 credit for completing 15 to 28 surveys, and 1.5 credits for completing 29 surveys or more. Equivalently, for those who received monetary compensations, participants were offered £5 for the first 14 surveys, £10 for completing between 15 and 28 surveys, and £15 for completing 29 surveys or more.

Analytical Approach

We applied the same analytical approach to all three studies. We fit logistic/linear mixedeffects models (using the glmer/lmer functions from the lme4 package in R) to examine associations between negative emotions, worsened mood, loneliness, and social load with current preference for solitude. Separate models were estimated for each negative experience variable as predictor. Each of those variables was person-mean centered. That means each occasion's deviation from each participant's mean was entered as the within-person predictor and each participant's mean across their observations was also entered into the model as the between-person predictor. Additionally, we also included fixed-effects of current situation (binary;1 = alone,0 = social) and the within-person effect × situation interaction. The models included random intercepts and random slopes for the withinperson predictor and situation at the participant level, and a random intercept for day nested within participant. Random slopes were not included for the day-nested-within-participant level because it made less theoretical sense to expect that the association between negative experiences and preference for solitude would differ, for example, between a Tuesday and a Thursday. If any models failed to converge, we examined the variance of the random slope; if it was very small, we dropped the random slope and switched to a random-intercept-only model. Within these models, the within-person effect reflects whether momentary changes in the predictor -above or below the participant's typical level -are associated with momentary changes in preference for solitude; the between-person effect reflects whether participants who typically report more negative experiences also tend to prefer solitude more. Because we tested four negative experience predictors within each set of models, we applied Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the tests to account for multiple comparisons, and reported both the uncorrected and BH-corrected p-values where relevant.

Sensitivity Analyses

A simulation-based sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine the minimum detectable All other model parameters, including the between-person experience effect, the situation main effect, the interaction, and the random-intercept and slope variances at the participant and day levels, were fixed at the values estimated from Study 1's results. The within-person main effect was then varied across a grid of odds ratios, with 300 simulated datasets at each grid point, and the MDES was estimated by interpolation across the resulting power curve. The analyses yielded an MDES at 80% power of approximately OR = 1.12 for the within-person effect of negative emotions, OR = 1.23 for worsened mood, and OR = 1.67 for social load. For the interactions, the simulations yielded an MDES of OR = 0.80 for negative emotions, OR = 0.70 for worsened mood, and OR = 0.48 for social load. All observed within-person effects and interactions for these negative experience variables were further from 1 than their corresponding MDES thresholds, suggesting that our study was adequately powered to detect the effects we observed.

Study 2

In Study 2, we preregistered our hypotheses to replicate Study 1's findings: 1) the associations between negative experiences and solitude preference, and 2) the interaction between negative experiences and situation (alone vs. social) in predicting preference for solitude, such that the association between negative experiences and solitude preference was stronger in social situations but non-significant in solitude. Our hypotheses in the preregistration are as follow:

Hypothesis 1 (H1).

Preference for solitude will be significantly predicted by negative experiences, including higher negative emotions, worsened mood, loneliness, and social load.

Hypothesis 2 (H2).

The association between preference for solitude and negative experiences will be moderated by the situation the participant is in at the time, such that (a) the association will be stronger in social situations than in solitude, and (b) when alone, participants will prefer solitude regardless of whether their experience is positive or negative.

To further investigate the reasons behind participants' solitude preference, we also assessed two forms of motivation that have been previously studied in the solitude literature: self-determined motivations referred to reasons such as seeking solitude for self-reflection, freedom from social demands, or relaxation, whereas non self-determined motivations involved preferring solitude to avoid potentially negative or unpleasant social experiences (Thomas & Azmitia, 2019). We preregistered hypotheses that negative experiences would predict non self-determined reasons for preferring solitude.

Hypothesis 3 (H3). Negative experiences will be positively associated with non selfdetermined reasons for preferring solitude, and the association will be stronger for non selfdetermined than self-determined reasons.

Finally, we continued to examine whether the heightened preference for solitude as a function of negative experiences would apply more to individuals with certain dispositions. We also preregistered hypotheses for personality moderators (numbered as H4-H7 in the Preregistration), but moved their reporting to the Supplementary Materials to keep the main manuscript focused on confirming the key findings of Study 1.

Methods

Participants

Study 2 recruited students from a single sixth-form classroom in the UK (equivalent to the final two years of high school) and undergraduate students in their first year of university. Recruiting from this younger population would be useful for our research questions: during this time, adolescents are still navigating between maintaining relationships with authority figures like parents and teachers and striving for independence. As such, time spent alone serves as an important space that supports autonomy-seeking, emotion regulation, and identity development (Larson, 1990).

The recruitment window fell during the Fall, so data collection took place between November 13 and December 22, 2024. Given both the age and timeframe restrictions, we realistically aimed for at least 50 participants but oversampled to 75 to account for those who might not provide any EMA data. We also aimed for an average of 30 surveys per participant (a 50% response rate), totalling up to 1,500 data points.

Out of 75 participants that signed up, six did not provide any EMA data and one had missing data on several focal variables. The final sample consisted of 68 young adults between the ages of 16 and 20 (M = 17.25, SD = 1.01), completing a total of 1,952 surveys (M = 28.7, SD = 15.98).

The sample included 55 female participants, 10 male participants, and 3 who preferred not to disclose or identified as another gender. Only 34 participants completed more than 30 surveys, whereas 22 completed between 10 and 29 surveys, and 12 completed fewer than 10 surveys.

Measures

Negative Experiences. We measured negative emotions, worsened mood, loneliness, and social load using the same items in Study 1; however, this time, we assessed all negative experience variables throughout the day with prompts sent out six times per day. Social load scale showed only moderate reliability for detecting within-person associations (Rcn = .51).

Aloneness and Preference for Solitude.

The same measures as Study 1 were used.

Reasons for Preferring Solitude.

If participants indicated that they preferred spending time alone at that moment, they were asked, "Which of the following options would you prefer doing right now?" and shown a list of items representing possible reasons for their preference. For selfdetermined reasons for preferring solitude, we included items reflecting a desire to be alone for its benefits, including rest (Hammond, 2016; Nguyen et al., 2018; Rodriguez et al., 2025), freedom and autonomy (Ost-Mor et al., 2020; Weinstein et al., 2023), self-reflection and emotion regulation (Long et al., 2003; Thomas, 2023). To assess non self-determined reasons, we included items reflecting a desire to be alone due to negative feelings, unpleasant social interactions, or a wish to avoid embarrassment or certain people (Thomas & Azmitia, 2019).

The initial set of items were "to take a break", "to have some freedom to do what I want", "to sit with my thoughts", "to avoid unpleasant social interactions", "to process some unpleasant feelings", "to hide away from the world!", "to avoid embarrassing myself", "to avoid people I do not like". To reduce burden, we only asked participants to indicate "yes" or "no" on each item.

Exploratory factor analyses showed two factors: three items "to take a break" (.41), "to sit with my thoughts" (.41), and "to process some unpleasant feelings" (.70) loaded onto one factor which we named self-determined reasons, whereas "to avoid unpleasant social interactions" and "to avoid people I do not like" loaded onto another factor, which we named non self-determined reasons. The rest of the items showed cross-loadings lower than .40 or did not load onto any of the two factors above. Following our preregistered plan, we excluded the cross-loading items and created two composites for non self-deteremined and self-determined reasons for preferring solitude based on these EFA results.

Procedure

Study 2 followed a similar procedure as Study 1. Participants first completed a questionnaire to report their demographics and then they received six signals every day between 9AM and 8PM, this time for 10 days. Compensation was awarded incrementally. Participants recruited through the Psychology participant pool could earn up to 3 credits depending on the number of surveys completed: 0.5 credits for the first 20 surveys, and an additional 0.5 credits for every subsequent 10 surveys completed, up to 2.5 credits for completing more than 50 surveys, plus a 0.5-credit bonus for completing all surveys. Sixth-form students were compensated £3 for every 10 surveys completed, plus a £2 bonus for completing all surveys.

Deviations from Preregistration

Study 2's analytic approach to testing the main effects of negative experiences on preference for solitude (H1), the interaction between each negative experience and situation (H2), and the associations between negative experiences and reasons for preferring solitude (H3) deviated from the preregistered plan. Rather than standardizing negative experience variables around each participant's day-level mean and standard deviation, as originally planned, we instead decomposed each predictor into a within-person (person-mean-centered) component and a between-person (person-mean) component.

Day-level centering makes it difficult to disentangle whether an observed effect reflects a person's general tendency (i.e., a stable, trait-like association) or moment-to-moment fluctuation within a person. Decomposing negative experience predictors at the person level instead allowed within-person and between-person effects to be estimated and interpreted separately, providing a clearer test of whether solitude preference is associated with momentary negative experiences, general negative-experience tendencies, or both.

Finally, we reported all analyses involving personality traits in both Studies 1 and 2 the Supplementary Materials. These included analyses conducted to test Hypotheses, 3, 5-7, in Study 2.

This change was made because Study 2's sample size was not sufficiently powered to reliably detect interactions between negative experiences and personality traits. Moving those analyses to the Supplementary Materials also helped keep the main manuscript focused on the main effects of negative experiences on preference for solitude and on testing the social homeostasis versus allostasis models.

Study 3

Because Studies 1 and 2 used samples with restricted age ranges (18-45 and 16-20, respectively), Study 3 recruited a nationally representative UK sample via Prolific Academic, matched to UK Census demographics. An a priori Monte Carlo simulation was used to estimate the minimum detectable effect size (MDES), assuming 200 participants completing up to five surveys in a single day (30% completing one survey, 70% ~ 140 participants completing an average of three; ~480 total observations).

Random slopes were not included in Study 3's planned model due to smaller number of observations per participant. This would make it insufficient to reliably estimate slope variability within participant. With a random-intercept-only structure, at 80% power, the MDES was approximately OR = 1.26 for the within-person main effect and OR = 0.65 for the within × situation interaction. Restricting analyses to entries where participants reported preferring to be alone, we again found that, on average, participants preferred solitude for more self-determined reasons (B = 0.27, 95% CI = [0.22 -0.33], p < .001), and this was not moderated by age or sex. Non self-determined reasons for preferring solitude was significantly predicted by both worsened mood (B = 0.06, 95% CI = [0.00 -0.11], p = .042) and social load (B = 0.17, 95% CI = [0.06 -0.29], p = .004). However, the within-person effect for worsened mood became no longer significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction (pBH = .084). Further, we did not find significant within-person effects for negative emotions and loneliness. Different from what we found in Study 2, only loneliness correlated with self-determined reasons (B = 0.03, 95% CI = [0.01 -0.05], p = .008) (see Table 8).

When we compared non self-determined and self-determined reasons against each other at different levels of negative experiences, we found similar interaction as in Study 2 for social load (B = -0.14, 95% CI = [-0.26 -0.01], p = .035); the simple slope of social load predicting solitude preference was significant for non-selfdetermined reasons (B = 0.24, 95% CI [0.10, 0.38], p = .002) but not for self-determined reasons (B = 0.11, 95% CI [-0.03, 0.25], p = .145). However, this interaction did not survive Benjamini-Hochberg correction (pBH = .140), so we interpret this replication as tentative rather than confirmed. Descriptively, participants generally preferred solitude for self-determined reasons at lower levels of social load, but as social load increased, non-self-determined reasons rose and approached similar levels (see Figure 3).

We found more significant associations between negative experiences with reasons for preferring solitude at between-person level. Negative emotions, loneliness, and social load all showed positive correlations with non self-determined. Self-determined reasons were also significantly predicted by loneliness and social load (see Table 8). This indicates that those who tended to experience more of those negative states were more likely to prefer solitude out of avoidance of other people and to gain benefits from solitude. It is worth noting, however, that Study 3's fewer observations per participant may have reduced our ability to detect within-person associations specifically, while our ability to detect between-person effects -which rely on aggregated, participant-level averages rather than moment-to-moment fluctuation -may have been enhanced by the larger number of participants.

Discussion

Across all three studies, we found support for an intuitive assumption: when people were in the situation they preferred -whether alone or with other people -this matching was associated with positive states. However, we also found something surprising: participants preferred solitude when experiencing negative emotions and worsened mood (Studies 1-3), as well as during heightened loneliness (Study 2) and high social load (Studies 2 and 3). These findings extend past findings that people tend to prefer solitude after being excluded by their group (Ren et al., 2016 (Ren et al., , 2020)), by demonstrating that the desire for solitude might not only be a response to unpleasant social experiences but might reflect a more general tendency while experiencing negative states. Across the three studies, participants who were already alone and feeling negatively did not show the shift toward wanting company that social homeostasis would predict; this pattern was clearest in Studies 1 and 3, with Study 2 showing preference for solitude near chance level rather than a decline towards preferring to be with others. An alternative explanation is that the preference for solitude during negative experiences might be due to unavailability of supportive others. However, our data also provided some insights to suggest this might not be likely: even in the presence of close others, who were generally rated as pleasant and positive, participants still preferred solitude when faced with negative feelings.

As such, our findings yielded stronger support for the allostatic explanation for solitude preference, as social homeostasis does not fully explain why people want to be alone even when they are already distressed in solitude. Further evidence in Studies 2 and 3 revealed that the desire to be alone when experiencing negative states do not necessarily only about avoidance: participants generally preferred solitude more for its own benefits, but as negative states increased, the desire to avoid others also became salient, so that both motivations operated together rather than one replacing the other.

These findings suggest that this preference for solitude while experiencing negative states may stem from self-protective motivations. Concealing distress around others serves to protect oneself from the stigma associated with expression of negative emotions (Larson et al., 2015). Being around others while distressed may also require attending and responding to others' input, whereas being alone may afford greater autonomy in how one chooses to deal with those emotions.

Alternatively, retreating to solitude allows privacy and prevents interruptions (Werner & Haggard, 1992). For some people, feeling distressed may already be taxing on the body, making it feel effortful to be around social interactions that require attention and energy (Black et al., 2023; Muris et al., 2026; Neville et al., 2026). All of these reasons behind solitude preference fit within the allostasis model, whereby an organism proactively withdraws from social contact to meet the demands of the moment, rather than prioritizing a return to a fixed social setpoint as in homeostasis.

A few limitations should be noted. Due to the fixed recruitment window and limited pool of eligible students in Study 2, the completion rate was low: only half of participants completed more than 50% of the possible surveys. While Study 2's sensitivity analysis indicates that the final sample was adequately powered to detect our observed effects, the lower completion rate may undermine the precision of our estimates.

Secondly, these findings are correlational, not causal. Our experience sampling design, with surveys spaced two to three hours apart, does not allow us to establish temporal precedence between negative experiences and preference for solitude, or the other way around. Although one might attempt to address this by testing lagged association (e.g., negative experiences at time t-1 predicting solitude preference at time t), such an approach assumes a specific interval over which the tested process (e.g., negative experiences à solitude preference) unfolds. Additionally, that approach also assumes there was no other unmeasured events that could occur within that window; for example, an independent, unexpected event such as realising a last-minute deadline might cause someone to prefer solitude regardless of how they felt a few hours before. Or, family conflicts may lead to both negative experiences and solitude preference, which could explain their associations at any time point, whether lagged or not.

Finally, our design didn't allow us to see whether some participants' solitude preference might be chronic. That said, because our between-person effects were derived by aggregating across timepoints within individuals, they suggest that people who tend to experience more negative experiences on average also tend to prefer solitude more on average -a pattern more consistent with a stable, trait-like tendency than a purely momentary response. However, because our data were collected over only 5 to 10 days, this window is likely too brief to determine whether this reflects a truly chronic pattern of solitude preference. A more intensive longitudinal design, with more frequent assessments, would be necessary to investigate solitude preference as a persistent desire rather than a short-term response.

Conclusion

Together, these findings offer a more nuanced understanding of solitude preference. While previous research indicates that the desire for solitude can be triggered by social exclusion (Ren et al., 2016; 2020), our results suggest that this desire may also be linked to broader experiences of negative states. Notably, reasons behind preference for solitude were generally more self-determined than non-self-determined, meaning that most of our participants preferred solitude for its benefits. At the same time, preference for solitude that occurred alongside negative experiences was also motivated by avoidance-driven reasons -suggesting that a person can prefer solitude for both types of reasons simultaneously. Potentially, when experiencing distress, a person may desire solitude to avoid further costs of social interactions, while also seeing the value of having time for self-reflection, rest, and emotion regulation. Future research would benefit from investigating whether solitude that is desired and pursued during times of distress can offer relief from those negative states, and if so, how, under which conditions, and for whom. Rather than disputing our social nature, we would argue that understanding how the pursuit of solitude fits within humans' behavioral repertoire would enrich not only our knowledge but also our appreciation of the fact that our inherent need for social contact coexists with a natural, and quite understandable, desire to withdraw from it now and then when we don't feel so great.
