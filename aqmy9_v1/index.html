Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Little is known about how Big Five personality traits manifest in specific conversational behaviors or whether these associations depend on the communication medium. We examined 154 individuals interacting with strangers face-to-face or via video. Using manual annotations, computer vision, and LLMs, we examined actor and partner personality associations with turn-taking dynamics, engagement signalling, and topic development.

Higher actor and partner extraversion predicted faster turn-taking and pronounced engagement signalling, whereas actor openness predicted slower turn-taking and reduced engagement signalling. In actors, conscientiousness predicted nodding and in partners it predicted broader topic exploration. However, we found no evidence that these associations differed between face-to-face and video-mediated interaction. Personality thus shapes conversational behavior and these interactional fingerprints appear to survive technological transmission.

1 Introduction

Conversations are the cornerstone of human sociality, through which information is shared, relationships are formed and maintained, and collaboration toward shared goals takes place.

The progression of conversation depends on a remarkable degree of coordination between participants. Interactants must continuously manage turns of speaking and listening, display their engagement as listeners, and contribute content that advances the conversation (Cooney & Wheatley, 2025). These processes are reflected in a wide range of verbal and non-verbal processeshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?3uF275 including turn-taking dynamics (Levinson & Torreirahttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UqMovW 2015; Sacks et al., 1974) , listener behaviors such as vocal backchanneling, gaze shifts, nodding, and smiling (Schegloff, 1982; Stivers, 2008) , and the development of conversational content through the generation, pursuit, and closure of topics (Button & Casey, 1985; Schmidt et al., 2025).

Together, these behavioral patterns constitute the building blocks through which human sociality manifests and related personality differences emerge. The idea that personality is manifested in fingerprint patterns of conversational behavior has a long history. More than five decades ago, Bales (1970) proposed that stable individual differences could be understood through recurring patterns of verbal and non-verbal behavior emerging during social interaction. Yet, despite this longstanding interactional perspective on personality, surprisingly few studies have systematically examined how personality traits manifest across the specific conversational processes. Moreover, as social interaction increasingly takes place across both face-to-face and technology-mediated environments, it remains unclear whether these behavioral manifestations of personality are preserved across communication media (Baumaister et al., 2026). The current study builds on this decades-old idea by providing a fine-grained, multimethod examination of how personality is expressed across turn-taking dynamics, listener engagement, and conversational content, and whether these "interactional fingerprints" differ between face-to-face and video-mediated interaction.

A fundamental feature of conversation is the turn-taking system through which interactants coordinate who holds the floor, for how long, and how much time elapses between successive turns (Levinson, 2016; Sacks et al., 1974). Because these processes are readily observable, researchers have long quantified turn-taking behavior using measures such as turn count, turn duration, and speech gaps (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?DCPaed Cassotta et al., 1964; Chapple, 1939). One of the most robust findings from this work is that conversational turn transitions occur remarkably rapidly (Meyer, 2023). That is, across languages and cultures, speakers typically begin their turns within 250 milliseconds of the previous speaker's turn ending, despite the substantial cognitive demands involved in planning and producing a consecutive turn (Levinson & Torreira, 2015; Meyer, 2023; Stivers et al., 2009). Since the earliest quantitative studies of conversation, turn-taking dynamic variables like average turn length and speech gap duration were viewed as potential indicators of personality and interpersonal communication style (Chapple, 1939). Laterhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MbaZsW it was foundhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PPN632 for example, that the Big Five traits are associated with turn dynamics: Extraversion is associated with higher speech rates in informal and formal interpersonal situations (Dewaele & Furnham, 1999) , higher speech coordination during argumentative conversations (Arellano-Véliz et al., 2025) , and shorter speech gaps in initial encounters (Cuperman & Ickes, 2009a; Wang & Ziano, 2022). Agreeablenesshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HlTnaR in turn, is associated with higher turn count and longer duration of talking in spontaneous initial encounters (Cuperman & Ickes, 2009a) , and generally lower speech coordination during argumentative exchange (Arellano-Véliz et al., 2025).

Beyond the coordination of speaking turns, conversations also depend on what is being said-that is, on the continuous development of shared content through the introduction, maintenance, and transition of conversational topics (Button & Casey, 1985; Schegloff, 1982). Throughout conversation, interactants move through the semantic topic space, creating a common ground and exploring new ideas. Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) through large language models (LLMs) have made it possible to quantify these semantic trajectories by measuring topic shift strength between consecutive turns and semantic exploration breadth across the entire encounter (Schmidt et al., 2025; Speer et al., 2024). To our knowledge, there is only one study that has used this methodological approach to examine personality differences in conversational topic development (Fischer & Ram, 2024). The authors examined to what extent differences in interlocutors' personality traits predicted the breadth of topics covered during a conversation and the semantic shift between turns. They found that especially dyads with different levels of openness explored the topic space more broadly whereas dyads with differing levels of extraversion showed decrease in semantic alignment along conversation. Yet, the focus on dyad-level effects makes it impossible to make individual-level inferences about actor effects (how individuals' own traits predict their own behavior) and partner effects (how their partners' traits predict their behavior). It is therefore crucial to examine also the actor and partner effects of Big-Five traits on the development of conversation content.

Yet, conversation involves more than the speaking turns. While the speaker holds the floor, the listener actively displays engagement through a range of verbal and non-verbal behaviors including turn dynamics, brief vocalizations ("hmm", "oh really?"), nods, gaze shifts, and smiles (Bavelas et al., 2000; Peräkylä et al., 2022; Stivers, 2008). Such behaviors serve an important interpersonal function by allowing listeners to demonstrate understanding, continued participation, and affiliation (Gardner, 2001; Schegloff, 1982; Stivers, 2008).

Moreover, speakers continuously monitor the listener's engagement signalling and adapt their own behavior accordingly (Bavelas et al., 2000; Peräkylä et al., 2015; Stevanovic et al., 2019). Therefore, engagement signalling is a fundamental mechanism through which conversations are maintained. However, relatively little research has examined how individual differences in personality shape these engagement displays. Existing studies suggest that especially extraversion, agreeableness, and openness are associated with differences in engagement signalling (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MKDcix Berry & Hansen, 2000; Cuperman & Ickes, 2009a; Funder & Sneed, 1993; Huang & Gratch, 2013). For example, Cuperman and Ickes (2009) examined researcher-induced spontaneous dyadic first encounters and found that both individuals' own (i.e., actor effect) and their partners' (i.e., partner effect) agreeableness were associated with more frequent vocal backchannels, nods, and smiles. Huang and Gratch (2013) used simulated interaction scenario called the Parasocial Consensus Sampling and found that high extraversion, conscientiousness and openness and low neuroticism predicted higher nodding rate whereas smiling was more pronounced in high extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness and low neuroticism. Finally, Berry and Hansen (2000) examined female unacquainted dyads and found higher agreeableness and openness to predict listener gaze orientation. Howeverhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?uD9a6n the existing evidence relies largely on manual annotation of conversational behaviors and self-reports (Berry & Hansen, 2000; Cuperman & Ickes, 2009a) , as well as laboratory tasks with limited ecological validity (Huang & Gratch, 2013).

Thus, there is a need to revisit the association between personality and engagement signalling using modern multimethod approaches in naturalistic social interaction.

The need to understand personality in conversational behavior has become increasingly important following the rapid adoption of video calling across diverse social contexts, ranging from job interviews and medical consultations to dating and workplace meetings.

While this technological transition has brought many benefits, it has also been associated with altered turn-dynamics and reduced engagement between interaction partners compared to face-to-face communication (Boland et al., 2022a; Brucks & Levav, 2022; Söderman et al., 2026). These effects have been observed both in micro-interactional processes and in broader patterns of social influence and emotional dynamics (Baumaister et al., 2026). Recently, Lepistö and colleagues (2026) found that video-mediated communication weakened emotional dynamics between speaker and listener during moments of self-disclosure, reducing the extent to which listeners' gaze shifts were reflected in speakers' autonomic arousal. Similar disruptions have been observed in turn-dynamics. In a large-scale field study, Brucks and Levav (2022) found that colleagues interacting via video call produced less overlapping speech and fewer speaker transitions than colleagues interacting face-to-face.

Likewise, Boland and colleagues (2022) reported that video-mediated communication more than tripled speech gap times in both a controlled question-answer task and unscripted conversation. Extending these findings to listener behavior, Söderman and colleagues (2026) showed that strangers meeting via video call produced fewer vocal backchannels than those interacting face-to-face. Thus, video-mediated communication seems to constrain several key conversational behaviors by hampering associated socio-emotional coordination.

Consequently, the influence of personality on engagement signalling may also differ between face-to-face and video-mediated interaction.

Finally, to examine the extent to which Big Five traits shape conversational behaviors and whether these effects are altered by communication technology, interaction should be observed in a context where these micro-interactional processes, such as speech gaps and nods, play a particularly important role. One such context is interaction between previously unacquainted interlocutors. First encounters are characterized by heightened uncertainty and a strong motivation to establish rapport and maintain a positive interactional climate (Flint, 2019; Haugh & Carbaugh, 2015). Participants are especially sensitive to turn-taking dynamics, pauses, and other cues that signify conversational flow (Templeton et al., 2023).

Moreover, first encounters are characterized by an active search for common ground that has been shown to result in semantic alignment and less broad exploration of topics (Speer et al., 2024). Both individuals' own and their partners' personality traits may shape the introduction, maintenance, and transition of conversational topics across turns, giving rise to variation in the semantic patterns of one's speech content. Finally, when strangers interact, engagement signalling becomes particularly consequential for the progression of the interaction and mutual affiliation. Behaviors such as vocal backchanneling, nodding, and smiles not only signify understanding and affiliation but also provide a natural arena through which stable individual differences may be expressed. Nowadays increasing amounts of consequential first encounters, including first dates, job interviews, and medical appointments, happen through video-calls. It is therefore important to understand whether the interactional fingerprints of personality emerge similarly across face-to-face and video-mediated interactions, or whether technological mediation alters how personality is expressed in conversation.

1.2 Current study

In the present study, we examined how general Big Five personality traits influence a wide range of conversational behaviors during first encounters and whether the medium through which the conversation takes place moderates these trait-effects. Individuals were randomly assigned to one of two communication medium conditions (face-to-face or video call) and were asked to be acquainted with a same-sex stranger. By focusing on previously unacquainted participants, the design minimizes the influence of shared interactional history while enabling a systematic examination of personality and medium-related differences in three different behavioral processes: turn-taking dynamics, engagement signalling, and content development.

To examine personality-related variation in conversational behavior, the present study focuses on Big Five personality traits: openness to experiences, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism (McCrae & John, 1992). The Big Five was selected as the framework for examining personality because it is still the most widely used, empirically validated, and extensively studied model of personality in contemporary psychology (John et al., 2008). Its broad acceptance facilitates comparison with previous research and enhances the interpretability and generalizability of findings. Of the Big Five traits, particularly extraversion, agreeableness, and openness have been previously associated with conversational behavior, as cited above. At the same time, traits like conscientiousness and neuroticism may also influence how individuals coordinate turns, participate in content development, and display engagement as listeners. Building on previous dyadic research (Cuperman & Ickeshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?AX8a64 2009a) , we examine both actor and partner effects, investigating how individuals' own personality traits and those of their interaction partners are associated with conversational behavior.

To capture the multimodal nature of conversational behavior and unilateral contributions of both speaker and listener, we examined three behavior clusters: turn-taking dynamics (turn length, speech gaps between turns), content development (topic exploration breadth across the entire encounter, topic shift strength between turns), and engagement signalling while listening (vocal backchannels, nods, smiles, gaze orientation). While turn-taking dynamics and content development primarily capture speaker behavior, engagement signalling captures the listener's verbal and nonverbal contributions to the interaction. Of the content development variables, topic exploration breadth captured how broadly a person explored different topics over time, whereas topic shift strength captured local, turn-to-turn transitions between topics. To characterize these complementary aspects of conversational behavior, we combined manual annotation of speaking turns, nods, and backchannels with natural language processing (NLP) of content development, and computer-vision-based analysis of listener non-verbal behavior.

Based on the reviewed research, we hypothesized that: H1.1: Higher openness in both actors and partners is associated with longer speaking turns and greater engagement signaling through eye contact and nodding as well as broader topic exploration and broader topic exploration and stronger semantic shifts between turns (Berry & Hansen, 2000; Huang & Gratch, 2013; Fischer & Ram, 2024) H1.2: Higher conscientiousness in both actor and partner is associated with greater engagement signaling through eye contact and nodding (Funder & Sneed, 1993; Huang & Gratch, 2013) H1.3: Higher extraversion in both actor and partner is associated with shorter speech gaps, broader topic exploration and stronger semantic shifts between turns, and greater engagement signaling through vocal backchanneling, nodding, and smiling (Arellano-Véliz et al., 2025; Fischer & Ram, 2024; Funder & Sneed, 1993; Huang & Gratch, 2013; Wang & Ziano, 2022) H1.4: Higher agreeableness in both actor and partner is associated with greater engagement signaling through vocal backchanneling, nodding, smiling, and eye contact (Berry & Hansen, 2000; Cuperman & Ickes, 2009a; Funder & Sneed, 1993; Huang & Gratch, 2013) H1.5: Higher neuroticism of both actor and partner is associated with reduced engagement signaling through smiling and nodding (Huang & Gratch, 2013). In addition, higher partner neuroticism is associated with greater actor engagement signaling through eye contact (Cuperman & Ickes, 2009).

Based on prior evidence, video-calling increases speech gaps and reduces overlapping turns (Basch et al., 2021; Brucks & Levav, 2022), disturbs engagement in interaction (Lepistö et al., 2025; Söderman et al., 2026) as well as reduces culturally rooted differences in vocal backchanneling and nodding (Söderman et al., 2026). We therefore hypothesized that the moderating effect of communication medium would be specific to agreeableness and extraversion, which have been most consistently associated with a high-engagement conversational style (Arellano-Véliz et al., 2025; Berry & Hansen, 2000; Cuperman & Ickes, 2009a; Fischer & Ram, 2024; Funder & Sneed, 1993; Huang & Gratch, 2013; Wang & Ziano, 2022):

H2: Video calling attenuates the associations of extraversion and agreeableness with greater engagement signalling, relative to face-to-face interaction (Basch et al., 2021; Brucks & Levav, 2022; Holler & Levinson, 2019; Lepistö et al., 2025; Söderman et al., 2026).

Finally, in dyadic interaction, both interactants can influence each other's conversational behavior. Previous research has demonstrated such actor-partner effects during spontaneous initial conversations (Cuperman & Ickes, 2009). However, given the limited prior evidence regarding the specific form of these effects, we did not formulate directional hypotheses and instead examined actor-partner interactions exploratorily.

In the face-to-face condition, participants were escorted to the same room, into designated chairs. Participants used a small keypad to initiate the session and give responses to self-reports according to the instructions displayed on the monitors. In the video-mediated condition, the participants also had a laptop for the Zoom video call and a microphone headset for auditory communication.

During preparation, the audiovisual recording setup was installed and calibrated. Audio was captured using chest-mounted microphones. By the end of the study the curtain was closed for the face-to-face condition, while the Zoom call ended in the video-call condition, after which participants completed a set of personality questionnaires.

2.4 Annotation of backchanneling behaviors

The head and torso recordings of each participant were first cut and temporally aligned based on the sound cues marking task onset and offset, and then paired with their dyadic partner's recording into a combined video using Filmora software (Wondershare Technology Co., Ltd.). After that, the backchanneling behaviors and turn-taking were annotated in ELAN (v.

6.9), which yielded 27 531 annotated instances. Only the first five minutes of interaction (the duration of the Getting Acquainted task) were annotated, aiming for comparability between dyads. For each participant, vocal backchannels and head nods were coded on different annotation tiers by two trained coders. All behavioral elements (turns, vocal backchannels, and nods) within any given dyad were coded by a single annotator. Each coder's annotations for their first dyad were reviewed by a researcher with training in conversation-analytical transcription to ensure consistency with the coding criteria (see Measures for inter-rater reliability analysis).

The onset and offset times of each turn and vocal backchannel were marked using the audible onset and offset of the instance, informed by the audio waveform. Turns were annotated following the framework of Sacks et al. (1974), according to which speech is organized into turn-constructional units (TCUs) that project possible completion points, or transition-relevance places (TRPs). A new turn was annotated when the interaction partner initiated a speaking turn or when the same speaker continued with a new turn following the completion of their previous TCU. Short vocal response tokens were coded as vocal backchannels when they occurred either within the current speaker's turn or at a transition relevance place (TRP) (Gardner, 2001; Yngve, 1970). Backchannels occurring at a TRP could be followed by a speaking turn produced by the same person. As an exception, short responses to polar questions occurring at a TRP were coded as turns rather than backchannels, as they constituted answers to the preceding question. When annotating the nods, the onset was coded manually from the perceived initiation of a vertical head movement, either downward or upward. A nod was defined as a discrete vertical head movement including both a downward and an upward turn, in either order (Voutilainen et al., 2018). Following the annotation process, all participant-specific instances with their onset and offset times were exported as CSV output files for further processing and statistical analysis. For NLP analyses, the turn onset and offset times were combined with the auto-transcribed speech content data to identify the turn transitions between different speakers, which were used to estimate. 2.5 Natural language processing of conversation content

The recordings were automatically transcribed using Whisper-Timestamped (version 1.15.8;

https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wu96H7) with the Whisper large model. Conversation content was analyzed using BERTopic (0.17.4), which represents utterances as contextual semantic embeddings using sentence transformers based on Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) (BERT; Grootendorst, 2022). Individual speaking turns/utterances were used as input to the BERTopic. The utterances were first preprocessed removing selected stop words identified during the annotation process (list in Supplementary Table X). Sentence embeddings were then generated using a multilingual Sentence-Transformer model from

Hugging Face (Sentence-BERT; Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). The multilingual model was selected because it can represent Finnish and Swedish sentences within a shared embedding space. This enabled the full dataset to be analysed jointly, providing a larger and more densely sampled basis for identifying semantic structures that might be insufficiently represented in smaller, language-specific subsets.

The resulting sentence embeddings were reduced in dimensionality using Uniform Manifold

Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to facilitate subsequent clustering and topic identification (UMAP; https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VjzXw8. UMAP was configured with n_neighbors = 10, n_components = 5, min_dist = 0.2, and cosine distance as the distance metric. The dimensionally reduced embeddings were subsequently clustered using HDBSCAN with min_cluster_size = 7 and min_samples = 2 (HDBSCAN; https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wA64a2.

Topic representations for the resulting clusters were generated using CountVectorizer (scikit-learn; Pedregosa et al., 2011) in combination with class-based term frequency-inverse document frequency (c-TF-IDF) (BERTopic; Grootendorst, 2022). CountVectorizer was configured to consider n-grams consisting of one to three words, thereby capturing both individual terms and short multi-word expressions. c-TF-IDF was then used to estimate the relative importance of these terms within each cluster, allowing the terms most representative of each identified topic to be extracted.

The model identified 244 distinct topics across the dataset and assigned each utterance to one of these topics. Each utterance was linked to the 384-dimensional embedding representing that topic in the semantic space. Each assignment was accompanied by a probability score indicating how strongly the utterance belonged to the assigned topic. The resulting utterance-level data, including person-specific topic assignments and their probabilities, were exported as CSV files for further processing. These data were subsequently used to derive two variables characterizing conversational content: topic exploration breadth and topic shift strength (see section 2.6.3). 2.6 Computer vision-based analysis of listeners' non-verbal behavior

Non-verbal behavior was extracted from the video recordings using OpenFace 2.0 (Baltrusaitis et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mLYPuY 2018) , an automated computer vision toolkit for facial behavior and gaze estimation. OpenFace provided frame-by-frame estimates at 25 fps of facial Action Unit activation and gaze direction. Frames with missing values were linearly interpolated. For gaze estimation, frames with low tracking confidence (≤ .80) or unsuccessful facial tracking were additionally treated as missing and interpolated, as gaze estimates become unreliable when facial features cannot be adequately tracked. The resulting signals were low-pass filtered to attenuate high-frequency noise, using a second-order Butterworth filter with measure-specific cutoff frequencies (0.5 Hz for AU12 activity and 3 Hz for gaze direction).

Because smiling and gaze were examined as listener engagement behaviors, the preprocessed signals were restricted to non-speaking periods, defined as periods outside the onset-to-offset interval of each participant's own speaking turns (Figure 2, panel C). Participant-level measures of smiling and gaze dispersion were subsequently derived from these preprocessed signals as described below.

2.7 Measures

The measures consisted of self-report ratings of personality traits and conversational behavior outcomes measured with a multimethod approach combining manual annotation of actions, natural language processing of conversation content, and computer vision analysis of listener's non-verbal behavior (see Figure 2 for overview).

2.7.1 Personality traits

Participants' personality traits were assessed with the Big Five Inventory (BFI; John et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?423bEp 2008 John et al., , 2012)) , a 44-item measure of the five-factor model. Participants rated the extent to which each statement described them. Each item corresponds to one of the five personality dimensions, for example: "Is talkative" (extraversion), "Tends to find fault with others" (agreeableness; reverse-coded), "Does a thorough job" (conscientiousness), "Is depressed" (neuroticism), and "Is original, comes up with new ideas" (openness). Responses were given on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Traits from both the participant and their dyadic partner were used to predict conversational outcomes in our analyses. These traits are referred to as actor and partner traits in later sections.

Internal consistency for the trait scales was acceptable to high: openness (α = 0.74), conscientiousness (α = 0.80), extraversion (α = 0.85), agreeableness (α = 0.75), and neuroticism (α = 0.82).

2.7.2 Turn-taking dynamics

Speech gap duration was calculated as the duration of the temporal gap (in seconds) between the offset of the partner's turn and the onset of the subject's response (a turn or a vocal backchannel response). Positive values indicate a gap between turns and negative values reflect overlapping speech. Only the cases where the response was associated with the partner's turn ending were used to calculate this metric, in order for it to reflect actual speaker transitions. Extreme values exceeding ±2.5 standard deviations from the mean were removed.

Mean speech gap duration was then calculated for each participant across all valid speaker transitions within the conversation.

Turn length was calculated as the difference between turn onset and offset (in seconds) for every turn occurring within the first five minutes of conversation. Mean turn length was then calculated as the subject-wise mean of this variable. A Box-Cox transformation was applied (λ = -0.38) for statistical testing, after which the assumptions related to model residuals were deemed satisfactory.

2.7.3 Conversational content development

The conversation content data with person-and utterance-specific topic assignments and their probabilities were used to derive topic exploration breadth and topic shift strength. Both variables were calculated based on utterances with a BERTopic assignment probability of at least .20. Of the 5,653 total utterances, 3,409 (60.3%) met this threshold. By excluding low-probability utterances, we aimed to reduce the influence of uncertain and noisy topic assignments on the analyses.

Topic exploration breadth was calculated as the mean pairwise cosine distance across all unordered pairs of a participant's retained utterances, using the topic embedding assigned to each utterance. Repeated assignments to the same topic were retained and therefore contributed zero-distance within-topic pairs. Topic exploration breadth therefore reflects the overall semantic diversity of a participant's contributions across the conversation, with higher values indicating that their utterances spanned more semantically distinct topics and lower values indicating greater concentration within a narrower range of topics.

Topic shift strength was calculated across consecutive utterances produced by different speakers. Speaker turns were first identified from the complete temporally ordered sequence of all utterances within each dyad, before applying the topic-probability threshold. In most cases, a manually annotated turn contained a single utterance, although some turns comprised two or more utterances. For each turn transition, the last utterance meeting the probability threshold in the immediately preceding partner speaker turn and the first utterance meeting the probability threshold in the current actor speaker turn were selected. Thus, when the utterance closest to the speaker boundary did not meet the threshold, the nearest eligible utterance within the same speaker turn was used. This procedure increased the number of usable transitions while retaining only utterances with sufficiently reliable topic assignments.

The cosine distance between the BERTopic topic embeddings assigned to the selected partner and actor utterances was then calculated, with the resulting value assigned to the speaker of the response utterance. Cosine distances at transition points were then averaged separately for each participant. Higher values therefore indicate that a participant's responses were, on average, more distant in topic space from the last partner utterance in the preceding speaker turn. To improve the robustness of the participant-level estimates, participants with fewer than five valid turn transitions were omitted from analyses involving this variable, resulting in n = 136.

2.7.4 Engagement signalling

Vocal backchannels and nods occurring within the first five minutes of interaction were counted separately for each participant from the annotation output csv files. To account for differences in listening time, these counts were standardized by dividing them by the participant's listening time (calculated by subtracting the total duration of the participant's turns from the five minutes of interaction time) and expressing them as backchannel rates (per minute). Thus, these variables reflect how frequently participants produced nods and vocal backchannels when listening to the speech of their dyadic partner. Since these instances were manually annotated by a research assistant, inter-rater reliability was assessed for both nods and vocal backchannels. For this, two dyads were randomly sampled from each of the pools defined by gender, communication channel, and language group, resulting in 16 total dyads. These dyads were then independently re-annotated by a second coder. Each coder calculated the participant-level rates per minute over the 5-min conversation. Agreement between coders was then estimated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Inter-rater reliability was high for both nodding rate (ICC = .90, 95% CI [.81, .95]) and vocal backchannel rate (ICC = .94, 95% CI [.65, .98]). Finally, in order to satisfy the residual-related assumptions of our statistical models, Box-Cox transformations were applied to vocal backchanneling rate (λ = 0.55) and nodding rate (λ = 0.30).

Smiling.

Smiling was operationalized using OpenFace estimates of Action Unit 12 (AU12; lip corner puller), which reflects activation of the zygomaticus major muscle and constitutes a core facial action underlying smiling (Ekman & Friesen, 1978; Rychlowska et al., 2017). To account for between-person differences in tonic AU12 activity, the preprocessed AU12 signal was mean-centered within participants and rectified such that values below the participant-specific baseline were set to zero. Smiling was quantified as the mean of this signal across listener periods, with higher values indicating greater average smiling activity.

A Box-Cox transformation was applied to the resulting participant-level variable to satisfy the residual-related assumptions of the statistical models (λ = 0.63).

Gaze dispersion.

Gaze dispersion was operationalized using OpenFace estimates of horizontal (gaze_angle_x) and vertical (gaze_angle_y) gaze direction, expressed in radians.

For each participant, the standard deviations of the preprocessed horizontal and vertical gaze angles across listener periods were calculated separately and summed to obtain an overall gaze dispersion score. Higher values indicated greater variation in gaze direction across the interaction, whereas lower values indicated more concentrated and stable gaze orientation.

2.8 Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v. 4.5.3). As preliminary checks of the success of random assignment, we first examined whether participants in the two communication media differed in age or gender distribution. We then tested whether personality trait scores differed between the communication media using linear models in which each personality trait was predicted by communication medium. Having established the comparability of the two groups, we used the same modeling approach to examine the effects of communication medium on the eight conversational behaviors. Here, behavioral variables were z-standardized; thus, the reported estimates (b) represent differences between the two communication media expressed in standard deviation units of the respective variable. To account for potential dependence between dyad members, cluster-robust standard errors were calculated using dyad as the clustering variable, with CR2 correction and Satterthwaite-adjusted degrees of freedom.

For our main hypothesis tests, we examined whether the Big Five traits of both actor and partner predicted interactional behavior across three outcome clusters: turn-taking dynamics (average speech gap and turn duration), content development (topic exploration breadth, topic shift strength), and engagement signalling of the listener (vocal backchanneling, nodding, gaze, smiling). First, each outcome was predicted by the main effects of actor and partner traits and the communication medium in an ordinary least squares regression framework. To control for possible confounding effects of partner's turn count on backchanneling (since the amount of partner's turns was assumed to covary with the amount of listener backchanneling), it was included as a covariate in the models for nodding and vocal backchanneling. The same cluster robust framework and the same data transformations for engagement variables were used for these models as in the previous step. For the personality effects, p values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure separately within each trait and each of the three outcome clusters. Traits were treated as separate groupings in the FDR correction because the hypotheses concerned the effects of each personality trait independently. Accordingly, the correction pool comprised four effects (actor and partner effects on two outcomes) for each trait in the turn-taking cluster, four effects for each trait in the content development cluster (actor and partner effects on two outcomes), and eight effects for each trait in the engagement-signalling cluster (actor and partner effects on four outcomes).

As secondary hypothesis tests (H2), we examined whether the effects of actor and partner personality traits on the outcome variables depended on communication medium. For this purpose, we included communication medium-by-trait interaction terms alongside the corresponding main effects in the models. The p-values from these secondary hypothesis tests were FDR-corrected separately from those of the primary analyses. Correction was performed separately within each trait and outcome cluster, with the correction pools containing only the interaction terms.

Finally, as an exploratory analysis, we examined whether the effects of actor personality traits on the outcome variables depended on the partner's personality traits. For this analysis, we included actor-by-partner interaction terms for each trait alongside the trait and communication-medium main effects. Here, the FDR correction was not applied due to the exploratory nature of the analyses.

3 Results

3.1 Preliminary checks of group comparability following random assignment

First, as a preliminary check, we examined whether the face-to-face and video-mediated groups differed in participant characteristics. The groups did not differ significantly in age, t(147) = 0.23, p = .819, or gender distribution, χ²(1) = 0.39, p = .530. Personality trait scores likewise did not differ significantly between the two communication medium groups (Table 2). Together, these results indicated that the sampling procedure produced broadly comparable groups with respect to demographic characteristics and personality traits.

3.2 Influence of communication medium on conversational behaviors

Differences in conversational behaviors between the communication mediums were examined. Regarding turn-taking dynamics, we found shorter speech gaps in the face-to-face condition than in the video-mediated condition (b = -0.631, 95% CI [-0.931, -0.331], p < .001), indicating faster responses to partner's turn during face-to-face interactions. There was no significant difference in turn length between the two communication mediums (p = .635).

There were also no significant differences in the variables related to conversation content between the two groups (ps ≥ .406). Regarding the engagement signalling, we found less smiling (b = -0.516, 95% CI [-0.873, -0.159], p = .005) and more gaze dispersion (b = 0.753, 95% CI [0.454, 1.051], p < .001) in the face-to-face conversations compared to the video-mediated conversations, with no significant differences in vocal backchanneling or nodding (ps ≥ .070). The conversation content family included topic exploration breadth (across individual's own utterances) and topic shift strength (in turn-switch situations). 4 Discussion

The present study examined how Big Five personality traits are associated with conversational behavior within a dyad and whether these associations depend on the communication medium. Using a multimethod approach, we examined both actor and partner personality effects on turn-taking dynamics, conversational content, and listener engagement signaling during first encounters. The findings provided partial support for H1, according to which different personality traits were expected to be associated with distinct patterns of conversational behavior. Specifically, higher actor openness predicted longer speaking turns and longer gaps between turns but was negatively associated with engagement signaling and unrelated to content development, providing only partial support for H1.1. Similarly, H1.2 concerning conscientiousness received partial support: higher actor conscientiousness was associated with more frequent nodding, but the hypothesized association with gaze orientation was not observed. Unexpectedly, partner consciousness was associated with the actor's broader exploration of topics. Extraversion emerged as the most consistent predictor of conversational behavior. As hypothesized in H1.3, higher extraversion in both actors and partners was associated with shorter speech gaps. Higher extraversion in both actors and partners was further associated with more frequent vocal backchanneling and with increased smiling. The hypotheses concerning agreeableness (H1.4) and neuroticism (H1.5) received no clear support as none of these effects survived correction for multiple testing. Exploratory analyses of actor-partner interactions further indicated that combinations of dyad members' openness, neuroticism, and extraversion were associated with turn-taking dynamics. Finally, with regard to H2, the analyses provided no evidence that personality-behavior associations differed between face-to-face and video-mediated interactions. Thus, although communication medium itself influenced several aspects of conversational behavior, there were no substantial personality-related differences in conversational behavior across the two interaction settings. Together, these findings suggest that personality is expressed through distinct patterns of conversational behavior that generalize across face-to-face and video-mediated first encounters. We next discuss these findings in more detail trait by trait, starting with those that showed the most robust effects.

Consistent with our hypotheses and with previous studieshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nUPECu people with higher extraversion exhibited faster and more active conversational style (Cuperman & Ickes, 2009; Dewaele & Furnham, 1999; Wang & Ziano, 2022) , marked by rapid turn-taking and greater engagement signalling. For turn-dynamics, the associations emerged for both actor and partner extraversion: actors higher in extraversion took the turn more rapidly, but actors also responded more rapidly when their partners were more extraverted. This pattern highlights the fact that extraversion of both interlocutors contributes to the pace of conversational exchange. The same energetic fast-pace style was present in engagement cueing. Extraverted actors and people interacting with extraverted partners provided more vocal backchannels and smiled more. Given that rapid turn transitions and active backchanneling as listeners are particularly important in defining interactional satisfaction during initial conversations (Itzchakov & Wheelerhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?XRukAk 2024; Kawana, 1986; Templeton et al., 2023) , the finding may help explain why extraverted individuals are particularly successful in forming new social ties and tend to develop larger social networks (Feiler & Kleinbaum, 2015; Rollings et al., 2023).

Openness, in contrast, was associated with an almost opposite conversational style. People high in openness produced longer speaking turns and took longer to respond. These outcomes are likely related to each other, as people inhale deeper in preparation for a longer response, thereby taking longer to initiate their response (Torreira et al., 2015). People with higher openness also displayed less engagement through vocal backchannels, and their partners smiled less and maintained more eye contact, although these engagement-related effects did not remain significant after correction for multiple testing. The overall pattern was consistent with a slower-paced interactional style characterized by greater consideration and deliberation (Tannen, 1984). This interpretation is compatible with the broader characterization of openness as involving greater intellectual engagement and receptiveness to complex or novel ideas (DeYoung, 2015; McCrae & Costa, 1997). One exception to this pattern was that people high in openness did not explore the conversational topic space more broadly, contrary to our hypothesis. The contrasting associations of extraversion and openness are nevertheless informative: whereas extraversion appears to manifest in fast-paced interpersonal exchange characterized by overt mutual engagement, openness appears to manifest in slower-paced, more deliberative actor turns resulting in less overt partner engagement.

Conscientiousness has received relatively little attention in previous research on conversational behavior, but the present findings suggest that it may nevertheless shape specific aspects of interaction. In prior research utilizing the Parasocial Consensus Sampling approach, the trait was associated with more frequent nodding (Huang & Gratch, 2013). In the current study, this finding was replicated in naturalistic conversation, with actors higher in conscientiousness nodding more frequently. Two additional partner effects provide an interesting perspective on how conscientiousness may influence the development of conversational content. Interestingly, higher partner conscientiousness was associated with broader topic exploration by the actor but with smaller semantic shifts between consecutive utterances. One possible explanation is that more conscientious individuals use what Mishler (1978) termed interrogative series to initiate and maintain conversational flow through successive questions. Such interrogative series could explain why partners covered a broader range of conversational topics while simultaneously showing smaller turn-to-turn semantic shifts: successive questions may elicit information across different topics, while partners' responses remain semantically closely related to the questions that elicited them. These effects suggest that conscientiousness may shape conversation not only through individuals' own behavior but also through the way they scaffold the conversational contributions of their partners.

In regard to agreeableness and neuroticism, the effects were found to be surprisingly weak.

As prior research had associated high agreeableness with greater engagement signaling (Berry & Hansenhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?dntn75 2000; Cuperman & Ickes, 2009a; Funder & Sneed, 1993; Huang & Gratch, 2013) , we assumed this link to be found also in our data but none of the direct actor or partner effects reached significance. This suggests that, at least during brief interactions between previously unacquainted individuals, the rapidity of conversational exchange or the expressions of engagement may be more strongly associated with the energetic and socially assertive tendencies characteristic of extraversion than with the cooperative and affiliative tendencies characteristic of agreeableness. Based on prior research (Huang & Gratch, 2013), we also expected higher neuroticism to be associated with reduced engagement signaling through smiling and nodding. This hypothesis was, likewise, not supported by the data. The only effects related to neuroticism came from the exploratory analyses of actor-partner interactions showing that gaps between speaker turns were shorter if both actor and partner were scoring high in neuroticism. Together, these findings suggest that agreeableness and neuroticism have limited direct associations with conversational behavior during brief initial encounters, although neuroticism may become behaviorally relevant if both partners are high on the trait.

Turning to the communication medium, the current study revealed clear effects of video-calling on conversational behavior, replicating and extending previous evidence.

Compared with face-to-face interaction, video-mediated conversations were characterized by longer speech gaps and less frequent vocal backchanneling, consistent with previous findings (Boland et al., 2022; Brucks & Levav, 2022; Söderman et al., 2026). Importantly, however, the effects of video calling on engagement signalling were not uniformly suppressive.

Participants smiled more frequently and maintained more direct gaze toward their partners during video-mediated than face-to-face interaction, suggesting that some visual engagement signals may be enhanced rather than diminished by the video-call setting (Croes et al., 2019; see also Walther, 1992). The most notable finding, however, was the consistency of personality-behavior associations across communication media. Contrary to H2, none of the trait associations differed significantly between face-to-face and video-mediated interaction.

This is particularly noteworthy given previous evidence that video calling not only alters conversational dynamics (Boland et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?JA7iaZ 2022; Brucks & Levav, 2022) but can also attenuate individual or group differences in engagement behavior, such as culturally rooted variation in vocal backchanneling and nodding (Söderman et al., 2026) , and disrupt interpersonal emotional dynamics (Lepistö et al., 2025). The present findings therefore suggest that although video-mediated communication changes the overall level and timing of several conversational behaviors, we found no evidence that it alters how personality differences are expressed through these behaviors. In this sense, personality-related patterns of conversational behavior appear to survive the technological transmission of first encounters.

5 Limitations and future directions

The present findings should be interpreted in light of several features of the interactional setting. Participants engaged in five-minute conversations with previously unacquainted same-sex partners, with the explicit goal of getting acquainted. This standardized setting provided a controlled context for examining individual differences in conversational behavior and for comparing face-to-face and video-mediated interactions, but it also defines important boundary conditions for the generalizability of the findings.

First, the instructions to become acquainted, together with participants' knowledge of the limited time, may have restricted the range of topics discussed and the extent to which participants explored topics. Consequently, the absence of other personality effects on topic development should not be interpreted as evidence that conscientiousness is the only trait related to conversational content more generally. As conscientiousness is related to goal directed behavior (Wilmot & Oneshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MDwDvj 2019) , merely giving participants the goal of getting acquainted might have brought out the observed effect. Longer and more naturally unfolding conversations, in which participants can establish their own conversational goals, may therefore provide greater opportunities for personality differences to emerge.

Second, the sample consisted exclusively of same-sex dyads. There is evidence showing that overt behavior such as eye contact and time spent talking is different in same-sex and different-sex dyads (e.g. Garcia et al., 1991; Mulac, 1989; Mulac et al., 1987). Different-sex dyads may thus involve distinct interactional dynamics that influence how personality is behaviorally expressed and how partners respond to one another. Future research should directly compare same-sex and different-sex interactions to determine whether dyadic sex composition moderates the effects of personality traits on conversational behavior.

Third, the study only examined interactions between strangers. This was useful for examining personality-related differences without the influence and complexity of an established relationship, but it also limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized to conversations in established relationships, such as between friends, colleagues, or romantic partners. Familiarity may change both the topics that participants discuss and the interactional meanings and frequencies of behaviors such as pauses, nodding, and smiling. For examplehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?EYpi0s it has been shown that friends explore broadening conversational topic spaces while strangers converge (Speer et al., 2024) , suggesting that personality too may manifest differently when participants already share interactional history. Future research should compare stranger and familiar-partner interactions to determine whether the observed personality-behavior associations are stable across relationship contexts.

Finally, although the present sample was relatively large for a dyadic interaction study, statistical power was limited for detecting small personality effects. Sensitivity analyses indicated that the study had 80% power to detect standardized actor and partner effects of approximately β = .23-.28 and trait × medium interaction effects of β = .24-.28, depending on the trait and outcome. Several of the observed personality effects were smaller than these values, suggesting that effects of comparable or smaller magnitude could have remained undetected. This is particularly important when interpreting the results for H2: although the findings indicate that trait effects did not differ substantially between face-to-face and video-mediated interaction, smaller moderating effects of communication medium cannot be ruled out. Larger dyadic samples will therefore be needed to determine whether the communication medium moderates some trait-behavior associations to a smaller degree.

Despite these boundary conditions, the present study provides a broad and methodologically rigorous examination of how personality is expressed in naturalistic conversational behavior.

By combining manually annotated turn-taking, computational analysis of conversational content, and computer vision measures of listener engagement with dyadic actor-partner analyses, the study moves beyond single behavioral indicators and examines personality expression across multiple interacting layers of conversation. Empirically, the findings identify distinct conversational fingerprints associated particularly with extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness, while demonstrating that personality can shape not only individuals' own conversational behavior but also the behavior of their interaction partners.

Finally, the absence of substantial differences in personality-behavior associations between face-to-face and video-mediated interaction suggests that these interactional expressions of personality persist across rather different communication environments.

6 Conclusions

The present findings demonstrate that personality is expressed in distinct fingerprint patterns of conversational behavior that extend beyond individuals' own actions to the behavior of their partners. Most notably, the findings point to different interactional modes through which conversational flow can be maintained. Extraversion is associated with a fast-paced, highly responsive mode characterized by rapid turn transitions and active engagement signaling, whereas openness is associated with a slower and more deliberative mode characterized by longer speaking turns, longer response latencies, and less overt engagement signaling.

Conscientiousness, in turn, is associated with nodding and with partners' development of conversational content, illustrating how personality can shape not only one's own behavior but also the way one's partner is contributing to the encounter. Thus, there may be actor and partner-linked fingerprint patterns of conversational behavior, rather than a single behavioral profile emerging from the actors' own personality traits. Importantly, the absence of substantial differences in personality-behavior associations across face-to-face and video-mediated interaction suggests that interactional fingerprints of personality survive substantial changes in the technological conditions of communication. Together, the findings provide an empirical and theoretical foundation for conceptualizing these fingerprints as a multimodal, dyadic phenomenon, shaped not only by individuals' own traits but also by those of their interaction partners.
