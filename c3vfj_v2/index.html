Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Remembering everyday events involves retaining multiple components, including the scenes that provide the spatial and contextual setting in which events occur. The medial temporal lobe (MTL) supports scene construction and complex event memory. However, less is known about its contribution to retaining the details of an event's scene over time, as scene information is typically embedded within narrative and event structures that are assessed for memory shortly after encoding. To examine scene memory retention, fifty-seven cognitively healthy older adults viewed 360-degree panoramic videos depicting real-world environments and freely recalled each scene shortly after viewing and again one week later. We tested whether individual differences in MTL volume were related to retention of scene memory and whether these relationships changed across the one-week delay. The proportion of episodic detail retrieved did not significantly change across the one-week delay, but the details recalled changed and became more participant specific, as measured by several measures of text-based similarity. Critically, relationships between MTL volume and scene memory also changed over time such that greater MTL volume was associated with being more consistent with what was originally recalled. Exploratory subregional analyses further showed that perirhinal cortex volume was robustly associated with the text-based similarity measures. These findings suggest that MTL is related to memory for the scene component of complex experience, particularly the ability to consistently recall the details of a scene over time.

INTRODUCTION

Remembering everyday events often involves retrieving a complex combination of details about the people, objects, places, and activities that comprise them. Traditional laboratory paradigms, however, typically reduce event memory stimuli to words, images, or objects in ways that may limit what they can reveal about memory for complex everyday experiences. Researchers have therefore increasingly turned to using naturalistic approaches that capture the richness of daily events (Rubin & Umanath, 2015; Zacks, 2020), including assessing memory for story narratives (Lee et al., 2020; Cohn-Sheehy et al., 2022; Delarazan et al., 2026a) and films that contain complex visuals and dialogue (Delarazan et al., 2023; Fenerci et al., 2024; Delarazan et al., 2026b), as a means to complement the foundational insights revealed by traditional laboratory experiments. The present study builds on this naturalistic approach to event memory research, with a particular focus on understanding how the medial temporal lobe (MTL) may support memory for the scenes in which complex events occur.

Scenes are an essential component of complex everyday events, serving as a spatial and contextual stage on which people, objects, and activities can be perceived and reconstructed in a coherent memory (Bar, 2004; Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Robin et al., 2018; Robin, 2019; Rubin & Umanath, 2015). A substantial body of neuropsychological and neuroimaging research implicates the MTL, particularly the hippocampus and parahippocampal cortex, in scene memory, supporting not only perception (Byrne et al., 2007; Bird and Burgess, 2008) and mental scene construction (Hassabis et al., 2006; Irish et al., 2017), but also the retrieval of scene-related details and maps (O' Keefe and Nadel, 1978; Cabeza et al., 2004). While these studies highlight a connection between the MTL and scene memory, questions remain about the nature of this brainbehavior relationship.

Specifically, an unresolved question is how the MTL supports the retention of details about complex scenes. Much of the existing literature has focused on perception and recognition paradigms that do not directly assay memory for the details of the scene (Eichenbaum et al., 2007; Lee et al., 2005; Staresina et al., 2012), or mental scene construction paradigms that do not test memory for recently encoded information (Hassabis et al., 2007; Schacter et al., 2012; Sheldon & Levine, 2016). Studies that have examined the details for recently encoded information have used films and narratives that contain sequences of events, characters, dialogue, and developing storylines (Lee & Chen, 2020; Antony et al., 2024; Delarazan et al., 2026a), which makes it difficult to determine how scene-specific information is retained independently of other components of an event, such as event order and narrative structure. Prior research using naturalistic approaches has indicated that the MTL may be an important neural network hub for multiple features of event memory. For instance, studies using films, narratives, and autobiographical memory suggest that the MTL may detect event boundaries (Reagh et al., 2020; Bailey et al., 2013), encode and retrieve a range of episodic and semantic details (Cabeza et al., 2004; Staresina et al., 2013), and combine information into a unified memory representation (Reagh & Ranganath, 2023). Studying the recall of details from recently encoded naturalistic scenes, in the absence of narratives and unfolding events, could therefore provide clarity on the role of the MTL in detailed scene retention.

Understanding the role of the MTL in scene memory for complex events also requires considering how this relationship may change over time, as the content of a memory evolves. It is well established that what is available for retrieval after several days often differs from what can be accessed immediately after an experience (Sekeres et al., 2016; Oedekoven et al., 2017). Prior research also suggests that, over time, memories do not simply become uniformly weaker (Frankland & Bontempi, 2005; Winocur & Moscovitch, 2011; Squire et al., 2015). According to trace transformation theory, the MTL has a critical role in retaining central details of event memories, even if these details, and the role of the MTL, evolve over time (Wincour & Moscovitch, 2011). Notably, a prior study using short stories found that MTL volume was associated with how much detail for the stories was retained after several days of delay (Saloner et al., 2018). Interestingly, there was no relationship between MTL volume and immediate story recall, suggesting that, over time, the importance of the MTL to the retention of detailed event narratives may become more apparent. Whether similar outcomes apply to the retention of naturalistic scene details remains unclear.

In the present study, we sought to examine how individual differences in MTL volume relates to the retention of scene details across a one-week delay. We developed a novel behavioral task where participants viewed a series of 360-degree panoramic videos depicting real-world environments and freely recalled each scene shortly after viewing and again after a one-week delay. We selected 360-degree panoramic videos because they capture naturalistic scenes without the narrative structure or unfolding sequence of events present in films and stories. We tested whether among a group of older adults, individual differences in MTL volume were associated with individual differences in scene memory, and whether these associations changed over time.

Critically, we characterized retention in complementary ways. Detail-based scoring captured the episodic composition of recall, whereas text-based similarity measures captured the consistency of remembered scene content relative to the original scene, what others recalled, and an individual's own recall across two sessions. Together, these measures allowed us to distinguish the overall composition of recall from specific scene information that remained represented across the delay. Finally, because different MTL subregions may make distinct contributions to complex event memory, we conducted exploratory analyses examining relationships with hippocampal, parahippocampal, perirhinal, and entorhinal cortex volume.

Consistent with the link between the MTL and complex event memory (Rubin, 2006; Bailey et al., 2013), along with the theorized importance of this region to scene perception and memory (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Lee et al., 2005), we hypothesized that variability in MTL volume would be associated with the retention of detailed scene memories. Given evidence that both the content of memory and its relationship with MTL can change across retention intervals (Saloner et al., 2018), we further expected that the MTL-scene memory association would evolve over the course of the one-week delay, with the MTL maintaining a relationship with the retention of scene memory at delayed retrieval.

METHODS

Transparency and Openness

Primary data, demographics, neuropsychological tests, and analysis scripts are publicly available on https://osf.io/c5pxq/overview and https://github.com/aidelarazan/scene360recall. Publicly shared data use generic participant labels rather than study IDs. No aspect of the experiment was preregistered.

Participants

Participants were recruited from the Tucson, AZ community, through The Arizona Alzheimer's Prevention Registry and department advertisements. Participants were eligible if they were between 50 and 89 years of age. Exclusion screening criteria included low proficiency in English, a history of neurological disorder, a history of moderate/severe head injury, performance below normative cutoffs on neuropsychological tests using a profile-based approach (Bondi et al., 2014), and safety concerns during their MRI screening (e.g., magnetic bodily implants, claustrophobia, etc.).

Sixty-seven participants were initially enrolled. Based on an a priori exclusion criterion requiring recall of at least six of the eight scenes, ten participants were excluded. Our final sample included 57 older adults (M = 68.77, SD = 6.11, Range = 60-81, 35 Female). Participants selfidentified as White (n = 53), Black/African American (n = 2), Asian (n = 1), and Hispanic/Latino (n = 1).

Participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Institutional Review Board of the University of Arizona, Protocol ID #1607703006. Participants were compensated $10 per hour, with an additional $5 for travel expenses if testing occurred in-person. Participants received an additional $40 for completing the MRI scan. If any sessions were completed virtually, compensation was provided in the form of a Visa, Amazon, or Walmart gift card.

Procedure

Participants completed a total of three to four study visits as part of the broader study. The present analyses focused on data collected during two sessions that were one-week apart. Some study visits were conducted remotely via Zoom for Health due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Neuropsychological Tests.

Participants completed a battery of neuropsychological tasks to test for cognitive impairment (Table 1). These tasks included the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Fourth Edition (WAIS-IV; Wechsler, 2009) Vocabulary and Similarities to assess verbal comprehension; California Verbal Learning Test, Second Edition (CVLT-II; Delis et al., 2000) to assess episodic verbal learning and memory; Rey Complex Figure copy and recall conditions (Rey, 1941) to assess visuospatial skills and memory; Trail Making Test Part A and B (Reitan & Wolfson, 1985) to assess speed of information processing and executive functions; WAIS-IV Block Design (Wechsler, 2009) to assess visuospatial skills; and Animal Fluency (Benton, 1968) and Boston Naming Task (Goodglass et al., 2001) to assess language.

Encoding. Participants viewed eight 40-second panoramic videos depicting a variety of real-world environments (e.g., a hotel lobby, stadium, distillery, and coastal setting) along with unique titles (Fig. 1A) and were instructed to remember the scenes for a later memory test. Each video was created from a static 360° panoramic image obtained from Google Street View and other publicly posted advertisements. For experimental purposes, each clip was standardized such that the viewpoint panned continuously around the panoramic scene, allowing participants to see different portions of the environment while the people, objects, and other elements within the scene remained static. Thus, changes over the course of each video reflected movement of the viewpoint through the panorama rather than changes occurring within the depicted environment.

Immediate Recall. Participants were cued with the title of each scene, presented in random order, and instructed to picture the scene in their mind and tell the experimenter everything they remember about the scene (Fig. 1B). Participants were provided a maximum time of four minutes and probed to continue if they stopped recalling before two minutes had elapsed.

Delayed Recall.

One week later, participants completed the same recall procedure as Immediate Recall. Scenes were presented in random order, and participants were cued with the same title and instructed to picture the scene in their mind and tell the experimenter everything they remember about the scene. Participants were given a maximum time of four minutes and probed to continue if they stopped recalling before two minutes had elapsed.

MRI Acquisition.

Participants completed an MRI screening form prior to scanning to confirm safety. Imaging was conducted at the Brain & Body Imaging Center at the University of Arizona on a Siemens 3T Skyra (syngo MR E11) with a 32-channel head coil. Participants were given MRI-compatible foam earbuds and additional pads were placed by their ears to protect hearing and further reduce motion. Participants were also given a foam cushion for under their knees, blankets, and MRI safe glasses as needed. Participants were instructed to hold onto a squeeze ball and squeeze it if they needed to be removed from the MRI immediately.

A T1-weighted 3D MPRAGE anatomical image was acquired in the sagittal plane (TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.95 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, matrix = 256 × 240, 176 slices, voxel size = 1.0 × 1.055 × 1.055 mm, slice thickness = 1 mm, pixel bandwidth = 240 Hz/pixel, phase FOV = 93.75%). Resting-state and task-based functional runs were also acquired as part of the broader study protocol but were not analyzed here.

Recall Measures

Our goal was to characterize both the overall episodic composition of recall and the specific scene content that remained represented across a one-week delay. We used detail-based scoring (Levine et al., 2002; Fig 1C) to characterize the composition of recalled content and used textbased similarity measures (Cer et al., 2018; Fig. 1D) to characterize the consistency of scene recall relative to the original scene, to what other participants recalled, and to an individual's own recall across sessions.

For the text-based similarity measures, we used the Universal Sentence Encoder (USE; Cer et al., 2018) to quantify the semantic content of participants' scene descriptions. Each scene recall was converted into 512-dimensional embedding vectors, and cosine similarity was used to quantify the similarity between two pairs of descriptions. We used these embeddings to derive three complementary measures of the consistency of remembered scene content: Description Similarity, Group Similarity, and Group Consistency.

Internal Detail Proportion.

We used the Autobiographical Interview scoring protocol (Levine et al., 2002) to characterize the internal and external details produced during recall. For each trial, the transcript was segmented into individual details, then tagged by detail type. Internal Details included event, time, place, perceptual, and thought/emotion details whereas External Details included semantic information, details external to the target scene, and metacognitive statements. We additionally included a False Memory detail type for details describing information that was clearly not a part of the scene. All raters completed a standardized training procedure using a training set before scoring study data and achieved excellent reliability for internal and external details (Cronbach's alpha > .90). For trials scored by two raters, the number of details within each detail type was averaged across raters, and these averaged values were used in subsequent analyses. Although all detail types were coded (Table 2), the present analyses focused on Internal Detail Proportion, calculated by dividing the number of Internal Details by the total number of details produced (Fig. 1C). This measure characterized the episodic recall composition of recall, with higher Internal Detail proportion values indicating a greater proportion of the participant's recall consisted of Internal Details.

Description Similarity.

To quantify the consistency of recalled scene content with the original scene, we compared each recall description with reference descriptions generated by OpenAI GPT-5.6 Sol and Anthropic Claude Opus 5 (Fig. 1E). Similarity was calculated separately for each reference description and then averaged across AI-models. Higher Description Similarity reflected greater semantic correspondence between a participant's recall and the AI-generated reference descriptions of the original scenes.

Consensus Similarity.

To characterize the consistency of recalled scene content across individuals, we calculated the pairwise similarity between each participant's recall description and the descriptions produced by the remaining participants for that same scene and same recall session (Fig. 1F). Thus, immediate recall was compared with other participants' immediate recall, and delayed recall was compared with other participants' delayed recall. Pairwise similarity values were then averaged to obtain a Consensus Similarity score, where higher values indicated recall that was more similar to that of the larger group.

Self-Consistency.

Finally, we examined the consistently of an individual's recalled scene content across the one-week delay by calculating the similarity between their Session 1 and Session 2 descriptions of the same scene (Fig. 1G). Higher values indicated greater consistency in recall across sessions.

Volumetric Measures

Regions of Interest. MTL volumes were derived using Automatic Segmentation of Hippocampal Subfields (ASHS; Yushkevich et al., 2015), a multi-atlas segmentation approach that registers manually labeled atlas images to each participant's anatomical image and combines the transformed labels via joint label fusion to derive participant-specific segmentations. We used the ASHS-PMC-T1 atlas (Xie et al., 2019), developed for 3T T1-weighted imaging, which includes explicit modeling of the dura to improve segmentation of medial temporal lobe cortical regions. This atlas yields labels for anterior and posterior hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, Brodmann areas 35 and 36, and parahippocampal cortex. Hippocampal volume was computed as the sum of anterior and posterior hippocampus, and perirhinal volume as the sum of BA35 and BA36. Total intracranial volume was estimated by ASHS. Volumes were taken from the "corr_nogray" output in native T1-weighted space.

Quality Control.

All ASHS segmentations were visually inspected in ITK-SNAP by two independent raters (A.I.D. and A.N.G.), with each participant's T1-weighted image loaded as the main image and the multi-label segmentation as an overlay. Each hemisphere was rated as Pass, Check, or Fail. Fail indicated gross registration failure or mislabeling severe enough to render regional volumes uninterpretable, and Check indicated minor boundary imprecision judged unlikely to substantially affect volumes. No segmentations were rated as Fail, and no manual editing was necessary.

MTL Composite.

To derive a composite measure of medial temporal lobe (MTL) volume, volumes for the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, perirhinal cortex, and parahippocampal cortex were summed across hemispheres and standardized across participants. Following similar approaches used in prior work (Bailey et al., 2013; Pettigrew et al., 2017), each regional volume was standardized across participants and separately adjusted for intracranial volume (ICV) by regressing the standardized volume on ICV and retaining the residual. The ICV-adjusted volumes were averaged across the four regions to produce a single MTL composite, with higher values reflecting relatively greater MTL volume after accounting for ICV.

RESULTS

Scene recall became longer and less similar across the one-week delay

We first examined how recall changed across the one-week delay. To do so, we fit separate linear mixed-effects models for each session-varying recall measure, examining the effect of session while controlling for age (mean-centered), with random intercepts for participant and scene. Overall, features of scene recall showed distinct patterns of change across the one-week delay.

Internal Detail Proportion did not significantly differ between immediate and delayed recall (b = -0.014, SE = 0.008, t(838.15) = -1.72, 95% CI [-0.030, 0.002], p = .086; Fig. 2). Description Similarity, however, decreased across sessions, indicating that delayed recall was less similar to AI-generated descriptions of the original scenes (b = -0.011, SE = 0.004, t(838.16) = -2.43, 95% CI [-0.019, -0.002], p = .015; Fig. 2). Consensus Similarity also decreased after the one-week delay (b = -0.024, SE = 0.003, t(838.46) = -7.20, 95% CI [-0.031, -0.018], p < .001; Fig. 2), suggesting that participants' delayed descriptions were less similar to those produced by other participants relative to their similarity at immediate recall. In contrast, participants produced more words after the one-week delay (b = 50.96, SE = 7.63, t(837.06) = 6.68, 95% CI [36.00, 65.93], p < .001).

Importantly, age was not significantly associated with Internal Detail Proportion (b = -0.005, SE = 0.003, t(55.03) = -1.56, p = .124) or Description Similarity (b = -0.002, SE = 0.001, t(55.02) = -1.73, p = .089), though older age was associated with lower Consensus Similarity (b = -0.0014, SE = 0.0006, t(55.15) = -2.27, p = .027).

MTL-memory relationships changed after the one-week delay

We next examined whether individual differences in MTL volume were related to scene memory and whether these relationships changed across the one-week delay. Separate linear mixed-effects model included MTL volume, session, and their interaction while controlling for age (mean-centered), with random intercepts for participant and scene for each of our dependent variables including Description Similarity, Group Similarity, Internal Detail Proportion, and Self-Consistency. Overall, we found that the relationship between MTL volume and memory differed across memory measures and varied over a one-week delay.

Relationship between MTL volume and Internal Detail Proportion weakened across the delay.

There was no significant main effect of MTL volume (b = 0.035, SE = 0.021, t(59.21) = 1.67, p = .100) or Session (b = -0.013, SE = 0.008, t(837.14) = -1.57, p = .117). However, there was a significant interaction between MTL volume and Internal Detail Proportion (b = -0.026, p = .003; Fig. 3), indicating that the relationship between MTL volume and Internal Detail Proportion weakened across the delay. However, MTL volume was not significantly associated with Internal Detail Proportion at immediate recall (b = 0.035, 95% CI [-0.007, 0.078], p = .100) or at delayed recall (b = 0.009, 95% CI [-0.033, 0.052], p = .657).

Description Similarity and Consensus Similarity were associated with MTL volume after the one-week delay.

For Description Similarity, there was no significant main effect of MTL volume (b = 0.012, SE = 0.009, t(63.27) = 1.34, p = .184). There was, however, a significant main effect of Session (b = -0.011, SE = 0.004, t(837.15) = -2.57, p = .010), reflecting lower Description Similarity after the one-week delay. There was also a significant interaction between MTL volume and Description Similarity (b = 0.011, SE = 0.005, t(837.27) = 2.37, p = .018; Fig. 3). MTL volume was not significantly associated with Description Similarity at immediate recall (b = 0.012, 95% CI [-0.006, 0.029], p = .184), but greater MTL volume was associated with higher Description Similarity after the one-week delay (b = 0.023, 95% CI [0.006, 0.041], p = .011), suggesting that individuals with better retention of the original scenes tended to have greater MTL volume.

Consensus Similarity showed a comparable pattern. There was no significant main effect of MTL volume (b = 0.006, SE = 0.005, t(76.60) = 1.36, p = .179), whereas the main effect of Session was significant (b = -0.024, SE = 0.003, t(837.50) = -7.32, p < .001), reflecting lower Consensus Similarity after one week. The MTL × Session interaction, while not significant, was exactly at the alpha significance threshold (b = 0.007, SE = 0.004, t(837.77) = 1.96, p = .050; Fig. 3). Aligning with this result, MTL volume was not significantly associated with Consensus similarity at immediate recall (b = 0.006, 95% CI [-0.003, 0.015], p = .179), whereas greater MTL volume was associated with higher Consensus Similarity after one week (b = 0.013, 95% CI [0.004, 0.022], p = .005). This indicates that individuals who tended to retain a normative description of the scenes tended to have larger MTL volume.

Greater MTL volume was associated with more consistent recall across sessions. We next asked whether MTL volume was related to the consistency of each participant's recall across the two sessions. Given that Self-Consistency was calculated by comparing immediate and delayed recall, this model did not include session as a predictor. We found greater MTL volume was associated with higher Self-Consistency (b = 0.024, SE = 0.008, t(55.38) = 2.96, p = .005; Fig. 3), such that participants with larger MTL volumes produced more similar descriptions of the same scene across the one-week interval.

MTL volume was unrelated to words recalled.

Although word count was not a primary measure of interest, we also examined the number of words produced during recall to assess whether the observed relationships might reflect differences in verbal output rather than remembered scene content. There was no significant main effect of MTL volume on Word Count (b = 9.37, SE = 30.40, t(56.18) = 0.31, p = .759). The main effect of Session was significant (b = 51.47, SE = 7.64, t(836.05) = 6.74, p < .001), reflecting greater verbal output after the one-week delay. The MTL Volume × Session interaction was not significant (b = -10.85, SE = 8.19, t(836.11) = -1.32, p = .186; Fig. 3). Lastly, age was not significantly associated with any measure in these models (Internal Detail Proportion, p = .352; Description Similarity, p = .477; Consensus Similarity, p = .266; Self-Consistency, p = .529; Word Count, p = .501).

Distinct MTL subregions tracked different features of scene memory

To determine whether the relationships observed for the MTL composite were associated with specific subregions, we next examined the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, perirhinal cortex, and parahippocampal cortex separately. For each dependent variable, we fit linear mixed-effects models including subregion volume, session, and their interaction while controlling for age, with random intercepts for participant and scene. Given that these analyses were exploratory, p-values for the Subregion Volume × Session interaction were FDR-corrected (q < .05) across the four subregions within each dependent measure. For Self-Consistency, which was calculated across sessions, FDR correction was applied to the main effect of subregion volume. Overall, the exploratory analyses showed that the MTL composite relationships were not uniform across regions.

Relationship between entorhinal volume and Internal Detail Proportion changed across sessions.

There was no significant overall main effect of entorhinal volume (b = -0.008, SE = 0.018, t(54.10) = -0.43, p = .673) or Session (b = -0.014, SE = 0.008, t(837) = -1.73, p = .083) for Internal Detail Proportion. However, the Entorhinal Volume × Session interaction was significant (b = -0.030, SE = 0.008, t(837) = -3.69, p < .001, q = .001), reflecting a change in the relationship across the one-week delay. However, entorhinal volume was not significantly associated with Internal Detail Proportion during either immediate recall (b = 0.007, 95% CI [-0.030, 0.044], p = .701) or delayed recall (b = -0.023, 95% CI [-0.060, 0.015], p = .229). Thus, the interaction effect appears to reflect a change in association across sessions rather than a significant relationship at either session alone. This pattern did not extend to the similarity-based measures. Entorhinal volume was not significantly associated with Description Similarity (b = 0.001, SE = 0.008, t(54.10) = 0.11, p = .917, q = .917) or Consensus Similarity (b = 0.002, SE = 0.004, t(54.30) = 0.63, p = .534, q = .534), and did not show significant Session interactions for Description Similarity (b = -0.001, SE = 0.004, t(837) = -0.13, p = .894, q = .894) or Consensus Similarity (b = 0.002, SE = 0.003, t(838) = 0.59, p = .555, q = .740). Description Similarity and Consensus Similarity nevertheless decreased across sessions (ps = .015 and < .001, respectively). Entorhinal volume was also not significantly associated with Self-Consistency (b = 0.007, SE = 0.008, t(54.8) = 0.90, p = .370, q = .376).

Perirhinal volume was related to similarity-based measures after the one-week delay.

Perirhinal cortex showed the most consistent pattern across the similarity-based measures. For Description Similarity, the overall main effect of perirhinal volume did not survive FDR correction (b = 0.017, SE = 0.007, t(54.00) = 2.50, p = .015, q = .062), whereas the main effect of Session was significant (b = -0.011, SE = 0.004, t(837) = -2.44, p = .015). The relationship with perirhinal volume differed across sessions (b = 0.012, SE = 0.004, t(837) = 2.77, p = .006, q = .023).

Perirhinal volume was not significantly associated with Description Similarity during immediate recall (b = 0.011, 95% CI [-0.003, 0.026], p = .123), whereas greater volume was associated with higher Description Similarity after the one-week delay (b = 0.023, 95% CI [0.009, 0.037], p = .002).

Consensus Similarity showed a similar pattern. The overall main effect of perirhinal volume did not survive FDR correction (b = 0.007, SE = 0.003, t(54.10) = 2.12, p = .039, q = .136), whereas the main effect of Session was significant (b = -0.024, SE = 0.003, t(838) = -7.23, p < .001). The Perirhinal Volume × Session interaction was significant (b = 0.008, SE = 0.003, t(837) = 2.58, p = .010, q = .040). Perirhinal volume was not significantly associated with Consensus Similarity during immediate recall (b = 0.003, 95% CI [-0.004, 0.011], p = .409), but greater perirhinal volume was associated with higher Consensus Similarity at delayed recall (b = 0.012, 95% CI [0.004, 0.019], p = .004).

Perirhinal volume was also associated with Self-Consistency (b = 0.024, p = .0008, q = .003), such that participants with greater volume produced more similar descriptions of the same scene across sessions. In contrast, perirhinal volume did not show significant interaction with session for Internal Detail Proportion (b = -0.003, SE = 0.008, t(837) = -0.32, p = .753, q = .753).

Hippocampal effects did not survive correction.

None of the Hippocampal Volume × Session interactions survived FDR correction. The interactions for Description Similarity (b = 0.011, SE = 0.005, t(837) = 2.16, p = .031, q = .062) and Consensus Similarity (b = 0.008, SE = 0.004, t(838) = 2.05, p = .040, q = .081) were significant prior to correction but did not remain significant after accounting for multiple comparisons. The interaction for Internal Detail Proportion was also not significant (b = -0.009, SE = 0.009, t(837) = -1.00, p = .317, q = .423).

Main effects of hippocampal volume likewise did not survive FDR correction for Internal Detail

Proportion (b = 0.039, SE = 0.021, t(54.1) = 1.89, p = .065, q = .258), Description Similarity (b = 0.012, SE = 0.009, t(54.1) = 1.31, p = .196, q = .392), or Consensus Similarity (b = 0.008, SE = 0.004, t(54.2) = 1.86, p = .068, q = .136). Hippocampal volume was also not significantly associated with Self-Consistency (b = 0.012, SE = 0.009, t(54.4) = 1.32, p = .192, q = .376).

Parahippocampal volume was not significantly related to the scene-memory measures after correction.

There were no significant main effects of parahippocampal volume for Internal Detail Proportion (b = 0.014, SE = 0.019, t(54.1) = 0.76, p = .453, q = .673), Description Similarity (b = 0.006, SE = 0.008, t(54.1) = 0.82, p = .415, q = .554), or Consensus Similarity (b = 0.003, SE = 0.004, t(54.2) = 0.72, p = .478, q = .534). The Parahippocampal Volume × Session interaction for Internal Detail Proportion was significant prior to correction but did not survive FDR correction (b = -0.018, SE = 0.008, t(837) = -2.22, p = .026, q = .053). The Volume × Session interactions were also not significant for Description Similarity (b = 0.005, SE = 0.004, t(837) = 1.14, p = .254, q = .339) or Consensus Similarity (b = -0.001, SE = 0.003, t(838) = -0.29, p = .776, q = .776).

Parahippocampal volume was also not significantly associated with Self-Consistency (b = 0.007, SE = 0.008, t(54.5) = 0.89, p = .376, q = .376).

DISCUSSION

Scenes are thought to provide the spatial and contextual framework in which everyday events occur, but in naturalistic experiences, they are embedded within other information that unfolds across the event. Although the MTL has been implicated in reconstructing complex events, less is known about how the MTL relates to the retention of detailed scene information over time.

To address this question, we isolated scene memory using 360-degree panoramic videos of realworld environments that participants recalled shortly after viewing and again one week later.

Consistent with our hypothesis, individual differences in MTL volume were associated with multiple features of scene memory, with this relationship emerging only after a one-week delay.

Together, these findings provide evidence that the MTL may play a role in retaining the details of complex scenes over time, ensuring that the stage upon which event memories unfold is set for an elaborative reconstruction of the past.

Across the one-week delay, scene recall changed in several ways. Participants produced more words, and their descriptions became less similar to descriptions of the original scenes, as well as to those produced by other participants. Interestingly, the proportion of internal or episodic details did not significantly change across the delay. This dissociation suggests that the overall episodic composition of recall remained relatively stable even as the specific scene information being retained changed over the week. Prior work similarly shows that naturalistic memories are selectively retained, with central information remaining relatively stable while peripheral details are more likely to be lost over time (Sekeres et al., 2016). Our findings extend this work to scenes, where there is no unfolding event structure that defines which information is central or peripheral.

Although individuals may have imposed their own organizational strategies during intentional encoding, such self-generated structure would likely vary across individuals, leaving greater room for individuals to focus on different features of the same environment and, over time, to retain different details of the same scene.

The selective changes in retained scene content were accompanied by changes in its relationship with MTL. At immediate recall, MTL volume was not reliably associated with how closely recall corresponded to the original scenes or to what others recalled. In contrast, greater MTL volume was associated with both features of scene memory after one week and with greater self-consistency across sessions. Thus, individual differences in MTL became particularly informative about the scene content that remained represented after the delay, whether retention was assessed relative to the original scene, to what other participants recalled, or to an individual's own earlier memory. This pattern is consistent with prior work indicating that MTL volume is associated with memory for short stories after one week but not at shorter delays (Saloner et al., 2018). The present findings extend this work to naturalistic scenes that were viewed once and later freely reconstructed from memory, showing that this memory retention relationship with MTL structure is also evident for the scene component of an experience in the absence of an unfolding narrative.

Importantly, the emergence of MTL-memory relationships after a delay may reflect changes in the memory representation rather than changes in whether the MTL supports memory.

Immediately after encoding, many features of a scene may remain accessible through non-MTL specific cognitive processes (e.g., mental rehearsal), and individual differences in structural volume of the MTL may therefore provide relatively little leverage for distinguishing among individuals. Across the week, however, as fewer features remain available and memories becomes more selective, stable individual differences in MTL volume may become increasingly informative about which scene details are retained and available for later reconstruction. This interpretation is broadly compatible with memory models proposing that memory representations are reorganized or transformed over time (Winocur & Moscovitch, 2011; Squire et al., 2015), although the present findings cannot distinguish among specific memory accounts.

Although the MTL volumetric composite provided the primary evidence for these delayed relationships, prior models suggest that the MTL's constituent regions contribute differently to the information that makes up a remembered scene. According to these models, the hippocampus, perirhinal cortex, entorhinal cortex, and parahippocampal cortex participate in partially distinct but interacting memory pathways, with different regions contributing more strongly to item information, contextual information, and the relationships among them (Davachi, 2006; Diana et al., 2007; Ranganath & Ritchey, 2012). Because scene memory draws on each of these features, the composite MTL finding may reflect contributions distributed across this broader system rather than a process localized to a single neural region with in the MTL. Exploratory regional analyses provided some support for this possibility. Hippocampal effects for the similarity measures were in a similar direction as the composite findings but did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. Entorhinal cortex showed a session-dependent relationship with episodic detail composition, whereas parahippocampal cortex showed no reliable relationships with the scenememory measures.

Within this broader regional pattern, perirhinal cortex showed the clearest relationship with scene memory, with greater volume associated with retention of scene content relative to the original scenes, what others recalled, and an individual's own earlier memory. One possible account of this pattern follows from the broader organization of MTL memory systems, in which perirhinal cortex is especially important for representing specific objects or items, while other MTL regions contribute more strongly to contextual and relational information (Cohen and Eichenbaum, 1993). Although scenes may have a context-setting quality to them, that context may be inherently supported by the objects or items that are present. Our similarity measures therefore may have been particularly sensitive to details about the objects or items retained over the delay, and this emphasis may partly explain the stronger relationship with perirhinal cortex (Bussey et al., 2002; Bussey et al., 2005; Clarke & Tyler, 2014; Frick et al., 2023). These analyses were exploratory, however, and the prominence of perirhinal cortex should be interpreted as one possible clue to the type of scene information contributing to the broader MTL effect rather than as evidence that scene memory depends primarily on this region.

The older adult sample provides an important context for these findings, but the present study cannot determine whether the observed relationships are specific to cognitive and brain aging. Perirhinal cortex and other MTL regions are vulnerable to age-related change and are affected early in the course of Alzheimer's disease pathology (Braak & Braak, 1991 , 1995; Berron et al., 2020). This makes individual differences in MTL structure particularly relevant in older adults. At the same time, our participants were cognitively healthy, age itself did not account for our brain-behavior relationships, and we did not include a younger adult comparison group. We therefore cannot determine whether the same relationships between MTL structure and scene memory would also be observed earlier in adulthood or whether they become more pronounced with age-related neural change. Future work comparing age groups and incorporating markers of Alzheimer's disease pathology could help distinguish general features of scene memory from relationships that may become important in aging.

The use of an immediate recall also may have influenced the results of this study. One process that may reinforce individualized scene memory is retrieval itself, as retrieval can strengthen recovered information while also modifying what is subsequently remembered (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Bridge & Paller, 2012). Given that participants recalled each scene shortly after encoding, initial retrieval may have sharpened which details remained available one week later. The relatively high within-individual consistency across sessions aligns with this possibility. That is, although participants became less similar to one another, they tended to reconstruct the same scene in a largely reliable manner. However, without a condition where there is no initial, immediate retrieval, we cannot separate effects of prior retrieval from other changes across the retention interval. Future studies that manipulate whether an initial recall occurs could test whether early retrieval stabilizes an individualized representation of the scene and, in turn, shapes which scene details are retained at a later recall.

Several additional limitations point to important directions for future work. First, volumetric measures characterize individual differences in MTL structure but cannot reveal how these regions are engaged during memory processing. Functional imaging could complement these findings by examining MTL activity while encoding and retrieving complex, naturalistic scenes over time. Second, although our similarity-based approach provides a scalable way to characterize naturalistic recall without extensive manual scoring, correspondence to the original scene was in part based on AI-generated reference descriptions. Although these descriptions were reviewed by experimenters, they may not fully capture the information available in a complex visual scene.

Notably, we supplemented this measure with comparisons to other participants' recall and to each participant's own recall across sessions, providing complementary measures of consistency with which scene content was retained. Finally, the central strength of the present design is also an important limitation. That is, we intentionally removed the scene from the broader event in which scenes are normally experienced. Isolating scenes allowed us to identify a relationship between MTL structure and scene memory without the confounding influence of character building, actions, dialogue, and narrative structure, but everyday scenes typically form part of richer unfolding experiences. Future work should determine how memory for the scene interacts with memory for the people, actions, and events that occur within it.

In summary, these findings demonstrate that the MTL is related to the retention of detailed scene information and that this relationship changes as scene memories become more selective over time. Across one week, scene memories became less similar to the original scenes and to what other people remembered, even though the overall proportion of episodic content remained relatively stable. Individual differences in MTL volume became particularly informative about which details remained represented and how consistently events were reconstructed after the delay.

Our findings provide a foundation for understanding how the places in which experiences occur are selectively retained and reconstructed as part of the complex events we later remember.
