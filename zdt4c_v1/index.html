Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

The investigation of visual time perception has uncovered a large variety of phenomena, but how these phenomena are related to each other is unclear. The ambition of the present review is to propose a principled way to class visual time phenomena into four clusters. We suggest that these four clusters result from four ultimate goals that the visual system is attempting to solve to stay well connected to our dynamic environment. These goals are to link successive events into an apparent continuous flow, to anticipate when forthcoming events will occur, to integrate sensory information to consolidate a sufficiently stable percept at each moment in time, and to hold on a perceived present so that it can be used to label events on a timeline. We then review twelve classic visual time phenomena and assign them to one of the four clusters. These twelve phenomena include four basic ones: temporal segmentation, temporal order, temporal integration, and subjective simultaneity. More complex phenomena include temporal continuity, the passage of time, perceived duration, anticipation, backward masking, postdiction, specious present, and perceived nowness. For each of the twelve phenomena, we also present the main methodologies that have been used to investigate them. Ultimately, identifying similarities and differences between visual time phenomena will advance our efforts to create a taxonomy of these phenomena.

1. Introduction

Visual time perception plays a major role in structuring our interaction with the external world. Some successive visual events are linked together to form an apparent continuous flow, while other events are kept isolated and determine a causal chain. Visual time perception is rich of a large variety of phenomena, some very basic like integration and segmentation, and others much more complex.

Yet, the links between these phenomena are unclear. Moreover, the literature in experimental psychology, integrative neuroscience and computational modelling is cluttered with concepts that are often not very well defined, and terms that have different meanings for different authors. As Meck & Ivry (2016) wrote in their introduction to a large collection of papers on time perception and action, "a modern 'taxonomy of time' is very much needed in order to meet the challenge of this inter-disciplinary field where researchers coming from different disciplines tend to invoke different terminology, use different experimental approaches, and sometimes focus on distinct questions within a specific context. " (p. viii). Similarly, in their recent review, Coull & Giersch (2022) state that "The number of studies focusing on timing and time perception has exploded in the last two decades, so precisely defined terminology will be essential to move the field forwards." (p. 257). The purpose of the present review is to select some classic visual time phenomena, to clarify what they denote by presenting how they are measured, and to discuss how they are potentially related to each other. It is hoped that once the links between phenomena are established, a taxonomy will naturally emerge. Our review of phenomena is not exhaustive, but we expect that the missing ones will effortlessly find their place among the ones presented here.

To make the classification of perceptual temporal phenomena manageable, we restrict ourselves to time intervals between tens of milliseconds and a few seconds. Even though timing tasks based on some interval durations are very popular (Grondin, 2010), we also consider a wide variety of phenomena other than perceived duration. Since we concentrate on vision, we leave out temporal phenomena that focus on other senses, and those that involve the interaction between vision and other senses. We mostly leave out the very important motor timing tasks (Kock et al., 2021; Paton & Buonomano, 2018), and we minimize as much as possible the interactions between time and space (Coull & Droit-Volet, 2018). Finally, we only discuss here the behavioural aspects of the phenomena, not the computational models that could explain these phenomena, nor the neural mechanisms that could implement these models.

One difficulty in studying visual time phenomena comes from the temptation to make direct analogies between perceived time and physical time. To take an example, imagine a basketball player who is throwing the ball through the hoop of the opposing team (Figure 1). The ball flies over the court, approaches the hoop, crosses the rim, touches the net, and falls on the ground. The whole sequence took a couple of hundreds of milliseconds, but what we have experienced was a single event, a successful throw. Therefore, it is critically important to distinguish a succession of physical events from the experience of that succession. As William James (1890) famously wrote, "a succession of feelings, in and of itself, is not a feeling of succession" (p. 628). Our subjective experience of the successful throw is not necessarily lasting as long as the throw itself. It has its own duration that can be very brief because the game goes on and we now pay attention at the opposing team that is already engaged in the next action, or it can last a long time because we are savouring this moment of the game. The important distinction between "perceived succession" and "perceived instantaneity" is too easily overlooked. Perceived succession is based on a temporal sequence of multiple perceived elements. It involves the ability to perceptually resolve each element and to build a link between them. In contrast, perceived instantaneity refers to a single perceptual instant, but this instant can involve one or multiple physical elements. To illustrate this latter point, consider a light that flickers at a rate of 20Hz for a quarter of a second. Each flash may be clearly identifiable (they may have different colours), but when they are very brief and follow each other rapidly, it is difficult to clearly perceive their temporal ordering. In this case, we have experienced an event that felt instantaneous (a briefly flickering light), but that single perceived event contained multiple elements (about five flashes).

The distinction between perceived succession and perceived instantaneity may reflect the outcome of some temporal goals that the visual system has to solve. Presumably, these goals help us stay well connected with our dynamic environment, allowing us to make sense of the rapidly changing flow of visual stimulations. We posit here four ultimate temporal goals that are arguably functionally important for the organism. We propose that the first ultimate goal is to link successive events into an apparent continuous flow. Resolving this goal helps us estimate the passage of time, that is whether at any particular time, we either feel that time flies too quickly for us to complete a task, or instead too slowly to experience anything but boredom. The second ultimate goal is to anticipate when forthcoming events will occur. These anticipations can benefit, for instance, from a discovered regularity in the experienced sequence of events. The third ultimate goal is to integrate sensory information to consolidate a stable percept at each moment in time. This consolidation may necessitate to update the content of the current percept with future information, a phenomenon known as "postdiction". Finally, the fourth ultimate goal is to hold on a perceived present so that it can be used to label events on a timeline. Because the perceived present can stretch in physical time, this raises the fundamental question of "when is now?". The first two goals are linked to perceived succession, determining how fast successive events occur and when future ones will occur. In contrast, the last two goals are linked to perceived instantaneity, determining what we perceive and when we perceive it. We will use these four ultimate goals to classify twelve classic visual time phenomena into four clusters.

The distinction of visual time phenomena described here differs from previous ones. A popular organization of temporal perception is in terms of different time scales, from milliseconds to seconds and beyond (Mauk & Buonomano, 2004). A related, but more subtle classification is in terms of three scales of duration (Varela, 1999), namely elementary events, large-scale integration, and the level of descriptive-narrative assessments. The distinction of different time scales constrains the mechanisms that can explain the phenomena (in particular whether they require a memory or not), but it is less critical to define different classes of phenomena. Another popular organization is in terms of levels of processing in the hierarchy of the visual system, from the retina to early visual cortical areas to multimodal cortical areas (Merchant & de Lafuente, 2014). This hierarchy is important to isolate the neural implementations of the mechanisms underlying each temporal category, but less crucial for a classification at the behavioural level. Finally, a popular classification was provided by Fraisse (1984), who divided perceived time into two main concepts, succession and duration. To anticipate our own classification, Fraisse's notion of succession better corresponds to the "temporal segmentation" phenomenon discussed below, and thus is not one of the ultimate goals that we have identified for the visual system. Likewise, duration helps the visual system to better anticipate the future, but may not be an ultimate goal in itself.

To avoid misunderstandings over the visual time phenomena discussed here, we try to define each of them with some precision. Some confusions may arise from misuse of common language, for instance "duration" that sometimes refers implicitly to the "passage of time" when one says "I waited for you for a long time". Other confusions result from the name of some paradigms having different meanings in different communities, such as for instance the "temporal bisection" task discussed below. On top of the definition that we provide for each phenomenon, we also detail at least one behavioural method to measure each one. Numerous other methods exist that are not reviewed here, but we have made an effort to select methods that are currently believed to be precise and robust.

We hope that the present classification will be useful for any general theory of visual time perception that ambitions to explain more than one phenomenon. Ultimately, this classification should help us define a taxonomy of temporal phenomena where a hierarchy between phenomena could be established in terms of their complexity. Such a taxonomy will be helpful to study subgroups of participants, including the aging population (Balci et al., 2009), and some clinical populations that present some temporal deficits (Hinault et al., 2023), in particular individuals with schizophrenia (Coull & Giersch, 2022). We now proceed with our selection of twelve phenomena that we present in relation to one of the four posited ultimate goals.

2. Linking successive events into an apparent continuous flow

To perceive a succession of events, these events first need to be segmented in time. Temporal segmentation is a fundamental mechanism to process the rapid flow of visual information. Once events are perceptually segmented, a link can be established between them to create a temporal continuity, and the experienced flow of this continuity is known as the "passage of time".

2.1. Temporal segmentation

Temporal segmentation is the ability to see two rapidly successive events as two rather than one.

Being able to separate events in time defines the temporal resolution of the visual system. This resolution is limited by biophysical constraints that impose a lower bound on the temporal separation between two events (see also below the section on "temporal integration").

To measure how well the visual system processes temporal segmentation is to measure how fast successive stimuli can be presented and still be perceived as individual events. A classical measure of this kind is the critical fusion frequency (CFF). This critical frequency is the maximum alternation rate between two events, where each event is typically an impulse of light, for which the observer still sees two events rather than one. A common way to measure this critical fusion frequency is to use a two-interval two-alternative forced choice paradigm with the method of constant stimuli (a classical psychophysical procedure; see e.g. Kingdom & Prins, 2016). One experimental interval contains a flickering stimulus at some test frequency and the other interval contains a standard stimulus showing continuous light (sometimes the continuous stimulus is replaced by a very high flickering stimulus, e.g. 120 Hz, so as to equate the mean luminance of the flickering stimulus; Eisen-Enosh et al., 2017). Over a block of trials, the test frequency is varied and each frequency is presented the same number of times. The task of the participant is to choose the interval that contains the stimulus that flickers. From these data, the experimenter builds a psychometric function that plots the fraction of time that the participant correctly chose the experimental interval that contained the test stimulus against the temporal frequency of the test stimulus (Figure 2a). The CFF is the test frequency that leads to a particular level of performance (for instance, 75% correct) in the psychometric function. The CFF varies with stimulus intensity, background luminance, and the stimulus size and position on the retina, but a typical value is around 30 Hz (Eisen-Enosh et al., 2017).

Instead of using a flickering stimulus to measure temporal segmentation, one can present only two brief events separated by some interval of varying duration. The measure of interest is then the smallest duration for which the two events are still perceived as two rather than a single one. One can then report this threshold interval duration (critical fusion duration), or the time between the onsets of each event at threshold (critical fusion period).

Instead of manipulating the delay between successive events, one can fix the delay and manipulate the contrast of the events. This is the method used to measure the temporal contrast sensitivity function (TCSF), that is the minimal contrast of a flickering grating for which the grating can still be discriminated from a uniform field. While contrast threshold varies with both temporal and spatial frequencies, the maximum visible temporal frequency is again around 30 Hz (Robson, 1966).

2.2. Temporal continuity

Once events are temporally segmented, what is happening between the end of one and the beginning of the next one? Temporal continuity helps establish a relationship between successive events in order to create the appearance of a continuous temporal flow (Giersch, 2021). But why is this a problem to start with? While physical time is continuous, sensory time is not. This is because every 300 milliseconds or so, we make rapid eye-movements that last tens of milliseconds, and during that time, visual information is suppressed (Burr et al., 1994; Yarrow et al., 2001). Temporal continuity is also disrupted during blinks (Duyck et al., 2021). How then are events stitched together in perceptual time?

One way to demonstrate that there is perceived temporal continuity is to show that interruptions of sensory stimulation have limited impacts on what is perceived. This demonstration of temporal continuity is particularly striking when the stimulus is ambiguous, that is when two interpretations (or more) compete for being chosen. Well-known examples of ambiguous visual stimuli are the Necker cube, binocular rivalry, or the motion quartet (e.g. Maloney et al., 2005). By default, ambiguous stimuli generally lead to bistability (or multistability if there are more than two interpretations) when they are presented continuously for several seconds, that is the percept naturally alternates between the different interpretations every two to five seconds (e.g. Mamassian & Goutcher, 2005). The effectiveness of temporal continuity can then be assessed by presenting an ambiguous stimulus intermittently, i.e. interrupting phases when the stimulus is present ('ON') with phases when it is absent ('OFF'), and measure whether the percept stays stable across the intermissions (Figure 2b). Critically, when the ambiguous stimulus is presented intermittently rather than continuously, the alternation rate of its interpretation slows down, and in the extreme, participants get stuck on a single interpretation whenever the stimulus reappears (Pearson & Brascamp, 2008). In other words, the interpretation of an ambiguous stimulus becomes frozen in time when presented intermittently. This stabilisation suggests that there is a mechanism in place that links together consecutive presentations. A discussion of the mechanism that implements temporal continuity is beyond the scope of the current review, but we note one property that will constrain this mechanism. The stabilisation of bistability depends strongly on the duration of the OFF period. Stabilisation is strong when the intermission lasts a few seconds (e.g. 5 s OFF periods when the ON periods are 3 s; Leopold et al., 2002) or very short (less than about 250 ms), but weaker for intermediate OFF periods (Noest et al., 2007). This dependence on the OFF period, together with other properties of the stimulus and the observer, affects the effectiveness of temporal continuity.

In summary, we have an illusion of temporal continuity, in high temporal resolution, even when events are interrupted. Temporal continuity has been exploited in the film industry. Contemporary popular movies contain several thousand cuts (Cutting, 2019). Some of these cuts are very salient visual changes when the camera is changing angle in a scene. And yet, naive viewers often fail to detect these cuts and instead experience continuity.

2.3. The passage of time

The passage of time is the subjective experience of the speed at which events unfold. It is theoretically distinct from perceived duration (see "duration" section below) in the sense that the passage of time is continuously experienced whereas duration is defined for discrete temporal intervals. The passage of time is fast when we are constantly processing new temporal objects, and reversely it is slow, to the point of experiencing "ennui", "when the mind is without internal or external distractions, when there is nothing to think about and nothing to do" (Montague, 1904, p. 5). Attention and expectations play a particularly important role in the passage of time, presumably because attending to events that fail to come will slow down the passage of time (Block, 1979; James, 1890). Emotions are known to affect the passage of time, such that time flies when we are having fun and drags when we are bored (Droit-Volet & Meck, 2007).

The classical method to measure the passage of time is the subjective passage of time judgment (Figure 2c). In this paradigm, participants are prompted to report their experience of the speed of the passage of time on a scale from fast to slow, for instance using a 5-point Likert scale where 1 is "time flew fast", 3 is "normal", and 5 is "time dragged" (Sucala et al., 2010; Wearden, 2005). Being a subjective method, there is a large inter-individual variability due to the different use of the Likert scale. An alternative method is a match-to-samples where participants first learn two extreme sample stimuli, one where few events occur over a long period of time and another where a lot of events occur over a short period, and then new stimuli are presented that have to be categorized as "slow" or "fast" (Lamprou-Kokolaki et al., 2024). This latter method is less subject to the subjectivity of the use of a Likert scale, but still not completely objective because participants have to choose where to place the boundary between "slow" and "fast" categories, and this choice is somewhat arbitrary.

3. Anticipating when forthcoming events will occur

While temporal continuity is emphasizing the link between consecutive events, it is sometimes important to keep a representation of their temporal order. In particular, to estimate the duration between two events is to estimate how old the first event is when the second event is perceived.

An accurate estimation of durations will help anticipate future events, for instance expecting when the red traffic light will turn green.

3.1. Temporal order

Even if two successive events have been segmented successfully, we might still be confused to establish the order in which they occurred. Temporal ordering, or sequencing, is the capacity to resolve which of two successive events occurred first. Being able to represent temporal order is important because it is the basis of our perception of causality. When a moving object hits another static one, like two balls on a pool table, there are two temporal events separated by the moment of the impact, and the temporal order between these two events determines which object causes the motion of the other.

The typical method to measure this capacity is the temporal order judgment (TOJ; Figure 3a) where two events are presented in succession, separated by an interval that varies from trial to trial, and participants have to report which event was presented first (Hirsh & Sherrick, 1961). The two events to be ordered can be discriminated by a visual attribute (e.g. different colours) or placed at different locations. This experiment provides two measures, a point of subjective equality (PSE) and a TOJ threshold (sometimes called TOT for "temporal order threshold"; van Wassenhove, 2009). The PSE is the delay between the two events for which the participant is at chance to choose which one was presented first. The TOJ threshold is the smallest delay for which participants can still correctly discriminate the order at some pre-determined performance level (e.g. 75% correct). Estimates of PSEs are typically variable across participants but stable within participants (Grabot & van Wassenhove, 2017).

As simple as it looks, this TOJ task is problematic in vision. When the two events differ in colour (or any other attribute) but are presented in the same location, some masking is likely to occur that will make the stimuli less visible (see section below on "backward masking"). To avoid this masking, it is tempting to use two distinct spatial locations for the two stimuli, but this choice is also not without consequences. As Exner (1875) noted already in his monograph, if the two stimuli are spatially close to each other, the two successive flashes can produce apparent motion (see section on "postdiction" below), and this perceived motion would be an artefact (the task now measures a motion direction threshold rather than a temporal order sensitivity). To alleviate this problem, the two successive stimuli can be oriented and orthogonal to each other, thereby reducing, but not completely suppressing, the impression of motion (Westheimer & McKee, 1977). If instead the two stimuli are far from each other, a shift of attention will be necessary to go from one event to the other. Because of these issues in the TOJ task, measured thresholds vary greatly across studies, from very precise (about 3 ms; Westheimer & McKee, 1977) to much poorer (at least 30 ms; Pöppel, 1997).

Note that temporal order should not be confused with time-order error (TOE). When two stimuli "A" and "B" are presented sequentially, TOE refers to differences in their discriminability depending on whether they are presented in the order "A-B" or "B-A" (Harrison et al., 2017).

3.2. Duration

Once the order between two events has been established, the time between these two events can be evaluated. The perceived duration is the subjective experience of how much time has passed between two events. In a sense, perceived duration is the magnitude of the interval between two events, whereas temporal order was just its sign. Being able to represent durations is useful for planning a sequence of actions.

It is fair to say that perceived duration has attracted most of the research in time perception (Grondin, 2010). There are well-studied biases and variabilities in the estimates of temporal durations. In particular, Weber's law (or the so-called "scalar property" in the context of temporal duration) makes estimates of longer durations more uncertain. However, psychophysical data do not seem to support the simple form of Weber's law (Allan, 1979).

We shall distinguish here two types of perceived duration depending on whether the duration to estimate is defined by the interval between two brief events or by a continuous interval bounded by its onset and offset events. In the former case, we will talk of the perceived "time span" between the two events, and in the latter case of the perceived "elapsed time" between the onset and the offset of the interval. This distinction is important because these two conditions generate different perceived durations. This phenomenon is known as the "filled duration illusion" and captures the fact that a continuous interval appears longer than an empty interval of the same physical duration (Goldfarb & Goldstone, 1963). In our terminology, perceived elapsed time is longer than perceived time span. Numerous studies have looked at the impact on perceived duration of intervening events during the interval, and usually introducing more events increases perceived time span (Buffardi, 1971). In the following, unless explicitly stated otherwise, duration will refer to the perceived elapsed time of a continuous interval.

To measure perceived elapsed time, the simplest method is probably the reproduction task. In this task, participants are exposed to intervals of varying durations from trial to trial and they have to reproduce the perceived elapsed time, for instance by pressing a key for the duration of their estimated elapsed time (Grondin, 2010).

As simple as the reproduction task is, it includes a motor component that might affect the accuracy and precision of elapsed time perception. For this reason, a second method involving a two-interval two-alternative forced choice (2AFC) is sometimes preferred. In the 2AFC task, participants are exposed to two intervals of different durations, one that is constant in the block of trials (the standard) and the other variable from trial to trial (the test), and they have to report which interval is perceived to last longer (Figure 3b). Usually, the two intervals are presented one after the other, but they can also overlap in the sense that the second interval can start before the first one ends.

Interestingly, such an overlap deteriorates only slightly the sensitivity of elapsed time, and this has consequences for the models that can account for perceived duration (de Montalembert & Mamassian, 2012).

A third popular method to study perceived elapsed time is the match-to-samples. In this paradigm, participants are first trained on two different template durations (one "short" and another one "long") and then asked to categorize new stimuli on the basis of being more similar to the short or long template. Note that in the animal timing literature, this method is called a bisection task (Allan & Gibbon, 1991), and consequently this term has also been used when it was brought to human testing (Wearden, 1991). However, we think it is a misnomer (participants do not bisect the stimulus, at best they bisect their internal representations of potential stimuli on a continuum between the two templates). To avoid confusions with another bisection task defined later (see section on perceived "nowness" below), we will use the match-to-samples terminology here as it is the one more classically used in psychophysics. For new stimuli in-between the two templates, a psychometric function is obtained when the fraction of times the long template is chosen over the short one is plotted against stimulus duration. From this function, we can extract the bias and sensitivity in the match-to-samples task, that is the point of subjective equality (the stimulus duration categorized as long 50% of the times) and the difference limen (the half distance between the stimulus categorized as long 75% of the times and the one categorized as long 25% of the times), respectively. A variation of the match-to-samples task is to train participants on only one duration and then ask them whether new stimuli are shorter or longer than the reference, or whether new stimuli have the same or different durations than the reference. This latter task is sometimes called a temporal generalisation task (Bartolini et al., 2025; Piras & Coull, 2011), although to follow the terminology of psychophysical methods, it is probably less confusing to just call it a temporal samedifferent task (Kingdom & Prins, 2016).

Our presentation of elapsed time judgments assumes that the participant knows in advance that they will be asked to estimate these quantities in the future. These experiments are known as prospective time judgments (Hicks et al., 1976; Wearden, 2005), as opposed to retrospective time judgments where participants are told that they have to estimate duration only at the end of the interval (e.g. "how much time has passed since you started to read this paragraph?"). Retrospective time judgments are often one-shot experiments (once the participants know that they will have to estimate duration, they subsequently pay attention to any salient event), but they can also involve repeated trials. For instance, imagine that you are driving around a block of houses looking for an address, and your copilot recognizes that you have already passed in front of that building. You can genuinely ask yourself how long it has been since you passed here the last time, but the onset of the time interval is only defined once you have identified its offset: when you passed in front of that building the first time, you did not know yet that you would have to estimate duration from this moment on. Interestingly, the cost is minimal to evaluate these intervals that are defined implicitly (Jovanovic & Mamassian, 2018).

3.3. Anticipation

When a first event is systematically followed by a second one, the occurrence of the second event can be anticipated if the temporal relationship is noticed. When the time span between events is regular, a perception of rhythm can be generated. Examples of regular visual patterns in the natural environment include the swinging of tree branches in the wind and the sea waves crashing on sandy shore. Being able to represent anticipations at the perceptual level is useful to plan appropriate actions, such as catching a fruit on a moving tree or avoiding getting wet on the beach. In some extreme cases, to precisely time the future will help avoid injuries (Lee & Reddish, 1981). These anticipations of future events help us compensate for irreducible neural delays in the processing of visual information.

The relationship between two consecutive events can be encoded implicitly, and the benefits of this encoding can be measured. In particular, if a target stimulus occurs with a high probability after a cue with a systematic foreperiod, it will be detected faster and more accurately (Niemi & Näätänen, 1981). We then talk about a predictive timing task or an implicit timing task (Piras & Coull, 2011). It is as if participants have implicitly learned that a target will occur after a set duration, and so they naturally attend to it at that time (Nobre & van Ede, 2018). Participants can also be trained to attend to a specific foreperiod rather than another one (Coull & Nobre, 1998). Similarly, in temporal expectancy tasks, the experimenter manipulates the hazard rate to induce an expectation that a target will occur at some specific time (Janssen & Shadlen, 2005). The hazard rate is the conditional probability that a target will occur at some time given that it has not occurred yet. Whether observers encode the hazard rate or the actual probability of occurrence is still being debated (Grabenhorst et al., 2026).

When the same visual object is presented at a fixed rate, a perception of rhythm will sometimes arise. This kind of isochronous pattern is used for most warning signals such as a flashing amber light at a traffic intersection. More complex rhythms are generated when either the presented objects are not all identical or when the intervals between objects are not identical (Kotz et al., 2018), as for instance in the Morse code. Whether rhythm is perceived or not depends strongly on the period of the stimulations, sometimes called the inter-onset interval (Repp, 2003). There appears to be only a small range of inter-onset intervals that generate perceived rhythm. Beyond a couple of seconds, events start to appear to be independent from each other. If instead the period between consecutive events is too short, then it becomes very hard to count the individual events.

In this case, we will argue that observers have a different percept, they no longer experience rhythm but instead perceive flicker (see section on "specious present" discussed below).

A classical approach to investigate anticipation is to instruct participants to synchronize a motor action with a rhythm. For instance, a series of brief events is presented, once per unit time, and participants have to tap with their finger in synchrony with these events. In vision, synchronization to rhythms is quite poor, with an inter-onset interval threshold of about 400 ms, which is about four times worse than for auditory rhythms (Kolers & Brewster, 1985; Repp, 2003). Although the synchronization method has been widely used, it incorporates a motor component that may influence the accuracy and precision of the prediction.

In an effort to reduce the effects of motion actions, one can use an anticipation task where only one action per trial is necessary. In such a task, three events are presented in a regular rhythm and participants have to synchronise a motor action with the last event (Jazayeri & Shadlen, 2010; Mamassian, 2008). To be efficient in this task, participants should not react to the third event, but instead should anticipate when the third event will occur from the knowledge that the time delay between the first two events is the same as between the last two. If the first two events are distinct ("A" and "B"), and if the third event is removed, one can compare the anticipation to an absent third event for the different orders of the sequence, "A-B" and "B-A". The difference in these anticipation times gives us the relative perceived times of stimulus B relative to that of stimulus A.

For instance, this method has been used to infer that moving objects are perceived later than static ones (Mamassian et al., 2007).

The motor action can be removed altogether to produce purely perceptual judgments of prediction. In these methods, successive events are separated by a common time span, and the measure is the sensitivity to violations of regularity of the temporal sequence. Typically, the experimenter presents a sequence of items separated by an equal duration except for the last one, and participants have to report whether the last item was presented too early or too late (Figure 3c; e.g. Giomo et al., 2025).

Instead of manipulating when the last item of a sequence is presented, one can focus on the duration of the first one and ask observers whether this duration is longer or shorter than the subsequent ones. In this case, there is a bias to perceive this first duration as longer than it really is, a phenomenon known as "chronostasis" (Yarrow et al., 2001), or sometimes "time-shrinking" (when the emphasis is put on the second or third item in a series relative to the first; Nagaike et al., 2016).

When one item in the sequence is unexpected (i.e. an oddball), it will also be perceived longer, a phenomenon known as "time's subjective expansion" (Tse et al., 2004).

4. Integrating sensory information to consolidate a stable percept

Instantaneity refers to a collection of temporal phenomena that occur at a single perceptual instant.

What is experienced at this instant is under the control of several processes, the most fundamental of which is temporal integration. Some sensory signals may be lost because of temporal masking, and some initial percepts may be updated because of postdiction.

4.1. Temporal integration

Temporal integration refers to the process that merges all the sensory signals that occur within a physical temporal window into a single percept. When multiple signals are fused into a unique percept, the resulting experience may be qualitatively distinct from that of each of the individual components. For instance, when a brief (20 ms) red disk is followed by a brief (20 ms) green disk, observers perceive a fused yellow disk (Efron, 1967). However, when a delay (e.g. 500 ms) is introduced between the red and green disks, red is first seen, followed by green, without the intervening yellow percept. A similar albeit less spectacular effect occurs with the alternation of black and white stimuli that results in a grey percept when the stimuli are fused (Talbot-Plateau law).

This qualitative change allows us to define a critical size for the temporal window of fusion, which corresponds to the longest onset asynchrony between two consecutive stimuli for them to still be fused. Whether two signals are fused into a single percept rather than perceived individually depends on whether the signals undergo temporal integration rather than temporal segmentation (see section on "temporal segmentation" above).

Although not making consensus across authors, we extend here the definition of temporal integration to include the case where only one stimulus is presented but for a relatively long time.

According to this extended definition, temporal integration is the reason for the increase in perceived brightness when a stimulus is presented for increasing durations, a phenomenon known as Bloch's law (Gorea, 2015). Just like the qualitative change occurs only when stimuli are separated by less than some critical duration, the quantitative increase in brightness also occurs up to a critical duration. Interestingly, brighter stimuli are in turn perceived to last longer (Karsilar et al., 2025).

When a light is flashed for a very brief time, it produces a visual experience that can last for tens or even hundreds of milliseconds. This phenomenon, known as "visual persistence", results from the integration of the flash with the subsequent blank (Mamassian & Wexler, 2026). It originates from the relative sluggishness of receptors in the retina and also includes other integration mechanisms throughout the visual system (Coltheart, 1980).

The properties of Bloch's law and visual persistence can be explained by a "temporal impulse response function" (TIRF) that characterizes the response of the visual system to an impulse of light. Therefore, the TIRF gives us a measure of temporal integration. To estimate this function, a powerful method consists in presenting a pattern (e.g. a Gabor patch) rapidly followed by the same pattern but with its contrast reversed (Figure 4a). Then, there is an intensity of the second pattern that makes the sequence invisible, and this intensity depends on the delay between the two patterns, (Watson & Nachmias, 1977). The integration that makes the sequence invisible works as long as the delay is shorter than some critical duration. The collection of the pairs of delay and associated intensity of the second pattern can be explained by a filter that defines the TIRF. The critical duration determines the duration of the integration window.

While it seems detrimental to lose some temporal resolution because of integration, there are also sometimes some benefits to integrate over time. When a sensory signal is weak or noisy, integrating it over time increases the chances to see it. Likewise, when a visual pattern is not visible all at once, increasing the window of integration improves the chances to see the whole pattern. For instance, in the Di Lollo paradigm (Di Lollo, 1977), a regular 5x5 dot grid is divided into two frames of 12 dots each, leaving one dot out. The detection of the missing dot is easy when the two frames are presented briefly (10 ms) with a brief (10 ms) inter-stimulus interval (ISI), but becomes increasingly difficult when the ISI is more than 80 ms (Di Lollo, 1977; Wutz et al., 2016). Interestingly, the detection of the missing dot is also harder when the first frame is presented for a longer time (more than 80 ms; Di Lollo, 1980), a property that has yet to be properly explained.

4.2. Backward masking

Visual backward masking is the reduction of the visibility of a target stimulus by a mask presented afterwards (for a review, see Breitmeyer & Ogmen, 2000). In the extreme, the target is completely obliterated from perception by the mask presentation, while it was clearly visible when the mask was absent. In a sense, backward masking is temporal integration that goes wrong.

Among the different types of masking, we focus here on backward masking by noise. In this scenario, the mask typically consists of random-dot noise, or filtered noise, that spatially overlaps the target. Masking by noise is most effective when the mask immediately follows the target and the masking strength diminishes when the time delay between the target and the mask increases (Figure 4b). The effects of masking disappear when the mask is too late after the target, and the limit defines the duration of an integration window.

Another important type of backward masking is metacontrast masking where the mask does not overlap the target spatially (for the special case of "sequential metacontrast", see below in the "postdiction" section). In contrast to masking by noise, metacontrast masking presents a stereotypical U-shaped function where masking strength is maximal when the mask onset comes around 20 to 100 ms after the target onset, and is weaker for both longer and shorter latencies (Breitmeyer & Ganz, 1976; Francis, 1997).

4.3. Postdiction

Postdiction encompasses a set of processes by which the perception of a physical event is affected by a later event (Herzog et al., 2020; Shimojo, 2014). In other words, postdiction describes an ensemble of phenomena where the perception of an object presented at time t acquires some of its properties from another event that occurs at a time later than t. But importantly, the object is not first perceived with its own intrinsic properties before being modified by the later object, nor is there an active mechanism that goes back in time to erase the memory of this first percept (Dennett & Kinsbourne, 1992). Meanwhile, in the absence of the second object, the first is properly perceived with its own properties. Therefore, postdiction updates "what" is perceived by delaying the decision process. This is in opposition to backward masking (discussed in the section above) that just makes an object invisible by the presentation of a subsequent mask.

A classic example of postdiction is apparent motion. In this case, an object, say a small disk, is briefly presented at some location, and a few milliseconds later, the disk is presented again but at a different location (Figure 4c). For a range of displacements and a range of delays between the two frames, the disk appears to move smoothly between its two locations. This phenomenon has interested Gestalt psychologists who called it either beta or phi motion depending on how fast the two frames are presented (Sekuler, 1996; Wertheimer, 1912). Interestingly, once motion is perceived, the first frame is no longer perceived on its own. In the colour-phi phenomenon, where the first object has one colour and the second object has a different colour, a change of colour might be perceived halfway between the two frames (Kolers & von Grünau, 1976). In apparent motion, perception of an object can be revised by an event presented up to 200 ms later (Braddick, 1980).

The effects of postdiction appear to extend to surprisingly long durations. This has been demonstrated for "Sequential Metacontrast" (SQM) where the feature of an object presented at the beginning of a short movie can be revised by an event presented up to 400 ms later (Drissi-Daoudi et al., 2019). The feature in question is a Verner offset, that is two abutting vertical lines that have a small horizontal offset. At the beginning of the stimulus, the top line might be briefly shifted to the right relative to the bottom one, then the two lines are perfectly aligned for the duration of the stimulus. If the stimulus stops at this point, the initial Vernier offset is clearly visible. But if a brief Vernier is presented in the opposite direction (top line shifted to the left relative to the bottom one) at the end of the stimulus, the initial Vernier offset is no longer visible, suggesting that the two shifts have been integrated before being perceived. A similarly long duration is found in "retroperception" where a late cue presented up to 400 ms after a target still helps discriminating that target (Sergent et al., 2013). A target can become invisible if it is followed by a mask that spatially overlaps it. Backward masking by noise is maximally effective when the mask immediately follows the target. (c) Postdiction. When a disc is flashed at one location and later at a nearby location, apparent motion between the two locations can be perceived, and the first flash is no longer perceived on its own (it becomes invisible). The timing between the two flashes controls the perceived direction of motion.

5. Holding on a perceived present to label events in time

A single perceived instant can be composed of multiple physical events that are not necessarily synchronous. To perceive these events simultaneously implies that the experience of now can be extended in physical time, a phenomenon called "specious present" by William James.

5.1. Subjective simultaneity

Subjective simultaneity is the perception of multiple distinct events that all seem to occur at the same time. It is not exactly the opposite of temporal order: two events might not be seen simultaneously and yet the observer could still be unable to determine which one occurred first (Hirsh & Sherrick, 1961). The perception of simultaneity involves events that do not occupy the same spatial location, otherwise we run the risk of confusing simultaneity with integration (see section on "temporal integration" above). The representation of simultaneity is useful to determine whether events have a common cause (Körding et al., 2007).

The classical method to measure subjective simultaneity is the simultaneity judgment (SJ). In this task, participants are presented with two events that can be physically synchronous or successive, and they have to report whether they perceived them at the same time. Judgments of simultaneity can focus on the onset of events, or, less frequently, their offsets (Allan, 1975). A hybrid method, in which one event terminates as the other begins (Figure 5a), is also possible and offers some advantages: it mitigates the issue of determining the appropriate brevity of the events, and it also eliminates the spatial uncertainty of not knowing which object is presented first. From the data, we can trace a response curve of the fraction of simultaneity judgments as a function of the time between the two events. We can then extract a point of subjective simultaneity (PSS) that corresponds to the time that leads to the maximum of the response curve. A finer analysis consists in estimating two transition points, the transition from "A-precedes-B" to "simultaneous" and the transition from "simultaneous" to "A-follows-B", and take the PSS as the midpoint between these two transition points (Yarrow et al., 2011).

5.2. Specious present

The specious present refers to the duration of the perceived present. In the words of James who popularized the concept, "the practically cognized present is no knife-edge, but a saddle-back, with a certain breadth of its own on which we sit perched, and from which we look in two directions into time." (James, 1886, p.378). Therefore, the psychological present differs dramatically from the physical present since "The present of metaphysical or conceptual time is a point, separating past and future; the present of psychological time-the specious present-is a continuous segment extending appreciably into the past." (Montague, 1904, p. 1). Representing the specious present is useful to summarize the salient visual properties of a perceptual moment, for instance the fleeting motion of a flying basketball, with the integration of all its visual properties, in particular its colour, shape, location and speed.

The specious present has taken different names in the history of time perception. Von Baer called it the perceptual moment (Pöppel, 2004; von Baer, 1864). It is also the duration of the lived present (Varela, 1999, p. 272) or the experienced moment (Wittmann, 2011). The very fact that the present can have a duration makes it specious, so we will adopt this terminology. If the perceived present has a duration, anything that happens during this physical duration will appear instantaneous.

Fraisse used this notion to define instantaneity as the inability to distinguish the "ON" moment from the "OFF" moment (Fraisse, 1984). However, attempts to measure the smallest physical duration that leads to instantaneity have produced variable estimates, from about 40 ms (Mach, 1865; Pöppel, 2004) to 60 ms (once perceived brightness has been taken into account; Servière et al., 1977), to about 130 ms (Efron, 1970; Fraisse, 1984). The very existence of such a smallest duration for instantaneity has itself been questioned (Allan, 1976). Alternatively, one could be interested in the longest possible duration of an interval that is encompassing a single specious present, and while various estimates have also been provided here, a duration of 3 s is often reported (Pöppel, 1997; Wittmann, 2011).

Part of the difficulty to measure the specious present comes from the lack of a clear definition that makes consensus. As an operational definition, we propose that the specious present is the interval duration over which the percept cannot be divided into shorter temporal segments. For instance, looking at a pendulum swinging back and forth, each swing might constitute a single specious present because the weight at the end of the rope is perceived to move smoothly in a single direction. The perceived present can encompass multiple physical events (Elliott & Giersch, 2016), as long as a single percept is experienced during that time. Measuring the duration of the specious present, and for that matter demonstrating that it has a duration, is notoriously hard.

One possible method to measure the specious present is to estimate how long different perceptual attributes remain bound together. In some original work, Moutoussis & Zeki (1997) presented a stimulus that was changing direction of motion (up or down) every 270 ms. The stimulus was also changing colour (red or green) at the same rate but at different phases relative to the motion changes, and participants had to report which colour they saw when the motion was upward (the experiment can also be run the other way around, asking participants to report the perceived direction of motion when the colour was red). For instance, for one particular phase, obervers might be perceiving mostly an alternation of red-up and a green-down. From these data, the authors extracted the phase between the two changes that led to the most consistent colour reports. Surprisingly, and in contrast to some predictions obtained from known latencies in the visual system about cortical areas that are critical for the processing of colour and motion, the authors found that motion lagged behind colour changes: perceived colour changes preceded perceived motion changes. Importantly for the specious present point of view, for most phases, observers report seeing only two alternating events, say a red-up and a green-down, that represent the binding of colour and motion attributes within a moment (Figure 5b). Rarely do they see the other two brief events, namely red-down and green-up, even though these associations were physically present in the stimulus. If the period of alternation is increased, these two other percepts will start to be experienced. If the period is reduced too much, participants will just experience flicker of colour and motion with no consistent binding. Therefore, there appears to be a small range of alternation periods for which only two percepts are experienced, and we argue that in this range, specious present is experienced.

In the paradigm of Moutoussis & Zeki (1997), there is a motion-colour binding that seems to extend beyond the physical duration over which one particular motion direction and one particular colour are presented. A possibly related phenomenon is the so-called "temporal freeze" (Motoyoshi, 2007). An object (the illusory square of Kanizsa) is flashed for a brief duration (200 ms) while the background varies continuously in colour. Surprisingly, the inside of the object is seen with a single colour for the duration of the flash, even though the colour of the background is clearly seen to be changing during that same time. In other words, over the duration of the flash, the colour percept inside the object is frozen in time. The freezing duration in this phenomenon is compatible with our definition of the specious present.

5.3. Nowness

Nowness represents "when" each event is perceived. It refers to the time of the perceived present in the subjective narrative of experienced events. Because of neural delays in the visual system, there is a mandatory minimum latency between a physical stimulus and its perceptual awareness (Hogendoorn, 2022). Interestingly though, this latency is not constant, and in particular, the latency of nowness decreases when stimulus luminance increases (Roufs, 1963).

Nowness separates the perceived past from the perceived future. Imagine that you are walking around a new town, determining when you perceive a landmark is important to assign a temporal tag to this perception (these tags are also called "time markers"; Nishida & Johnston, 2002; White, 2023). Nowness separates the time before seeing the landmark from the time after. While temporal prediction is useful to expect when the passing of the landmark will occur in the future, nowness marks when the landmark is being passed, and is used later to remember when the passage occurred in our personal timeline. Therefore, to represent nowness is useful to memorize the order of past events.

While the concept of nowness is one of the oldest in time perception, it is very difficult to agree on a good method to measure it. The problem is exacerbated once we consider that the perceived present may have a duration (see previous section on the "specious present"), in which case nowness is no longer a point in physical time but rather an interval.

More than a century ago, Fröhlich (1923) thought he had found the perfect paradigm to study nowness. An object in motion is suddenly revealed and participants have to report where the object was at its onset. In this experiment, participants are typically biased, such that the reported onset position is displaced in the direction of motion. From the equation relating spatial displacement and temporal interval to speed, Fröhlich inferred that the spatial shift corresponded to a temporal delay of nowness of about 100 ms relative to the physical event. This paradigm and interpretation have since been strongly disputed (Kerzel, 2010). A related phenomenon is the "flash-lag effect" (FLE). In this case, a flash is presented near a moving object and participants have to report where the object was at the time of the flash. Similarly to the Fröhlich effect, the object is misperceived in the direction of motion (MacKay, 1958; Nijhawan, 1994). When the dynamic stimulus is linear motion, the perceived position at the time of the flash is mislocalised to a location on the trajectory that is later by about 80 ms (Eagleman & Sejnowski, 2000). When the dynamic stimulus is changing colour, the perceived colour at the time of the flash is one that is also later in the sequence (Sheth et al., 2000). However, just like the Fröhlich effect, the explanations for the flash-lag effect are still debated (Hubbard, 2014), and so its use for estimating nowness is risky.

We believe that a bisection task might provide a simple objective method to measure nowness. In this paradigm, participants are exposed to three successive events and they have to estimate whether the time span between the first two is shorter or longer than the span between the last two (Figure 5c). In other words, participants in a bisection task have to estimate whether the middle event is closer to the beginning or the end of an interval that is composed of three successive events. One final method to measure nowness is to convert time to space. For instance, participants can be trained to map a two second interval onto a full revolution of the hand of a clock (Jovanovic & Mamassian, 2019). This method has been used to show that there are different nowness perceptions at different eccentricities (Jovanovic & Mamassian, 2020a), and that when two events are presented, their nowness tends to be attracted to each other (Jovanovic & Mamassian, 2020b). If the target is an event ("B") distinct from the other two ("A"), the PSE determines the perceived nowness of B relative to that of A.

6. Discussion

A classification of the large variety of perceptual time phenomena can be useful to identify links between them, and common mechanisms to explain a subgroup. In this review, we have focused on twelve visual time phenomena, provided descriptions for each of them, and highlighted at least one behavioural method to study each one. Based on a proposal that the visual system has four ultimate temporal goals, these twelve phenomena were naturally divided into four clusters. Two of the four clusters collected visual time phenomena related to perceived succession, and the other two to perceived instantaneity.

Is there a way to separate further the two ultimate goals that are classified as perceived succession and perceived instantaneity? One such distinction might be between the processing of temporal information and the representation of that information. The processing of visual time focuses on how sensory signals are measured by the early visual system, and how these signals are transformed to be transmitted to other parts of the visual system. In contrast, the representation of visual time carries the content of some visual perception, and preserves this content even after the stimulus at the origin of the perception has disappeared.

To appreciate the distinction between processing operations and represented contents, let us take the example of how well we can separate two successive events. The processing part of this problem involves the mechanisms that segment the flow of sensory stimulations into individual events, whereas the representation part details how temporal order between the individual events is encoded. Markers of temporal processing include how efficient are its mechanisms, and the extent to which they are encapsulated (i.e. independent of other processes). In contrast, markers of temporal representations include how accurate they are, or how long they can be held in memory.

The distinction between processes and representations has been extremely important in multiple areas of cognitive science (Fodor & Pylyshyn, 1988), and may be fruitful to classify perceptual time phenomena. While we acknowledge that it is not always straightforward to make the distinction between processes and representations in time perception, some temporal phenomena seem to be better conceived as processing, that is to operate and transform temporal information, while other phenomena appear to be better conceived as representation, that is to carry some content of temporal information.

Crossing the distinctions between succession and instantaneity on one hand, and between processing and representation on the other hand, yields the four clusters of temporal phenomena that have been discussed here (Table 1). At the level of primary processes, the processing of perceived succession is temporal segmentation, whereas the processing of perceived instantaneity is temporal integration. At the level of primary representations, the representation of perceived succession is temporal order, whereas the representation of perceived instantaneity is subjective simultaneity. The distinction between processing and representation is also informative to classify our proposed ultimate goals. At the level of ultimate goals for processing visual time, the processing of perceived succession is the experience of the passage of time, whereas the processing of perceived instantaneity is the consolidation of a percept through the collection of postdictive phenomena. At the level of ultimate goals for representing visual time, the representation of perceived succession is the anticipated time of the next event, whereas the representation of perceived instantaneity is the labelled time of perceived nowness. Between primary phenomena and ultimate goals, there is a group of phenomena that may be categorized as subgoals. The method of distinguishing goals and subgoals has helped structuring complex problems in computer science (Newell & Simon, 1972). For a classification of visual time phenomena, distinguishing primary phenomena, subgoals, and ultimate goals defines a hierarchy of more and more complex phenomena. Overall, the grouping of phenomena into four clusters and different levels of complexity is a first step to define a taxonomy of visual time phenomena. The proposal that there are four separate clusters of visual time phenomena entails that there should be some relationship between the phenomena within a cluster. One potentially powerful tool to test whether such a relationship exists would be to apply the sensory adaptation technique for one phenomenon and check whether there is transfer to another phenomenon. For instance, adaptation to a short duration makes a test stimulus appear longer (Heron et al., 2012). Using this adaptation technique, we note the interesting effect between flicker adaptation and perceived duration: flicker adaptation at a high rate (20Hz) reduces perceived elapsed time, whereas adaptation at a low rate (5Hz) does not change perceived duration (Johnston et al., 2006). Because "duration" belongs to the same cluster as "temporal order" and "anticipation", we would predict that adaptation to high rate flicker should also affect these two other temporal phenomena.

In contrast, phenomena that belong to different clusters might rely on different mechanisms, and thus should be relatively independent from each other. For instance, in our classification, "temporal order" belongs to a cluster different from "subjective simultaneity", and thus measures from tasks in these two clusters do not have to correlate strongly. There is ample evidence for such a dissociation. The point of subjective equality in a temporal order judgment task does not correlate with the point of subjective simultaneity, even though these two measures are supposed to both monitor whether events are synchronised (Linares & Holcombe, 2014; Parise et al., 2026; Vatakis et al., 2008).

The cluster that includes "temporal order" also seems distinct from the one that includes "temporal segmentation". Observers are able to detect that two flashes separated by 50 ms are not simultaneous, and yet they are unable to correctly discriminate their order (Mitrani et al., 1986). This is even more striking in audition, where the critical fusion duration is much smaller than in vision, around 1 or 2 ms, but the TOJ threshold is an order of magnitude larger, around 20 or 40 ms (Exner, 1875; Pöppel, 1978).

The study of the relationship between perceived duration and other phenomena is interesting. In particular, there should be a relationship between "duration" and "anticipation". However, in the auditory sensory literature, there is evidence that the perception of a rhythmic pattern does not help the estimation of the time span at the end of the pattern (McAuley & Jones, 2003; Pashler, 2001; Teki et al., 2011). If a similar result holds for vision, this would indicate that ultimate goals (like anticipation) are influenced by simpler phenomena (like perceived duration), but that the reverse is not necessarily true. As for the relationship between "perceived duration" and the "passage of time" that belong to different clusters, it seems that these phenomena are very distinct (Droit-Volet & Wearden, 2016; Lamprou-Kokolaki et al., 2024).

The classification proposed in this paper focused on time with little acknowledgment that visual time necessarily interacts with space. There are well-known effects on the interaction of time and space that have been described in the literature (Gorea, 2011). The Kappa effect refers to perceived time span that increases with spatial distance. Reversely, the Tau effect refers to perceived spatial distance that is affected by the temporal delay between stimuli. There is also evidence that perceived duration is spatially tuned (Ayhan et al., 2009; Burr et al., 2007; Johnston et al., 2006).

An extension of the present classification should integrate these spatial properties adequately, as well as the interactions with motor actions (see Coull & Droit-Volet, 2018).

Finally, we tried to restrict our classification to the visual sensory modality. It will be interesting to generalize this work to other sensory modalities, in particular auditory and tactile, for which some phenomena like segmentation or rhythm might be more prevalent in humans (but not necessarily in non-human primates; Gámez et al., 2018). It will be also important to review how different sensory modalities interact (van Wassenhove, 2009).

In summary, we have proposed here a simple classification of visual time phenomena as a first step to define a taxonomy of temporal perception. We hope that this classification will help clarify the similarities between different categories of temporal phenomena and identify the appropriate methods to study these phenomena. Ultimately, this classification should help determine the mechanisms underlying these visual time phenomena.
