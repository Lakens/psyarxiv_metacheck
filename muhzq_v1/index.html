Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

Curriculum learning, the deliberate ordering of learning experiences, is central to how humans and animals learn, yet its benefits in neural networks are inconsistent. We argue that curricula succeed when they prevent or correct early commitments that might otherwise predispose learners to underfit or overfit to training data. Early experience can lead learners to overlook relevant information, or to rely on irrelevant details that do not generalise well.

Commitment: the tendency for early learning to establish particular features or patterns that continue to dominate later decisions.

Related schedules include blocking versus interleaving, pacing, and schedules over the information a learner can use. Curricula differ from spacing schedules, which concern when material is revisited rather than the order in which its difficulty unfolds.

Highlights

• Curricula are central to how humans and animals learn, yet in artificial neural networks their benefits are inconsistent and often vanish in large models.

• New work in humans and neural networks shows that easy-to-hard curricula help learners recover stimulus dimensions that earlier learning compressed away, and help most when there is more to recover.

• In perceptual learning, easy-first training instead leads learners to rely on fewer dimensions, which transfer better, so curricula can push representations towards fewer dimensions or more.

Curricula are central to human and animal learning Curriculum learning (CL; see Glossary) -the deliberate ordering of learning experiencesis foundational to how humans learn. Developmental psychologists and educational theorists broadly agree that learners acquire knowledge progressively. They build complex skills and ideas by mastering simpler problems first [1, 2], and benefit from revisiting familiar concepts at increasing levels of difficulty [3]. One reason is that learning in smaller steps helps manage the amount of information a learner must process at once [4]. Animal trainers also teach complex behaviours in small steps, a method known as shaping [5, 6]. Mathematical models show that shaping can be necessary when a task must be built from simpler parts [7]. Empirical studies across species and domains -from auditory identification in humans and rats [8], visual perceptual learning [9, 10], perceptual decision-making [11] and compositional generalisation [12, 13] -show that the structure of a training sequence can change both how quickly a skill is acquired and how far it transfers. But curricula do not always help. Practice methods that help people learn simple motor skills do not always help with complex ones [14], and in human category learning, fading from easy to hard helped only when the relevant dimension had to be found among irrelevant ones [15] -that is, only when there was a risk of focusing on the wrong features. Curricula are therefore well grounded in psychology, but their benefits depend on the learner and the task.

Human studies also show that what learners bring to a task matters. People can combine familiar rules in new ways, a form of generalisation that standard neural networks often struggle with [12]. They benefit from training in which examples vary one feature at a time and are grouped into blocks [12, 16]. Networks can reproduce this pattern when learning is controlled by context [12, 13], although an alternative explanation is that learners rely more heavily on recent experience [17]. Either way, curricula interact with, rather than override, the learner's existing representational biases.

Neurophysiological studies show that learning in small steps can matter because of limits in the brain itself. Monkeys learning to control a brain-computer interface could quickly master tasks that reused familiar patterns of neural activity, but struggled when success required entirely new patterns [18]. This suggests that, despite having many neurones, the brain may initially be limited to a much smaller set of activity patterns it can readily produce, known as its intrinsic manifold. However, this constraint is not absolute. Monkeys could learn these harder tasks over several days, producing genuinely new activity patterns, but learning was supported by an incremental training procedure that progressively increased the demand, and was inconsistent without it [19]. This is the clearest neural case of a curriculum creating new directions for learning. help when they move learners towards it, from either side.

Underfitting: learning a solution too simple to capture the structure the task requires.

Unlearning: reopening or reshaping the representational directions that currently dominate behaviour -recovering dimensions compressed away, or releasing directions the task cannot use. This is not forgetting, and is distinct from 'machine unlearning', the removal of specific training data from a trained model. Curricula can make unlearning unnecessary (by shaping what is committed) or possible (by helping a committed learner recover what was compressed).

Weight decay: a common training penalty that keeps a network's weights small, discouraging reliance on many features.

XOR rule: a category rule in which the answer depends on a combination of two features, so neither feature alone predicts it.

Curriculum benefit for machines is mixed

In machine learning, the same principle is applied by deliberately sequencing training examples, often from easier to harder ones. The concept was introduced to deep learning by Bengio et al. [20], who showed that training order can improve generalisation in neural networks. Curricula have since improved transfer in visual learning [10, 21], stabilised early phases of language model pretraining [22, 23] and helped on tasks requiring multi-step reasoning [24, 25].

But there are also many cases in which curricula make no difference. In standard supervised training with sufficient compute, curricula often perform comparably to random ordering [26]; in transformers, exhaustive evaluations conclude that data-based curricula rarely help [27]; and in post-training of language models for compositional reasoning, difficulty-ordered schedules show no reliable advantage over random sampling [28]. Some apparent successes and failures also reflect design choices rather than learning (Box 1). So it remains unclear why curricula help in some cases but not others.

This article asks what properties of a learner determine whether curricula promote learning. We propose that the benefit of a curriculum depends on how readily a learner can change what it has already learned. Some learners have enough spare capacity to accommodate new information without disturbing existing representations. Others must reorganise what they already know, which can be difficult once early learning has established a particular way of solving the task. Building on recent work [29], we call this reorganisation 'unlearning'. Unlearning here does not mean forgetting, nor what AI researchers call 'machine unlearning', the removal of specific training data from a trained model [30]. It means reopening or reshaping the dominant representational directions that currently govern task behaviour. By a representational direction, we mean a feature or combination of features that a learner can use to make a distinction, such as colour or shape when learning to categorise objects. In a recent study, curricula helped learners recover information they had previously overlooked [29]; in another study, curricula helped learners avoid relying on unnecessary information [10]. Although these effects might seem to be in tension, we argue that both reflect the same principle -curricula are useful when they guide learning towards the representations a task actually requires.

Different ways curricula might benefit learning

Curricula can help learning via at least three routes. They can provide useful feedback earlier (bridging time), make learning signals more reliable (aligning gradients), or guide learners towards the features needed for the task (finding the right directions). We briefly cover the first two before turning to the third, which is the focus of this article.

Bridging time

In reinforcement learning, reward often arrives only at the end of a long sequence of actions, and a learner that rarely reaches the goal receives almost no signal to learn from. Curricula bridge this gap. Training a robot from start positions increasingly far from the goal gives it feedback from the start of training [31], and automated curricula that choose tasks the learner can only just solve work in the same way [32, 33]. The same gap arises when recurrent networks must hold information over time, and they learn memory tasks far more easily when the task is built up gradually [5, 34]. Elman's early simulations of language learning made the same point by limiting a network's memory at first [35], although later work found this unnecessary, and even harmful, once the artificial languages included semantic as well as syntactic constraints [36].

Aligning gradients

A second route concerns the direction of each learning step. Hard or noisy examples can push a learner in unhelpful directions, whereas easy examples give cleaner signals. Weinshall and colleagues proved that, for a linear model trained by gradient descent, learning converges faster on easier examples [37], suggesting that easy examples produce updates that point more directly towards the solution. A related account suggests that curricula help by favouring examples that are more representative of what the learner will eventually encounter, rather than allowing noisy examples to dominate early learning [38, 39]. Wu et al. [26] trained thousands of orderings across convolutional networks and found that, in standard training, curricula, anti-curricula and a random ordering that expanded the training pool at the same pace all performed comparably. Under limited training budgets or with label noise, however, easy-to-hard curricula outperformed standard training. In simple settings with a single best solution, such curricula make learning faster but do not improve the final result [40]. To change where learning ends up, and not just how fast it gets there, a curriculum must change which directions a network commits to.

Finding the right directions

Mannelli et al. [41] give the clearest mathematical account of how curricula help a network find the right directions, and we develop it further here. They use a small network learning a classification task that requires combining two features, rather than relying on either one alone. The smallest network capable of solving the task has only four hidden units, each of which must learn to distinguish a different group of stimuli. If these units initially learn the wrong distinctions, the network can become trapped in a poor solution. An easy-first curriculum makes this less likely. A larger network is less vulnerable because it has more units available, increasing the chance that some are already well placed to learn the task (a 'lottery ticket') [49]. With these spare resources, the order of training matters less. A learner with spare capacity can absorb new demands in unused directions without reorganising existing ones, and so has nothing to unlearn. This might seem to imply that humans, with billions of neurones, should rarely need curricula. But capacity is relative to the task and the time available, and on short timescales people attend to only a few dimensions at once [4, 29] and can readily produce only a small set of neural activity patterns [18], so their effective capacity for a new task is often small. Constrained learners, biological and artificial, behave like the small network. Strittmatter et al. [11] trained a parsimonious two-layer network and human participants on an equivalent XOR-like decision task. In both, an ascending (easy-to-hard) curriculum produced the best test performance and a 'bad' blocked sequence impaired it. In networks the damage persisted through extended training, because the network had committed to a sub-task solution from which its few units could not escape, whereas networks trained on hard or random sequences eventually caught up. Whether the human impairment lasts was not tested. For a learner with little spare capacity, what it learns first matters more than how much it sees.

This also explains why curricula so often fail in large models, which usually have little to unlearn. In heavily overparameterised networks, suitable directions are likely to exist already [41]. A related explanation may apply when fine-tuning pretrained models. Rather than learning everything from scratch, these models can often adapt to new tasks through relatively small changes to what they already know. Indeed, many fine-tuning objectives can be optimised by adjusting only a small number of directions in parameter space [46]. This may leave less need for a curriculum to reorganise existing representations.

Box 1. Testing curriculum effects

Choices about design, controls and evaluation can determine whether a curriculum appears to succeed. Six common problems can create or hide curriculum effects.

Pacing and exposure.

A curriculum can change both the order in which examples appear and how quickly new examples are introduced. These are different interventions -a learner might benefit simply because it sees a gradually expanding training set, rather than because easier examples come first [26, 51]. Wu et al.'s 'random curriculum', which expands the training set in random order, performed comparably to the best curricula under standard conditions, so many reported curriculum gains are pacing gains -implicit regularisation through a growing dataset -rather than ordering gains. Some studies labelled as curriculum learning test pacing alone. The document-understanding 'curriculum' of Hamdan et al. [52], for instance, expands the data in stages without ordering by difficulty, and its benefit appeared for a text-only language model but not for one that also sees page layout. The cleanest control presents exactly the same examples, with the same difficulty distribution, in shuffled order [41, 42]. A related confound is recency. A curriculum that ends on the hardest material may look better at test simply because its final block most resembles the test. Appending an identical balanced block to every condition before testing controls for this, and in Rubruck et al.'s networks the curriculum benefit for exceptions remained after this control, whereas the cost of the reverse curriculum disappeared [29].

Difficulty depends on the learner.

An example that is difficult for one model may be straightforward for another, so common measures of difficulty, such as example length or prediction error, do not reliably identify which examples should come first [27]. The best measure also depends on the task and training conditions [45, 53, 54], and even which examples are most useful changes with how much data are available [55]. And proxies for difficulty carry other variables with them, so that sorting a pretraining corpus by linguistic difficulty also reorders its source domains [42]. It is not that difficulty is undefinable, but rather that it depends on the model, the objective and the training conditions.

Compute parity.

If one training schedule uses more computation than another, its apparent advantage may have little to do with the curriculum itself. Studies should therefore match training examples, steps or total computation, not just epochs, because computation can differ even at a matched number of steps when example length tracks difficulty [28].

The learning rate. A curriculum's effect depends on how the learning rate changes during training. In solvable models, the optimal joint schedule drops the learning rate sharply at the easy-to-hard transition, and with a properly adjusted learning rate an easy-tohard curriculum becomes optimal where it otherwise was not [56]. Comparisons should either hold the learning-rate schedule fixed across conditions or optimise it separately for each, and say which.

The choice of test. A curriculum may leave overall accuracy unchanged while improving how well learners handle unfamiliar examples or exceptions to a rule. Volk et al.'s key outcome is transfer to new conditions [10], Curriculum by Smoothing reports transfer and domain-adaptation gains [21], and Maharana and Bansal [24] found curriculum benefits for out-of-distribution generalisation but not for in-distribution accuracy. In the rule-plus-exception task, the benefit is concentrated on the exceptions -the items that require unlearning -rather than on the rule items [29]. Studies evaluating only aggregate in-distribution metrics may miss representational effects entirely. Time course. Readout subspaces can stabilise within ∼200 steps [10], Wu et al. [26] find effects under time constraints, and Zhang et al. [23] report gains in early and mid-training. At the other extreme, some effects appear only late. In small language models, the stability benefits of curricula became apparent only as training approached 300 billion tokens, and were invisible at 10-60 billion [42]. Studies that look only at final performance, or at a single point in training, can miss how a curriculum works. Tracking how a learner's representations change during training, for example their dimensionality along task-relevant axes, can show what the curriculum is doing [57].

Future studies should therefore include pacing and matched-exposure controls, control for recency at test, enforce compute parity, hold or co-optimise learning-rate schedules, report learning dynamics over both early and late training, and evaluate transfer, out-ofdistribution or item-specific outcomes.

the easiest path downhill, carving channels that later water follows and deepens. This can go wrong in two ways. If the water settles into just one channel, some plants are left dry. If it follows every small, temporary dip in the ground, it is wasted on channels that reach no plants. Once channels are deep, redirecting the water is difficult, especially when there is little room to form new ones. A gardener can help by guiding the first flow towards the main plants, then gradually opening new channels as needed, but guiding it the wrong way makes matters worse.

Learning works much the same way. In our analogy, plants are the task, water is experience, and the channels represent directions in the internal representations of a neural network. Early learning commits a learner to a small set of these directions, often within the first few hundred training steps [10]. A learner can underfit by focusing on the most obvious pattern in the data while overlooking other information needed to solve the full task [29, 58]. Alternatively, it can overfit by relying on details that happen to predict the training examples but do not hold for new ones [10, 56]. Fixing underfitting means recovering a dimension that was compressed away, and fixing overfitting means letting go of an unhelpful one. Curricula, the gardener in this picture, can help with both, to the extent that they move a learner towards task-appropriate dimensionality (Box 2).

Early commitments matter because they are hard to reverse. Early training is a critical period in deep networks -deficits imposed at the start can permanently impair what a network learns [59], and networks that continue from earlier training generalise worse than those trained from scratch [60]. Restoring lost plasticity requires explicit interventions such as reinitialising little-used units [61]. Compression is especially hard to undo. Networks trained from small initial weights tend to learn the strongest patterns first [58]. Once a relevant feature has been neglected, it contributes little to the network's responses, leaving little learning signal to reveal its importance [29]. Humans show a similar difficulty. Having learned that a stimulus dimension is irrelevant, people can perform hundreds of trials without noticing that it has become fully predictive [62].

Box 2. Measuring capacity, commitment and dimensionality

Three things need to be distinguished -what a learner could represent (its model capacity), what it currently represents (its representational dimensionality), and how easily those representations can change. A network may have many parameters but use only a few representational dimensions when solving a particular task. Conversely, a smaller network may already use most of the resources available to it. Our account predicts that curriculum benefits depend on this difference between available and already-used capacity, and on whether the learner's existing features are useful for the task. Effective capacity should be assessed before, or independently of, training on the curriculum. Width relative to the minimal sufficient architecture, initialisation scale, training time and the rank of the readout all contribute, and so does prior knowledge, since a learner whose features are already aligned with the task needs to recruit little. In small networks, capacity can be indexed by width relative to the minimal architecture that can solve the task -four hidden units for the XOR-like Gaussian mixture [41] -and by the training budget. A second axis is how freely internal features can change. Networks initialised at large scale learn 'lazily', using their existing features differently while barely changing them, whereas small-scale initialisation produces 'rich' learning, in which the network changes its internal representations as it trains [63]. The degree of richness can be estimated from how task-relevant representational manifolds become separable during learning [57], and rich dynamics tend to learn easy examples before hard ones even without a curriculum [64]. Having a large set of fixed features does not necessarily make a learner insensitive to training order. Even if the features themselves barely change, early experience can determine which ones the learner comes to rely on. In Volk et al.'s perceptual-learning model, readout weights changed about 200 times more than those of earlier layers, and freezing the sensory network left behaviour and generalisation unchanged, yet with short training and few samples, which readout directions were selected still depended on the training trajectory [10].

Commitment can be tracked as the stability of the set of units that dominate the outputfor example, the overlap between the most influential readout units at successive training steps, which for the ∼150 most influential neurones in Volk et al.'s model reached its ceiling within ∼200 steps [10].

Dimensionality relative to the task can be summarised by the participation ratio or effective rank of a representation -a continuous count of how many dimensions carry meaningful variance [65] -but total dimensionality is ambiguous, because a curriculum may need to raise it (to recover a compressed dimension) or lower it (to release an unhelpful one). It is better measured separately along task-defined axes. Rubruck et al.

projected hidden representations onto a rule axis and an exception axis, and found that the curriculum expanded the exception axis while leaving the rule axis unchanged [29].

The direction of change differs across settings. In perceptual learning, curricula lowered readout rank [10]; in category learning, they raised representational dimensionality [29]; and in small language models, whose output layer is too low-rank to represent the distribution over words (the 'softmax bottleneck') [43], random data ordering drove the output layer's singular-value spectrum to become sharply concentrated late in training, and curricula prevented this [42]. The measure that predicts benefit, and the expected direction of change, must therefore be specified in advance from the task's structure. Unlearning in humans cannot yet be measured directly, but there are behavioural and neural proxies. Reaction-time costs for exception items, and slowing when a given stimulus feature changes between trials, index which dimensions a participant is attending to [29]; and representations in ventromedial prefrontal cortex compress during concept learning [66].

How curricula support learning -a new theory

Extending Mannelli et al.'s analysis [41], our proposal is that curricula help learners avoid or overcome early commitments that would otherwise be difficult to change. They can do this in two ways. First, a curriculum can guide early learning towards the features that matter, preventing the learner from relying on irrelevant ones, which makes unlearning unnecessary. Second, when earlier learning has caused important information to be overlooked, a curriculum can introduce new demands gradually enough for that information to be recovered, which makes unlearning possible. In the garden analogy, the first approach prevents water from entering unnecessary channels, while the second helps it reach plants that were initially left dry. Curricula should therefore help most when learners have limited capacity or training time, and when their initial commitments are difficult to reverse. When learners can easily accommodate new information, the order of training matters less. A curriculum's value, in short, is the unlearning it saves.

A mathematical analysis supports this account. Using a simplified network model that can be solved exactly, Mignacco and Mori [56] show that good generalisation requires aligning weights with the task-relevant directions while keeping weight on irrelevant directions small. Easy-tohard curricula mainly serve the second goal, suppressing growth along irrelevant directions early. Interestingly, this benefit disappears when the network is already discouraged from relying on unnecessary features through weight decay, a common form of regularisation. Under these conditions, the best schedule instead runs from hard to easy. A curriculum is therefore only one way to keep the wrong features from dominating learning, and when another mechanism already does this, a curriculum may have little to add.

However, the absence of anything to unlearn is not the only cause of failure. Curricula also fail, and can actively harm, when what is learned from the easy examples cannot be built on for the hard ones. Controlled studies show this clearly. In Strittmatter et al.'s study [11], a badly ordered sequence of blocks trapped the network, and in Mannelli et al.'s analysis [41], starting with very easy, low-noise examples committed the network to the nearest poor solution.

How transitions between difficulty levels are managed also matters. Learners with limited capacity may struggle when task demands change abruptly. Gradual transitions allow them to build on what they have already learned, rather than having to abandon it and start again. In a theoretical model, the benefit of a curriculum also depends on preserving what was learned before moving to the next difficulty level [40].

Human learning research offers a further counterpoint. Interleaving examples of different categories, rather than blocking them, often improves category induction [67]. This need not contradict the present account, since interleaving keeps every discriminating dimension in play and so guards against compression, but it cautions against equating a curriculum with blocked, easy-first ordering.

Two examples, underfitting and overfitting

The clearest evidence that curricula help learners unlearn comes from Rubruck and colleagues [29], who studied how humans and neural networks learn rules with exceptions, and who name the phenomenon 'curriculum unlearning'. Imagine learning to classify objects by colour, only to discover that a few exceptions also require you to consider their shape. In their version of a classic task [68], six of eight stimuli could be classified using a single feature, but two required an additional distinction. Both humans and networks initially learned the simpler rule and applied it even to the exceptions, classifying them below chance, which suggests that they had overlooked information needed for the full task. Presenting rule stimuli first and introducing exceptions gradually increased the proportion of participants who reached the full rule-plus-exception strategy at test (65.3% against 44.4% without a curriculum), whereas the reverse order selectively harmed exception learning. An earlier study using the same kind of task found that delaying exceptions also improved their learning, in humans and in a model of the hippocampus [69]. On our account, gradually introducing exceptions may have helped learners adapt the initial rule, rather than continuing to apply it indiscriminately. In the networks, the curriculum raised the dimensionality of the hidden representation, specifically by separating the exceptions along a direction orthogonal to the rule, and final dimensionality predicted exception accuracy (𝑟 = 0.70).

A second experiment directly tested whether curricula help more when there is more to unlearn. Networks first trained on a simple rule came to rely on almost a single dimension of information (participation ratio 1.09), whereas networks trained on a more complex rule retained several (2.93). The curriculum benefit was roughly three times larger in the networks whose representations had been more strongly compressed. The same interaction appeared in over a thousand human participants, and a reaction-time index of selective attention during pretraining -a behavioural proxy for compression -predicted who would later benefit from the curriculum. When the networks' hidden layer was frozen, so that pretraining could not compress it, a curriculum benefit remained but no longer depended on pretraining. As Rubruck et al. conclude, curricula on this evidence help more with unlearning than with learning from scratch -and the more there is to unlearn, the more they help.

Volk et al. [10] studied the opposite case, a simple task in which the risk is overfitting. Human observers, and a network pretrained to recognise everyday images, learned to discriminate the orientation of visual patterns, with easy trials using large differences in orientation and hard trials small ones, and were then tested on new conditions. Some learned easy trials before hard ones, while others saw only hard trials, or both kinds shuffled together. In the network, nearly all learning took place in the readout, the final stage that turns features into a decision (Box 2). Easy-first training led this decision-making layer to rely on fewer dimensions of information.

Importantly, networks that relied on fewer dimensions also performed better when tested on unfamiliar conditions (𝑟 = -0.69, and 𝑟 = -0.95 when the proportion of early easy trials was varied). Transferring this simpler decision-making layer to a network trained only on hard examples also improved its generalisation, even beyond the sequential curriculum, suggesting that the way learning selects features is itself important. Humans given shuffled easy and hard trials generalised, on average, as well as those given an explicit sequence, but with much greater variability, and observers who spontaneously learned easy trials first transferred best (𝑟 = 0.45). Human learners, it seems, can partly supply their own curricula.

The following interpretation goes beyond Volk et al.'s framing. Easy-first exposure prevents premature commitment to a high-dimensional, idiosyncratic readout. Easy examples appear to help the learner identify the features that matter most. Harder examples can then refine those distinctions, rather than encouraging the learner to rely on additional, less reliable features. This is unlearning made unnecessary.

At first, these findings seem contradictory. In one study, curricula helped learners focus on a smaller set of distinctions; in the other, they helped learners represent additional information.

But the difference makes sense once we consider what each task actually requires. Rubruck et al.'s curriculum rescues a complex task from underfitting, whereas Volk et al.'s protects a simple task from overfitting. Orientation discrimination needs only one key direction, and harder trials refine it, so the best solution is compact. The rule-plus-exception task needs a second direction that the rule does not contain, so the best solution is larger than the learner's first guess. In both cases, the curriculum helps learners establish what matters before harder examples shape their representations. Sometimes this prevents unnecessary features from being learned; other times it allows previously neglected features to emerge. The goal, then, is not always to represent fewer distinctions or more of them, but to represent the right ones for the task, which we call task-appropriate dimensionality. Rubruck et al.'s frozen-network control suggests that the two ways in which curricula help can be dissociated, since the benefit that remained, like Volk et al.'s, lived in the readout and did not depend on prior compression.

Concluding remarks

Curriculum learning is often framed as a universal heuristic -teach easy before hard. The evidence reviewed here supports a more precise and psychologically aligned conclusion. Curricula are most valuable when learning is constrained and early experience risks establishing patterns that are difficult to change. Under these conditions curricula succeed in one of two ways. A curriculum can make unlearning unnecessary by helping learners identify the right features from the start. Alternatively, it can make unlearning possible by gradually helping learners recover information they previously overlooked. Either way, the target is task-appropriate dimensionality -a solution neither too simple nor too complex for the task -not low dimensionality as such. Curricula fail in two complementary ways -when abundant capacity, aligned priors or regularisation render unlearning unnecessary, and when what is learned from easy examples cannot be built on for hard ones, making unlearning impossible. The account makes falsifiable predictions (Box 3).

The account has several limitations. It is best supported in small or analytically tractable learners, and the interaction between prior compression and capacity is predicted rather than shown. Direct evidence that curricula help learners unlearn comes mainly from one category-learning paradigm [29] and from brain-computer interface learning in monkeys [19]. The relationship between readout dimensionality and generalisation does not hold for every architecture [10], and the theory is most developed for easy-to-hard ordering. Not every curriculum effect is about unlearning either. Bridging time and aligning gradients work differently, and the three routes should be distinguished experimentally (see Outstanding questions ).

The important question is therefore not simply whether curricula work, but what a learner has already learned, what prevents it from learning more, and whether its existing representations can accommodate new demands. A theory of when curricula help is really a theory of what makes learning hard, and testing it means measuring capacity, compression, and interference in both brains and networks. Box 3. Predictions The account makes testable predictions. For each, we state what result would show it to be wrong. 1. Dimensionality explains the benefit. A curriculum should help when it changes which features a learner relies on, bringing them closer to those the task actually requires. Curriculum benefit should be statistically mediated by an early shift towards task-appropriate dimensionality, measured along task-defined axes -contraction along unhelpful directions where the risk is over-commitment, expansion along compressed task-relevant directions where the risk is compression. Equalising this shift across conditions, for example by transplanting a readout subspace as Volk et al. did [10], should abolish most of the curriculum benefit. Rubruck et al.'s finding that final dimensionality predicts exception accuracy is consistent with this [29], but the stronger, causal test remains open. The account is undermined if curricula improve performance without any shift in task-relative dimensionality. 2. Capacity gates unlearning. The more strongly prior learning has restricted a learner's representations, the more it should benefit from a curriculum, especially when capacity is limited. Rubruck et al.

showed that curriculum benefits scale with prior compression in small networks undergoing rich learning [29]. If effective capacity governs how much there is to unlearn, this interaction should be strongest in narrow, richly trained networks, weaker in wide ones -whose spare directions can absorb exceptions without recovering a compressed one -and absent when features cannot be compressed at all, as their frozen-network control already suggests. In Mannelli et al.'s analysis, curriculum gains already peaked at intermediate noise levels and, in networks trained on noise-corrupted images, first rose and then fell as width increased [41]. The account is undermined if the compression-by-curriculum interaction is unaffected by width or by whether learning is lazy or rich.

3. A double dissociation between kinds of constraint.

Not all limits on learning should increase the value of a curriculum. Reducing effective capacity without aligning it -fewer hidden units, a shorter training budget, a lower-rank readoutshould increase curriculum benefits in a model that normally shows none. Constraining capacity in a task-aligned way -weight decay that suppresses irrelevant directions [56], a pre-selected readout subspace [10], or pretrained features matched to the task -should reduce or abolish them. A generic 'small models benefit from curricula' account predicts the same direction for both manipulations, whereas this account predicts opposite ones. 4. Bandwidth substitution. If curricula work by controlling which information shapes early learning, restricting that information directly should produce some of the same benefits. Curriculum by Smoothing already shows that a schedule on internal information flow can help. It improved top-1 accuracy (for example, from 62.4% to 65.4% for ResNet-18 on CIFAR-100), whereas smoothing the input images instead hurt, and holding the smoothing constant rather than annealing it fell below the unsmoothed baseline [21]. Sinha et al. motivate it as suppressing noise from untrained parameters, and the reading offered here, that it restricts the directions available for early commitment and then releases them, is an interpretation. If curricula act by scheduling which directions are available for commitment, then gating the information available to internal features should reproduce part of the benefit of ordering even when the data are shuffled, and combining the two should yield sub-additive gains, because both act on the same early commitment.

A similar test could be run in transformers. 5. Scale and persistence in language models. Larger language models should benefit less from curricula, but benefits established early in pretraining may persist even after training becomes randomly ordered. Both patterns have already been reported at a fixed token budget [23, 42]. The stronger, untested prediction is that the persistence of a warm-up benefit should be predicted by the readout spectrum at the moment of the switch. 6. Non-monotonic alignment. Curricula should help most when the easy and hard phases share some, but not all, of the same structure. More precisely, the benefit should be negligible when the easy phase already contains the full solution, largest at intermediate overlap, and negative when what is learned in the easy phase cannot be built on in the hard phase. Interference in continual learning likewise depends non-monotonically on task similarity [70], and task similarity determines whether networks learn new tasks lazily, with little forgetting, or richly, with substantial forgetting [71]. 7. Human learners. People should benefit most from explicit curricula when earlier learning has caused them to overlook important information. Beyond category learning [29], this means that learners who arrive more compressed, and those who do not spontaneously learn easy material first when given shuffled practice [10], should benefit most. Explicit curricula should also help less when there is more opportunity for consolidation and replay between sessions, since in theory replay removes the advantage of curricula in standard networks [40].

Outstanding questions

• Can effective capacity and prior compression be measured before training, in brains as well as networks, well enough to predict who will benefit from a curriculum? • Does having more spare capacity reduce the extent to which prior compression determines who benefits from a curriculum?

• Do learners with stronger biases towards simple solutions benefit more from curricula on complex tasks, and less on simple ones?

• What distinguishes making unlearning unnecessary from making it possible, in behaviour and in neural data?

• Why do some people create their own easy-first curricula from shuffled experience, and does consolidation between sessions reduce their need for explicit ones?

• How do the three routes, bridging time, aligning gradients and finding the right directions, combine in a single learner?
