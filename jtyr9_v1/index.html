To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 25 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Patterns are designed to capture:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Time perspective refers to a cognitive and emotional orientation toward the past, present, or future, shaping how individuals interpret experiences, set goals, make decisions, and regulate behavior. Given its proposed links to diverse mental illnesses and related transdiagnostic personality traits, this systematic review and multilevel random-effects meta-analysis examined the transdiagnostic role of time perspective. In total, 191 publications (1,718 effect sizes) between January 1990 and March 2025 were included. Lower Future-Positive time perspective showed broad and consistent associations with higher internalizing symptoms, addiction, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, externalizing symptoms, and impulsivity (rpooled = -.063 to -.314). In contrast, higher Future-Negative time perspective was primarily associated with higher internalizing symptoms (rpooled = .520). Higher Present-Hedonistic time perspective showed more condition-specific associations and was correlated with addiction, PTSD symptoms, and impulsivity (rpooled = .147 to .350). Higher Present-Fatalistic (rpooled = .158 to .299) and higher Past-Negative (rpooled = .170 to .431) time perspectives demonstrated the broadest transdiagnostic associations spanning internalizing symptoms, addiction, PTSD symptoms, externalizing symptoms, and neuroticism. Lower Past-Positive time perspective was specifically associated with higher internalizing symptoms, externalizing symptoms, and neuroticism (rpooled = -.168 to -.201). Effect size differences across mental illnesses and related personality traits indicated distinct time perspective profiles across conditions. Overall, Present-Fatalistic, Past-Negative, and Future-Positive time perspective emerged as highly transdiagnostic, whereas Present-Hedonistic and Past-Positive time perspective showed more condition-specific associations. These findings support the transdiagnostic relevance of time perspective and highlight the value of incorporating it into theoretical models and potentially into clinical practice.

Time Perspective as a Transdiagnostic Process Across Mental Illnesses and Associated Personality Traits: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Introduction

The concept of "time perspective" has gradually evolved from initial philosophical reflections on temporality into an operational psychological construct with clear behavioral, emotional, and motivational implications. Early philosophical work established time as a subjective framework of experience, rather than an objective property of the physical world. Immanuel Kant (1781 Kant ( /1999)), in Critique of Pure Reason, proposed that one's perception of time structures the experience of both external events and internal states. This emphasis on subjectivity provided a foundation for later psychological accounts of lived time. William James (1890), in The Principles of Psychology, built on this foundation by introducing the concept of the "specious present," describing time as a subjectively experienced flow that integrates past, present, and future with affective meaning.

Phenomenological approaches (i.e., accounts of the structures of lived experience as given from a first-person perspective) extended these insights by focusing on how time is experienced in everyday life. In On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time , Edmund Husserl (1991) conceptualized time as an experiential process involving the retention of the past and the anticipation of the future. In Being and Time, Martin Heidegger (1927 /1962) introduced an existential perspective, framing the human experience of time as fundamentally oriented toward future possibilities, shaped by past experience and by the awareness that life is finite. These accounts reframed time from an abstract dimension of the physical world into a defining feature of human experience.

Building on these philosophical and phenomenological foundations, early psychological theorists began to examine how the subjective experience of time shapes motivation, behavior, and mental health. Pierre Janet (1928) extended this perspective by analyzing how the experience of time becomes disorganized in clinical conditions, highlighting how past emotional memories constrain future expectations. In the mid-20th century, Kurt Lewin (1943) translated these ideas into a functional psychological framework. In his field theory, behavior at any given moment is conceptualized as a function of the individual and their psychological environment within a "life space." In this framework, past experiences and future goals are understood as dynamic forces shaping present action. Lewin's framework laid the groundwork for later formulations of time perspective.

Subsequent work further refined the motivational and developmental implications of this framework. In Childhood and Society, Erik Erikson (1950) embedded time perspective within developmental theory, underscoring its role in identity formation through the integration of past experience and future self-projection. In Psychologie du Temps, Paul Fraisse (1957) introduced the notion of a "temporal horizon" to distinguish psychological from physical time and to emphasize its emotional qualities. Later, Joseph R. Nuttin (1985 Nuttin ( /2014) ) focused explicitly on the future dimension, conceptualizing future time perspective as a motivational link between present behavior and distant goals, thereby paving the way for contemporary empirical research in which future time perspective has been a major focus.

The modern psychological framework of time perspective was solidified in the late 20th century by Zimbardo and Boyd (1999), who proposed Time Perspective Theory and established time perspective as an empirically supported construct. They conceptualized time perspective as an orientation toward the past, present, or future that involves motivational, emotional, cognitive, and social processes, and that shapes how individuals interpret experiences, set goals, make decisions, and regulate behavior. Zimbardo and Boyd (1999) further characterized time perspective as both situationally determined and a relatively stable individual difference. This conceptualization enabled time perspective to be operationalized through standardized assessment tools, revealing time perspective as relatively stable yet modifiable individual differences associated with diverse mental health related phenomena (e.g., Baird et al., 2021; Chew et al., 2024; Hallford et al., 2018; Kooij et al., 2018).

Theoretical Foundation for Time Perspective as a Transdiagnostic Construct

Understanding time perspective as a transdiagnostic construct is grounded in phenomenological accounts of temporality in psychopathology. Temporality refers to the lived experience of time as a dynamic, subjective structure that intertwines past, present, and future within consciousness, rather than as an objective sequence of chronological moments (Fuchs, 2013).

Phenomenological psychopathology has long emphasized temporality as a core structure of subjective experience and a central dimension of mental illness (Stanghellini et al., 2019 (Stanghellini et al., , 2022)).

Rather than treating temporal disturbances as peripheral symptoms, this tradition conceptualizes disruptions in lived time experience as fundamental alterations in how individuals experience themselves, others, and the world. Across a wide range of disorders, disturbances in temporal experience have been described as affecting anticipation, continuity, and the integration of past, present, and future. This motivates a transdiagnostic approach to temporality in psychopathology.

However, phenomenological accounts of temporality and empirically operationalized time perspective constructs have developed largely in parallel and remain insufficiently integrated.

Among phenomenological models, Fuchs (2005 Fuchs ( , 2007 Fuchs ( , 2009 Fuchs ( , 2013 Fuchs ( , 2019) ) provides an influential framework by distinguishing implicit and explicit temporality across micro-and macro-levels of experience. Implicit temporality refers to the pre-reflective flow of lived time that underlies continuity of consciousness and embodied agency (the tacit, bodily sense of being the initiator and owner of one's actions). It is grounded in Husserl's synthesis of retention (past), primal impression (present), and protention (future) (Fuchs, 2013; Fuchs & Pallagrosi, 2018; Husserl, 1991). In this account, lived time is not passively registered but continuously generated by the body's drive, the felt momentum that pushes experience toward the next moment, and by its engagement with the world. As long as this flow runs smoothly, time itself goes unnoticed. Explicit temporality refers to time as an object of conscious awareness, typically structured into past, present, and future and emotionally experienced in states such as regret, hope, or anticipation. When the smooth flow of lived time is interrupted, because drive weakens or one's own pace falls out of step with the pace of others, time shifts from the unnoticed background of experience into conscious awareness: it seems to slow, stand still, or break apart (Fuchs, 2013). In brief, the micro-level concerns the moment-to-moment structure of lived experience, whereas the macro-level concerns temporality as embedded in biographical narratives through which individuals integrate past experiences and future goals into a coherent sense of self.

Importantly, different forms of temporal disturbance can thus be mapped onto these micro-and macro-levels of experience.

Within this framework (Fuchs, 2013), time perspective, defined as an individual's conscious cognitive and emotional orientation toward the past, present, and future (Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999), corresponds primarily to explicit, macro-level temporality. Time perspective captures how individuals habitually reflect on their past, experience their present, and anticipate their future. These orientations, in turn, shape meaning-making, motivation, and goal-directed behavior. By contrast, implicit and micro-level temporality operate outside conscious awareness and are not directly assessed through self-report measures of time perspective. This conceptual distinction clarifies that time perspective does not exhaustively capture the domain of temporality but instead represents a specific, operationalizable subset that is accessible to empirical study.

Fuchs further proposes that disruptions in temporality constitute a transdiagnostic mechanism in psychopathology, though this manifests as disorder-specific temporal profiles rather than uniform deficits across disorders (Fuchs, 2009; Fuchs & Pallagrosi, 2018; Pritzkau et al., 2022). For example, in depression, explicit and macro-level temporal disturbances are prominent: individuals often experience time as slowed or burdensome, exhibit persistent preoccupation with past guilt, and anticipate a closed or hopeless future. At the biographical level, difficulties integrating past and future may result in a stagnant life narrative dominated by perceived failures. In schizophrenia, by contrast, disturbances are often more pronounced at the micro-level, involving fragmentation of moment-tomoment experience that may intrude into conscious awareness and contribute to disorganized thought, impaired self-monitoring, and disrupted narrative identity. These contrasts illustrate how altered temporality can be central across disorders while taking qualitatively distinct forms. Stanghellini et al. (2022) extended this perspective, reviewing a century of phenomenological and clinical scholarship documenting disturbances in temporality across a broad range of conditions. These included schizophrenia spectrum disorders, depressive disorders, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, borderline personality disorder, anxiety disorders, addictions, eating disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, and related traits. This literature qualitatively indicates that different disorders are characterized by distinct patterns of temporal disruption, which Stanghellini et al. organized into seven categories comprising 42 specific anomalies of temporal experience. For example, in schizophrenia spectrum disorders, the implicit continuity of time itself may fragment, such that experience no longer flows coherently from one moment to the next. In depressive disorders, the felt momentum toward the future is inhibited, time is experienced as slowed, stagnant, or momentarily blocked, and the future appears emptied of attainable possibilities.

In mania, individuals become desynchronized from shared social time, experiencing others as too slow. Across mood disorders and post-traumatic stress disorder, narrative temporality is altered, with the past relived in a predominantly negative mode and the future experienced as predetermined, threatening, or holding no possibilities at all. These qualitative accounts provide a theoretical rationale for systematically examining time perspective across mental illnesses. Although time perspective captures only part of the broader structure of temporality, it represents a tractable and empirically measurable construct through which disorder-specific and transdiagnostic temporal patterns can be compared.

Time Perspective Assessment Tools

The accumulation of empirical evidence on time perspective has been made possible by the development of standardized assessment tools. In psychological research, time perspective is treated as a multidimensional construct because individuals can hold multiple, partially independent temporal orientations toward the past, present and future simultaneously. This conceptualization has motivated the development and use of multidimensional instruments. Mohammed and Marhefka (2019) critically reviewed the psychometric properties of existing time perspective measures, highlighting the Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory (ZTPI) and its shortened and cross-culturally adapted versions as the most widely used and empirically supported instruments; examples include a 36-item version validated across 24 countries (Sircova et al., 2014), the Swedish S-ZTPI (Carelli et al., 2011), and the ZTPI-short (Košťál et al., 2016).

The ZTPI represents a refinement and formal validation of the earlier Stanford Time Perspective Inventory (STPI), an unpublished instrument developed by Zimbardo and Boyd (1985) that served as a conceptual and empirical precursor. Building on the STPI, the ZTPI operationalizes time perspective as a five-dimensional construct. The five dimensions are: Past-Negative time perspective, reflecting regretful or aversive views of past events (e.g., "I often think of what I should have done differently in my life"); Past-Positive time perspective, capturing warm, nostalgic connections to fond memories (e.g., "Familiar childhood sights, sounds, smells often bring back a flood of wonderful memories"); Present-Hedonistic time perspective, emphasizing immediate pleasure-seeking with minimal concern for consequences (e.g., "I believe that getting together with one's friends to party is one of life's important pleasures"); and Present-Fatalistic time perspective, indicating a resigned, hopeless view of the present as predetermined (e.g., "Fate determines much in my life"). The original version of the ZTPI included a single Future dimension, focusing on goaldirected planning and delayed gratification (e.g., "I believe that a person's day should be planned ahead each morning"; Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999). As described below, this single Future dimension was later differentiated into Future-Positive and Future-Negative dimensions in extended versions of the inventory. The ZTPI items are worded such that respondents rate their agreement with statements assessing general attitudes and beliefs about life, using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very untrue,5 = very true). Of the 56 ZTPI items (see Supplementary Material S14), 54 use first-person pronouns ("I" or "my"), one is pronoun-free, and one uses a generic "you" to reflect believed universal truths (e.g., "You can't really plan for the future because things change so much"), emphasizing personal attitudes rather than judgments about others' behavior. The original validation study by Zimbardo and Boyd (1999) demonstrated acceptable to good internal consistency across its five dimensions (Cronbach's α = .74-.82) and adequate test-retest reliability over a four-week period (r = .70-.80).

As the original ZTPI identified only a single Future dimension emphasizing planning, goal pursuit, and delay of gratification, it did not distinguish a separate "Future-Negative" factor (Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999). This may have reflected the fact that the ZTPI was developed using predominantly nonclinical, healthy samples, primarily undergraduate and community participants, which could have limited the salience or differentiation of negative future representations. As later noted by Zimbardo and Boyd, additional future subdimensions might emerge in broader populations. Building on this observation, subsequent studies, such as Carelli et al. (2011 Carelli et al. ( , 2015) ) and Košťál et al. (2016), further supported a Future-Negative dimension with items such as "I often think that I do not have time for everything I have planned to do in one day", highlighting ongoing interest in the role of negative future representations within time perspective frameworks. The original ZTPI was then extended to a sixdimensional version, the Swedish Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory (S-ZTPI), by adding eight new items, all constructed to capture negative future thinking (Carelli et al., 2011). Accordingly, throughout this review, the original ZTPI Future dimension is reported as "Future-Positive," reflecting its primary emphasis on positive, goal-oriented future thinking, which largely corresponds to the Future-Positive dimension in the extended version. Findings based on extended measures are reported differentiating Future-Positive and Future-Negative dimensions.

A systematic review by Peng et al. (2021) examined 25 short versions of the ZTPI identified from published studies across diverse national contexts (e.g., Greece, China, Romania, Germany).

The review showed that a short form composed of the most frequently retained items demonstrated stronger structural validity (CFI = .966-1.000; RMSEA = .000-.038) and higher internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .516-.798; ω = .533-.799) than forms composed of less frequently retained items.

High-frequency items (retained in more than 60% of the reviewed short versions) included Past-Negative (items 16, 34, 50), Past-Positive (items 2, 7, 20), Present-Hedonistic (items 26, 31, 42), Present-Fatalistic (items 37, 38, 39), and Future (items 13, 21, 40, 45). Medium-frequency items (retained in 40-60% of instruments) included Past-Negative (items 4, 22, 54), Past-Positive (items 11, 15, 29), Present-Hedonistic (items 8, 23, 44), Present-Fatalistic (items 14, 47, 53), and Future (items 10, 30, 43, 51). Low-frequency items (retained in fewer than 40% of instruments) included Past-Negative (items 5, 27, 33), Past-Positive (items 25, 41, 49), Present-Hedonistic (items 1, 12, 19), Present-Fatalistic (items 3, 35, 52), and Future (items 6, 9, 18, 56).

While the ZTPI delineated different dimensions of time perspective, scores on any single dimension may not fully capture the aspects of time perspective most relevant to mental illness.

Specifically, recognizing that rigid dominance of any single time orientation may undermine adaptation, Zimbardo and Boyd (1999) introduced the concept of a Balanced Time Perspective (BTP) to capture the capacity to flexibly engage past, present, and future orientations in response to situational demands in ways that promote psychological well-being. BTP was operationalized as a profile combining high scores on adaptive time perspectives (Past-Positive, Present-Hedonistic, and Future [i.e., Future-Positive]) and low scores on maladaptive ones (Past-Negative and Present-Fatalistic). The "Deviation from Balanced Time Perspective" (DBTP) was subsequently developed as a composite index derived from the five ZTPI dimensions to quantify the extent to which an individual's time perspective deviates from this ideal balance (Stolarski et al., 2011). The "Deviation from Balanced Time Perspective-Revised" (DBTP-r) further refines the originally specified "optimal" scores of DBTP, improving its precision and cross-cultural validity through a more empirically derived and robust index (Jankowski et al., 2020). In contrast, the "Deviation from Negative Time Perspective" (DNTP; Oyanadel & Buela-Casal, 2014) captures directional bias toward maladaptive temporal orientations by quantifying deviation relative to a negative temporal profile (i.e., high scores on maladaptive time perspectives and low scores on adaptive dimensions). This makes DNTP particularly informative when examining negative temporal biases commonly associated with psychopathological symptoms. These indices (DBTP, DBTP-r, and DNTP) deepen understanding of the association between imbalanced time perspective and mental health outcomes (e.g., Fuentes et al., 2022; Klamut et al., 2022; Olivera-Figueroa et al., 2015 , 2023a , 2023b , 2023c), complementing the ZTPI's dimensional framework. Other than the ZTPI and its variations, several additional time perspective assessment tools are also widely used (Mohammed & Marhefka, 2019). The Consideration of Future Consequences Scale (CFC; 12 items; 5-point Likert scale; Strathman et al., 1994), for example, measures the extent to which individuals consider the future outcomes of their current behaviors versus prioritize immediate consequences. It also captures how this temporal orientation influences the respondent's decision-making, attitudes, and actions. The scale has been tested in both a unidimensional (Strathman et al., 1994) and a two-factor model (Joireman et al., 2012), including CFC-Future (e.g., "I consider how things might be in the future, and try to influence those things with my day to day behavior.") and CFC-Immediate (e.g., "I only act to satisfy immediate concerns, figuring the future will take care of itself."), with reliability estimates ranging from Cronbach's α = .80 to .86 (Strathman et al., 1994). It also demonstrates strong test-retest reliability (r = .76 over two weeks and .72 over five weeks; Strathman et al., 1994).

The Temporal Focus Scale (TFS; 12 items; 7-point Likert scale; Shipp et al., 2009) is another commonly used instrument, developed to assess the extent to which individuals direct their attention toward the past, present, and future. Unlike other time perspective measures that blend cognitive and affective components, the TFS specifically focuses on cognitive engagement with these temporal domains, with items such as "I replay memories of the past in my mind" (past focus), "I live my life in the present" (current focus), and "I imagine what tomorrow will bring for me" (future focus). The TFS has demonstrated good psychometric properties, with Cronbach's α ranging from .74 to .92 across different samples and small to moderate intercorrelations among its dimensions (r = .09-.48; Shipp et al., 2009).

Finally, the Adolescent and Adult Time Inventory-Time Attitudes Scale (AATI-TA; Mello & Worrell, 2007; Mello et al., 2016) explicitly expands measurement of time perspective to both adolescent and adult populations. The scale focuses on the affective component of time perspective and comprises six subscales: Past Positive, Past Negative, Present Positive, Present Negative, Future Positive, and Future Negative. It is widely used to examine how these dimensions relate to psychological, behavioral, and health outcomes, with validation across young, middle, and older adult samples indicating good psychometric properties (α = .81-.94; RMSEA = .042-.075; Mello et al., 2016), although the Future-Negative subscale has slightly lower reliability (α = .81).

Empirical, Clinical and Biological Support for the Transdiagnostic Relevance of Time Perspective

Empirical evidence consistently supports transdiagnostic associations between time perspective and mental health outcomes, although prior syntheses have largely emphasized future-oriented constructs rather than the full range of time perspective dimensions currently recognized. For example, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Kooij et al. (2018) synthesized 212 studies comprising 110,186 participants (mean age = 32.5 years, SD = 15.4) and reported that future time perspective (e.g., ZTPI Future-Positive) was significantly and negatively associated with depression (k = 16, mean true-score correlation ρ = -.34, 95% CI [-.41, -.26]), anxiety (k = 13, ρ = -.23, 95% CI [-.36, -.09]), substance use (k = 30, ρ = -.22, 95% CI [-.28, -.17]), and risk-taking behaviors (k = 7, ρ = -.22, 95% CI [-.29, -.15]). Similarly, a meta-analysis by Hallford et al. (2018) examined 19 studies and found that psychiatric diagnoses, including depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia, were significantly associated with reduced episodic future thinking (Hedges' g = -.84, 95% CI [-1.06, -.62]), indicating less specific and detailed representations of future events across diagnostic groups.

These findings underscore the transdiagnostic relevance of future-oriented cognition but provide limited insight into other temporal dimensions. Stolarski et al. (2020) shifted attention toward a more global conception of time perspective by focusing on the DBTP index discussed above. Their narrative synthesis of 49 studies reported significant negative associations between DBTP and subjective well-being (r ≈ -.35 to -.65), executive functioning (r ≈ -.19 to -.21), and conscientiousness (r ≈ -.27 to -.46), alongside significantly positive associations with neuroticism (r ≈ .38 to .49), psychological distress (r ≈ .22 to .24), depressive symptoms (r ≈ .31 to .51), and PTSD symptoms (r = .36). Their findings indicate that greater deviation from a balanced time perspective is broadly and moderately associated with poorer mental health and well-being across domains, supporting it as a marker of general psychological vulnerability. However, its focus on the composite DBTP index and broad outcome categories offered limited insight into dimension-specific and disorder-specific patterns, a gap the present review aims to address.

The clinical relevance of time perspective is further reflected in the way several transdiagnostic psychotherapies explicitly or implicitly target temporal orientations. Cognitive behavioral approaches address maladaptive beliefs about the future, reinterpret past experiences, and promote future-oriented goal setting, whereas acceptance-and mindfulness-based therapies emphasize present-moment awareness and values-driven future action to reduce rigid past-or futurefocused thinking (A-Tjak et al., 2015; Balcı et al., 2022; Beck & Emery, 1979; Dindo et al., 2017; Hayes, 2012; Roepke & Seligman, 2016). Similarly, psychoanalytic approaches emphasize the integration of past, present, and future into coherent personal narratives, underscoring the centrality of temporality for meaning-making and psychological integration (e.g., Loewald, 1972; Meisel, 2019; Seligman, 2016; Tordjman, 2011).

Consistent with the centrality of time perspectives in therapeutic approaches, a systematic review of 16 empirical studies by Erguler et al. (2023) found that maladaptive time perspectives, particularly Past-Negative and Present-Fatalistic, were consistently associated with lower dispositional mindfulness, whereas Past-Positive and Future (i.e., Future-Positive) perspectives were positively related to mindfulness. Associations involving Present-Hedonistic were mixed and appeared to depend on whether mindfulness was assessed as a global construct or at the facet level (e.g., present-moment awareness). It was generally negatively associated with global dispositional mindfulness but showed small positive associations with the present-moment awareness facet in some studies, suggesting that different components of mindfulness may relate differently to presentfocused temporal orientations. Beyond these correlational findings, intervention studies further suggest that modifying time perspective can change behavior. For example, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Chew et al. (2024) synthesized 16 studies (N = 1,914), including 13 randomized controlled trials and 3 quasi-experimental studies, and found that time perspective-based interventions significantly reduced food intake (k = 4, Hedges' g = -0.93, 95% CI [-1.21, -0.66]). Most interventions (n = 12) involved episodic future thinking; for example, participants generated personally meaningful future events and repeatedly listened to audio recordings of themselves vividly imagining these scenarios in daily life, thereby increasing future salience at eating-related decision points (e.g., Hollis-Hansen et al., 2020). Although existing intervention and related reviews suggest that time perspective is clinically meaningful and modifiable, they remain limited in scope and do not clearly link specific time perspective-related processes to specific mental disorders, leaving the mechanisms underlying intervention effectiveness insufficiently specified.

Although limited, a small number of studies provide evidence that specific time perspective dimensions are associated with distinct neural and physiological processes relevant to mental health, underscoring their potential clinical significance. For example, Wittmann et al. (2011) examined neural correlates of time perspective using fMRI during a time reproduction task among 15 impulsive and 12 control participants. In the task, participants encoded auditory intervals of 3, 9, or 18 seconds and reproduced each interval by stopping a second tone when they perceived it to match the original duration. Lower ZTPI future time perspective was significantly associated with greater activation in the right inferior frontal cortex (r = -.68), a region supporting inhibitory control and attention to time, right cerebellum, which contributes to event timing and temporal prediction (r = -.65), and left inferior parietal cortex, implicated in attention and the representation of time (r = -.64), whereas higher Present-Fatalistic orientation was significantly associated with reduced activation in the posterior cingulate cortex (r = -.54), a hub of the default-mode network involved in self-referential thought and autobiographical memory (Buckner et al., 2008). Complementing these neural findings, Bourdon et al.

(2020) examined chronic physiological stress in 204 healthy adults using a composite allostatic load index derived from 23 biomarkers. Present-Fatalistic orientation significantly predicted higher allostatic load (β = .16), whereas Future time perspective significantly predicted lower allostatic load (β = -.15), suggesting opposing physiological profiles associated with different temporal orientations.

In addition, evidence from Olivera-Figueroa et al. (2015) indicates that for cortisol, the primary stress hormone of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, higher DBTP scores were associated with lower cortisol output (AUCg; r = -.27), whereas higher DNTP scores were associated with higher cortisol output (AUCg; r = .26) among 61 healthy adults. While these studies provide valuable initial biological evidence linking time perspective dimensions to neural and physiological markers relevant to mental health, they do not clarify how such associations relate to specific mental disorders or whether consistent transdiagnostic patterns emerge, underscoring the need for a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis.

Existing Systematic Reviews on Time Perspective: Scope and Limitations

As noted above, despite the multidimensional nature of time perspective, most existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses have predominantly focused on future time perspective, with relatively few synthesizing associations across multiple temporal dimensions. One narrative review by Cunningham et al. (2015) proposed a dual-pathway framework in which time perspective influences subjective well-being through (a) a direct cognitive-affective pathway shaping emotional and cognitive interpretations of life events, and (b) an indirect behavioral pathway influencing habitual behaviors with long-term consequences. Drawing on a narrative synthesis of primarily cross-sectional, correlational studies, most using the ZTPI in undergraduate and community samples, the authors suggested that Past-Negative time perspective is associated with greater distress, pessimism, and lower life satisfaction, whereas Past-Positive time perspective fosters resilience and gratitude.

Present-Fatalistic orientation was linked to hopelessness and depressive affect; Present-Hedonistic orientation to short-term happiness alongside increased risk-taking; and Future orientation to optimism and long-term goal attainment, sometimes accompanied by planning-related stress. A balanced time perspective, flexibly integrating past, present, and future, was proposed to yield the highest well-being. The evidence cited for these claims consisted mainly of small-to-moderate zeroorder correlations between ZTPI scores and well-being indicators (e.g., Past-Negative with negative affect, rs = .38 to .56, and with life satisfaction, rs = -.42 to -.59; Present-Fatalistic with depressive symptoms, r = .37), supplemented by a smaller set of studies linking time perspective to health-risk behaviors and to adjustment in trauma-exposed and other special populations. Although conceptually informative, this review did not provide a systematic or quantitative synthesis of associations between specific time perspective dimensions and mental health outcomes.

To date, only one systematic review and meta-analysis has comprehensively examined all major dimensions of time perspective (Baird et al., 2021). This meta-analysis synthesized 420 independent samples comprising 110,186 participants across 350 papers reporting 378 studies, situating time perspective within a self-regulation framework rather than a mental illness context.

Outcomes were aggregated into a single composite "Outcomes" variable encompassing health-risk behaviors, antisocial behaviors, academic achievement, and broader life consequences. Future time perspective was positively associated with goal setting (k = 91, average sample-weighted correlation r₊ = .25, 95% CI [.22, .30]), goal monitoring (k = 9, r₊ = .19, 95% CI [.13, .24]), goal operating (k = 204, r₊ = .24, 95% CI [.20, .29]), self-regulatory ability (k = 237, r₊ = .35, 95% CI [.30, .39]), and outcomes (k = 367, r₊ = .18, 95% CI [.16, .20]). Past-Positive perspective showed weak positive associations with goal setting (k = 5, r₊ = .12, 95% CI [.06, .18]), goal operating (k = 39, r₊ = .07, 95% CI [.03, .12]), self-regulatory ability (k = 58, r₊ = .09, 95% CI [.04, .14]), and outcomes (k = 56, r₊ = .07, 95% CI [.03, .11]). In contrast, Present-Fatalistic perspective was negatively associated with self-regulatory ability (k = 64, r₊ = -.27, 95% CI [-.31, -.23]) and outcomes (k = 69, r₊ = -.15, 95% CI [-.21, -.09]).

Past-Negative perspective showed negative associations with self-regulatory ability (k = 62, r₊ = -.20, 95% CI [-.24, -.15]) and outcomes (k = 57, r₊ = -.15, 95% CI [-.22, -.08]). Present-Hedonistic perspective was also negatively related to goal operating (k = 58, r₊ = -.09, 95% CI [-.17, -.00]), selfregulatory ability (k = 67, r₊ = -.23, 95% CI [-.29, -.17]), and outcomes (k = 81, r₊ = -.14, 95% CI [-.19, -.09]). Finally, a Balanced Time Perspective showed a positive association with self-regulatory ability (k = 8, r₊ = .39, 95% CI [.17, .41]). However, because outcomes were aggregated across diverse behavioral and life domains, this meta-analysis was unable to determine how specific time perspective dimensions relate to specific mental disorders, limiting its applicability to research on mental illnesses.

A recent systematic review (initiated in January 2024 and published in July) narratively described how patients with ADHD, alcohol dependence, anxiety disorders, depression, bipolar disorder, personality disorders, PTSD, and schizophrenia exhibit distinct temporal profiles based on 21 publications (Stolarski et al., 2024). Although this synthesis offers valuable descriptive insights and underscores the relevance of temporality-focused interventions, it was not preregistered (Page et al., 2021) and did not include meta-analytic quantification of associations across disorders. As a result, the extent and consistency of disorder-specific temporal profiles could not be statistically evaluated, limiting the empirical robustness and generalizability of the conclusions.

Transdiagnostic Relevance of Time Perspective Across Mental Illnesses

The concept of "transdiagnostic" factors and approaches has emerged in response to the limitations of categorical systems like the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), which often fail to account for comorbidity, symptom overlap, and the continuum of mental distress. The transdiagnostic approach in psychiatry represents a paradigm shift away from traditional categorical diagnoses, aiming to cut across and transcend existing diagnostic boundaries. Two leading frameworks contribute to this shift: the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP; Krueger et al., 2018) and the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC; Insel et al., 2010; Morris et al., 2022). HiTOP offers a psychometric evidencedriven, symptom-based classification system that organizes mental disorders (e.g., those in the DSM) into dimensional hierarchies, capturing broad clusters such as internalizing and externalizing disorders, as well as the overarching general psychopathology factor (p-factor; Caspi et al., 2014 Caspi et al., , 2020 Caspi et al., , 2024)). In contrast, RDoC is grounded in multilevel data integration and adopts a mechanistic, neurobiologically grounded approach that focuses on functional domains (e.g., cognitive control, negative valence) cutting across disorders and measurable across multiple levels, from genes to behavior. Together, HiTOP and RDoC provide complementary perspectives: HiTOP structures the phenotypic expression of symptoms, whereas RDoC seeks to explain these expressions through underlying brain-behavior systems. Both frameworks support a transdiagnostic view that transcends diagnostic boundaries and they promote more precise research, refined clinical assessment, and mechanism-based interventions.

A critical systematic review by Fusar-Poli et al. (2019) analyzed 111 publications and found that the term "transdiagnostic" in the existing literature has been used in three main ways: (a) as an umbrella for shared psychological processes (e.g., emotional dysregulation, cognitive distortions) that appear across multiple disorders; (b) to describe interventions (e.g., Unified Protocol, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy) targeting these shared processes rather than disorder-specific symptoms; and (c) to reflect a dimensional understanding of psychopathology that emphasizes continuity between normative and pathological traits.

Some transdiagnostic factors show lower-level transdiagnostic breadth, being shared primarily within smaller clusters of disorders-for example, neuroticism within anxiety and depression, and impulsivity/disinhibition within the externalizing and substance use disorders (Beauchaine et al., 2017; Kotov et al., 2010). Others demonstrate higher-level transdiagnostic breadth, spanning internalizing, externalizing, and psychotic-spectrum disorders.

Emotion dysregulation, for instance, is implicated across diverse conditions, including internalizing and externalizing disorders, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, and personality disorders (Aldao et al., 2016; Carmassi et al., 2022). Fusar-Poli et al. (2019) argued that to be truly transdiagnostic, a construct or approach must (a) span multiple diagnostic categories rather than merely reflect symptom overlap within a single domain or cluster; (b) involve shared mechanisms that are not disorder-specific; and (c) be grounded in empirically derived models that restructure existing nosology (e.g., HiTOP, RDoC). Time perspective aligns with these criteria by spanning disorders such as depression, anxiety, and substance use; capturing shared mechanisms such as distorted temporal experiences; and being supported by empirically validated models such as the ZTPI dimensional framework. Nonetheless, its current clinical application lacks granularity and integration into structured diagnostic or mechanistic systems.

In summary, time perspective has accumulated substantial empirical support linking it to a wide range of mental illnesses, while the transdiagnostic nature of temporality has long been explored within phenomenological psychopathology and is also frequently targeted in clinical interventions.

However, no effort to date has bridged these complementary yet insufficiently aligned areas.

Strengthening the connection between theoretical foundations, clinical application, and empirical evidence may inspire new approaches to integrating time perspective and temporality into psychopathology research, diagnosis, and treatment. With further development, time perspective and temporality may help inform a transdiagnostic framework that reorganizes mental disorder classification around characteristic temporal dysfunctions, structures therapeutic approaches according to their temporal targets, and links specific temporal disturbances to tailored intervention strategies. A rigorously conducted systematic review and meta-analysis is therefore needed to quantitatively synthesize the associations between the full range of time perspective dimensions and diverse mental illnesses and associated traits. Such an approach would provide a stronger empirical foundation for identifying disorder-specific time perspective profiles and guiding future intervention efforts aimed at modifying maladaptive temporal orientations.

Present Study

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses a key gap in the literature by synthesizing studies that examine associations between time perspective and mental illness constructs, either as defined by the DSM-5 and ICD-11 or as aligned with transdiagnostic dimensions, such as internalizing and externalizing clusters, outlined in transdiagnostic frameworks such as HiTOP (Krueger et al., 2018). Additionally, transdiagnostically relevant personality traits, such as neuroticism and impulsivity, were included given their frequent appearance in the reviewed literature and their established crosscutting relevance to disorders defined in the DSM-5 and ICD-11 (Griffith et al., 2010; Kozak et al., 2019; Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2015; Vassileva et al., 2019). Inclusion Criteria and Study Selection

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) time frame and publication status: studies published in peer-reviewed journals between January 1, 1990, and March 1, 2021. This window corresponds approximately to the emergence of the modern multidimensional conceptualization of time perspective, anchored by the Stanford Time Perspective Inventory (an unpublished precursor developed in 1985), the CFC (Strathman et al., 1994), and the formally published ZTPI (Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999); (b) mental illness constructs and relevant risky personality traits: studies examining associations between time perspective and mental illness categories defined by DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria, or dimensional constructs outlined in transdiagnostic frameworks such as internalizing and externalizing clusters (e.g., HiTOP; Krueger et al., 2018), as well as transdiagnostically relevant personality traits such as neuroticism and impulsivity, which have been empirically linked to DSM-5 or ICD-11 disorders; (c) study design: cross-sectional or longitudinal quantitative studies reporting associations between time perspective and the target variables (mental illness constructs or relevant personality traits) using correlation coefficients, regression coefficients, odds ratios (ORs), or sufficient data to calculate correlation coefficients; (d) time perspective evaluation: assessment of at least one time perspective dimension (past, present, or future) or a composite index such as DBTP, DBTP-r, or DNTP; (e) assessment tools: instruments with established validity and reliability for both time perspective and mental illness symptom severity (rather than formal diagnoses) and personality traits, such as the ZTPI (or its short and translated forms) and the CFC for time perspective, the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995) for mental illnesses, and the Personality Inventory for DSM-5 (Krueger et al., 2012) for relevant personality traits; (f) population: inclusion of clinical and nonclinical samples to enhance comprehensiveness and generalizability; and (g) language: full text available in English. and (e) studies that did not report, or allow for the calculation of, effect sizes.

Violin plot. Violin plots were used to display the distribution of effect sizes within each category, integrating measures of central tendency and dispersion with kernel density estimates (Hintze & Nelson, 1998). This visualization facilitates inspection of skewness, heterogeneity, and potential multimodality in meta-analytic data characterized by substantial between-study variability.

Robust variance estimation (RVE) for multilevel random-effects meta-analysis on mental illness and personality trait categories.

All extracted effect sizes were included in RVE analyses. Mental illness constructs and personality traits reported in the included studies were classified into broader clusters (e.g., internalizing, externalizing), each treated as a separate analytic category. Separate models were estimated for each time perspective dimension in combination with each mental illness or personality trait category. Building on the structure of the multilevel randomeffects meta-analytic model described below, RVE with small-sample corrections was adopted as the primary analytic approach. Traditional multilevel random-effects models assume two levels of variability: sampling error of individual effect sizes (Level 1) and between-study heterogeneity (Level 2). To more accurately account for dependencies among effect sizes within studies, a third level was introduced between the conventional two levels (Tanner-Smith & Tipton, 2014). The three-level model included (a) sampling variance of individual effect sizes (Level 1), (b) variance capturing dependence among effect sizes originating from the same or overlapping samples, including cases in which the same dataset was reported across multiple publications (Level 2), and (c) between-sample variance reflecting heterogeneity across independent samples (Level 3). The resulting three-level model allowed estimation of two additional heterogeneity components beyond sampling error: within-cluster (e.g., same study or overlapping sample) variance among dependent effects and between-cluster variance across studies (Assink & Wibbelink, 2016; Cheung, 2014). Compared with traditional multilevel random-effects models, RVE more effectively accounts for dependence among effect sizes and yields more accurate standard errors because it is less sensitive to assumptions about heteroscedasticity and correlation structures (Hedges et al., 2010; Tipton, 2015). RVE estimation was conducted using the clubSandwich (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022) and robumeta (Fisher & Tipton, 2015) R packages to obtain robust confidence intervals and p values. Across all analyses, associations with p values at or above .05 but below .10 are described as marginal. These packages implement cluster-robust sandwich variance estimators applicable to both ordinary and weighted least squares regression models. The working correlation parameter was specified as ρ = .60 in the RVE analyses, reflecting the assumption that correlations among effect sizes within clusters are constant.

Because ρ must be specified a priori, sensitivity analyses were conducted using values of 0, .20, .40, .60, .80, and 1.00. If pooled correlation estimates remained stable across values of ρ, the selected parameter value was considered justified. As Cochran's Q test (Borenstein et al., 2009) is less reliable with small samples, I² and τ² were used to quantify between-study heterogeneity. I² represents the percentage of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. Conventional benchmarks indicate that I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% correspond to low, moderate, and substantial heterogeneity, respectively (Higgins & Thompson, 2002). Forest plots were generated based on the RVE analyses using the clubSandwich and robumeta packages.

Robustness analyses.

To assess the robustness of the primary RVE findings, traditional multilevel random-effects meta-analyses was conducted using the metafor R package (Harrer et al., 2021; Viechtbauer, 2010). I² and Q were used to estimate the between-study heterogeneity. Full results of these analyses are provided in the Supplementary Materials S6.

Outlier analysis and multilevel meta-analysis after outlier removal. Given the large heterogeneity observed in the meta-analyses, additional RVE analyses were conducted as robustness checks in which outliers were identified and removed using established influence diagnostics (Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010), implemented via the dmetar package (Harrer et al., 2019).

An effect size was classified as an outlier if its confidence interval did not overlap with the confidence interval of the pooled effect estimate. After removing outliers, both multilevel random-effects metaanalyses and RVE analyses were re-estimated using the same modeling procedures.

RVE meta-analysis for mental illnesses subcategories.

The multilevel random-effects meta-analytic approach using RVE was also applied at a more granular level to examine mental illness subcategories. Subcategories within each broader mental illness category were defined according to the labels and assessment measures used in the original studies. For example, "depression," "anxiety," and "internalizing symptoms" were all treated as subcategories within the broader "internalizing symptoms" category, as some studies only examined the symptoms at a more general level, resulting in an "internalizing symptoms" subcategory. Similarly, within the addiction category, the "substance use" subcategory encompassed multiple substances, whereas "alcohol use" only included alcohol consumption in the original studies.

Contour-enhanced funnel plots. Contour-enhanced funnel plots (Peters et al., 2008) were used to visualize asymmetry and identify potential outliers in the distribution of effect sizes, as well as to provide a graphical summary of the overall effect (Egger et al., 1997; Sterne & Egger, 2001).

Asymmetry in the funnel plot may indicate publication bias, whereas extreme points may reflect studies with atypical characteristics or methodological limitations.

Trim-and-Fill analysis.

The trim-and-fill method (Duval & Tweedie, 2000) was applied to assess and adjust for potential small-study effects. This procedure imputes hypothetical "missing" studies to restore funnel plot symmetry and provides an adjusted pooled effect estimate based on the extended dataset.

PET-PEESE publication bias adjustment methods (Test and adjust for publication bias).

To further evaluate publication bias and estimate adjusted effect sizes, we employed the Precision Effect Test (PET) and Precision Effect Estimate with Standard Error (PEESE), implemented as regression-based methods using weighted regression in R with the metafor package. Consistent with recommended practice, these analyses were conducted only when at least 10 studies were available, to ensure reliable results.

PET regresses effect sizes on their standard errors (i.e., the square root of the sampling variance) to detect small-study effects. A significant slope indicates potential publication bias, whereas the intercept represents the estimated effect size for a hypothetical study of infinite precision (i.e., a standard error of zero), taken as the bias-adjusted estimate (Stanley, 2005). Although PET is useful for detecting publication bias, it may underestimate the true effect size, particularly in the presence of heterogeneity or nonlinear bias patterns. When the PET slope was significant, PEESE was applied to adjust for potential publication bias by regressing effect sizes on the squared standard errors. In this model, the intercept provides a bias-corrected estimate of the effect size under the assumption of a nonlinear relationship between effect size and precision (Stanley & Doucouliagos, 2014). PEESE generally provides more accurate effect size estimates in the presence of bias but may overestimate the true effect when publication bias is minimal or when small-study effects are driven by heterogeneity rather than selection processes. Results were interpreted by examining the statistical significance and confidence intervals of both slopes and intercepts. A nonsignificant or near-zero PET intercept was interpreted as evidence of a negligible underlying effect, whereas a significant PEESE intercept indicated a robust effect after adjustment for small-study bias (Carter et al., 2019).

Vevea and Woods' weight function model. The Vevea and Woods (2005) weight-function model was used as a sensitivity analysis approach, as recommended for smaller meta-analyses and as a complement to PET-PEESE for assessing publication bias (Carter et al., 2019; Mathur & VanderWeele, 2020). This approach evaluates how pooled effect sizes change under hypothetical selection scenarios based on selective reporting of statistically significant results, particularly in metaanalyses with a limited number of studies. The model was applied when both PET and PEESE indicated potential small-study effects. Because the weight-function model assumes independent effect sizes and does not accommodate multilevel dependence structures, an average effect size was calculated for each cluster of dependent effect sizes prior to analysis. Rather than estimating the selection weights from the data, which requires many effect sizes per p-value interval (Vevea & Hedges, 1995), the model imposes a priori weights representing the assumed probability that effects in each p-value interval are observed, and the pooled estimate is then re-estimated by maximum likelihood under that assumed selection function. Analyses were conducted using the weightr package, applying the moderate and severe one-tailed selection patterns from Table 1 of Vevea and Woods (2005) over their 14 p-value intervals.

Meta-regression for moderator analysis and risk of bias using three-level mixed-effects models. Multilevel meta-regression analyses were conducted to examine potential moderators and subgroups that could account for heterogeneity. Moderators tested in the overall models included mental illness subcategories (e.g., subtypes within internalizing conditions), gender, age, publication quality, and the assessment tools used to evaluate mental illness constructs. Because differences in pooled effect sizes across mental illness categories and subcategories were a primary focus of this study, significant F tests from meta-regressions using mental illness subcategories as moderators supported further examination of time perspective profiles across these groups.

In addition, publication region, publication year, risk of bias, and study quality were included as moderators to evaluate whether methodological and contextual factors influenced effect size estimates thereby contributing to heterogeneity. A significant Cochran's Q statistic indicates residual heterogeneity remains unexplained by the model, whereas a significant F test for a moderator indicates that the moderator accounts for a significant portion of between-study variability in effect sizes.

Comparison of effect sizes across mental illness and personality trait categories.

Differences in pooled effect sizes for the same time perspective dimension across the mental illness and personality trait categories were calculated using Cohen's q (Cohen, 1988), defined as the difference between Fisher's z-transformed pooled effect sizes. This statistic allows comparison of time perspective profiles across the mental illness and personality trait categories. Results were examined for RVE models using data with and without outliers, as well as before and after imputation (see below). The following benchmarks were used for interpretation: q <.10, no effect;.10 ≤ q < .30, small effect;.30 ≤ q < .50, intermediate effect; q ≥ .50, large effect.

Multiple imputation for meta-analysis.

Because some time perspective dimensions were unreported in subsets of studies, resulting in limited degrees of freedom in certain pooled analyses, and because the RVE analysis has strict restrictions on the number of studies (df ≥4), we conducted an exploratory multiple imputation analysis to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates (Shu et al., 2023). Missing effect sizes were imputed within each diagnostic domain using multivariate imputation by chained equations (mice package) with predictive mean matching (20 imputations; van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). Each imputed dataset was analyzed using the same multilevel robust variance estimation model, and estimates were pooled using Rubin's rules. Given the extent and domain-specific nature of missingness, imputed analyses were treated as sensitivity checks. Primary conclusions reported in the manuscript are based on observed data; full imputation procedures and results are provided in the Supplementary Materials 9 on OSF.

1. Internalizing Symptoms Category

RVE Meta-analysis: Higher internalizing symptoms were significantly associated with higher Future-Negative (ZTPI: r = .520, k = 6, p < .001), higher Past-Negative (ZTPI: r = .406, k = 60, p < .001), higher DBTP (ZTPI: r = .390, k = 25, p < .001), higher DBTP-r (ZTPI: r = .269, k = 4, p = .033; df < 4, interpret with caution), higher Present-Fatalistic (ZTPI: r = .221, k = 54, p < .001), and higher CFC Present (CFC: r = .178, k = 6, p < .001), as well as with lower Past-Positive (ZTPI: r = -.189, k = 57, p < .001; AATI: r = -.145, k = 8, p = .048) and lower Future-Positive time perspectives (ZTPI: r = -.103, k = 67, p < .001); the association with Present Positive was marginal (AATI: r = -.228, k = 8, p = .053). To assess potential publication bias, PET and PEESE analyses were conducted for each ZTPI time perspective dimension in relation to internalizing symptoms. For Future-Positive, PET indicated no statistically significant association between internalizing symptoms and Future-Positive time perspective, whereas PEESE showed that higher internalizing symptoms were associated with lower Future-Positive time perspective (b = -.086, p = .025), suggesting potential small-study effects. Higher internalizing symptoms were associated with higher Future-Negative time perspective in both PET (b = .400, p = .058; marginal) and PEESE (b = .485, p = .010). For Present-Fatalistic, both PET (b = .167, p = .004) and PEESE (b = .219, p < .001) indicated that higher internalizing symptoms were associated with higher Present-Fatalistic time perspective. In contrast, Present-Hedonistic showed no statistically significant association in either model. Higher internalizing symptoms were associated with lower Past-Positive time perspective (PET: b = -.103, p = .025; PEESE: b = -.158, p < .001), whereas higher internalizing symptoms were consistently associated with higher Past-Negative time perspective (PET: b = .302, p < .001; PEESE: b = .379, p < .001).

These findings suggest that internalizing symptoms are more strongly linked to negative time perspectives (e.g., Past-Negative, Future-Negative) than to positive time perspectives, although some associations may reflect small-study effects.

In addition, higher internalizing symptoms were consistently associated with higher DBTP across both PET (b = .308, p = .001) and PEESE (b = .356, p < .001). For DBTP-r, both PET (b = .417, p = .094) and PEESE (b = .352, p = .066) suggested associations with higher internalizing symptoms, although these effects were marginal, likely due to limited degrees of freedom. In contrast, DNTP showed no reliable association with internalizing symptoms, with nonsignificant results in both PET and PEESE.

Across the RVE, PET, and PEESE analyses, higher Past-Negative, higher Present-Fatalistic, and lower Past-Positive time perspectives were consistently associated with internalizing symptoms; the Future-Negative and Future-Positive associations were supported by RVE and PEESE but were marginal or nonsignificant in PET.

In summary, people with higher addiction symptoms were more hedonistic and more fatalistic about the present, more focused on immediate rather than future consequences, viewed their past more negatively, and were less positively oriented toward the future.

Higher addiction symptoms were consistently associated with lower Future-Positive time perspective across both PET (b = -.204, p = .003) and PEESE (b = -.192, p < .001). In contrast, higher addiction symptoms were associated with higher Present-Hedonistic time perspective in both PET (b = .291, p < .001) and PEESE (b = .246, p < .001). Higher addiction symptoms were also associated with higher Past-Negative time perspective in both PET (b = .299, p = .006) and PEESE (b = .229, p < .001). In contrast, Past-Positive and Future-Negative time perspectives showed no reliable associations with addiction across models. As none of the PEESE slopes were significant, no evidence of publication bias was detected. Across the RVE, PET, and PEESE analyses, lower Future-Positive, higher Present-Hedonistic, and higher Past-Negative time perspectives were consistently associated with addiction; the Present-Fatalistic and DBTP associations were significant in RVE and PEESE but not in PET.

3. Externalizing Symptoms Category

RVE Meta-analysis: Higher externalizing symptoms were significantly associated with higher Present-Fatalistic (ZTPI: r = .299, k = 6, p = .003) and higher Past-Negative time perspectives (ZTPI: r = .294, k = 7, p = .002), as well as with lower Future-Positive (ZTPI: r = -.241, k = 9, p = .008) and lower Past-Positive time perspectives (ZTPI: r = -.168, k = 6, p = .026). The association with ZTPI Present-Hedonistic was not statistically significant, and the CFC Future estimate could not be reliably estimated (df < 4; Table 2). In summary, people with higher externalizing symptoms were more fatalistic about the present, viewed their past more negatively and less positively, and were less positively oriented toward the future.

PET-PEESE Intercept:

For Future-Positive, PET showed no reliable association with externalizing symptoms, whereas PEESE suggested that higher externalizing symptoms were associated with lower Future-Positive time perspective, although this effect was marginal (b = -.179, p = .081), indicating a potential small-study effect. For Present-Hedonistic, neither PET nor PEESE showed statistically significant associations, despite consistently positive effect estimates. For Present-Fatalistic, PET indicated no reliable association, whereas PEESE showed that higher externalizing symptoms were associated with higher Present-Fatalistic time perspective (b = .308, p = .012). Past-positive time perspective showed no reliable association with externalizing symptoms in either model. In contrast, higher externalizing symptoms were associated with higher Past-Negative time perspective, with a marginal effect in PET (b = .276, p = .080) and a statistically significant association in PEESE (b = .317, p = .011), suggesting a robust relationship after adjustment for potential small-study bias. These findings indicate that higher Present-Fatalistic and higher Past-Negative time perspectives are more consistently linked to externalizing symptoms, particularly in bias-adjusted analyses.

PET-PEESE Slope: None of the PEESE slopes were significant, thus no evidence of publication bias was detected. Across the RVE, PET, and PEESE analyses, higher Past-Negative and higher Present-Fatalistic time perspectives showed the most consistent associations with externalizing symptoms; the associations of externalizing symptoms with lower Future-Positive and lower Past-Positive time perspectives observed in RVE were marginal or nonsignificant in the biasadjusted models.

4. PTSD Symptoms Category

RVE Meta-analysis: Higher PTSD symptoms were significantly associated with higher Past-Negative (ZTPI: r = .325, k = 14, p < .001), higher DBTP (ZTPI: r = .287, k = 5, p = .014; df < 4, interpret with caution), higher Present-Fatalistic (ZTPI: r = .229, k = 13, p < .001), higher Future-Negative (ZTPI: r = .196, k = 3, p = .041; df < 4, interpret with caution), and higher Present-Hedonistic time perspectives (ZTPI: r = .147, k = 11, p = .048), as well as with lower Future-Positive time perspective (ZTPI: r = -.063, k = 13, p = .043). No statistically significant association was observed with ZTPI Past-Positive time perspective. In summary, people with higher PTSD symptoms viewed their past more negatively, were more fatalistic and more hedonistic about the present, and were slightly less positively oriented toward the future.

PET-PEESE Intercept:

For Future-Positive, PET showed no reliable association with PTSD symptoms, whereas PEESE suggested that higher PTSD symptoms were associated with lower Future-Positive time perspective, although this effect was marginal (b = -.077, p = .078). In contrast, higher PTSD symptoms were consistently associated with higher Future-Negative time perspective in both PET (b = .142, p = .011) and PEESE (b = .178, p = .013). For Present-Hedonistic, PET indicated that higher PTSD symptoms were associated with lower Present-Hedonistic time perspective (b = -.302, p = .010), but this association was not supported in PEESE, suggesting possible small-sample bias. For Present-Fatalistic, PET suggested an association in the positive direction (b = .183, p = .068), which was statistically significant in PEESE, indicating that higher PTSD symptoms were associated with higher Present-Fatalistic time perspective (b = .215, p = .002). For Past-Positive, PET showed no reliable association, whereas PEESE suggested an association of marginal significance with lower Past-Positive time perspective (b = -.180, p = .059). In contrast, higher PTSD symptoms were associated with higher Past-Negative time perspective, with a marginal effect in PET (b = .150, p = .077) and a statistically significant association in PEESE (b = .233, p < .001). Finally, the DBTP index showed a robust positive association with PTSD symptoms in PEESE (b = .517, p = .019), with a marginal association in PET (b = .736, p = .092). These findings indicate that higher negative time perspectives, particularly Future-Negative, Present-Fatalistic, and Past-Negative, as well as DBTP, are most consistently associated with higher PTSD symptoms, while discrepancies between PET and PEESE estimates likely reflect publication or small-sample bias.

PET-PEESE Slope and Vevea-Woods: Most PET and PEESE models showed nonsignificant slope estimates, suggesting limited evidence of publication bias overall. However, a significant slope in the PEESE model for the Future-Negative dimension indicated potential smallstudy effects in that specific association. The Vevea and Woods sensitivity analysis for Future-Negative could not be conducted: with only four independent clusters, the underlying random-effects model could not be reliably estimated, consistent with the df < 4 flag for this estimate in Table 2. For the PTSD dimensions with sufficient clusters, sensitivity results are reported in Table S6-12: the Past-Negative association was essentially unchanged under assumed moderate and severe selection (b = .314 and .305, vs. .320 unadjusted), whereas the Present-Hedonistic association attenuated under moderate selection (b = .074) and reversed sign under severe selection (b = -.043), indicating that it is not robust to strong publication bias. Across the RVE, PET, and PEESE analyses, higher Future-Negative, higher Present-Fatalistic, higher Past-Negative, and higher DBTP were most consistently associated with PTSD symptoms, whereas the Future-Positive and Present-Hedonistic associations were not confirmed in the bias-adjusted models.

5. Neuroticism Category

RVE Meta-analysis: Higher neuroticism was significantly associated with higher Past-Negative (ZTPI: r = .431, k = 13, p < .001) and higher Present-Fatalistic time perspectives (ZTPI: r = .209, k = 11, p = .013), as well as with lower Past-Positive time perspective (ZTPI: r = -.201, k = 10, p = .001). In contrast, associations with ZTPI Future-Positive and Present-Hedonistic time perspectives were not statistically significant. In summary, more neurotic individuals viewed their past more negatively and less positively, and were more fatalistic about the present.

PET-PEESE Intercept:

For Future-Positive, Present-Hedonistic, and Present-Fatalistic, neither PET nor PEESE showed reliable associations with neuroticism (all p > .10), although Present-Fatalistic exhibited a significant slope term in PEESE (b = 24.09, p = .043), suggesting a potential small-study effect. Higher neuroticism was associated with lower Past-Positive time perspective, with a marginal effect in PET (b = -.221, p = .063) and a statistically significant association in PEESE (b = -.220, p = .011). In contrast, higher neuroticism was associated with higher Past-Negative time perspective in PEESE (b = .325, p = .024), whereas the PET estimate was not statistically significant.

These findings indicate that higher neuroticism is more consistently linked to higher Past-Negative time perspective, while a more positive view of the past may be associated with lower neuroticism.

Evidence for publication bias was limited across most models.

PET-PEESE Slope and Vevea-Woods:

Because Present-Fatalistic showed a significant slope term in PEESE (b = 24.09, p = .043), a Vevea and Woods sensitivity analysis was conducted.

Under an assumed moderate one-tailed selection pattern, the adjusted estimate (b = .169) remained close to the unadjusted estimate (b = .215), but under severe selection it attenuated to near zero (b = .047), indicating that this association is not robust to strong publication bias. Across the RVE, PET, and PEESE analyses, higher Past-Negative and lower Past-Positive time perspectives were the most consistent correlates of neuroticism; the Present-Fatalistic association found in RVE was not supported in the bias-adjusted models and may partly reflect small-study effects.

6. Impulsivity Category

RVE Meta-analysis: Higher impulsivity was significantly associated with higher Present-Hedonistic (ZTPI: r = .350, k = 12, p < .001) and lower Future-Positive time perspectives (ZTPI: r = -.314, k = 14, p < .001). The association with Present-Fatalistic was marginal (ZTPI: r = .212, k = 8, p = .089). In contrast, associations with ZTPI Past-Positive and Past-Negative time perspectives were not statistically significant. The CFC Future estimate could not be reliably estimated (df < 4; Table 2).

In summary, more impulsive individuals were more hedonistic about the present, less positively oriented toward the future, and marginally more fatalistic about the present.

PET-PEESE Intercept:

For Future-Positive, PEESE indicated that higher impulsivity was associated with lower Future-Positive time perspective (b = -.285, p = .002), whereas the PET estimate was not statistically significant (b = -.207, p = .110). Higher impulsivity was consistently associated with higher Present-Hedonistic time perspective in both PET (b = .328, p = .039) and PEESE (b = .339, p = .006), indicating a robust association. Present-Fatalistic showed no reliable association with impulsivity in either model. For Past-Positive, both PET (b = -.264, p = .155) and PEESE (b = -.165, p = .081) suggested marginal associations in the negative direction, indicating a potential but inconsistent protective role. In contrast, higher impulsivity was associated with higher Past-Negative time perspective, with a statistically significant association in PEESE (b = .278, p = .018) and a marginal effect in PET (b = .443, p = .075). Across time perspective dimensions, slope estimates were nonsignificant, indicating no consistent evidence of publication bias.

PET-PEESE Slope:

All slope terms in PET and PEESE models were nonsignificant, suggesting no systematic evidence of publication bias in the associations between time perspective and impulsivity. Across the RVE, PET, and PEESE analyses, higher Present-Hedonistic time perspective was consistently associated with impulsivity, and lower Future-Positive was supported by RVE and PEESE (nonsignificant in PET); associations with the past-oriented dimensions were inconsistent across methods.

Meta-Analysis Using Imputed Data (Supplementary S9)

Across the six diagnostic domains, 602 of the 1,765 effect-size entries (34.1%) were missing and imputed (range across domains: 11.4% to 42.4%; Table S9-1). The imputed results were generally consistent with the complete-case results: no association changed direction, pooled magnitudes were essentially unchanged, and no significant association became nonsignificant. The only changes were that some associations that were nonsignificant with observed data reached significance after imputation, namely Present-Hedonistic with internalizing symptoms and Present-Fatalistic, Past-Positive, and Past-Negative with impulsivity in the main analyses, and Past-Positive and Past-Negative with the internalizing symptoms and substance use subcategories (Tables S9-2, S9-4, and S9 -6). This is consistent with the fact that imputation uses more available data than deletion approaches; however, these newly significant estimates depend on the imputation model's assumptions and should not be interpreted as new findings. Full results and interpretations are reported in Supplementary Material S9. A version of the ZTPI dataset with originally missing values explicitly marked as NA (i.e., values that were subsequently imputed) is provided in Supplementary Material S16.

Discussion

This study provides the most comprehensive quantitative synthesis to date of the associations between all major dimensions of time perspective recognized in contemporary theory and both mental illnesses and the transdiagnostically relevant personality traits of neuroticism and impulsivity. Our findings reinforce the transdiagnostic relevance of time perspective, particularly in relation to internalizing symptoms, addiction, PTSD symptoms, externalizing symptoms, neuroticism, and impulsivity. Overall, among the available instruments, the ZTPI and its adaptations emerged as the most widely used and psychometrically established tools for assessing time perspective across clinical and nonclinical populations. Although the CFC demonstrated broad utility, it lacks the multidimensional scope of the ZTPI for profiling psychopathology. The AATI, despite assessing six dimensions, has been used less frequently in mental illness research, currently limiting conclusions regarding its relevance to psychopathology.

Transdiagnostic Breadth of Time Perspective Dimensions

RVE meta-analyses supported the transdiagnostic relevance of all time perspective dimensions. Specifically, (1) higher Future-Positive was associated with lower levels of nearly all mental illnesses and relevant personality traits (except neuroticism), including internalizing symptoms, addiction, PTSD symptoms, externalizing symptoms, and impulsivity; (2) higher Future-Negative (based on fewer studies) was associated with higher internalizing and PTSD symptoms; (3) higher Present-Hedonistic was associated with higher addiction, PTSD symptoms, and impulsivity; (4) higher Present-Fatalistic was consistently associated with higher levels across all categories; (5) higher Past-Positive was associated with lower internalizing symptoms, externalizing symptoms, neuroticism, and PTSD symptoms; (6) higher Past-Negative was associated with higher levels of nearly all conditions except impulsivity; and (7) higher DBTP was only associated with higher internalizing and PTSD symptoms. Although PET analyses attenuated some associations, PEESE results largely aligned with the RVE findings. Because PET is the more conservative approach, the convergence of RVE and PEESE supports the robustness of the transdiagnostic associations and highlights distinct patterns across mental illnesses and relevant personality traits.

An important qualification concerns the substantial heterogeneity observed across analyses.

Because most I² values exceeded 75%, the pooled correlations represent averages over widely varying study-level effects, and the strength of any given association may differ markedly across samples, measures, settings, and study designs. What remained stable, however, is direction of association: Past-Negative and Present-Fatalistic orientations were consistently elevated and Past-Positive and Future-Positive orientations consistently diminished across nearly every condition examined. A common temporal signature that emerges in direction, if not in uniform magnitude, from such varied evidence supports rather than undermines the transdiagnostic account, indicating patterns that generalize across contexts rather than reflecting idiosyncrasies of particular samples or measures. The transdiagnostic patterns identified here are therefore best interpreted as robust average tendencies whose expression varies across contexts. Identifying moderators that help explain this heterogeneity, including population characteristics, measurement instruments, and study design features, will be valuable for translating these average associations into clinically useful predictions.

Varying Levels of Transdiagnostic Breadth Among Time Perspective Dimensions

Distinct Time Perspective Profiles Across Mental Health Clusters

Distinct profiles were supported by Cohen's q comparisons (Tables 7 and 8) and the time perspective profiles presented in Table 2 and Figures 3 and 7. Based on the primary analyses using the full dataset, the broad internalizing symptoms category and neuroticism did not differ significantly across the five ZTPI dimensions and showed highly similar temporal profiles (Table 7), supporting their conceptualization as comorbid clusters. However, a clinically meaningful distinction emerged in the Future-Positive time perspective. Internalizing symptoms were significantly associated with reduced Future-Positive time perspective, whereas neuroticism was not (Table 2). This nuanced difference suggests that diminished future positivity, when occurring alongside negative past and present time perspectives, may reflect a symptom-related constriction of future-oriented cognition, such as hopelessness and pessimistic expectancy, characteristic of internalizing disorders rather than a general feature of trait negative emotionality. In contrast, neuroticism appears to be more strongly linked to heightened affective reactivity and negative appraisal of past and present experiences, without a comparable reduction in Future-Positive time perspective.

Addiction and impulsivity were primarily associated with higher Present-Fatalistic and Present-Hedonistic time perspectives and lower Future-Positive time perspective. Addiction was additionally characterized by a stronger Past-Negative time perspective (Table 2) and, relative to impulsivity, by significantly higher Future-Positive and lower Present-Hedonistic time perspective (Table 7). These patterns largely support their conceptualization as comorbid clusters. Their similar temporal profiles may reflect impaired temporal integration that favors immediate reward or resignation toward future outcomes, highlighting a pattern distinct from that observed in the internalizing symptoms and neuroticism cluster. Furthermore, compared with the present-and futurefocused profile of impulsivity, addiction additionally involved a more pronounced negative past orientation, suggesting that substance-related behaviors may be reinforced not only by reward immediacy but also by persistent negative autobiographical representations.

Externalizing symptoms showed a similar profile to internalizing symptoms across all five ZTPI dimensions, but with consistently weaker associations (Table 2) and significantly lower Future-Positive and Past-Negative time perspectives (Table 7). This pattern indicates partial overlap in transdiagnostic vulnerability rather than identical temporal profiles, and suggests that, in externalizing symptoms, negative time perspective is relatively more projected toward the future than the past. In addition, the stronger and more consistent associations observed for internalizing symptoms (n = 54-67), compared with externalizing symptoms (n = 6-9), are supported by a substantially larger evidence base. This pattern indicates that temporal distortions may be more central and pervasive in internalizing conditions, aligning with sustained affective distress, rumination, and hopelessness that constrain future-oriented cognition. In contrast, the weaker and less stable associations for externalizing symptoms, based on smaller effect sizes and fewer contributing studies, may suggest that time perspective plays a less central and more variable role. Overall, internalizing and externalizing symptoms overlap at a broad transdiagnostic level but remain differentiated by the strength, consistency, and pervasiveness of time perspective disruption.

These interpretations are consistent with prior work on the comorbidity of internalizing symptoms and neuroticism (Griffith et al., 2010; Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2015; McNaughton & Glue, 2020), addiction and impulsivity (Grant et al., 2010; Kozak et al., 2019; Vassileva et al., 2019), and shared psychopathology across internalizing and externalizing domains (e.g., Caspi et al., 2014 Caspi et al., , 2020 Caspi et al., , 2024)). The present findings suggest that time perspective may capture distinct temporalcognitive signatures across clusters and may further help differentiate mental illnesses from related negative personality traits while clarifying patterns of comorbidity among them.

Similar Time Perspective Patterns Within Mental Illness Clusters

Subcategory analyses (Tables 5 and 6) and Figure 7 support the hypothesis that mental illnesses within the same cluster share similar time perspective profiles, except where insufficient data precluded such analyses. Within the internalizing cluster, anxiety did not differ significantly from depression (Table 8), differing only descriptively in that depression showed a slightly stronger negative association with Future-Positive time perspective (Table 5). Similarly, alcohol use did not differ meaningfully from substance use within the addiction category (Table 8), differing only descriptively in that substance use showed weaker associations with Present-Fatalistic and Past-Negative time perspectives based on fewer contributing studies (Table 6). Notably, internet addiction demonstrated a time perspective profile more closely aligned with internalizing symptoms, which may indicate highly shared underlying psychopathology.

Theoretical Contributions

Our results offer three central theoretical contributions. First, this study bridges categorical and dimensional approaches in psychopathology. The transdiagnostic relevance of time perspective dimensions helps explain the widespread existence of comorbidities across mental illnesses (Caspi et al., 2014; Krueger et al., 2018). Its dimensional structure illuminates how various conditions are interconnected, grounded in a comprehensive and methodologically rigorous meta-analytic synthesis of empirical evidence. These findings reinforce the view that psychopathology is not merely a collection of isolated symptom clusters, but is fundamentally structured and interconnected, including through varying degrees of disruption in individuals' time perspectives. Meanwhile, time perspective can differentiate mental illness clusters through structured temporal profiles. For instance, present fatalism appears to be particularly shared across addiction subcategories and impulsivity, whereas future negativity and lack of past positivity are more characteristic of internalizing conditions. These findings offer a temporally grounded framework for understanding psychopathology as both convergent and divergent across disorders, highlighting shared disruptions in temporal experience alongside distinctive time perspective profiles for specific conditions.

Second, this study bridges phenomenological psychopathology and clinical practice by providing empirical evidence for the transdiagnostic relevance of time perspective across mental illnesses. Although phenomenological theories have long posited temporality as central to psychopathology, the empirical time perspective literature has developed largely independently of these theories, and the evidence it offers for their claims has remained fragmented across individual studies. Meanwhile, clinical interventions such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), and psychodynamic therapy frequently address distorted temporal experiences (e.g., hopelessness, rumination, impulsivity) without grounding them in a unified theoretical or empirical framework. Our findings support key phenomenological insights. For example, the association between a negative past perspective and internalizing disorders is consistent with Fuchs' notion of temporal stagnation in depression (Fuchs, 2013). In this phenomenological account, depression involves a slowing, and at its extreme a standstill, of lived time: the person falls out of step with the shared time of their social environment, basic drive and motivation are inhibited, and experience becomes dominated by an unalterable past while the future appears blocked. Similarly, a diminished future orientation maps onto impairments in goal setting and motivation across diagnoses, including reduced positive future-directed thinking in depression (MacLeod & Salaminiou, 2001), diminished goal-directed motivation and avolition in schizophrenia (Foussias & Remington, 2010), difficulty sustaining goal-directed behavior over time in ADHD (Barkley, 1997; Carelli & Wiberg, 2012), and steeper discounting of delayed rewards in substance use disorders (MacKillop et al., 2011). By linking specific temporal disruptions to diagnostic patterns and treatment targets, our results support the integration of time perspective theory into both classification systems and therapeutic design.

Strengthening this connection among theory, evidence, and clinical practice offers a promising foundation for advancing psychopathology research, diagnosis, and treatment.

Third, this study promotes deeper exploration of temporality as a broader framework for organizing mental illnesses. While time perspective captures explicit, macro-level aspects of temporality, additional dimensions such as implicit, micro-level, and embodied time may offer more nuanced and precise insights into the structure of conscious experience and consequently, psychopathology. Further empirical work is needed to investigate how these dimensions relate to mental illness in order to establish a more comprehensive and empirically grounded framework.

Temporality holds promise as a robust transdiagnostic model that could complement existing dimensional approaches (e.g., HiTOP, RDoC) and help mitigate the limitations of current diagnostic systems, such as the DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) and the ICD-11 (World Health Organization, 2019).

Emerging tools such as the Transdiagnostic Assessment of Temporal Experience (TATE; Stanghellini et al., 2022) represent promising steps toward structuring mental illnesses around disruptions in temporal experience. TATE is a structured interview designed to assess abnormalities in lived temporality across psychiatric conditions, emphasizing the transdiagnostic nature of temporal disturbances. Rather than organizing psychopathology solely around symptom clusters, TATE focuses on how mental disorders alter an individual's relationship with time by operationalizing subjective temporal experience into seven core dimensions: anomalies of synchrony (difficulty aligning internal time with external or social time), anomalies of time structure (fragmentation or disorganization of temporal flow), anomalies of implicit time flow (altered felt passage of time, such as acceleration, slowing, or stagnation), anomalies of explicit time flow (distortions in the perceived directionality of time), anomalous experiences of the past (e.g., fixation, rumination, or fragmented autobiographical memory), anomalous experiences of the present (e.g., fatalistic detachment, urgency, or disengaged repetition), and anomalous experiences of the future (e.g., impaired future imagination, hyper-planning, or anticipation of threat). By rating the intensity and impact of these experiences, TATE has the potential to provide a systematic framework for examining temporal disruptions across psychiatric conditions and illustrates how disturbances in past, present, and future experience may cut across traditional diagnostic boundaries. Our meta-analytic findings converge with this framework: the transdiagnostic pattern of elevated Past-Negative and Present-Fatalistic orientations and lower Past-Positive and Future-Positive orientations parallels the anomalous experiences of the past (e.g., negative view of the past), the present (e.g., fatalism), and the future (e.g., negative view of the future, no future horizon) that TATE is designed to capture, suggesting that questionnaire-based time perspective dimensions and phenomenological assessments of temporality index overlapping disturbances.

Implications For Clinical Intervention

Demonstrating whether specific time perspective dimensions are associated with particular mental illnesses or clusters of mental illnesses may enhance the precision of clinical interventions. On the one hand, if these associations prove to be causal, identifying the time perspective profile of a given disorder or cluster could inform targeted intervention strategies and support the customization of treatment programs, particularly in cases of comorbidity or dual diagnosis. On the other hand, many existing therapies incorporate temporal components, which may help explain their transdiagnostic effectiveness. For example, transdiagnostic cognitive behavioral therapy has demonstrated efficacy for anxiety, depression, eating disorders, personality disorders, and substance use disorders (Joaquim et al., 2023). Some CBT therapeutic approaches explicitly target time perspective. Competitive Memory Training (COMET), for instance, directly addresses negative and positive past perspectives by encouraging patients to recall positive memories in order to activate adaptive emotional states (Balcı et al., 2022). COMET has been shown to be more effective in reducing symptoms of depression (Ekkers et al., 2011; Korrelboom et al., 2012), anxiety (Staring et al., 2016), and eating disorders (Korrelboom et al., 2009) compared with other common treatments (e.g., CBT combined with interpersonal psychotherapy) in randomized controlled trials. Direct evidence that time perspective itself is malleable is emerging but still limited. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 studies (13 randomized controlled trials; N = 1,914), Chew et al. (2024) found that time perspective-based interventions, most of which used episodic future thinking to increase the salience of the future at decision points, produced significant behavioral change (e.g., reduced food intake; Hollis-Hansen et al., 2020), and prospection-focused techniques have likewise been proposed for treating depression (Roepke & Seligman, 2016). However, most of these studies assessed shortterm behavioral outcomes rather than change in trait-level time perspective, and evidence in clinical populations is still scarce. Whether dispositional time perspective profiles can be durably modified by intervention therefore remains to be established.

Notwithstanding these open questions, framing interventions through a time perspective lens may facilitate the selection of more precise strategies tailored to the temporal profile of specific mental illnesses. For example, interventions targeting Past-Negative and Present-Fatalistic time perspectives may be central to treating depression, whereas strategies addressing Present-Hedonistic time perspective may be particularly relevant for substance use. Furthermore, intervention strategies across therapeutic approaches could be systematically organized according to their temporal focus.

Such organization may support the development of a structured "toolkit" of strategies that can be flexibly selected based on individual patient needs.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations warrant consideration and point to directions for future research. First, despite employing a comprehensive literature search strategy, the number of studies within certain disorder categories (e.g., externalizing symptoms, PTSD symptoms) was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of findings in these domains. Second, although DBTP, DBTP-r, and DNTP emerged as informative indices, relatively few studies have examined their applicability beyond internalizing symptoms and addiction, potentially constraining conclusions about their broader relevance. Third, there remains a notable lack of research examining time perspective profiles in disorders such as bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, borderline personality disorder, and other personality disorders, limiting understanding of their temporal dynamics. Fourth, because many studies did not report correlations directly, alternative effect sizes (e.g., β coefficients) were converted into correlations, which may introduce methodological variability and affect the precision of pooled estimates. Fifth, most included studies were cross-sectional, limiting causal inference and the ability to examine temporal relationships over time. This highlights a notable gap in longitudinal, experimental or natural experimental designs needed to track within-individual changes and to support causal inference in the association between time perspective and mental illness. Sixth, although existing time perspective measures provide valuable insights, their capacity to precisely profile specific mental illnesses or clusters of disorders requires further refinement and validation. ZTPI items intentionally integrate both cognitive and evaluative components, consistent with the original conceptualization of time perspective. This raises the question of the relative contribution of temporal cognition and valence appraisal to the observed associations with mental illnesses. Future measurement development could aim to more clearly differentiate these components, which may help refine theoretical models and inform more precisely targeted intervention strategies. Finally, temporality encompasses broader and multifaceted dimensions beyond those captured by standard time perspective measures, including those assessed by instruments such as TATE (Stanghellini et al., 2022). These dimensions warrant further investigation to clarify their relevance for psychopathological mechanisms and clinical practice.

Methodological limitations of the evidence synthesis should also be noted. Although RVE was used to account for statistical dependence among multiple effect sizes from the same studies, substantial between-study heterogeneity remained across most models. This heterogeneity indicates that the synthesized effects likely reflect multiple underlying population effects rather than a single common effect size; therefore, summary estimates should be interpreted as average associations. In addition, publication-bias diagnostics (e.g., PET-PEESE and selection models) were applied as sensitivity analyses. Although PET-PEESE provides a regression-based framework for detecting and adjusting for small-study effects and for evaluating potential publication bias, these approaches assume a relatively homogeneous underlying effect and may be less stable when between-study heterogeneity is substantial or when the number of studies is small. Accordingly, evidence of smallstudy effects should be interpreted cautiously and considered in conjunction with the overall pattern of results. The a priori weight-function sensitivity analyses complement these methods: the examined associations remained directionally stable under moderate assumed selection, although the neuroticism-Present-Fatalistic, PTSD-Present-Hedonistic, and depression-Future-Positive associations were not robust to severe selection.

Beyond the gaps discussed above, future research could explore several promising directions. First, the development and validation of assessment tools specifically tailored to measure time perspective or temporality within the context of mental illness represents a critical avenue for advancing this field. Such instruments could provide more nuanced and clinically relevant insights into the temporal dynamics underlying psychiatric disorders. Second, future research could focus on developing tools that more directly assess structural aspects of temporal projection, such as the capacity to imagine and anticipate future states that support goal-directed behavior (Hanks, 1990).

Clarifying these processes would enhance understanding of how individuals conceptualize and relate to different temporal frames. In turn, such tools could inform the development of targeted interventions aimed at addressing maladaptive temporal experiences commonly observed in clinical practice, such as persistent past negativity or present fatalism.

Future research should also extend beyond ZTPI dimensions by more fully leveraging the intertwined relationship between temporal experience and psychopathology to enhance both diagnostic and intervention practices. Clinical studies could further explore theoretical frameworks from phenomenological psychiatry, such as those proposed by Fuchs (2005 Fuchs ( , 2007 Fuchs ( , 2009 Fuchs ( , 2013 Fuchs ( , 2019)), by incorporating more comprehensive assessments of temporality into research aimed at developing effective intervention strategies. For instance, employing TATE (Stanghellini et al., 2022) could provide a broader and more nuanced evaluation of temporal disturbances across mental disorders. Intervention strategies targeting embodiment, defined as the integration of temporal experience with bodily states and action readiness (Fuchs, 2013), represent another promising direction. For example, mindfulness-based interventions, whose core practices such as the body scan cultivate present-moment bodily awareness (Kabat-Zinn, 2003), have been linked to altered subjective time experience (Morin & Grondin, 2024) and may thereby engage the implicit, prereflective flow of lived time (Fuchs, 2013), complementing cognitive approaches that target explicit time perspective. In addition, the growing research focus on self-continuity, defined as the subjective sense of coherence between past, present, and future self-representations (Sedikides et al., 2023), highlights the need to move beyond conventional time perspective models to capture the complex, multilayered nature of temporal experience in mental illness.

Conclusion

Our systematic review and meta-analyses provide comprehensive evidence for the role of time perspective as a transdiagnostic process across mental illnesses and related personality traits.

The findings indicate robust associations between multiple time perspective dimensions and internalizing symptoms, addiction, neuroticism, PTSD symptoms, externalizing symptoms, and impulsivity. Future-Positive, Present-Fatalistic, and Past-Negative time perspectives emerged as the most broadly transdiagnostic dimensions across mental illnesses and relevant personality traits, whereas Present-Hedonistic time perspective showed more differential associations, particularly with addiction and impulsivity. Subcategory analyses revealed largely similar time perspective profiles within internalizing and addiction subcategories, suggesting shared underlying mechanisms across these conditions. Overall, these findings deepen understanding of the transdiagnostic relevance of time perspective and underscore the need for further research to examine its implications for intervention and treatment development.

Across the RVE, PET, and PEESE analyses, higher Past-Negative time perspective was the most consistent correlate of anxiety (significant in RVE and PEESE, marginal in PET); the Present-Fatalistic and DBTP associations were significant in RVE and PEESE but not in PET, and the association of higher anxiety with lower Past-Positive time perspective was observed only in RVE.

RVE meta-analyses revealed varying levels of transdiagnostic breadth across time perspective dimensions. Consistent with prior meta-analyses, the present study confirms that the ZTPI Future-Positive dimension is broadly associated with a range of mental illnesses and relevant personality traits. Notably, it is the first to synthesize evidence showing that ZTPI Present-Fatalistic and Past-Negative dimensions demonstrate the strongest and most consistent associations across nearly all conditions, with the exception of impulsivity. These results indicate that Past-Negative, Present-Fatalistic, and diminished Future-Positive perspectives demonstrate broader transdiagnostic breadth across mental illnesses, whereas other time perspective dimensions exhibit more conditionspecific relevance, reflecting lower levels of transdiagnostic breadth. Importantly, Present-Fatalistic and Past-Negative perspectives showed significantly stronger transdiagnostic associations with mental illnesses and relevant personality traits than the Future-Positive dimension, despite being understudied. These findings highlight present fatalism and past negativity as potential core targets for intervention and underscore the need for greater emphasis on these dimensions in future transdiagnostic research.
