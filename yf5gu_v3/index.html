To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Stress generation describes processes by which individuals actively contribute to the occurrence of self-generated stressful events. Personal characteristics like neuroticism or psychopathology have been found to predict the occurrence of major life stressors over several months or years. However, a profound understanding of stress generation over shorter time periods in people's daily lives is still missing. Here, we integrated recent research on personenvironment transactions from personality psychology into the clinical stress generation literature to develop a process model of stress generation. This process model posits that stress generation unfolds across multiple timescales and that short-term dynamics between momentary, state-level personal characteristics and daily hassles are the building blocks for established long-term stress generation effects. To evaluate a core aspect of this model, we conducted a systematic literature search on stress generation in daily life (45 studies, Ntotal = 9'818). Results show that both dispositional traits and momentary states predict the occurrence of daily hassles, supporting the existence and relevance of stress generation in daily life. However, future research on stress generation across timescales is needed to test the theorized link between stress generation in daily life and long-term stress generation effects.

A Systematic Review and Theoretical Framework of Stress Generation in Daily Life

Stress is a common and normative experience, but some people face more stressful events than others (Liu & Alloy, 2010; Rnic et al., 2023). Stressful events are not entirely random but systematically linked to personal characteristics -a phenomenon called stress generation (Hammen, 1991). For example, high levels of depressive symptoms, neuroticism, or personality pathology have been found to predict the occurrence of stressful events like divorce or job loss (Allen et al., 2022; Goldstein et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2024; Liu & Kleiman, 2012; Luo et al., 2023; Santee et al., 2023; Taylor & Snyder, 2023). These stressful events, in turn, can lead to mental health problems, decreased well-being, or unwanted personality changes (Bühler et al., 2024; Haehner, Krämer, et al., 2025; Hammen, 2005; Luhmann et al., 2012; Rnic et al., 2023). Understanding stress generation processes and developing effective interventions is thus of central practical relevance to prevent such unfavorable developments.

Although there is convincing evidence for the general phenomenon of stress generation, existing theory and research have primarily focused on variables predicting the occurrence of major life stressors over several months or years (Hammen, 2006; Harkness & Washburn, 2016; Liu et al., 2024; Rnic et al., 2023; Santee et al., 2023). For example, various studies have used long-term longitudinal designs to examine whether and how personality pathology is linked to the occurrence of major life stressors (Allen et al., 2022; Conway et al., 2018; Powers et al., 2013). This perspective has been valuable to prove the practical relevance of stress generation, but it provided little insight into stress generation processes over shorter time periods in people's daily life. A process perspective on stress generation that focuses on momentary person-stressor transactions is needed to (1) better understand the mechanisms of stress generation, (2) develop a comprehensive theoretical model of stress generation across different timescales, and (3) identify ways to disrupt stress generation processes (Liu, 2013; Liu et al., 2024).

In this review, we propose a process model that focuses on stress generation in daily life by integrating insights and methods from personality psychology into clinical stress generation literature. This model seeks to inspire future research on stress generation across timescales and is intended to serve as a starting point for a thorough investigation of within-person processes leading to stress generation. Furthermore, we summarize and integrate existing research on stress generation in daily life in a meta-analytic review to evaluate core aspects of this model.

We conclude this review by outlining key directions for future research.

Theory and Research on Stress Generation

In the following sections, we provide a brief overview of existing theory and research on stress generation that provided the basis for the development of the process model.

Defining Stress and Stress Generation

An essential part of many stress definitions is the differentiation between stress exposure and stress response (e.g., Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; McEwen, 2000). Stress exposure describes the confrontation with stressful events that exceed personal resources and can elicit a stress response. The stress response is an alarm reaction that comprises physiological and psychological changes, such as hormone releases and feelings of distress. Comprehensive theoretical models have been developed to describe how individual differences in personal characteristics -individual patterns of thoughts, feelings and behaviors -predict differences in stress reactions (e.g., Almeida, 2005; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Stress generation theory, in contrast, seeks to explain how individual differences in personal characteristics predict differences in stress exposure.

In this review, we differentiate between two types of stress exposure, daily hassles and major life stressors, and we use the term stressful events when we refer to both types. Daily hassles like relationship conflicts or a traffic jam describe irregular life experiences that can disrupt daily life in the short term, whereas major life stressors like divorce or job loss are rarely occurring events that lead to lasting changes in people's lives (Haehner et al., 2024; Kanner et al., 1981; Luhmann et al., 2021; Monroe, 1983). As such, both definitions focus on the objective impact of these stressful events on people's life (instead of the subjective experience) to allow for a clear distinction between stress exposure and stressor response, which is necessary to test stress generation theory.

The concept of stress generation was initially introduced by Hammen (1991) to explain the observation that depressed people experience more stressful events than non-depressed people. According to the Stress Generation Hypothesis (Hammen, 1991), individuals with depressive symptomatology tend to contribute more to the occurrence of self-generated stressful events, like job loss or divorce, compared to those without depressive symptomatology. Such self-generated stressful events are referred to as "dependent events" (Hammen, 1991, p. 555), while "independent events", like natural catastrophes or the death of a loved one, are considered to be largely unrelated to personal characteristics and behavior.

As follows from the Stress Generation Hypothesis, research has traditionally focused on the role of depression in stress generation (Hammen, 2006). However, a growing literature suggests that other personal characteristics are also systematically linked to the occurrence of dependent stressful events (Liu et al., 2024; Rnic et al., 2023; Santee et al., 2023). Thus, stress generation can be more generally defined as processes by which individuals actively contribute to the occurrence of dependent stressful events.

Empirical Evidence for Stress Generation

Over the last 30 years, the Stress Generation Hypothesis has been tested in numerous longitudinal studies and the theoretical basis of stress generation has been successfully expanded (Allen et al., 2022; Davila et al., 1997; Elliot et al., 2011; Flynn & Rudolph, 2011; Goldstein et al., 2021; Harkness & Washburn, 2016; Hasegawa et al., 2023; Howe et al., 2017; Liu, 2013; Luo et al., 2023; Moriishi & Maeda, 2025; Skobic et al., 2024). The lion's share of existing theory and research has focused on long-term stress generation, relying on longitudinal designs with assessments every few months or years to investigate whether and how personal characteristics predict the subsequent occurrence of dependent major life stressors.

In a recent meta-analysis, Rnic et al. (2023) integrated evidence from 95 longitudinal studies on clinical predictors of long-term stress generation. Across these studies, a mediumsized link between general psychopathology and the occurrence of dependent stressful events was found (r = .23), suggesting that people with elevated psychopathology experience more dependent stress than people with lower psychopathology levels. These predictive links seemed to generalize across different types of psychopathology, highlighting that stress generation is not specific to depression (Hammen, 2006; Liu et al., 2024).

Furthermore, there is evidence that not only clinical disorders and general psychopathology but also broader risk factors, such as maladaptive interpersonal behaviors, can predict the occurrence of dependent major life stressors. Santee et al. (2023) meta-analytically examined different clusters of risk factors that were theorized to predict stress generation.

Integrating evidence from 70 longitudinal studies, the authors found that (1) maladaptive emotion regulation behaviors, (2) disinhibition and antagonism, (3) avoidance, (4) repetitive negative thinking, (5) negative cognitive content, (6) excessive self-standards, (7) pervasive negative affect, and (8) other-oriented focus predicted the occurrence of dependent stressful events (.10 ≤ r ≤ .26). For example, pervasive negative affect as captured by the personality trait neuroticism has emerged as a robust predictor of several dependent major life stressors such as unemployment and divorce (E. D. Beck & Jackson, 2022; Santee et al., 2023; Soto, 2019).

The practical relevance of these stress generation effects is underlined by evidence that major life stressors predict the onset of mental disorders, well-being decreases, and increases in maladaptive personal characteristics (Bühler et al., 2024; Cohen et al., 2019; Hammen, 2005; Kendler et al., 1999). Stress generation processes may thus lead to a vicious cycle of maladaptive personal characteristics and stressful events (Hammen, 2006; Liu, 2013). For example, Goldstein et al. (2020) found that depressive symptoms predicted the occurrence of dependent major life stressors, which, in turn, predicted an increase in depressive symptoms over time. Similarly, in their meta-analysis, Rnic et al. (2023) found a significant indirect effect of stressful events on increases in psychopathology ( = .04), indicating that generated stress can exacerbate psychopathology.

In summary, existing research and meta-analytic reviews provide convincing evidence for the existence and significance of long-term stress generation effects, with different types of psychopathology and psychological risk factors predicting the occurrence of dependent major life stressors over several month or years.

Theoretical Perspectives on Stress Generation

Based on this evidence, initial theoretical models of stress generation have been developed to explain the relationship across different predictors of stress generation and integrate different research lines into a coherent framework (Harkness & Washburn, 2016; Liu, 2013). These models converge on a causal chain that links distal risk factors to dependent stressful events through cognitive/personality risk factors and proximal behavioral risk factors.

For example, genetic differences are theorized to be distal risk factors of high levels of neuroticism; high levels of neuroticism, in turn, are proposed to lead to maladaptive behaviors like reassurance seeking, which should cause the occurrence of dependent stressful events. These theoretical propositions reflect the conceptual origin of the Stress Generation Hypothesis as an action theory (Hammen, 2006), suggesting that people's behaviors are powerful predictors of environmental changes (Liu, 2013).

However, contemporary theory and research on stress generation share several limitations that inhibit their ability to explain the mechanisms of stress generation and spark the development of effective interventions. First, existing theoretical accounts remain relatively vague regarding the timescale on which stress generation unfolds (Starr et al., 2025). That is, it remains unclear whether stress generation predictors, such as personality pathology or reassurance seeking, are theorized to predict the occurrence of dependent stressful events over hours, days, weeks, or years. This limitation is not specific to stress generation theory but is a common problem across psychological theories, providing little guidance for design choices in longitudinal research (Haehner, Driver, et al., 2025; Hopwood, Bleidorn, et al., 2022). So far, stress generation has mostly been examined over months and years, which likely has to do with the typical length of assessment waves in longitudinal designs in psychological science. For example, the average time lag between assessments in the meta-analyses by Rnic et al. (2023) and Santee et al. (2023) were 21 months or 16 months, respectively. As a result, we still have a limited understanding of whether and how stress generation processes unfold over shorter time periods of several hours or days.

Second, existing stress generation theories describe the predictors and outcomes of stress generation in a broad way. For instance, Liu (2013) posited that behavioral risk factors like excessive reassurance seeking predict the occurrence of dependent stressful events. However, this description leaves it unclear whether reassurance seeking predicts the occurrence of daily hassles or major life stressor and whether research should assess reassurance seeking in a given moment or a person's general tendency to seek reassurance. Thus, contemporary stress generation theories may be not specific enough to derive testable hypotheses about stress generation.

Third, existing research has focused primarily on between-person differences in stress generation, linking individual differences in depressive symptoms, neuroticism, or personality pathology to the occurrence of dependent stressful events (Allen et al., 2022; Elliot et al., 2011; Flynn & Rudolph, 2011; Goldstein et al., 2021; Hasegawa et al., 2023). This perspective matches the original proposition of the Stress Generation Hypothesis, which states that people with depressive symptomatology tend to experience more dependent stressors than those without (Hammen, 1991). However, within-person analyses examining how momentary thoughts, feelings, and behaviors predict the occurrence of stressful events for a specific individual are needed to complement this between-person approach (Modersitzki et al., 2025; Phan et al., 2025). Investigating how stress generation unfolds within individuals may also guide the development of effective interventions aimed at disrupting stress generation.

To overcome these limitations, we suggest integrating the clinical literature on stress generation with theory and research on person-environment transactions from personality science. Person-environment transactions capture the various ways in which personal and environmental variables relate to each other over time (Rauthmann, 2021). Stress generation can be conceptualized as a specific type of person-environment transactions. Theory and research on stress generation may thus benefit from integrating recent advancement in the description of person-environment transactions.

An Integrative Approach to Stress Generation

The study of person-environment transactions has a long tradition in personality science -ranging from early research on person-situation interactions (Allport, 1961; Lewin, 1936) to recent examinations of event-related personality change (Bleidorn et al., 2020; Bühler et al., 2024). These extensive research efforts have led to important advancements in the conceptualization, measurement, and explanation of person-environment transactions (Bleidorn et al., 2020; Hopwood, Wright, et al., 2022; Luhmann et al., 2021; Rauthmann, 2021; Rauthmann & Sherman, 2020). Specifically, personality science has studied processes similar to stress generation but has used different terms (e.g., selection effects), resulting in limited exchange across these disciplines (E. D. Beck & Jackson, 2022; Bleidorn et al., 2020; Denissen et al., 2019; Haehner, Naudé, et al., 2025; Lüdtke et al., 2011; Luhmann et al., 2014). Thus, integrating personality science into stress generation research is a way to advance both fields by merging theories and aggregating research findings.

Process Models on Person-Environment Transactions

One of the most important theoretical advancements in personality science has been the adoption of a process perspective, connecting momentary person-environment dynamics to long-term transactions between individuals and their environment (Baumert et al., 2017; Bleidorn et al., 2020). Prominent examples of this perspective include the Whole Trait Theory (Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015), the TESSERA Framework (Wrzus & Roberts, 2017), the Integrative-State-Trait Process Model (Geukes et al., 2018), and the Person-Environment Relations Model (Rauthmann, 2021). These theories have three key aspects in common. First, they distinguish between state-and trait-level personal characteristics. Trait-level characteristics describe people's stable patterns of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors across situations, whereas states capture momentary thoughts, feelings, and behavior in a given situation (Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; A. J. Wright et al., 2026; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). For example, neuroticism is typically assessed as a stable trait capturing people's general tendency to perceive the world as threatening place, experience negative affect, and worry in stressful situations (Goldberg, 1992; Soto & John, 2017), whereas state neuroticism captures instances of negative affect or rumination in a given moment.

Second, contemporary theories on person-environment transactions focus on withinperson dynamics, describing processes and effects within individuals (Modersitzki et al., 2025).

For example, the Integrative-State-Trait Process Model (Geukes et al., 2018) posits that a person selects a behavior based on social cues in the environment and mental processing of these cues, which may become a habit over time and elicit environmental changes. This model emphasizes the dynamic interactions between people and their environment at an individual level and may be helpful for the development of effective intervention approaches.

Third, another common theme across different process theories of person-environment transactions is the causal relevance of momentary dynamics for long-term longitudinal developments (Bleidorn et al., 2020; Geukes et al., 2018; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). For example, the TESSERA Framework (Wrzus & Roberts, 2017) suggests that momentary dynamics across situations, states, and reactions are the building blocks for long-term personality trait changes.

According to this model, enduring changes in the characteristics of everyday situations and in momentary personality state expressions may accumulate over time to manifest as long-term personality development.

Integrating these ideas about person-environment transactions into stress generation research may offer new perspectives on this type of transaction and guide future research on stress generation across timescales.

A Process Model of Stress Generation

We propose a process model of stress generation that (1) distinguishes between stateand trait-level personal characteristics as predictors of stress generation, (2) connects short-term dynamics to long-term stress generation processes, and (3) offers insights into the timescale of stress generation processes (Figure 1). This model advances a set of testable hypotheses about stress generation with varying degrees of empirical support (see Table 1).

Similar to existing stress generation theories, we suggest that genes and childhood maltreatment act as distal risk factors of stress generation by making maladaptive personal characteristics more likely (Harkness & Washburn, 2016; Liu, 2013). Strong empirical evidence supports this prediction. For example, meta-analytic reviews and genome-wide association studies illustrate the effects of genetic variants on trait characteristics (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014; De Moor et al., 2012; Terracciano et al., 2010). Likewise, childhood maltreatment has been identified as a robust predictor of psychopathology and other maladaptive personal characteristics (Baldwin et al., 2024; A. T. Beck & Bredemeier, 2016; Jaffee, 2017). Furthermore, both genes and childhood trauma have been found to predict the occurrence of dependent stressful events in long-term longitudinal studies, supporting their relevance in the overall causal chain (Bahji et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024).

The next part of the causal chain -trait-level personal characteristics -are likely the most researched predictors of stress generation. Drawing on evidence from recent meta-analytic reviews, it can be expected that (1) psychopathology, (2) maladaptive emotion regulation, (3) disinhibition and antagonism, (4) avoidance, (5) repetitive negative thinking, (6) pervasive negative affect, (7) negative cognitive styles, (8) other-oriented focus, and (9) excessive selfstandards are trait characteristics that prospectively predict the occurrence of dependent stressful events (Rnic et al., 2023; Santee et al., 2023). However, in contrast to existing stress generation theories, the current process model posits that these trait-level characteristics do not directly predict the occurrence of dependent stressors but that these effects are mediated by short-term dynamics that unfold in people's daily lives. In other words, we hypothesize that links between momentary, state-level characteristics and daily hassles are the building blocks of long-term stress generation effects. For example, people with higher levels of trait neuroticism may be more likely to experience a divorce because of the accumulation of relationship conflicts triggered by momentary neurotic behaviors in daily life.

At the core of the process model are state-level personal characteristics, which depend on a person's traits and the characteristics of the situation (Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015; Rauthmann et al., 2015; Sherman et al., 2015; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). We conceptualize states as causally dependent networks of momentary thoughts (e.g., rumination), feelings (e.g., sadness), and behaviors (e.g., seeking reassurance), with behavioral states being the active ingredient in causing stress generation, reflecting the conceptual origin of stress generation theory as an action theory (Hammen, 2006). For example, momentary, state-level negative affect and negative thoughts are causally linked to momentary behaviors like reassurance seeking or flippant reactions to a question, which then cause the occurrence of dependent daily hassles like a relationship conflict or an argument with a friend. This part of the model resonates with theory and research on personality pathology proposing that personality pathology is linked to stressful events via maladaptive daily behaviors (e.g., White et al., 2020). However, there have been few integrative research approaches linking momentary behavior to the occurrence of dependent daily hassles.

For example, relationship research found that the accumulation of relationship conflicts over time increases the likelihood of break-ups (Amato & Hohmann-Marriott, 2007; Finn et al., 2020; Fučík, 2023; Lee & Sbarra, 2013). Evaluating a Core Aspect of the Process Model: Stress Generation in Daily Life

Stress generation in daily life can be defined as links between trait-level characteristics, state-level characteristics, and the occurrence of dependent daily hassles. Similar to major life stressors, daily hassles have been found to predict lasting well-being decreases and the onset of mental disorders (Asselmann et al., 2017; Chan et al., 2016; Haehner et al., 2024; Mroczek & Almeida, 2004; Zheng et al., 2023), underlining the practical relevance of examining stress generation in daily life. Stress generation in daily life can be investigated with intensive longitudinal designs such as experience sampling and daily-diary research. With these designs, participants typically rate their trait-level characteristics at baseline and report state-level characteristics and the occurrence of daily hassles once per day or multiple times per day (Eberhart & Hammen, 2009; Mandel et al., 2018; Vize et al., 2024).

In the last years, some studies have examined stress generation in daily life using intensive longitudinal designs, providing initial insights into a core aspect of the stress generation process model (Cummings et al., 2013; Eberhart & Hammen, 2009; Kiang & Buchanan, 2014; Vize et al., 2024). However, this research is only sparsely integrated into the stress generation literature. Furthermore, research on stress generation in daily life seems to be scattered across research fields and methodological approaches, which may have impaired knowledge integration (Liu et al., 2024; Mandel et al., 2018; Meyer et al., 2023; Sahl et al., 2009). To overcome these problems, we conducted a systematic review that summarizes and integrates existing research on stress generation in daily life.

Methods

The systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement (Page et al., 2021). All coding material, extracted data, and R scripts are available on https://osf.io/dejts/?view_only=0d6027db00454d85a358ae976376bf38. The review was not preregistered.

Literature Search

We conducted a systematic literature search using PsycINFO on October 9, 2024 and updated the literature search on February 27, 2026 with the following search string: ("stress generation" OR "generation of stress*" OR "generated stress*" OR "dependent stress*" OR "life event*" OR "stressful event*" OR "minor event*" OR "daily stressor*" OR "hassle*" OR "daily stress*") AND ("daily diary" OR "daily-diary" OR "ESM" OR "experience sampling"

OR "momentary assessment" OR "ambulatory assessment" OR "daily life" OR "diary"). Search results were restricted to studies written in English and published from 1991 onward (i.e., the year when the Stress Generation Hypothesis was introduced). This database literature search resulted in 1,488 studies that were then screened for eligibility. Furthermore, we conducted a backward search by screening the reference list of existing review articles on stress generation (Liu et al., 2024; Rnic et al., 2023; Santee et al., 2023). Figure 2 illustrates our search and coding procedure.

Studies that fulfilled the following criteria were included in the systematic review: (1) empirical, quantitative study with human subjects, (2) written in English, (3) experience sampling or daily-diary design, (4) assessment of stressor occurrence 2 (i.e., stress assessment did not confound subjective stress perception and stress exposure), (5) no intervention, and (6) examination of stress generation processes in daily life. The latter was defined as studies that examined a predictive link between a personal characteristic and the occurrence of daily hassles.

Meta-Analytic Aggregation

To facilitate knowledge integration, we meta-analytically aggregated available effect size information if standardized effect size measures (i.e., correlations or odds ratios) on personstressor links were provided in at least three independent studies. To do so, we first transformed all effect size measures to correlations and then transformed all correlations to z values using Fisher's r-to-z transformation. Second, we aggregated these effect sizes using multi-level random effect meta-analysis with effect sizes nested in studies as implemented in the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010). Third, we computed cluster-robust standard errors using the clubSandwich package to deal with the nested data structure of multiple effect sizes stemming from the same study (Pustejovsky, 2016) and back-transformed z values to correlations to increase interpretability. Fourth, we conduced meta-analytic moderator analyses to investigate differences in effect sizes between experience sampling and daily-diary studies. The results of these analyses are described in Section 2 of the https://osf.io/dejts/files/osfstorage?view_only=0d6027db00454d85a358ae976376bf38 but findings should be interpretated with caution as power to identify significant moderation effects was limited.

Fifth, we evaluated publication bias by visually inspecting funnel plots and by using the standard error of effect sizes as predictor of effect sizes. In these models, a positive and significant predictive effect indicates publication bias as this suggests that more imprecise studies are more likely to be published when they reported larger effects.

Results

This manuscript is accompanied by https://osf.io/dejts/files/osfstorage?view_only=0d6027db00454d85a358ae976376bf38 containing all analysis code, additional descriptive statistics, and results on publication bias. Furthermore, we developed a ShinyApp (https://personalityresearch.shinyapps.io/StressGeneration/) that allows researchers to retrieve more details about the included studies and to filter, select, and sort findings according to their interests.

Overview of Research on Stress Generation in Daily Life

Overall, 45 studies with 44 unique samples and 161 unique person-stressors links were included in this review. The included studies were published between 1991 and 2025 (M = 2014.84, SD = 8.40), with 93% (k = 42) being published journal articles and 7% (k = 3) being PhD theses. On average, intensive longitudinal data collections lasted for 14.02 days (SD = 10.34) with 2.71 (SD = 3.04) assessments per day.

Sample sizes ranged from 60 to 2'016 participants, with an average sample size of N = 223.14 (SD = 303.31). Samples were mostly college samples (k = 18, 41%) or convenience samples (k = 17, 39%), relatively young (M = 33.47, SD = 14.23), and primarily female (M = 68%, SD = 19.41). Furthermore, samples were almost exclusively recruited in the US (k = 32, 97%).

Daily hassles were mostly assessed using checklist methods (k = 113, 70%), with only 23% of measures (k = 37) distinguishing between dependent and independent hassles. On average, hassles were assessed 2.25 times per day (SD = 2.78), whereas personal characteristics were assessed less than once per day (M = 0.59, SD = 0.94). Furthermore, personal characteristics were primarily assessed using self-reports (k = 132, 82%), with sleep behavior (k = 31), negative affect (k = 18), and neuroticism (k = 13) being assessed most frequently.

Complicating knowledge aggregation, only 40% of person-stressor links were quantified using standardized effect sizes such as correlations (k = 42) or Odds Ratios (k = 23), whereas most person-stressor links were only reported as unstandardized coefficients in multilevel models (k = 76, 47%). Finally, of the 161 unique links on stress generation in daily life, 93 examined traitlevel predictors of daily hassles and 68 examined state-level predictors.

Stress Generation in Daily Life: Trait-Level Predictors of Daily Hassles

The examination of trait-level predictors of daily hassles allows insights into whether variables that have been found to predict stress generation in the long run can also predict stressful events over shorter time periods. Table 2 provides an overview of the number of studies and effect sizes for trait-level predictors of daily hassles (see Appendix A for details on study characteristics). In the following, we summarize findings for trait-level characteristics that were examined in at least three independent studies, with meta-analytic estimates being provided if standardized effect size estimates from at least three independent studies were available (i.e., neuroticism and depression).

Neuroticism. Neuroticism was the most frequently examined predictor of daily hassles (12 studies). Overall, there was consistent evidence that higher levels of neuroticism predict stress generation in daily life. This conclusion was also supported by the meta-analytic aggregation of standardized effect size estimates (see Figure 3A). Higher levels of neuroticism showed a small positive relationship with the occurrence of daily hassles, r = .10, p = .039, 95% CI = [.01, .18]. However, effect sizes varied substantially across studies (I² = 60%) and none of the included studies had examined the hypothesis that neuroticism predicts the occurrence of dependent but not independent daily hassles.

Depression.

In line with the initial proposition of the Stress Generation Hypothesis (Hammen, 1991), higher levels of depressive symptoms or the diagnosis of a depressive disorder predicted the occurrence of daily hassles (9 studies). Similar to neuroticism, the metaanalytic aggregation of standardized effect sizes revealed a small positive relationship between depression and daily hassles (see Figure 3B), r = .11, p = .026, 95% CI = [.02, .20], with substantial heterogeneity in effect sizes across studies (I² = 57%). Furthermore, consistent with stress generation theory, Cummings et al. (2010) found that a depression diagnosis only predicted the occurrence of dependent but not independent hassles. However, this result was not replicated by Hamilton and Alloy (2017), providing only partial support for the role of depression in stress generation in daily life.

Extraversion. Extraversion was examined as predictor of the occurrence of daily hassles in four intensive longitudinal studies. In line with meta-analytic research on long-term stress generation (Santee et al., 2023), these studies converge on the finding that extraversion is not a significant predictor of daily hassles.

Personality Pathology.

Personality pathology was examined as predictor of stress generation processes in daily life in four intensive longitudinal studies. This initial research showed that higher levels of personality pathology predict more frequent occurrences of daily hassles. For example, Haliczer et al. (2021) found that higher levels of Borderline psychopathology were associated with a higher likelihood of interpersonal conflicts (OR = 1.05). Furthermore, Cummings et al. (2013) found that symptoms of avoidant personality disorder predicted the occurrence of dependent hassles. However, personality disorders symptoms also predicted the occurrence of independent hassles in their study.

Trait Negative Affect.

Three intensive longitudinal studies investigated trait negative affect as predictor of daily hassles. Two of these studies found that trait negative affect did not predict the occurrence of dependent daily hassles (Kelso, 2023; Shih & Auerbach, 2010), whereas Veilleux et al. (2020) found that a more negative affect balance predicted a higher number of daily hassles. Thus, there is no clear evidence that trait negative affect predicts stress generation in daily life.

Anxiety. Anxiety was considered as predictor of daily hassles in three intensive longitudinal studies. While DeMeo et al. (2023) and Farmer and Kashdan (2015) found that higher levels of anxiety or the diagnosis of a social anxiety disorder were positively related to the occurrence of daily hassles, Gunthert et al. (2002) found that anxiety arousal predicted a lower number of academic hassles. Again, no study distinguished between dependent and independent events, leaving the role of anxiety for stress generation in daily life unclear.

Other Personal Characteristics. Several other personal characteristics such as interpersonal competence, reassurance seeking, health, dependency, or avoidance were examined as predictors of daily hassles (Cummings et al., 2013; Eberhart & Hammen, 2009; Meyer et al., 2023; Shih & Auerbach, 2010). Stress Generation in Daily Life: State-Level Predictors of Daily Hassles

The examination of whether state-level characteristics predict the occurrence of daily hassles provides a most direct test of the unfolding of stress generation in daily life. Table 2 provides an overview of the number of effect sizes and studies available for different state-level predictors of daily hassles (see Appendix B for details on study characteristics). In the following, we focus on state-level predictors that were examined in at least three independent studies, with meta-analytic estimates being provided if standardized effect size estimates from at least three independent studies were available (i.e., sleep).

Sleep.

Sleep was examined most frequently as state-level predictor of the occurrence of daily hassles (7 studies). However, evidence on whether worse sleep predicts stress generation in daily life was mixed. For example, Marcusson-Clavertz et al. (2022) reported inconsistent findings on the link between sleep duration and hassle occurrence across three examined samples. Similarly inconclusive, the meta-analytic aggregation of standardized effect size estimates revealed a marginally significant negative relationship between sleep and daily hassles (see Figure 3C), r = -.06, p = .057, 95% CI = [-.12, .00], I² = 0%. Possibly explaining these mixed findings, the sleep-hassle link tended to differ depending on the examined sleep characteristics (e.g., sleep quality vs. sleep duration; Harris et al., 2022) and the sample composition (e.g., clinical vs. convenience; Marcusson-Clavertz et al., 2022). In sum, although there is tentative evidence that worse sleep predicts the occurrence of daily hassles, future research is warranted to clarify under which circumstances sleep may or may not predict the occurrence of (dependent) daily hassles.

State negative Affect.

Six studies considered momentary negative affect as state-level predictor of stress generation in daily life. The available research suggests that the link between negative affect and daily hassles depends on the time lag between assessments. That is, while most studies found that higher levels of negative affect predicted the occurrence of daily hassles at the same assessment (Haliczer et al., 2021; Kiang & Buchanan, 2014), negative affect did not predict subsequent hassle occurrences (Sahl et al., 2009; Vize et al., 2024). For example, Kelso (2024) found that momentary negative affect predicted same time point but not next time point dependent stressors. Thus, negative affect seems to be a state-level predictor of daily hassles, but the temporal unfolding of this link requires further research.

Other State-Level Characteristics.

Other state-level characteristics such as momentary reassurance seeking, emotion regulation, or attachment were examined in only one or two studies (Eberhart & Hammen, 2009; Sahl et al., 2009; Sears et al., 2018). For example, Eberhart and Hammen (2009) found that a higher momentary anxious attachment and higher love dependency predicted the occurrence of relationship conflicts on the same day. However, more research on the links between momentary personal characteristics and the occurrence of daily hassles is needed to evaluate the replicability of these effects.

Insights into Stress Generation in Daily Life and Directions for Future Research

Integrating ideas from personality psychology into clinical psychology, we proposed a process model of stress generation that provides a roadmap for future research on stress generation across timescales. This process model posits that dynamics between state-level characteristics and daily hassles accumulate over time and explain well-established long-term longitudinal links between trait characteristics and major life stressors.

We aimed to evaluate a core aspect of this model -stress generation effects in daily life -in a systematic review. This review showed that trait-characteristics like depression and neuroticism predict the occurrence of daily hassles (r = .10 to .11). In contrast, evidence was less clear regarding the predictive effects of momentary characteristics -particularly momentary behaviors -on stress generation in daily life. We found tentative evidence for negative affect and sleep problems as state-level predictors of daily hassles. However, future research is needed to test which momentary thoughts, feelings, and behaviors predict the occurrence of daily hassles within individuals. Furthermore, the theorized link between stress generation in daily life and long-term stress generation has yet to be tested.

Directions for Future Research

The systematic literature review revealed that empirical evidence for stress generation in daily life is still limited. There are at least five important directions for future research.

First, more intensive longitudinal research is needed to evaluate the replicability of person-stressor links in daily life and to examine a broader set of personal characteristics as predictors of daily hassles. Compared to the rich empirical literature on long-term stress generation processes, studies examining stress generation in daily life have only recently taken root in psychology (Liu et al., 2024; Rnic et al., 2023; Santee et al., 2023). However, with technological advancements, the required intensive longitudinal data collections on stress generation in daily life became more manageable, allowing researchers to examine the links between state-level personal characteristics and daily hassles in relatively large samples. Future research should continue these efforts and examine a broad set of (state-level) personal characteristics as predictors of daily hassles at a time. Such research would also allow us to investigate the unique contribution of each characteristic and to test whether behavioral states are indeed the most potent predictor of daily hassles. Furthermore, multi-method approaches are warranted that combine self-reports, other-reports, and mobile sensing to better capture momentary behaviors as active ingredients of stress generation in daily life.

Second, a more thorough investigation of daily hassles as outcomes of stress generation processes is needed. Stress generation theory posits that personal characteristics predict the occurrence of dependent but not independent stressors (Hammen, 1991; Liu, 2013). In longterm longitudinal studies, life event interviews are typically used to distinguish between dependent and independent major life stressors and to disentangle stressor occurrence and stressor perception (Dohrenwend, 2006; Hammen, 2006; Liu et al., 2024). However, such interview approaches can be too burdensome for intensive longitudinal data collections and frequently occurring daily hassles (Starr et al., 2025; but see Almeida et al., 2002). As a result, most studies included in the systematic review have not distinguished between dependent and independent hassles, providing only a partial test of stress generation theory. Technological advancements like the rise of large-language models may help to overcome this problem. That is, participants could describe daily hassles in an open-response format and large-language models could then be trained to categorize these descriptions as dependent or independent.

Critically, to minimize confounding between personal characteristics and the likelihood of reporting events as hassles, it may be advisable to ask participants to describe all significant event occurrences-not only negative ones-and subsequently have raters or large language models code whether each event is objectively stressful based on the provided free-text descriptions. Furthermore, checklist approaches, though often criticized in long-term stress generation research, may be well suited for intensive longitudinal designs, because the frequent assessments can help mitigate memory biases (Starr et al., 2025). Finally, the assessment of daily hassles may be enriched by new information sources, such as mobile sensing, to assess relevant characteristics of daily hassles (e.g., location) holistically and objectively (Harari et al., 2021; Mehl, 2024).

Third, the stress generation process model predicts that short-term dynamics between state-level personal characteristics and daily hassles contribute to long-term stress generation effects. However, direct evidence for this link of stress generation processes across timescales is still scarce. Eberhart and Hammen (2009) combined a 1-month longitudinal design and a 2- week daily-diary study in the same sample to show that trait-and state-level characteristics (e.g., anxious attachment) predicted the occurrence of interpersonal stressors across different timescales.

However, this study did not test the core hypothesis that accumulation of short-term effects mediated the long-term links between trait-level characteristics and interpersonal stressful events. A first study that has examined the relationship between stress generation in daily life and long-term stress generation found relatively small and inconsistent associations (Haehner et al., 2026). Clarifying the unfolding of stress generation across timescales likely is a challenging endeavor, because effects may differ across people and across time intervals. One important direction for future research would be systematically vary the number of assessments and time intervals between assessments to better characterize the timescale of stress generation processes. Such a systematic examination with varying time intervals between assessments would be critical to determine the temporal unfolding of stress generation (see Figure 1). Once research on the timescale of stress generation processes becomes available, a formalized theoretical model of stress generation including precise mathematical predictions on the links between personal characteristics and the occurrence of stressful events can be developed.

Fourth, the stress generation process model posits causal relationships across personal characteristics, dependent daily hassles, and major life stressors. In this systematic review, we focused on intensive longitudinal data collections that examined whether personal characteristics predicted the occurrence of daily hassles over time. The longitudinal perspective enabled a closer approximation to causal inference, because lagged effects of state-level personal characteristics at time point t on the occurrence of hassles at time point t + 1 establish a clearer temporal ordering of effects (Starr et al., 2025). However, longitudinal designs and the examination of lagged effects alone are not sufficient to prove causality. To strengthen causal inferences in observational data, researchers should specify hypotheses and assumptions regarding the underlying causal net of variables involved in the stress generation process using, for example, directed analytic graphs (Grosz et al., 2020; Rohrer, 2018). Furthermore, experimental approaches need to be incorporated into stress generation research more regularly.

So far, research on interventions aimed to prevent or disrupt stress generation is fairly limited (but see Jones et al., 2023). However, such research would be theoretically and practically relevant as it could help to examine the causal relevance of specific predictors of stress generation. Furthermore, if successful, such interventions could be applied in psychotherapeutic settings to disrupt vicious cycles between maladaptive personal characteristics and stressful events.

Fifth, research on stress generation in daily life is currently limited by the fact that evidence stems almost exclusively from the United States. Furthermore, samples included in the systematic review were primarily female and relatively young (Harrison et al., 2025). Thus, stress generation research using culturally diverse, gender-balanced, and age-heterogenous samples is warranted to better understand the cross-cultural generalizability and replicability of stress generation in daily life across different samples.

Clinical Implications of the Stress Generation Process Model

Although future research on the stress generation process model is needed, it may already inform the development of interventions to reduce or avoid stress generation. As can be seen in Figure 1, stress generation interventions could intervene at different points of the causal chain to disrupt stress generation processes. One strategy -consistent with this process model and other stress generation theories (Liu, 2013; Liu et al., 2024) -would be to change maladaptive trait levels to prevent stress generation. For example, reducing people's enduring patterns of negative affect or negative cognitive styles would decrease the likelihood of maladaptive momentary feelings or thoughts, and, as a result, disrupt both short-and long-term stress generation processes.

According to the stress generation process model, another intervention strategy would be to disrupt stress generation in daily life by reducing maladaptive states or by disrupting the link between maladaptive momentary behaviors and daily hassles. For example, offering support when people are in a vulnerable state with high levels of negative affect could reduce negative affect in this moment, decrease the likelihood of maladaptive behaviors, and thus disrupt short-and long-term stress generation processes (Everitt et al., 2021; Haehner, Wright, et al., 2025) Second, research on stress generation in daily life is still in its infancy. As a result, the number of studies providing standardized effect size estimates was limited, precluding sufficiently powered meta-analytic tests of several predictor and moderator variables. Future research on stress generation in daily life is needed to test, for instance, moderating effects of different assessment methods, different study designs, or different assessment intervals.

Third, we were more liberal regarding the study inclusion than existing meta-analytic reviews on stress generation (Liu et al., 2024; Rnic et al., 2023; Santee et al., 2023) such that we included studies that did not distinguish between dependent and independent hassles. We included such studies as they can help to identify predictors of daily hassles that should be examined with more rigorous designs in future research. However, studies without this distinction cannot test the Stress Generation Hypothesis in its strict sense (Hammen, 1991).

Conclusion

Stress generation is a phenomenon of critical public relevance as it may lead to vicious cycles between maladaptive personal characteristics and stressful events (Hammen, 2006; Liu et al., 2024; Rnic et al., 2023; Santee et al., 2023). However, future research is needed to fully evaluate its propositions on stress generation across timescales. We hope that our model inspires future research testing relevant model predictions and the development of intervention approaches disrupting stress generation processes.

As such, the proposed process model provides a starting point for the development of a just-in-time adaptive intervention against stress generation. Just-in-time adaptive interventions seek to provide the right amount of support at the right time to increase intervention effectiveness (Nahum-Shani et al., 2015). The development of such just-in-time adaptive interventions requires insights into the processes that mediate distal intervention outcomes to identify intervention points and tailoring options (Nahum-Shani et al., 2018). First, the non-representativeness of stress generation research in terms of the global population limits the generalizability of existing findings about stress generation, including the conclusions presented in this review. While we assume stress generation to be a culturally-universal phenomenon, the specific person-stressor links likely differ across cultures, outlining an important direction for future research.

Although the understanding of stress generation has increased substantially over the last decades, the unfolding of stress generation processes in people's daily lives has received little attention so far, limiting our ability to understand stress generation processes within individuals and develop effective interventions against it.To overcome these limitations, we developed a process model of stress generation that integrates research from personality psychology into clinical stress generation research. This model posits that short-term stress generation dynamics between state-level personal characteristics and dependent daily hassles are the building blocks of long-term stress generation effects.
