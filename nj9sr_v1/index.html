Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Despite longstanding interest in multiple orgasms, little is known about their prevalence or predictors, particularly during solo masturbation. Using data from a large U.S. national survey, we analyzed 2,483 adults (1,245 women, 1,238 men) who reported solo masturbation in the previous 30 days. Predictors were identified using random forest, a machine-learning method that accommodates many predictors simultaneously and nonlinear associations. Among women, 30% reported experiencing multiple orgasms during solo masturbation frequently (50%-100% of the time), compared with 7% of men; 26% of women and 25% of men reported experiencing them occasionally (10%-25% of the time). Prevalence declined with age among men but not women. Random forest models explained 14% and 21% of the variance in multiple orgasms among women and men, respectively. Important predictors included duration of masturbation, vividness of orgasmic sensations, sex toy use, syntribation ("hands free" masturbation via flexing thighs or pelvic/core muscles), cognitive absorption, imagination, and masturbation motives, with some differences between women and men. In sum, multiple orgasms were relatively common, particularly among women, with behavioral, cognitive, and motivational predictors suggesting pathways for future investigation.

Introduction

Multiple orgasms has been discussed for thousands of years, including in ancient Chinese Fangzhongshu texts ("bedroom arts" manuals; e.g., Wile, 1992). More recently, popular press books have described techniques for becoming multi-orgasmic (e.g., Chia & Abrahms, 1996; Chia & Abrams, 2006). Understanding multiple orgasms aligns with growing recognition of sexual pleasure as integral to sexual health (Ford et al., 2021), and may illuminate variability in human sexual response, including sex differences. However, rigorous empirical research on multiple orgasms is scarce, especially during solo masturbation. Nationally representative prevalence estimates are available for multiple orgasms during partnered sex among women (Kontula & Miettinen, 2016), but not men, and are unavailable for solo masturbation. Moreover, investigations of correlates of multiple orgasms tend to rely on small, non-representative samples. The present investigation estimates the prevalence of multiple orgasms during solo masturbation and identifies their most important predictors using a large U.S. national sample.

Given the limited research and lack of established theoretical frameworks to guide hypotheses, we used random forest, a machine-learning algorithm, to explore a broad range of potential predictors related to solo and partnered sexuality, well-being, and personality.

What is an Orgasm?

There are many conflicting definitions of orgasm, reflecting its complexity, variability across individuals, and differences in researchers' theoretical perspectives. Nevertheless, most researchers (e.g., Komisaruk et al., 2008; Levin, 2011; Mah & Binik, 2001; Masters & Johnson, 1966; Pfaus, 2025) recognize several common features. Orgasm is usually induced by rhythmic stimulation of the genitalia, or less commonly, other body parts such as the anus, breasts, or nipples. It typically involves a peak of intense pleasure, an altered state of consciousness, involuntary and pleasurable rhythmic contractions of pelvic floor, genital, and other pelvic musculature, release of accumulated sexual excitation and muscular tension, various other cardiovascular, respiratory, neurochemical, hormonal, and cognitive processes, and subsequent relaxation and feelings of well-being.

Physiological differences between women and men influence their experience of orgasm.

(Throughout, we use "women" and "men" to refer to female and male adults, while recognizing the relevance of sex differences in physiological processes underlying sexual arousal and orgasm that are central to this work.) In men, orgasm is typically accompanied by ejaculation, which triggers a post-ejaculatory refractory period (PERT) characterized by inhibited sexual excitation, arousal, and motivation that impedes subsequent arousal and orgasms. PERT may be shortened or overcome by strong or novel sexual stimuli, such as a new sex partner, consistent with the Coolidge effect (i.e., renewed sexual arousal in response to a novel sexual partner; Levin, 2009).

The causes and nature of PERT is poorly understood and controversial (Levin, 2009), as is the possibility that women experience a comparable refractory period. Evidence for a refractory period among women has historically been limited (Levin, 2009), although a recent study provides preliminary evidence of its occurrence (Bhat & Shastry, 2026). Nevertheless, a refractory period in women, if present, is generally thought to be less pronounced than in men.

Several observations indicate that in men, orgasm and ejaculation are somewhat independent processes: ejaculation without orgasm can be triggered by penile vibrostimulation or prostate massage (e.g., Arafa et al., 2007; Brackett et al., 2013), and orgasm without ejaculation can occur after spinal cord injury or prostatectomy (e.g., Dubbelman et al., 2010; Koeman et al., 1996). Reports from multiorgasmic men, discussed below, provide further evidence of this dissociation.

What are Multiple Orgasms?

Multiple orgasms remain poorly understood. Recent studies have relied largely on retrospective reports of experiences that likely involved intense arousal and altered states of consciousness rather than direct physiological measurement. This lack of recent, in-lab studies leaves uncertainty about the physiological responses underlying reported experiences.

Nevertheless, definitions consistently specify more than one orgasm during a single sexual session (e.g., Corre et al., 2025; Gérard et al., 2021; Kinsey et al., 1948; Masters & Johnson, 1966; Van Tuijl, 2026; Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016), the operationalization we adopted in defining multiple orgasms for participants in the current investigation. However, the literature suggests that multiple orgasm is not likely to be a single, uniform experience, as descriptions vary in terms of the timing of orgasms, importance of continued arousal and stimulation, the roles of pelvic musculature contractions, ejaculation, PERT, and especially, individuals' sex.

Multiple Orgasms among Women

Current understanding of multiple orgasms among women has been influenced particularly by Masters and Johnson (1966; see Levin, 2009, for a critical review) who observed that the refractory period typical of male orgasm did not occur in women. Consequently, women could experience repeated orgasms while remaining at a plateau of high arousal, if effective stimulation continued. Masters and Johnson reported this was more likely during masturbation, free from partner-related distractions. They also observed "status orgasmus," a rapid series of orgasms during sustained high arousal, which the individual might experience as a single extended orgasm.

Compared to their peers, women who experience multiple orgasms tend to report greater sexual desire, value sex and orgasms more, engage in a wider variety of sexual practices, and perceive themselves as more sexually adventurous, skilled, confident, and disinhibited (Cerwenka et al., 2021; Gérard et al., 2021; Kontula & Miettinen, 2016). During masturbation, they use a greater variety of techniques, including thigh pressure, sometimes termed "syntribation" (Cerwenka et al., 2021; Darling et al., 1991; de Jong, Uhlich, Fischer, Ginn, et al., 2026). Together, these findings suggest that among women, multiple orgasms are associated with a positive orientation towards sex, greater sexual confidence, and a broad repertoire of sexual practices.

Multiple Orgasms among Men

Among men, two types of multiple orgasms have been described (Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016). The first, "sporadic" multiple orgasms, entails orgasms several minutes apart, with involuntary and pleasurable pelvic muscle contractions. Erection may be maintained between orgasms, but more stimulation is needed for more orgasms. Ejaculation accompanies orgasm, but volume diminishes. Sporadic multiple orgasms may result from a short refractory period (e.g., in younger men) or a novel, intense sexual stimulus (i.e., Coolidge effect; Levin, 2009). Others achieve them via ejaculation-control techniques, including prolonged duration, modulation of physical or mental stimulation, the stop-start method (pausing stimulation near orgasm, then resuming), flexing pelvic muscles (Kegels), or pressure on the glans or perineum (Dunn & Trost, 1989; Hartman & Fithian, 1984). Such techniques are also described in some Tantric sexual practices (e.g., Chia & Abrahms, 1996). The other pattern involves "condensed" orgasms, which have received less research focus and is less common (Griffin-Mathieu et al., 2021). This type entails a rapid burst of orgasms, typically without ejaculation nor a refractory period, and without necessarily requiring more penile stimulation.

Several studies identify factors that facilitate men's multiple orgasms (Corre et al., 2025; Dunn & Trost, 1989; Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016). Factors identified include mental focus, controlled breathing, the aforementioned techniques of separating orgasm from ejaculation, a relaxed setting, prolonged sexual activity, sex toys (especially those that stimulate the prostate), group sex, and psychoactive stimulants. Some men report developing the ability to experience multiple orgasms through practice, whereas others describe having discovered this ability without deliberate training.

Prevalence of Multiple Orgasms

Prevalence of multiple orgasms is understudied. Early estimates that come from Kinsey (1948 Kinsey ( , 1953) ) who found that 15-20% of sexually active male teens and young adults regularly experienced multiples during coitus with prevalence declining with age to 3% among men 60 or older. Among sexually active women, prevalence ranged from 10-16% with little decline with age, exceeding prevalence among men across most adult age groups. More recent national estimates are limited to women's experiences during partnered sex (e.g., Kontula & Miettinen, 2016). Estimates for multiple orgasms during masturbation are especially limited, and none are from national samples. For example, Haning et al. (2008) found that in a convenience sample of 1,137 adults, 16% of women and 5% of men experienced multiples during masturbation, but they excluded orgasms less than 30 seconds apart, and the rationale for this was not specified.

Overall, multiple orgasms appear more common among women, during either masturbation or partnered sex.

Why Use Machine Learning to Predict Multiple Orgasms during Solo Masturbation?

Random forest (Breiman, 2001), a machine learning algorithm, has been used to investigate predictors sexual desire (L. M. Vowels et al., 2021), risky sexual behaviors (Li et al., 2023), satisfaction with partnered sex (L. M. Vowels et al., 2022a), and solo masturbation (de Jong, Uhlich, Fischer, & Vowels, 2026). In the domain of relationship science, random forest has been used to predict infidelity (L. M. Vowels et al., 2022b), relationship quality (Uhlich & Bojar, 2021), and relationship dissolution (Uhlich et al., 2026). Three characteristics of random forest motivated its use in the present investigation.

First, the limited empirical and theoretical literature on multiple orgasms suggests an exploratory approach rather than testing narrowly specified hypotheses. Random forest entails a broad search for informative predictors, including associations not anticipated by existing theory.

Such findings can guide future hypothesis-driven research (Fife & D'Onofrio, 2022; Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Second, the dataset we utilized includes many measures related to sexuality and psychological functioning, yielding a large set of candidate predictors with potentially overlapping information and nonlinear associations. Random forest can accommodate this, examining many predictors simultaneously without requiring specification of the form of associations or specific distributional assumptions.

Third, the predictive performance of random forest can be efficiently assessed via crossvalidation, in which a model is repeatedly trained on one portion of the data and evaluated on a separate, held-out portion. Performance on these unseen observations estimates generalizability to new samples and helps prevent overfitting to sample-specific noise. Cross-validation thereby reduces the risk of overly optimistic estimates of predictive performance (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017), which has contributed to the replication crisis in psychology (M. J. Vowels, 2023).

The Current Study

The current investigation addressed several questions: (1) How common are multiple orgasms during solo masturbation, and are there differences across sex and age groups? (2) What are the most important predictors of multiple orgasms? (3) How do these predictors contribute to predictions of multiples in terms of direction, magnitude, form (e.g., linear or nonlinear), and potential interactions? To answer these questions, we used data from a large, recent, U.S. national survey with a broad range of potential predictors related to solo and partnered sexuality, well-being, and personality.

Method Participants

Participants were drawn from the Masturbation and Sexual Health Survey, a U.S. national survey (de Jong, Uhlich, Fischer, Ginn, et al., 2026) which recruited U.S. adults aged 18 or older through Prolific.com, a nonprobability online opt-in panel of identity-verified participants. Recruitment, sample size, missing-data procedures, and data cleaning were preregistered (https://osf.io/mb2je); deviations due to participant availability on Prolific are detailed in the Online Supplement. The survey initially recruited 2,448 participants, quotamatched to U.S. census data on age, sex, and race/ethnicity, followed by oversamples of individuals who were aged 65 or older (n = 360), religious (n = 241), or identified as "LGBTQ+" (n = 427), resulting in 3,476 who reached the end of the survey (103 who did not reach the survey midpoint were excluded). There were 53 participants who did not meet data quality criteria (described below), resulting in a total sample of 3,423.

We restricted the current sample to those who reported solo masturbation during the previous 30 days. Because sex (e.g., female, male) was theoretically relevant to the prevalence and predictors of multiple orgasms, given physiological sex differences in arousal and orgasm (e.g., the post-orgasmic refractory period), participants were grouped according to sex rather than gender identity, for prevalence estimates and random forest models. Accordingly, we excluded four intersex participants, because assigning them to either group would have required assumptions about their physiological characteristics, and their small number (0.16% of otherwise eligible participants) precluded reliable prevalence estimates or assessment of intersex status as a predictor. We also excluded two participants missing data on the multiple-orgasm variable, resulting in a final analytic sample of 2,483 (1,245 women; 1,238 men; Table 1).

Procedure

Western Carolina University's Institutional Review Board approved the study. Data were collected August 4-7, 2025. Participants were recruited for an anonymous 45-minute online survey described as a "Study of Sex and Personality," and provided informed consent electronically by clicking "I consent" after reading the online consent form. Median completion time was 37 minutes. Participants received $8.50 each.

Measures

Candidate predictors included all variables in the Masturbation and Sexual Health Survey, excluding: open-ended text variables (which are not amenable to random forest modeling); five variables reflecting alternative operationalizations of strongly correlated constructs (and thus would contribute redundant information, e.g., frequency and recency of solo masturbation; r = .81); scales administered only to specific subgroups (e.g., relationship satisfaction) with structurally missing data that could not be meaningfully imputed; measures of childhood experiences with frequent "I do not recall" responses; and an item assessing multiple orgasms during partnered sex (its correlation with multiples during masturbation is reported separately).

After data preparation, described below, the random forest models included 129 candidate predictors (see Online Supplement for a full list). Predictors related to solo masturbation included recency, ratings of the affective and sensory experience of orgasm and pleasure (adapted from Mah & Binik, 2002), attitudes (de Jong & Adams, 2025), motives (de Jong & Adams, 2024), and items developed for this survey assessing use of toys, fantasy, sexual media, and psychoactive substances during masturbation. Predictors reflecting broader sexual dispositions included attitudes (Hangen & Rogge, 2022), desire (solitary, partner, and attractiveperson focused subscales; Spector et al., 1996), and compulsivity (Rahm-Knigge et al., 2025).

Predictors related to personality and psychological functioning included depression (Kroenke et al., 2001) and anxiety (Spitzer et al., 2006), emotion regulation (Kaufman et al., 2016), imagination (Feng et al., 2018), and religiosity (Grubbs et al., 2020). Continuous measures were computed as means of items; higher scores indicated higher levels of the construct. Measures of the outcome (multiple orgasms during solo masturbation) and inclusion criterion (solo masturbation in the past 30 days) are described next.

Multiple Orgasms during Solo Masturbation

Participants were asked, "During solo masturbation, have you ever experienced two or more orgasms in a single sexual session, without significant breaks?" The phrase "two or more orgasms in a single sexual session, without significant breaks" was adopted verbatim from Gérard et al. (2021) and is consistent with previous definitions (e.g., Corre et al., 2025; Kinsey et al., 1948; Masters & Johnson, 1966; Van Tuijl, 2026; Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016). As discussed above, this broad definition accommodates different experiences of multiple orgasms across women and men. Response options were "Never had this experience," "Rarely (e.g., once or twice but never again)," and "Approximately [10%][25%][50%][75%][100%] of the time." Recency of Solo Masturbation Participants were first provided with the following: "Masturbation refers to: Stimulating your own genitals or other body parts to sexually arouse yourself or because you are sexually aroused; You might or might not have had an orgasm; You might or might not have used hands, toys, objects, or sexually arousing material (e.g., videos, pictures, stories)." Next, they were asked, "Have you ever engaged in the following behaviors?" The item assessing recency of solo masturbation read, "While no one was with you, either in-person or via internet/phone/remote: You masturbated." Response options were, "No, I have never done this," "Yes, more than 365 days ago," and "Yes, in the past [365 days] [30 days][7 days][24 hours]."

Data Preparation

To reduce sparse cells in prevalence analyses, participants were grouped into three age categories (18-39, 40-64, and 65 years or older), and the seven multiple-orgasm response options were collapsed into four categories: Never, Rarely, Occasionally (10-25% of the time), and Frequently (50-100% of the time). For random forests, categorical variables were dummy coded (0/1). Five groups of highly correlated and conceptually related variables (e.g., depression and anxiety, r = .82) were combined into composite variables. Proportion of missing data was 0.10%. Little's Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test was not significant, χ²(6476) = 5669.00, p = 1.00, consistent with MCAR. Prior to fitting models, missing data were imputed using the R package missForest (Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2012).

Data Analysis

Prior to estimating prevalence, survey weights were generated via raking (Battaglia et al., 2009) to align the sample with population distributions for age, race/ethnicity, sex, religious affiliation, LGBT identification, and U.S. census region; benchmarks obtained from the 2020 US Census (U.S. Census Bureau, 2020), Pew Research Center (2025), and Gallup (2025). We used Korn-Graubard 95% confidence intervals to improve coverage for small proportions or cell counts (Korn & Graubard, 1998).

Machine learning uses computational algorithms to identify patterns in data (Molnar, 2018). Unlike traditional statistical methods that require specifying associations in advance (e.g., Y = X₁ + X₂), machine learning can identify predictive associations without prespecifying their forms. Random forest is one such method that generates predictions by aggregating results across many decision trees. Within each tree, observations are repeatedly partitioned into groups with increasing similar scores on the outcome. Random sampling of observations and predictors across trees helps reduce overfitting and improves generalizability. Performance can be assessed via k-fold cross-validation: participants are randomly divided into k (e.g., 10) groups (i.e., folds), the model is trained using all but one fold (which is held out for testing), this procedure is repeated until every fold serves as a test group, then performance metrics are averaged across all test groups. Because the held-out observations were not used in model training, cross-validation estimates predictive performance on new data.

Unlike linear regression, random forest does not yield a straightforward equation describing each predictor's contribution, leading to descriptions of it as a "black box" algorithm (Breiman, 2001; Fife & D'Onofrio, 2022). SHAP values help explain the model by quantifying the magnitude and direction of predictors' contributions to each participant's predicted outcome, relative to the model's baseline prediction (Molnar, 2018). A predictor's SHAP value can vary across participants even when they have the same predictor value because its contribution depends on the participant's values on other predictors. For example, the same score on sexual desire could contribute to predictions of orgasm differently depending on masturbation attitudes.

Thus, SHAP values characterize predictor contributions in the context of other predictor values, in contrast to standard linear regression coefficients which represent a predictor's contribution as constant unless interactions or nonlinear terms are specified.

Because sex differences in sexual physiology, particularly regarding the post-orgasmic refractory period (Levin, 2009 (Levin, , 2011)), may influence the occurrence and predictors of multiple orgasms, female and male participants were modeled separately (Model 1: women, n = 1,245;

Model 2: men, n = 1,238). Our approach to random forest and result visualization followed Vowels and colleagues (de Jong, Uhlich, Fischer, & Vowels, 2026; 2021 , 2022b , 2022a , 2023).

Analyses were conducted in R (4.6.0; R Core Team, 2026). Models were fitted with ranger (Wright & Ziegler, 2017) using default settings because they perform well (Probst et al., 2019).

Model performance was assessed with 10-fold cross-validation (Irizarry, 2024). Each model was then refitted to the full sample (Irizarry, 2024; Wilimitis & Walsh, 2023), and SHAP values were calculated using treeshap (Mayer et al., 2026). Predictor importance was quantified as the mean absolute SHAP value. SHAP importance plots summarized predictor importance, beeswarm plots depicted the magnitude and direction of predictor contributions, and dependence plots illustrated the shape of associations between predictors and model predictions. These visualizations were created with shapviz (Mayer, 2025b).

Because predictor importance rankings can be unstable (Gregorutti et al., 2017), we assessed stability using 500 bootstrap samples drawn with replacement. For each sample, the model was refitted and SHAP values were recalculated. Stability was quantified as the percentage of bootstrap samples in which the predictor ranked among the 20 highest-ranked predictors and reported in Figures 2 and 3.

Two-way interactions were estimated using hstats (Mayer, 2025a), which uses

Friedman's H statistic to quantify the proportion of two predictors' joint contribution to model prediction that is attributable to their interaction. Because evaluating all predictors pairs is computationally demanding (e.g., 129 predictors yield 8,256 unique two-way combinations), hstats defaults to using a random subsample of 500 observations. We instead calculated interaction strengths using the entire analytic sample on a high-performance computing cluster to obtain more stable estimates. The scan was restricted to interactions involving at least one of the top 20 predictors. We specified a priori that only interactions accounting for > 1% of the two predictors' combined contribution would be discussed.

Predictor importance and interaction strength are estimated in the context of other predictors in the model. When predictors share predictive information, their estimated importance or interaction strength may be reduced (Friedman & Popescu, 2008). Consequently, these models, with many overlapping predictors, may be less suited to identifying interactions of interest than more focused models with fewer predictors. Likewise, because importance depends on the other predictors in the model, a predictor's absence from the top 20 does not imply that it is unrelated to multiple orgasms. Pearson correlations between the outcome and the top 20 predictors are provided in the Online Supplement to provide descriptive information that may inform future research. These correlations need not correspond to random forest predictor importance, which also reflects nonlinear associations and interactions.

Discussion

This study aimed to estimate the prevalence and the most important predictors of multiple orgasms during solo masturbation. Given the limited research on multiple orgasms, particularly during solo masturbation, we took an exploratory, data-driven approach. Using random forest, we evaluated a broad range of candidate predictors spanning masturbation-related behaviors and experiences, sexuality, and personality.

Prevalence of Multiple Orgasms during Solo Masturbation

Multiple orgasms during solo masturbation were somewhat common, particularly among women. For example, experiencing multiple orgasms frequently (50%-100% of the time) was more common among women than men (30% vs. 7%), and experiencing multiples never or rarely was more common among men (30%, 38%, respectively) than women (19%, 25%, respectively). The greater prevalence among women aligns with previous findings that women are more likely than men to experience multiple orgasms during either masturbation or partnered sex (Haning et al., 2008; Kinsey et al., 1953; Van Tuijl, 2026). Our estimates were considerably higher than reported by Haning et al. (2008; 16% of women, 5% of men), possibly reflecting their apparent inclusion of all surveyed participants in prevalence estimates, rather than only recent masturbators, and their requirement of at least 30 seconds between orgasms, excluding closely spaced orgasms. In the current investigation, the prevalence of multiple orgasms declined with age among men but not women, consistent with Kinsey et al. (1953; Figure 151).

Important Predictors of Multiple Orgasms during Solo Masturbation

Random forest models explained 14% and 21% of the variance in multiple orgasms among women and men, respectively, toward the lower end of the range reported in previous random forest studies of other aspects of sexuality, such as sexual desire (13-45%; L. M. Vowels et al., 2021). This may partly reflect our use of sex-specific models, which excluded sex as a predictor (sex emerged as the most important predictor in preliminary models that combined both groups). Another possibility is that our single-item outcome measure captured distinct types of multiple orgasms (e.g., status orgasmus, sporadic, condensed) that may have different underlying mechanisms and predictors. The lower R² among women may reflect the possibility that their multiple orgasms depend more on physiological factors not assessed here.

Because predictor rankings vary across model runs, we emphasize general patterns rather than exact rankings, focusing on predictors that consistently ranked among the top 20 across bootstrap samples. We interpret these findings, together with observed sex and age differences, across four domains: (a) physiological capacity to experience multiple orgasms, (b) techniques and cognitive processes, (c) attitudinal, motivational, and affective factors, and (d) experiences and attitudes related to partnered sex.

Physiological Capacity to Experience Multiple Orgasms

The higher prevalence among women may partly reflect the absence of, or a much less pronounced, post-orgasmic refractory period among women (Levin, 2009; Masters & Johnson, 1966)(but also see Bhat & Shastry, 2026). Age may be another factor reflecting the physiological capacity to experience multiple orgasms, particularly among men. Our estimates indicated that multiples were less common among older men but not women, consistent with Kinsey (1953) and with evidence of greater age-related sexual difficulties, including difficulty reaching orgasm, among men than women (Laumann et al., 2005). Thus, the age-related increase in erectile difficulties and lengthening of the post-ejaculatory refractory period with age among men (Corona et al., 2010; Levin, 2009) may make it difficult to maintain the erection needed for subsequent orgasms. However, older age is also associated with worsening overall health and generational differences, which these cross-sectional findings cannot distinguish.

Techniques and Cognitive Processes that May Facilitate Multiple Orgasms

The specific techniques and behaviors individuals use when masturbating may influence whether one's physiological capacity for multiple orgasms is realized. Although the behaviors identified as important were not assessed specifically in the context of masturbation events with multiple orgasms, previous research suggests they might facilitate multiple orgasms. For example, longer duration of masturbation emerged as one of the most important and reliable predictors of multiple orgasms among both women and men. Longer sessions may provide time for several processes linked to multiple orgasms among women, men, or both: relaxing, building and sustaining arousal, regulating arousal by adjusting stimulation intensity (e.g., exploring the point of no return through the stop-start method), and experimenting with different forms of stimulation (Chia & Abrahms, 1996; Corre et al., 2025; Darling et al., 1991; Dunn & Trost, 1989; Hartman & Fithian, 1984; Kontula & Miettinen, 2016; Shtarkshall et al., 2008; Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016). Syntribation ("hands free" masturbation via flexing thighs or pelvic/core muscles; de Jong, Uhlich, Fischer, Ginn, et al., 2026) emerged as an important predictor among both women and men. Although understudied, syntribation is relatively common, with a recent U.S. national survey finding past-month prevalence rates of 20.1% among women and 14.6% among men (de Jong et al., 2026). Syntribation may facilitate multiple orgasms via stimulation, awareness, and control of muscles involved in orgasm (Levin, 2011). Control of these muscles has also been reported as a technique used by multiorgasmic men (Corre et al., 2025; Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016). Syntribation has previously been identified as an effective route to orgasm among women (Hite, 1976; Kinsey et al., 1953), and our findings suggest it is also relevant to men's orgasms. Sex toy use was an important predictor among both women and men, ranking first among men. This aligns with findings that some men use toys to have multiple orgasms (Corre et al., 2025; Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016) and that toy use is linked to better sexual and orgasmic functioning among both women and men (Herbenick et al., 2009; Reece et al., 2009). Toys may facilitate multiples by providing sustained or intense stimulation while allowing individuals to regulate arousal by adjusting intensity, rhythm, or location. Lubricant use, also important among men, may facilitate prolonged, varied, or anal stimulation (prostate stimulation is a method of reach multiples among men; Corre et al., 2025; Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016) by increasing arousal, enhancing pleasure, and reducing discomfort.

Among women, the importance of orgasm consistency during masturbation may reflect greater sexual self-knowledge and familiarity with effective stimulation techniques (Rowland et al., 2020), which is also suggested by earlier findings that women who experience multiples tend to report engaging in a wide range of behaviors during sex (Cerwenka et al., 2021). Thus, women who consistently achieve orgasm may have developed a better understanding of their sexual responses and identified the methods that work best for them, potentially facilitating both initial and subsequent orgasms. The lack of importance of orgasm consistency for men may partly reflect the lower variability in this predictor among men in the present sample, who were more likely than women to consistently experience orgasm during masturbation.

Predictors reflecting attentional focus emerged as important among both women and men, aligning with previous work highlighting the role of attentional focus on erotic cues (e.g., sensations, fantasies, external imagery) in facilitating and regulating sexual arousal (de Jong, 2009; Janssen et al., 2000; Vigil et al., 2021; Wyatt & de Jong, 2020). Vividness of the sensory qualities of pleasure was particularly important among women, potentially reflecting greater interoceptive awareness, which has been linked to women's sexual function (Seal & Meston, 2020). Prolonged masturbation in a trance-like state (aka "gooning" on social media) was particularly important among men, suggesting that cognitive absorption may help sustain or restore arousal following orgasm. Trait imagination was also important, suggesting that vivid erotic imagery may help sustain arousal for repeated orgasms.

Attitudinal, Motivational, and Affective Factors

Solitary sexual desire was important among both women and men, perhaps reflecting greater sexual arousability and motivating more frequent masturbation, thus providing more opportunities to explore techniques that facilitate multiple orgasms. However, other attitudinal, motivational, and affective predictors differed by sex. For women, predictors reflected a positive orientation toward masturbation, including masturbating to experience pleasure, positive emotions after masturbation, masturbation satisfaction, and positive masturbation attitudes. Thus, positive, rewarding experiences with masturbation may motivate women to explore techniques that facilitate multiple orgasms, while multiple orgasms may themselves reinforce positive attitudes and experiences. This aligns with findings that multiorgasmic women value sexual pleasure and orgasms and are sexually exploratory, skilled, and confident (Darling et al., 1991; Gérard et al., 2021).

In contrast, findings for men suggest a conflicted orientation toward masturbation, or, distinct subgroups who experience multiple orgasms. Improvement motives (i.e., masturbating to improve partnered sexual experiences and better understand one's sexual responses) may reflect deliberate efforts to achieve multiple orgasms (Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016). Conversely, coping motives (i.e., to manage negative affect), perceived sexual compulsivity, motives to abstain from masturbation, and less positive sexual attitudes may reflect conflicted feelings about experiencing multiple orgasms during masturbation. For some men, the pleasure of multiple orgasms may coexist with (or be followed by) shame over one's deliberate pursuit of selffocused, solitary sexual pleasure, or the time, effort, or forms of stimulation (e.g., toys) involved.

For example, men's solo sex toy use is judged more negatively than women's (Williams et al., 2026), and stigma surrounding men's sex-toy use extends even the sex-toy industry (Ronen, 2021).

Partnered Sexual Experience and Exploration

Partnered sexual experiences may also be relevant to multiple orgasms during solo masturbation. Masturbating with a partner, in person or remotely, was an important predictor among women and men, whereas recent group masturbation emerged as a predictor among men only. Such experiences may foster greater comfort and confidence with masturbation. Among women, unrestricted sociosexual attitudes may similarly reflect openness to sexual exploration.

Other predictors related to partnered sex emerged among the top 20 but are not discussed due to their lower bootstrap stability.

Additional Implications for Theory and Future Research

These findings, together with recent research linking cognitive absorption to satisfaction with solo masturbation (de Jong, Uhlich, Fischer, & Vowels, 2026), suggest that cognitive processes play an important but understudied role in masturbation. Free of partner-related distractions, masturbation may offer a particularly useful context for investigating how attentional focus and generation of internal sexually arousing imagery via imagination is used to regulate arousal, enhance pleasure, and influence the timing of or number of orgasms one might experience.

For example, the importance of trait imagination and focusing on internal sensations without using fantasy or sexual media might suggest two potential pathways through which attention facilitates sexual arousal, pleasure, and orgasms. First, the ability to generate vivid mental imagery may facilitate arousal through deeper engagement with erotic fantasies. Mosher's (1980) depth of involvement theory offers one possible explanation, proposing that erotic cognitions (e.g., fantasies) congruent with one's sexual schemas foster cognitive absorption and more emotionally profound, pleasurable sexual experiences. Thus, vivid, personally meaningful fantasies may enhance arousal, pleasure, and orgasmic capacity by deepening attentional engagement with erotic cues (de Jong, 2009; Janssen et al., 2000).

Second, the importance of focusing on internal sensations without fantasy or sexual media suggests another pathway to arousal involves heightened attention to internal bodily sensations rather than erotic imagery. Thus, sustained attention to genital and other bodily sensations may enhance sexual function by amplifying awareness of arousal-related sensations and pleasure, even in the absence of sexual fantasy or media. Attention-focused approaches already used in sex therapy, such as mindfulness-based interventions (e.g., Velten et al., 2020) and sensate focus (Masters & Johnson, 1970), train this kind of sustained attention to bodily sensations, and future research could examine whether they also facilitate multiple orgasms.

Strengths and Limitations

This investigation used a large, demographically broad U.S. national sample, with oversampling of older adults, religious individuals, and LGBT individuals, strengthening generalizability of findings. The wide range of candidate predictors spanning sexuality, personality, and well-being was well suited to random forest, which accommodates nonlinear associations and correlated predictors. Because participants reported masturbation in the past 30 days, recall bias was likely reduced. This investigation had several limitations. Multiple orgasms were assessed with a single item that could not distinguish status orgasmus from other forms among women (Masters & Johnson, 1966) or sporadic versus condensed among men (Wibowo & Wassersug, 2016), potentially underestimating prevalence and obscuring form-specific predictors. Intense arousal or altered consciousness during multiple orgasms may also impair recall, and the correspondence between self-reported and physiologically verified multiple orgasms remains unclear. Quota matching and survey weights maximized population alignment, but a probability sample would improve estimate generalizability. Behaviors identified as important predictors were not assessed specifically in the context of multiple orgasms, leaving unclear whether they facilitate multiple orgasms or merely characterize individuals who experience them. Predictor importance and interaction strength are model-dependent and may be stronger in models with fewer predictors (Friedman & Popescu, 2008). Few intersex participants precluded estimating prevalence or predictors for this group, limiting generalizability to these individuals. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; laboratory or experience sampling studies could help clarify these processes (e.g., Henry et al., 2025). Finally, the models explained a modest proportion of variance in multiple orgasms (women: 14%; men: 21%), suggesting that additional, unmeasured factors are likely relevant; future research should examine efforts to achieve multiples, sexual excitation and inhibition, pelvic floor exercises, and sexual function as potential predictors.

Conclusion

Using a large U.S. national sample, this investigation provides the first national prevalence estimates of multiple orgasms during solo masturbation and identified their important predictors using random forest. Multiple orgasms were common, particularly among women, 30% of whom experienced them frequently compared with 7% of men, and multiple orgasms declined with age among men but not women. Predictors reflected not only physiological capacity but also behavioral techniques, cognitive processes, and motivational and attitudinal factors. Specifically, predictors reflected how individuals masturbate including longer sessions, sex toy use, syntribation, and attentional engagement with sensations and imagery. These findings provide a foundation for future research on multiple orgasms and sexual function more generally.

Recruitment, Data Cleaning, Missing Data, and Survey Weights

Recruitment

From August 4-7 2025, participants were recruited and paid via Prolific.com, an online platform by which researchers can recruit a nonprobability online opt-in panel of identityverified participants. On Prolific, the survey was described as a study of "Sex and Personality," in which all responses were anonymous, and with a duration of approximately 45 minutes. Participants were paid $8.50 via the platform.

We aimed to recruit participants in accordance with our preregistered plan (https://osf.io/mb2je) but were constrained by availabilities of groups on Prolific. We first recruited 2,448 participants (18 or older, residents of USA) using the Prolific function that quotamatches the sample to US census data on sex, age, and race/ethnicity. Next, to ensure adequate representation in the dataset and to allow for subgroup analyses, we oversampled 241 religiously affiliated individuals, 360 individuals aged 65 or older, and 427 individuals who identified as LGBTQ+. These recruitment efforts resulted in 3,476 participants who reached the end of the survey.

Data cleaning

Data were cleaned in accordance with our preregistered plan (https://osf.io/mb2je) with four deviations noted below. Of the 3579 individuals who began the survey, 28 did not proceed past the first page of the survey (demographics), and another 75 did not proceed past the midpoint of the survey. These cases were removed, leaving 3,476 who reached the end of the survey. Among these, no participants' survey completion time was below 3 SD from the mean. Inattentive responses were removed from the dataset for the following reasons: 1. There were 24 participants who failed more than one of the three instructional attention checks (e.g., "Please select very frequently, this is an attention check"). 2. Age was asked for twice, and four participants' reports differed by more than one year. 3. In terms of open-ended questions, there were 31 participants who left more than one blank, entered text that was generic and could apply to any survey topic, that was an obvious placeholder text such as "nothing," or repeated the survey question. Some of these instances of poor data were within the same case, and in total, 53 cases were removed from the dataset for the above reasons, leaving 3423 participants in the final sample.

There were four deviations from our preregistered data cleaning plan: 1. In addition to removing participants who did not proceed past the first page of the survey, we also removed the 75 participants who did not complete the entire survey (nor did they proceed past the mid-point). On the Prolific platform, participants withdraw from studies on the understanding they also forgo the compensated promised on the consent form; thus, we interpreted their withdrawal from the study as withdrawal of consent to use their data. 2. The plan stated that we would remove cases with straight-lined extreme responses (e.g., "Not at all true" or "Completely true") on either the Brief Attitudes Toward Solo Masturbation Scale (de Jong & Adams, 2025) or the Sex Positivity/Negativity Scale (Hangen & Rogge, 2022). However, we realized that such cases are, in fact, plausible (e.g., consistent responses of "Not at all true" could reflect indifference towards the topic, and consistent responses of "Extremely" could reflect very strong mixed feelings). Thus, these criteria for exclusions were not used. 3. The plan stated that Qualtrics' ReCAPTCHA v3 security feature would be used to flag responses suspected to be bots; no cases flagged by this feature showed signs of bot activity; thus, this criterion was not used. 4. The plan stated that cases with any discrepancy in the two reports of age would be excluded; however, we realized a one-year difference could reflect an honest mistake, and we set a criterion of a discrepancy of more than one year for exclusion.

Missing Data

In accordance with our preregistered plan (https://osf.io/mb2je), we calculated the percentage of participants who were missing data at the construct level (i.e., have responses on some but not all scales). This calculation was made across the 14 multi-item scales that were administered in all versions of the survey (i.e., excluding measures administered only to participants who had masturbated or had partnered sex in the past 30 days, etc.). There were 0.1% of participants with missing data; as per Newman's (2014) guidelines, this is well below the 10% threshold above which maximum likelihood or multiple imputation approaches likely have any benefit over pairwise deletion.

Survey Weights

Survey weights were used to align the dataset with known US population benchmarks, thereby maximizing the representativeness of the sample and generalizability of prevalence estimates. Survey weights were calculated using raking (Battaglia et al., 2009), which allows for weighting on multiple variables without requiring complete cross-classification. Other approaches requiring complete cross-classification can result in cells with few or no cases, instability, and extreme weights. Using the IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29) Complex Samples module, we raked on variables known to be associated with masturbation and related constructs and to adjust for under-and over-representation of key demographic factors, including for overrepresentation due to oversampling. Variables raked on were age, race/ethnicity, sex, affiliation with a religion, identification as LGBT, and census region. Control totals were drawn from the 2020 US Census (U.S. Census Bureau, 2020), Pew Research Center (2025), and Gallup (2025). Following guidelines on raking (Battaglia et al., 2009; Izrael et al., 2009), weights were examined for extreme high values and high variability. No weight was greater than the criterion of four times the mean weight. However, the coefficient of variation (SD/mean) was 0.56, which is greater than the criterion of .50 above which excessively high variability is indicated and leads to increased standard errors.
