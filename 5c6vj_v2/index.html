Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. 1.1 Why Epistemic Quality Matters

Large language models (LLMs), particularly those deployed as conversational chatbots, are increasingly used for more than information retrieval. People turn to these systems for everyday tasks, decision-making, emotional support, and health-related questions. Nearly half of U.S. adults (49%) report regularly using a chatbot for everyday activities spanning information seeking, work, emotional support, and health (Gottfried et al., 2026). Research also suggests that users are increasingly integrating LLMs into everyday tasks and decisions (Kim et al., 2025), psychologically meaningful interactions (Huang et al., 2026), and mental health questions and emotional support (AXA, 2026; Rousmaniere et al., 2026). As these systems become embedded in daily life, they are increasingly positioned to influence not only what users know, but how they evaluate information, form beliefs, and make decisions.

Epistemic Quality. This growing influence raises important questions about "epistemic quality", a concept that goes beyond accuracy and truth and into systems of belief, explored in educational research. Hudson (2019) contrasts learning based on memorization and following fixed procedures with learning that encourages critical thinking, creative reasoning, problem-solving, and learning from mistakes. From this perspective, high epistemic quality involves not simply possessing knowledge, but developing the ability to question, evaluate, and use that knowledge in new situations. Hudson et al.'s work (2019 Hudson et al.'s work ( , 2022) ) frames epistemic quality as more than knowing correct facts, and involves understanding their meaning, recognizing how they connect, and applying that understanding in meaningful ways. Building on this perspective, we define epistemic quality in conversational AI as: the extent to which model responses support accurate understanding, appropriately calibrated confidence, and beliefs grounded in credible evidence (see Table 1 for disambiguation from related terms). High epistemic quality indicates that responses help users critically evaluate information, consider alternative perspectives, form accurate, evidence-based beliefs, and calibrate their confidence to the strength of the evidence, whereas low epistemic quality indicates that responses reinforce unsupported beliefs, distorted interpretations, or unwarranted certainty.

Misinformation. Conversational AI can also undermine users' understanding by generating or reinforcing misinformation. Although misinformation is commonly understood as false or misleading information (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017), its potential influence extends beyond the factual accuracy of individual claims. In conversational AI, misinformation-related risks may also arise when models reinforce unsupported beliefs, express unwarranted certainty, or fail to appropriately challenge misleading assumptions (Chen & Shu, 2024b). These behaviors may make questionable claims appear more credible, even when models do not explicitly generate new false information. Evaluating misinformation-related vulnerabilities therefore requires attention not only to the accuracy of model outputs, but also to how models engage with users' beliefs and reasoning over time.

The importance of epistemic quality, and the related proliferation of misinformation, is amplified by the scale at which conversational AI systems are being adopted. OpenAI reported that ChatGPT served more than 700 million weekly active users in January 2026 (OpenAI, 2026), increasing to 900 million the following month (Malik, 2026). Google's AI Overviews reportedly reached more than two billion monthly users in July 2025 (Joseph, 2025), while Anthropic's usage research documented greater personal use of Claude on weekends, including conversations involving medical questions and emotional support (Massenkoff et al., 2026). Together, these trends suggest that conversational AI systems are becoming influential intermediaries between users and the information they encounter. As a result, evaluating LLM performance requires attention to more than factual correctness. A model may provide accurate information while still reinforcing an unsupported assumption, communicating unwarranted certainty, or failing to introduce relevant evidence or alternative perspectives. Conversely, a model may support epistemic quality by helping users examine their assumptions, recognize uncertainty, and make more informed decisions. Understanding these distinctions is increasingly important as conversational AI systems become integrated into how people interpret information and reason about consequential issues.

1.2 What Leads to Epistemic Misconduct

Epistemic Misconduct. This benchmark focuses specifically on how model responses may support or undermine the quality of users' beliefs, reasoning, and decisions. We refer to these observable model behaviors as epistemic conduct. Understanding why models sometimes produce misleading or overly accommodating responses is therefore important for evaluating their potential influence on users. One source of these behaviors is the information on which LLMs are trained. During training data curation, false, outdated, biased, or otherwise low-quality information may remain in the data, creating opportunities for models to reproduce unsupported claims (Ferentinos et al., 2026). Because LLMs learn patterns from large datasets rather than independently verifying the truth of every statement, they may reproduce misinformation encountered during training (Naveed et al., 2025). Here, misinformation refers to false, inaccurate, or misleading information, regardless of whether there is an intention to deceive (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017).

Epistemic misconduct may also arise during post-training, when models are optimized to produce responses that satisfy human preferences or other specified performance criteria. These processes include supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023), reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (Lambert et al., 2024), and reinforcement learning using AI-generated feedback (Lee et al., 2024). Although these techniques can improve model behavior, the signals used to reward responses may not fully capture epistemic quality. For example, if a model is rewarded for producing confident, fluent, or agreeable responses, it may learn to prioritize those characteristics even when acknowledging uncertainty or challenging a user's assumptions would be more appropriate. This illustrates a broader concern in machine learning: optimizing for an imperfect measure of performance can produce behavior that does not fully reflect the intended objective (Skalse et al., 2022). Importantly, epistemic misconduct does not always result in explicitly false statements. A model may provide factually accurate information while still accepting an unsupported premise, expressing unwarranted certainty, or reinforcing a user's questionable interpretation. Conversely, a model may acknowledge uncertainty, challenge an assumption, or help the user evaluate competing explanations.

Hallucinations. A related failure mode is hallucination, in which models generate plausible but unsupported or factually incorrect information, sometimes with unwarranted confidence (Ji et al., 2023). Research suggests that training and evaluation procedures may contribute to this problem when they reward producing an answer rather than acknowledging uncertainty (Kalai et al., 2025). For example, a model may invent a citation, provide an unsupported explanation, or present a speculative claim as an established fact. Such responses can function as misinformation when users interpret them as trustworthy information. This concern is particularly relevant when a model communicates with confidence or apparent expertise, potentially encouraging users to rely on claims they have not independently verified (Ji et al., 2023; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

These distinctions illustrate why epistemic quality cannot be assessed through factual accuracy alone. We developed mPACT-MIS-v1.0 as an initial benchmark for evaluating observable model behaviors that may support or undermine users' epistemic quality, particularly in misinformation-related conversations.

1.3 Why Epistemic Conduct Might Deteriorate in Multi-Turn Conversations

Epistemic risk may become more pronounced during multi-turn conversations because each model response can influence the direction of subsequent exchanges. When a model accepts or reinforces an unsupported belief early in a conversation, later responses may build on that assumption rather than question it. Over time, repeated reinforcement may make the belief appear increasingly credible or well-supported, even when little or no new evidence has been introduced.

Research suggests that LLMs can perform less reliably during extended conversations than when responding to a single question, particularly when they must track new information or reconsider earlier assumptions (Laban et al., 2026). To manage longer conversations, researchers have explored techniques such as context compression and recursive summarization, which shorten or summarize earlier exchanges so that models can retain important information without processing the entire conversation history (Jung et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2023). For example, if a user initially expresses an unsupported belief that a financial institution is deliberately concealing an impending economic collapse, a model may need to revisit that assumption as the conversation progresses. If earlier exchanges are summarized without preserving the uncertainty surrounding the claim, the model could potentially treat the user's assumption as an established fact in later responses; this highlights the importance of evaluating how models maintain and respond to conversational context over time.

Sycophancy.

Beyond the challenges of maintaining conversational context, LLMs may also reinforce unsupported beliefs through sycophancy, or the tendency to agree with or accommodate users' expressed views even when those views warrant correction (Sharma et al., 2024). Research has also identified excessive accommodation and failures to challenge harmful assumptions as weaknesses in conversational AI (Cheng, Hawkins, et al., 2026). These behaviors may be particularly consequential during extended conversations, where repeated agreement can make a user's assumptions appear increasingly credible without introducing reliable evidence. Research further suggests that emotional framing can influence model responses (Gozzi & Fallucchi, 2026), raising additional questions about how models respond when users express strongly held or emotionally significant beliefs.

Together, these concerns highlight why evaluating individual model responses may provide an incomplete picture of epistemic quality. mPACT-MIS-v1.0 therefore examines how models reinforce, challenge, or escalate risky beliefs and behaviors across multi-turn conversations, rather than evaluating each response in isolation.

1.4 Real-World Implications of Epistemic Conduct

Epistemic conduct matters because model responses may influence more than the accuracy of information users receive. They may also shape how people interpret evidence, develop confidence in their beliefs, and make decisions. Research points to potential consequences at individual, interpersonal, and societal levels, although the strength and nature of the evidence differ across these contexts.

At the individual level, research suggests that LLM responses can influence users' beliefs and judgments. Danry et al. (2025) found that deceptive AI-generated explanations could shift participants' beliefs about true and false news headlines, sometimes more effectively than honest explanations. Research on source credibility also suggests that information presented by a seemingly authoritative source can be more persuasive, making the confident presentation of unsupported claims a relevant concern (Pornpitakpan, 2004).

Studies of sycophantic AI have identified additional effects, including greater attitude extremity and overconfidence (Rathje et al., 2026), increased dependence on AI advice, and reduced prosocial intentions (Cheng, Lee, et al., 2026). Although these studies measure different outcomes, together they illustrate why evaluating factual accuracy alone may overlook important aspects of how AI responses influence human reasoning.

These concerns may be particularly consequential when users seek guidance on health, personal circumstances, or emotionally significant beliefs. For example, research has documented LLMs generating false medical information in response to unsupported user assumptions (Chen et al., 2025). These findings illustrate the risks of presenting unsupported information as credible guidance, particularly when users are making consequential decisions. Other researchers have raised concerns about conversational AI reinforcing cognitive distortions, paranoia, or delusional beliefs in vulnerable users (Morrin et al., 2026). Public reporting has described cases in which repeated AI validation allegedly contributed to increasingly distorted or conspiratorial beliefs, although these accounts do not establish how frequently such outcomes occur or demonstrate causation (Jargon, 2025; Myers, 2026).

At the interpersonal level, conversational AI may influence how users interpret relationships, respond to disagreements, and seek support from others. As people increasingly use AI systems as confidants, companions, and sources of emotional guidance (Andoh, 2026; Zao-Sanders, 2026), the manner in which models validate or challenge users' interpretations becomes important. Ibrahim et al. (2026) found that exposure to sycophantic AI made subsequent human interactions feel more effortful and less satisfying, while Cheng, Lee, et al. (2026) identified reduced prosocial or reparative intentions and greater dependence following sycophantic advice. These findings suggest that the consequences of model behavior may extend beyond the immediate conversation and affect how users approach interactions with other people.

At the societal level, conversational AI may contribute to the production and circulation of misinformation (Chen & Shu, 2024a , 2024b; Park & Nan, 2026). Information-disorder research distinguishes misinformation from deliberately deceptive disinformation and emphasizes that their consequences depend on how information is created, communicated, and shared (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017). Public health research has also documented how misinformation can contribute to vaccine hesitancy and undermine trust in health institutions (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2021; Ruggeri et al., 2024). As conversational AI becomes more widely used, understanding whether models challenge or reinforce misleading narratives is therefore relevant not only to individual reasoning but also to broader information environments.

Taken together, this research suggests that the consequences of conversational AI may extend beyond factual errors to include changes in users' confidence, beliefs, decisionmaking, and interactions with others. Although the evidence spans different research methods and does not establish a single causal pathway to harm, it raises important questions about whether models appropriately challenge unsupported assumptions or reinforce them through excessive agreement and validation. These concerns highlight the need for evaluation frameworks that assess not only the accuracy of model responses, but also how models engage with users' reasoning across conversations.

1.5 Gaps in Existing Benchmarks of Conversational Epistemic Risk

Misinformation, hallucination, and sycophancy represent distinct but potentially overlapping ways that conversational AI can undermine users' epistemic quality. A model may provide factually accurate information while accepting a user's unsupported assumption, resist misinformation in response to a single question but become more accommodating over repeated exchanges, or avoid overt hallucinations while reinforcing distorted interpretations through excessive agreement. Evaluating these behaviors separately may therefore overlook how they interact within conversations and influence the quality of users' reasoning.

Existing benchmarks have made important contributions to evaluating these epistemic conduct concerns (Table 2). TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) assesses whether models reproduce common misconceptions, while HaluEval (Li et al., 2023) and FActScore (Min et al., 2023) evaluate hallucinations and factual accuracy. MisinfoBench (Yang et al., 2025) examines models' ability to recognize and resist misinformation, including in multi-turn settings. Other benchmarks focus on sycophancy, evaluating whether models inappropriately agree with users, accommodate their beliefs, or change their responses under conversational pressure (Duffy, n.d.; Fanous et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2025; Cheng, Yu, et al., 2026). Together, these approaches provide valuable tools for identifying specific problems in model behavior.

However, important evaluation gaps remain. First, benchmarks that focus on individual epistemic conduct concerns may not capture how multiple behaviors interact within the same response. For example, a model might challenge a user's unsupported belief while simultaneously reinforcing part of that belief or presenting itself as having unwarranted authority. Second, although some existing benchmarks evaluate multi-turn interactions, many evaluations continue to emphasize individual responses or relatively short exchanges, potentially overlooking how model behavior changes as conversations progress. Third, evaluating whether a response is factually correct does not necessarily establish whether it helps users question assumptions, tolerate uncertainty, or reason independently. Together, these limitations support the need for a broader evaluation of conversational epistemic quality.

Developing such evaluations also requires careful consideration of how scenarios and model responses are assessed. Publicly available benchmark materials may overlap with model training data, while human annotation and automated evaluation can introduce their own biases (Cui et al., 2025; Perez et al., 2023). These concerns do not invalidate existing benchmarks, but they highlight the importance of purpose-built scenarios, transparent annotation procedures, and clearly defined evaluation criteria.

Clinical and behavioral-science expertise may offer a valuable complementary perspective.

Evaluating conversational epistemic quality involves more than identifying false statements, it also requires attention to how a model responds to uncertainty, emotional vulnerability, strongly held beliefs, and requests for validation. For example, a response may appear supportive while reinforcing an unsupported belief, or it may correctly challenge a belief in a manner that discourages further reflection. Expertise in human behavior and communication can help inform judgments about these interactional patterns.

Taken together, these considerations highlight the need for a benchmark that evaluates multiple epistemic conduct concerns within realistic, multi-turn conversations. Rather than assessing only whether models produce accurate information, such a framework should examine how models respond to users' beliefs, how supportive and harmful behaviors can coexist, and whether their responses encourage independent reasoning or reinforce unsupported assumptions. This broader approach motivates the development of mPACT-MIS-v1.0.

1.6 Toward a Holistic Benchmark of Epistemic Quality

To address the limitations of existing evaluation approaches, we developed mPACT-MIS-v1.0, a clinician-informed benchmark for evaluating how LLM responses may support or undermine users' epistemic quality during multi-turn conversations. Rather than focusing exclusively on factual accuracy or isolated model failures, the benchmark examines observable conversational behaviors that may influence how users evaluate information, reason through uncertainty, and make decisions (i.e., epistemic conduct).

Because users often turn to conversational AI for guidance, reassurance, and help making decisions, evaluating model behavior requires attention to more than the accuracy of the information provided, it also requires considering whether responses support users' independent reasoning and decision-making or reinforce unsupported beliefs and dependence on the model. To establish a behavioral science foundation for this evaluation, we selected Self-Determination Theory (SDT; Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000). SDT identifies three basic psychological needs: autonomy, the experience of making decisions voluntarily rather than through external pressure; competence, the sense of being capable of understanding and navigating one's circumstances; and relatedness, the experience of feeling understood and connected to others. SDT provides a framework for understanding how interactions may support these psychological needs rather than rely on pressure or control. This perspective informed the selection of observable model behaviors that may support or undermine users' reasoning and decision-making.

From an SDT-informed perspective, responses that respect users' autonomy, support their capacity to reason independently, and maintain appropriate connections to others may be better suited to informed decision-making than responses that rely primarily on agreement, pressure, or unsupported authority. For example, a model might accurately correct an unsupported belief while discouraging further discussion or independent reflection. Alternatively, it might challenge the same belief while explaining the evidence, acknowledging uncertainty, and encouraging the user to consider alternative perspectives.

Although both responses challenge the belief, the latter may better support the user's capacity for independent reasoning.

This theoretical perspective informed the benchmark's behavioral labeling framework (Section 2.2). We identified four observable behaviors that may support users' epistemic quality. Assessing risk involves asking questions rather than making unsupported assumptions about the user's circumstances, allowing a more informed and individualized response. Challenging the user involves identifying risks, correcting unsupported beliefs, or introducing safer alternatives rather than reinforcing questionable assumptions.

Empowering the user involves providing information and opportunities for reflection that support independent reasoning and decision-making, most directly reflecting autonomy and competence. Redirecting to appropriate support recognizes the limits of the model's expertise and helps users access relevant professional guidance or external resources, potentially supporting both competence and relatedness. These behaviors were selected through an SDT-informed, clinically grounded approach; they are not intended as direct or exclusive measures of individual psychological needs.

The framework also identifies harmful behaviors that may undermine epistemic quality, including promoting risky beliefs or behaviors, escalating risk, misrepresenting authority, and providing shallow disclaimers that do not meaningfully address harmful content. By allowing helpful and harmful labels to coexist within the same response, mPACT-MIS-v1.0 captures situations in which a model may appear supportive while simultaneously reinforcing an unsupported belief or encouraging risky behavior. The benchmark evaluates these observable behaviors without requiring assumptions about the specific technical processes responsible for producing them.

To examine these behaviors in context, mPACT-MIS-v1.0 uses expert-authored, multi-turn conversations representing ten personas across diverse misinformation-related and psychologically salient scenarios. Conversations ranged from 5 to 17 model turns, allowing evaluation of how model behavior changed as users expressed beliefs, uncertainty, and increasingly risky intentions. All scenarios were developed specifically for this evaluation rather than adapted from existing public benchmarks, reducing the likelihood of direct overlap with previously released evaluation materials, although overlap with training data cannot be ruled out.

Together, these design choices distinguish mPACT-MIS-v1.0 from evaluations focused primarily on factual accuracy or isolated model responses. By combining an SDT-informed behavioral framework, expert-authored conversational scenarios, and multi-label evaluation across extended interactions, the benchmark assesses how models respond to users' reasoning and beliefs, including behaviors that may reinforce or interrupt potentially harmful conversational patterns. In doing so, it provides a framework for evaluating conversational epistemic quality while recognizing that downstream changes in users' beliefs and decisions require separate empirical investigation.

2.1 Multi-Turn Conversation Data Generation

A total of 60 multi-turn conversations (10 conversations per model) were generated from ten expert-authored personas evaluated across six LLMs, each audited for quality by a licensed clinician. To support consistency and realism, experts used a structured persona reference guide that specified key dimensions of character construction, including information seeking, epistemic validation, validation orientation, and authority orientation (Table 2). This structure helped ensure that roleplays were grounded in plausible human behavior.

2.1.1. Software and Model Selection

Experts role played each of the 10 personas across six models (see below), using the same intended conversational trajectory to support comparability across models. Experts accessed the models through the mpathic Studio™ benchmarking platform, which exposes public LLM APIs through a chat interface and supports annotation.

Six model arms were included in mPACT-MIS-v1.0: Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini arm (henceforth referred to as Gemini 2.5 Flash, albeit consisting of two Gemini 3 Flash Preview conversations when Gemini 2.5 Flash was deprecated), GPT-5.2, Grok 4.1, LLaMA 3.3 70B, and Mistral Medium 3. These model arms were selected to represent proprietary and openweight systems available through the mpathic Studio™ benchmarking platform during the evaluation period. All models were accessed through public APIs using the provider defaults available at run time. No additional system prompts, developer instructions, or safety instructions were provided. Temperature and other sampling parameters were left at provider defaults. Each conversation was conducted in a single uninterrupted session with no prior context or conversation history.

2.1.2 Expert Author Selection

Generating conversations that could meaningfully test epistemic quality required authors capable of more than producing plausible dialogue. Realistic role-play depends on knowing how psychological vulnerability actually presents and evolves. Namely, how disclosure escalates gradually rather than all at once; how emotional distress alters the way a person weighs new information; and how conversational pressure (a persuasive tone, a validating response, a confident claim) shifts someone's stated beliefs over the course of an exchange. Authoring roleplays without this training risks producing personas that are either implausibly consistent (vulnerability presented as a fixed trait from the first turn) or exaggerated in ways that make model failures easy to elicit but unrepresentative of ordinary interactions. We therefore recruited authors from an expert population specifically selected for expertise in behavioral risk assessment and therapeutic communication, rather than general-purpose annotators or writers.

Authors were selected from mpathic's independent expert network, mpathic Experts™. At project launch, 477 experts were contacted from the pool of experts available for project work. Because the project required rapid staffing, selection proceeded among those who responded within the specified time frame. Twenty-five respondents met initial screening criteria for relevant specialty expertise, and eight authors were selected after additional screening and calibration training. Experts were approved to contribute to dataset generation if their calibration conversations matched assigned vulnerability levels and met quality standards for realism and persona fidelity.

The selected authors represented a range of educational backgrounds and clinical experience. Six experts were clinicians who held master's-level degrees, and two held doctoral degrees. Five clinician authors held active clinical licensure. Clinicians reported experience working with diverse populations experiencing anxiety, depression, traumarelated symptoms, suicidality, emotional distress, interpersonal conflict, and other complex behavioral health concerns across community-based, outpatient, telehealth, and digital care settings. Several clinician authors additionally reported experience in crisis support, risk assessment, therapeutic communication, and human evaluation workflows involving nuanced interpretation of sensitive, ambiguous, or potentially harmful content. This combination of expertise was intended to support clinically informed identification of behavioral risk signals, emotionally harmful misinformation patterns, escalation failures, and communication issues in high-stakes conversational environments.

2.1.3 Well-being for Sensitive Content

Because clinical AI work involves frequent engagement with sensitive content, individuals actively assigned to projects were able to access available support resources, which included individual counseling sessions, peer support groups, vicarious trauma resiliency training, and flexible scheduling. In addition, anyone supervising received targeted training in mental health and well-being support.

2.1.4 Author Training and Roleplay Calibration

Authors completed training in harm evaluation and realistic conversational simulation before generating the dataset. A central component of this training addressed the distinction between trait-level and state-level vulnerability. Personas were deliberately constructed to reflect baseline psychological stability -the absence of an active mental health crisis, acute suicidality, or diagnosed condition impairing judgment at the outset of the conversationrather than a static vulnerability the model was merely required to detect. Instead, vulnerability was designed to emerge dynamically over the course of the interaction, through mechanisms such as escalating emotional disclosure, increasing reliance on the model's validation in place of outside perspectives, or the gradual narrowing of the user's stated options as the conversation progressed. This distinction shaped both the tone and progression of conversations wherein authors were trained to begin roleplays from a plausible, non-crisis baseline and to introduce vulnerability incrementally, in ways an ordinary conversation could plausibly produce, rather than presenting it as a fixed trait present from the first turn. Training further focused on how model responses can influence belief formation and quality over time, including recognizing patterns such as reinforcement, overconfidence, omission, insufficient correction, and escalation. Authors were taught to construct realistic conversations that could surface these failure modes while maintaining conversational plausibility. The objective was not to create adversarial jailbreaks, but to capture ordinary interactional conditions under which epistemic failures could emerge.

Before proceeding to full dataset generation, authors completed two calibration roleplays across different models. These exercises were reviewed for persona fidelity, conversational realism, adherence to the assigned persona, and their ability to elicit informative model behavior. Authors advanced to full-scale data generation only after demonstrating consistent performance across these criteria.

2.2 Evaluation Framework of LLM Responses

As described in Section 1.6, the behavioral evaluation framework was informed by Self-Determination Theory (SDT), selected collaboratively by three clinicians to provide a theoretical foundation for evaluating how model responses may support or undermine users' epistemic quality. The resulting labels operationalize observable helpful and harmful model behaviors, rather than directly measuring users' psychological needs or changes in their beliefs.

mPACT-MIS-v1.0 used a structured annotation framework to identify harmful and helpful signals in model responses (Table 5). The primary unit of annotation was the model talk turn, with multiple labels assigned when applicable. Each response was evaluated not only in isolation, but also in relation to the user's expressed beliefs, prior conversational context, and the evolving trajectory of the interaction. The framework distinguished between harmful behaviors that could degrade epistemic quality and helpful behaviors that could support user reasoning, calibration, and/or safer decision-making.

2.3 Human Annotation and Reference Labels

All annotations in this study were assigned by trained human annotators using the mPACT-MIS-v1.0 labeling guide in the mpathic Studio™ environment. Model responses were evaluated at the talk-turn level using four helpful and four harmful behavioral labels (Table 5). Multiple labels could be assigned to the same response, and each individual label was counted at most once per turn. If none of the predefined behaviors was identified, the response received no code (NC). The resulting annotations characterize observable model behavior rather than directly measuring changes in users' beliefs, reasoning, or decisions.

Participant (roleplayed user) turns were separately annotated using two tags: belief, indicating that the persona expressed a risky or unsupported belief, and behavior, indicating that the persona described a risky action already taken or intended. Both tags could be assigned to the same turn. These annotations identified the risk expressions used in the conditional analyses described below.

The final evaluation dataset consisted of 60 conversations, corresponding to one conversation for each of the ten persona-model pairings. A thorough quality review for realism and conversational fidelity to the assigned persona was conducted for the entire dataset. Two of the ten Gemini-arm conversations (gendered adversarial reasoning and high-risk financial reasoning) used the Gemini 3 Flash Preview endpoint; the remaining eight used Gemini 2.5 Flash. All ten were retained in the analysis, and this version heterogeneity is acknowledged as a limitation.

2.7 Statistical Analysis

We calculated the conversation-averaged mPACT-MIS-v1.0 score for all models (hereafter, the mPACT-MIS-v1.0 score) and the turn-pooled variant described in Section 2.4. We also conducted five supporting analyses:

Label rates. For each model, we calculated the percentage of turns carrying each model label.

(ii) Risk-conditioned behaviors. We partitioned model turns according to whether the immediately preceding participant turn carried a risky-belief or risky-behavior tag. We then calculated two contrasts: the rate of challenged user minus the rate of promoted risky beliefs or behaviors, and the rate of turns with any helpful label minus the rate with any harmful label. We evaluated three treatments of mixed turns: (a) counted on both the helpful and harmful sides, (b) counted only on the harmful side, and (c) excluded.

(iii) Transition-state dynamics. Across consecutive model turns, we estimated the probabilities of entering harm (a helpful-only or NC turn followed by a harmful-only or mixed turn), persisting in harm, recovering to helpful-only, and recovering to NC.

(iv) Correction latency. We counted the model turns between the participant's first risk expression and the model's first challenged user turn.

(v) Participant response. We estimated the probability that the participant expressed risk immediately after a model turn in each state. We restricted this analysis to cases in which the participant's previous turn carried no risk tag so that prior risk did not mechanically inflate the estimate.

Analyses were performed in Python 3.12 with pandas, NumPy, and SciPy; figures were produced with Matplotlib. For all reported confidence intervals, we set 95% confidence and implemented nonparametric bootstrapping that resampled conversations with replacement for 2000 replicates. Conversations were chosen rather than talk-turns given the contextual confounding of future talk-turns due to previous talk-turns in conversations.

A model that never produced a codable behavior at all would therefore 6.67, which is the reference point of reading the results below: a model above it helped often enough to do better than saying nothing, and a model below it would have avoided harming the user by saying nothing of substance.

3.3 Behavior After a Risk Expression

The participant's preceding turn was the strongest single predictor of harm. Pooled across models, a model turn was harmful-only or mixed 74% of the time when the participant had stated a risky belief in the preceding turn and 73% of the time when the participant described a risky behavior in the preceding turn, as compared to 28% when the preceding turn carried neither tag (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S4). 3.5 Correction was Prompt but Hedged

Across the full dataset, 49 of 60 conversations included at least one challenged user turn. In 27 conversations, the model challenged the user immediately, on the next model turn after the participant's first risk expression (Figure 8a). Claude Sonnet 4.5 and GPT-5.2 showed the most immediate challenging behavior, doing so in eight and seven of their ten conversations, respectively. LLaMA 3.3 70B, the Gemini arm, and Mistral Medium 3 each challenged the user immediately in only two of ten conversations, and each had two to three conversations in which no challenge occurred.

Most mixed turns involved a challenge that was paired with at least one harmful label. Of the 78 mixed turns, 77 included challenged user alongside a harmful label (Figure 8b). In many cases, the challenge was accompanied by multiple harmful behaviors: 46 mixed turns included two or more harmful labels, and 19 included three harmful labels. 3.7 Variation Across Personas

Model performance varied as much across personas as it did across models (Figure 11). Mean scores across the six models ranged from 6.3 for the cyberbullying -perpetrator persona to 3.1 for DIY legal reasoning, a difference comparable in magnitude to the 4.4-point spread between the best and worst models. In the four lowest-scoring personas (high-risk financial reasoning, bereavement, antivaccine reasoning, and DIY legal reasoning) only two conversations by a model other than Claude Sonnet 4.5 kept more than half of their turns within policy (LLaMA 3.3 70B on financial reasoning and GPT-5.2 on bereavement). Model rankings were not stable across personas: Grok 4.1 scored 8.7 on conspiracy thinking and 8.7 on looksmaxxing but 1.5 and 1.4 on the two cyberbullying personas, while Gemini arm scored 8.8 on cyberbullying -perpetrator -but 0.6 on gendered adversarial reasoning. Claude Sonnet 4.5 was the top scorer on five of the ten personas and the lowest on none, and GPT-5.2 was likewise never the lowest. The lowest score on a persona belonged most frequently to the Gemini arm or to Mistral Medium 3 (three personas each), followed by Grok 4.1 and LLaMa 3.3 70B (having two personas each).

Panels c and d of Figure 11 illustrate two distinct patterns of conversational performance: avoiding harmful responses and recovering after they occur. For five of the six models, conversations with a greater proportion of turns without harmful labels generally also had a later first harmful response. Claude Sonnet 4.5 showed a different pattern, maintaining a relatively high proportion of helpful-labeled responses (55% across personas) even when harmful responses occurred early in a conversation. For example, in the cyberbullyingperpetrator scenario, Claude Sonnet 4.5 first exhibited harmful behavior approximately 30% of the way through the conversation, yet 60% of its responses contained no harmful labels, illustrating that an early harmful response did not necessarily lead to persistent harm.

Model performance also varied by persona. Across all six models, the lowest average proportions of responses without harmful labels occurred in the DIY legal reasoning (28%) and high-risk financial reasoning (30%) scenarios. Both involved users seeking guidance on consequential legal or financial decisions, suggesting that these contexts may present particular challenges for models' conversational epistemic conduct.

3.8 Consistency of Rankings Across Metrics

Figure 12 compares model rankings across the evaluation metrics to examine whether models performed consistently or showed different strengths and weaknesses depending on the measure. The metrics are organized into two categories: level metrics, which summarize the overall distribution of response types, and dynamic metrics, which characterize how model behavior changes over the course of a conversation.

Rankings were highly consistent across the level metrics (Figure 12a; Supplementary Table S11). All six models maintained the same relative positions for helpful-only responses, harmful-only responses, responses with no coded behavior (NC), and the mPACT-MIS-v1.0 score under both aggregation methods. The proportion of mixed responses was the exception. GPT-5.2 ranked last on this measure, with 17% of its responses classified as mixed, while LLaMA 3.3 70B and Grok 4.1 ranked highest. Among responses containing at least one harmful label, 44.8% also contained a helpful label for Claude Sonnet 4.5 and 36.7% for GPT-5.2, compared with 16.2%-21.4% for the remaining four models. These findings indicate that harmful behaviors were more frequently accompanied by helpful behaviors in responses from Claude Sonnet 4.5 and GPT-5.2, illustrating how potentially corrective and harmful elements can coexist within a single response.

In contrast, rankings varied more substantially across the dynamic metrics (Figure 12b), which examined entry into harmful states, persistence of harmful responses, recovery, and correction. Claude Sonnet 4.5 ranked first in minimizing harmful-state persistence, recovering to helpful-only responses, and prompt correction, but third in avoiding entry into harmful states. GPT-5.2 ranked first in avoiding entry into harmful states and second in prompt correction, yet performed less favorably on persistence and recovery once harmful behavior occurred. LLaMA 3.3 70B showed a different pattern, ranking last in avoiding entry into harm but second in minimizing persistence and recovering to helpful-only responses, while performing particularly well in recovery to responses with no coded behavior. Grok 4.1 similarly ranked second in avoiding entry into harm but fifth in minimizing persistence.

Together, these findings show that aggregate scores and response distributions provide a relatively consistent picture of overall model performance, while conversational dynamics reveal additional differences in how models encounter, sustain, and recover from harmful behavior. Evaluating these complementary dimensions provides a more complete characterization of model performance than relying on a single composite score.

4. Discussion

>Discussion mPACT-MIS-v1.0 identified substantial variation in how six LLMs responded to users' risky beliefs and behaviors across 60 expert-authored, multi-turn conversations.

4.1 Principal Findings

Five principal findings emerged, highlighting differences in overall model performance, the prevalence of harmful and helpful behaviors, responses to expressed risk, conversational dynamics, and variation across personas.

First, Claude Sonnet 4.5 demonstrated the most favorable overall performance across the evaluated conversations, although it did not lead on every metric. It achieved the highest conversation-averaged mPACT-MIS-v1.0 score (6.98) and was the only model with more helpful-only than harmful-only responses. The remaining five models demonstrated less favorable overall performance, with scores ranging from 4.79 for GPT-5.2 to 2.60 for Mistral Medium 3. However, confidence intervals overlapped considerably, and models' relative rankings varied across the different behavioral and conversational metrics. Given the relatively small number of conversations per model (n = 10), these comparisons should be interpreted as preliminary rather than definitive evidence of differences in model capabilities.

Second, harmful behaviors were common across the evaluated conversations. More than half of all model turns (52.8%) received at least one harmful label, with promoted risky beliefs or behaviors being the most frequently identified harmful behavior. Misrepresented authority was also common, indicating that models sometimes presented themselves as possessing greater expertise or authority than their capabilities warranted. These findings suggest that epistemically problematic responses may involve more than factual inaccuracies; models may also reinforce unsupported assumptions or encourage risky behaviors while communicating in an apparently knowledgeable or authoritative manner. Among helpful model behaviors, challenged user was most frequent, while assessed risk, empowered user, and redirected to appropriate supports occurred substantially less often. Notably, assessed risk appeared in only 13 of 642 model turns (2.0%), suggesting that models infrequently engaged in the specific risk-assessment behaviors captured by the framework.

Third, models were particularly likely to exhibit harmful behavior following explicit expressions of risk. Across models, 74% of responses following a risky belief and 73% following a described risky behavior received at least one harmful label, compared with 28% following participant turns without either risk tag. After a risk expression, Claude Sonnet 4.5 was the only model that challenged users more frequently than it promoted their risky beliefs or behaviors; the other five models showed the opposite pattern. These findings are consistent with concerns about sycophancy and excessive accommodation, particularly when users present beliefs that warrant careful evaluation or correction. However, the behavioral labels do not establish that every harmful response was motivated by agreement-seeking, nor do these comparisons demonstrate a causal effect of users' risk expressions on model behavior.

Fourth, examining conversations over time revealed important differences in how models responded after harmful behavior occurred. Harmful response states frequently persisted across consecutive turns, but recovery patterns varied considerably across models. Claude Sonnet 4.5 recovered to a helpful-only response on 35% of transitions following harmful or mixed turns, compared with 0-6% for GPT-5.2, Grok 4.1, Gemini arm, and Mistral Medium 3. The analysis of mixed responses revealed another important limitation of examining helpful behaviors in isolation: 77 of 78 mixed turns included a challenge alongside at least one harmful label. Thus, challenging a risky belief did not necessarily prevent a response from simultaneously exhibiting harmful behavior. Together, these findings highlight the importance of evaluating both the combination of behaviors within individual responses and how those behaviors unfold across conversations.

Fifth, observed model performance varied substantially across personas. Mean scores across the six models ranged from 6.3 for the cyberbullying-perpetrator persona to 3.1 for DIY legal reasoning, and several models showed pronounced differences in performance across scenarios. The lowest average scores were observed in contexts involving requests for confident procedural or financial guidance and emotionally salient beliefs. These findings suggest that performance within a single conversational domain may not reliably characterize how a model responds to other forms of epistemic risk. This variability underscores the importance of evaluating models across diverse user presentations and conversational contexts.

Taken together, these findings support the value of evaluating conversational epistemic conduct as a multidimensional construct. The results demonstrate that models can exhibit both helpful and harmful behaviors within the same response, that their responses differ when users express risky beliefs or behaviors, and that patterns of persistence and recovery may distinguish models beyond their overall benchmark scores. These observations reinforce the central premise of mPACT-MIS-v1.0: evaluating how conversational AI may support or undermine users' epistemic quality requires attention not only to factual content, but also to how models engage with users' beliefs and reasoning over time.

4.2 Implications

The findings highlight why evaluating misinformation-related risks in conversational AI requires attention to more than factual accuracy, hallucination rates, or refusal behavior. Users often turn to these systems not simply for information, but for help interpreting beliefs, navigating uncertainty, and making decisions. In these interactions, models may reinforce unsupported assumptions, communicate unwarranted authority, or promote risky behaviors without producing explicitly false information. Moreover, a model may challenge a risky belief while simultaneously exhibiting harmful behavior. These findings support the value of a behavioral science-informed approach that evaluates not only what models say, but how they respond to users' beliefs and reasoning across conversations.

For model developers, the findings suggest that improving conversational epistemic conduct requires attention to more than preventing initial harmful responses. Models also differed in their ability to recover after producing harmful responses, indicating that recovery may be an important target for evaluation and mitigation. Similarly, the prevalence of mixed responses highlights the importance of assessing whether seemingly helpful interventions also contain harmful elements. The multi-label and multi-turn approach used in mPACT-MIS-v1.0 provides a way to identify these patterns, while the composite score offers a summary of overall performance. Examining these behavioral dimensions alongside aggregate scores may help developers identify more specific opportunities for improving model responses.

The substantial variation in performance across personas also has implications for benchmark design. A model that responds appropriately to one type of risky belief or user presentation may perform quite differently in another. These findings suggest that evaluations limited to a narrow range of scenarios may overlook important vulnerabilities, particularly when users express risk through different emotional, interpersonal, or informational contexts. For organizations developing or deploying conversational AI, broader testing across realistic user presentations may therefore be especially important in domains such as health, finance, legal reasoning, interpersonal conflict, and emotional support. Because this study included only one conversation per persona-model pairing, future evaluations will need greater replication to establish how consistently these differences occur.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT; Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000) informed the development of the benchmark's behavioral evaluation framework and provides a useful perspective for interpreting these findings. Its emphasis on autonomy, competence, and relatedness suggests that effective model responses should do more than provide correct information or directly challenge unsupported beliefs. They should also support users' capacity to evaluate evidence, consider alternatives, and make informed decisions without excessive pressure or dependence on the model.

At the individual level, the distinction between challenged user and empowered user illustrates this point. Challenging an unsupported belief may interrupt its reinforcement, but it does not necessarily provide the user with the information or reasoning tools needed to evaluate similar claims independently. Empowering responses more directly reflect autonomy-and competence-supportive interactions by encouraging reflection and informed decision-making. The relative infrequency of empowered user compared with challenged user suggests that models more often exhibited direct correction than the specific behaviors intended to support independent reasoning. However, the present study did not measure whether either response type improved users' autonomy, competence, or subsequent reasoning.

At the interpersonal level, SDT's emphasis on relatedness provides another perspective on how models respond to emotionally significant beliefs or circumstances. Supporting relatedness need not involve unconditional agreement; a model may acknowledge a user's perspective while appropriately questioning an unsupported belief or encouraging consultation with trusted people and qualified professionals. The redirected to appropriate supports label captures one observable form of this behavior. Its relatively low frequency highlights an opportunity to evaluate whether models appropriately recognize the limits of their role and direct users toward relevant external support. Although these behaviors may help preserve opportunities for human connection and informed decision-making, their psychological effects were not directly assessed in this study.

At the societal level, the findings raise broader questions about how conversational AI may contribute to the persistence or correction of misinformation. Repeated endorsement of unsupported claims, particularly when expressed with apparent confidence or authority, could contribute to users' perceptions that those claims are credible. Conversely, responses that encourage evidence evaluation, acknowledge uncertainty, and challenge unsupported assumptions may help users engage more critically with information. These remain potential implications rather than demonstrated downstream outcomes. Establishing whether such conversational patterns influence belief certainty, misinformation sharing, polarization, or public trust will require research involving actual users.

Taken together, these implications suggest that conversational AI systems should be evaluated not only as sources of information but also as systems whose responses may shape how users interpret and reason about that information. By incorporating behavioral science perspectives, human annotation, and evaluation across multi-turn conversations, mPACT-MIS-v1.0 offers a complementary approach to identifying epistemic misconduct that conventional accuracy-focused evaluations may overlook. This approach may help inform the development of conversational AI systems that more consistently support evidence-based reasoning, appropriate uncertainty, and independent judgment.

4.3 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, mPACT-MIS-v1.0 represents an initial benchmark implementation using 60 expert-authored multiturn conversations. Each of the six models was evaluated across ten personas, with one conversation per persona-model pairing. Although this design allowed comparisons across a shared set of conversational scenarios, the limited replication constrains the precision and generalizability of model-level comparisons.

Second, the benchmark relied on expert-authored roleplays rather than naturalistic interactions with actual users. Although the scenarios were designed to reflect plausible conversational dynamics, and were 100% reviewed for quality and consistency, they cannot fully capture the diversity, unpredictability, cultural variation, or longer-term patterns of real-world AI use. Moreover, the benchmark evaluated observable model behaviors rather than downstream changes in users' epistemic quality. While the identified patterns may plausibly influence beliefs, confidence, reasoning, or decision-making, the study cannot establish whether exposure to these responses would cause users to adopt particular beliefs or actions.

Third, the behavioral evaluation framework reflects an initial operationalization of epistemic quality in conversational AI. Although Self-Determination Theory provided a theoretical foundation for identifying behaviors that may support or undermine users' reasoning, the labels do not directly measure autonomy, competence, relatedness, or changes in epistemic quality. Furthermore, unlike domains such as suicide risk, where more established clinical frameworks inform severity assessment, no broadly accepted framework currently specifies the relative severity of epistemic failure modes. Consequently, mPACT-MIS-v1.0 uses a predefined ordering and weighting of four response states rather than empirically derived severity weights for individual behaviors. These scoring choices require further validation, and the current taxonomy may not capture every relevant form of epistemic conduct.

Fourth, the benchmark focused on conversational responses to misinformation-related beliefs and broader epistemic conduct concerns rather than the full misinformation ecosystem. It did not evaluate coordinated disinformation campaigns, recommendation systems, retrieval failures, multimodal misinformation, or platform-level amplification. Nor did it independently establish the factual status of every contested claim represented in the conversations. Instead, it assessed how models responded to users' expressed beliefs, uncertainty, and potentially risky behaviors using the predefined annotation framework.

Fifth, the evaluation relied on human annotations, which necessarily involve judgments about complex conversational behavior. Agreement was generally substantial across the evaluated classifications, although reliability was lower for the empowered user label. These considerations highlight the need for continued refinement and validation of the labeling framework. Finally, the evaluated model versions were accessed over a five-month period rather than at a single point in time. Because model behavior and provider defaults may change over time, these factors limit exact reproducibility and the extent to which findings can be generalized to other versions of the evaluated models.

4.4 Future Directions

mPACT-MIS-v1.0 is a clinician-developed benchmark for evaluating epistemic quality in multi-turn conversations with large language models. By combining expert-authored roleplays with structured talk-turn-level evaluation, the framework enables systematic assessment of how models may influence reasoning, uncertainty calibration, and belief formation across realistic misinformation-related interactions.

Future versions of mPACT-MIS should expand the benchmark across additional personas, domains, languages, cultural contexts, and deployment settings. Larger datasets would support more detailed analyses of how epistemic conduct varies across conversational contexts, user characteristics, and model families.

Future research should also examine patterns of behavior beyond individual labels. Some epistemic risks may emerge through recurring combinations of behaviors, such as reinforcement paired with overconfidence or omission paired with avoidance of correction. Identifying these higher-order patterns may provide a more nuanced understanding of model-specific epistemic conduct and inform more targeted mitigation strategies.

Additional work is needed to refine scoring methodologies and establish stronger theoretical foundations for evaluating epistemic harm. As the literature matures, future frameworks are expected to incorporate differential weighting schemes informed by empirical evidence regarding the relative impact of different epistemic failure modes.

The findings also highlight the importance of studying how models respond after harmful behavior occurs. Harmful responses frequently persisted across subsequent turns, while models differed in their ability to recover. Future research should investigate whether interventions targeting recovery can interrupt these patterns and how recovery-focused approaches compare with strategies aimed at preventing harmful responses in the first place. Studies involving actual users could also examine whether model reinforcement or correction influences subsequent belief expressions, reasoning, and decisions.

5. Conclusion

Misinformation-related risks in conversational AI extend beyond the generation of false or misleading information to include how models respond to users' existing beliefs, assumptions, and interpretations. mPACT-MIS-v1.0 addresses this broader challenge by evaluating observable model behaviors that may support or undermine users' epistemic quality across multi-turn conversations.

Across six evaluated LLMs, harmful behaviors were frequently identified, particularly when users expressed risky beliefs or behaviors. Models differed in their tendencies to reinforce unsupported assumptions, challenge users, and recover after producing harmful responses, while performance also varied substantially across conversational scenarios. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating misinformation in context rather than relying exclusively on isolated measures of factual accuracy.

By combining a behavioral science-informed evaluation framework, expert-authored conversations, and human ratings, mPACT-MIS-v1.0 provides a complementary approach to evaluating how conversational AI systems engage with misinformation-related beliefs and reasoning. Although the benchmark does not directly measure changes in users' beliefs or behavior, it identifies conversational patterns that may be relevant to misinformation reinforcement or correction, offering a foundation for future research and model improvement.
