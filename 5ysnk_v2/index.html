Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

p < .05 Results are presented as unstandardized fixed effects (b), SEs, t values, p values, and 95% confidence intervals; p < .05 was used to determine statistical significance.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .998 After controlling for assessment day, demographic variables, and daily short video use duration, the predictive association between behavioral boredom intolerance and state-level problematic short video use was not significant (b < 0.01, SE = 0.10, t = -0.002, p = .998).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

With digital applications becoming increasingly integrated into daily life, individuals may become less tolerant of boredom. However, existing measures of boredom intolerance have primarily focused on negative emotional responses to boredom, while providing limited insight into behavioral tendencies when encountering boredom. The present study developed a two-dimensional Boredom Intolerance Scale through five studies and examined the associations between boredom intolerance and problematic short-video use.

Study 1 used qualitative interviews and grounded theory analysis to identify the conceptual domains and dimensions of boredom intolerance, revealing two dimensions: emotional intolerance and behavioral avoidance. Study 2 conducted exploratory factor analysis with 444 participants (Mage = 23.99 ± 5.41 years; 66.89% female) and developed an eight-item two-dimensional scale. Study 3 conducted confirmatory factor analysis and criterion-related validity analyses with 347 participants (Mage = 21.79 ± 3.38 years; 74.64% female), further supporting the two-dimensional structure of boredom intolerance. Study 4 employed a two-wave longitudinal design with 219 participants (Mage = 21.62 ± 2.56 years; 78.5% female) and found that problematic short-video use positively predicted boredom intolerance. Study 5 used a 14-day diary design with 185 participants (Mage = 21.13 ± 1.54 years; 85.4% female) and found that boredom intolerance positively predicted state-level problematic short-video use.

1. Introduction

Since the emergence of the digital era, the rapid development of digital devices and software applications has enabled individuals to access information, interact socially, and engage in entertainment activities with greater convenience. In particular, short-video platforms such as TikTok continuously provide brief, novel, and instantly rewarding content through algorithmic recommendations, allowing individuals to experience frequent and rapidly changing entertainment stimuli within a short period. However, the widespread availability of entertainment stimuli has not reduced boredom as expected. Instead, emerging evidence indicates that contemporary digital environments may be accompanied by more frequent boredom and a diminished capacity to tolerate it (Gu et al., 2023; Pellegrini et al., 2025; Tam & Inzlicht, 2024).

Boredom intolerance refers to a relatively stable tendency to experience difficulty tolerating boredom-related states or experiences. It is characterized by emotional discomfort when encountering boring situations and behavioral tendencies to alleviate boredom through escaping from or seeking alternative activities. Like boredom itself, difficulty tolerating boredom appears to covary with poorer psychological functioning, including reduced wellbeing, greater depressive and anxiety symptoms, and difficulties in academic and occupational functioning. It may also increase individuals' vulnerability to engaging in maladaptive coping behaviors (Hunter & Eastwood, 2021; Pellegrini et al., 2025).

However, compared with the extensive research examining boredom experiences and their consequences, boredom intolerance, as a relatively stable individual difference characteristic, remains theoretically and empirically underexplored. Although previous studies have proposed and developed measures of boredom intolerance, existing assessments have primarily focused on individuals' emotional responses to boredom (e.g., feelings of annoyance, discomfort, and negative affect), while paying less attention to behavioral tendencies when facing boredom, such as actively escaping from or changing the current situation (Pellegrini et al., 2025). However, distress tolerance theory suggests that individuals' tolerance or intolerance toward negative experiences involves not only emotional responses but also behavioral tendencies (Zvolensky et al., 2010). Extending the construct to include behavioral tendencies may therefore offer a fuller account of how individuals experience and respond to boredom.

Therefore, the present study aimed to further clarify the conceptual nature and manifestations of boredom intolerance and examine its relationship with problematic shortvideo use. Specifically, the present study sought to understand individuals' psychological and behavioral responses to boredom experiences through a more comprehensive conceptualization of boredom intolerance, while also investigating whether problematic short-video use in the digital media era is associated with increasing difficulty in tolerating boredom.

1.1 Theoretical Background

Before conceptualizing boredom intolerance, it is first necessary to clarify the concept of boredom itself. Therefore, the present study first reviews major theoretical perspectives on boredom to establish a comprehensive conceptual understanding of this construct. Given that boredom is a multifaceted and abstract concept, previous research has often attempted to define boredom by examining the conditions under which it emerges.

Early perspectives primarily conceptualized boredom as a state resulting from insufficient external stimulation, suggesting that low-stimulation environments, monotonous activities, or inadequate levels of arousal contribute to the experience of boredom (Mikulas & Vodanovich, 1993; O'Hanlon, 1981). However, subsequent research has shown that insufficient stimulation or repetitive activities alone cannot fully explain boredom, as individuals may experience different levels of boredom under the same conditions, and repetitive activities with personal meaning or value do not necessarily induce boredom (Barbalet, 1999). Therefore, external characteristics of situations are more likely to function as triggering conditions for boredom rather than representing its core features (Barbalet, 1999).

With the advancement of research, scholars have increasingly emphasized the interaction between individuals and their environments in understanding boredom. Cognitive perspectives suggest that boredom is closely related to individuals' inability to effectively engage with their current activities. Fisher (1993) proposed that boredom may arise when individuals experience difficulty sustaining attention toward ongoing activities. Extending this view, Eastwood et al. (2012) suggested that boredom is an aversive state characterized by "wanting but being unable to engage in satisfying activity," in which individuals desire to participate in an activity but fail to achieve effective and satisfying engagement. This perspective highlights that boredom is not simply the absence of activity, but rather results from a mismatch between individuals' need for cognitive engagement and their current experiences.

Beyond attentional engagement, perceived meaning is another important component of boredom. When individuals fail to derive personal value or meaning from their current activities, they may experience boredom even when the activities themselves provide a certain level of stimulation (Van Tilburg & Igou, 2012). Based on this perspective, Westgate and Wilson (2018) proposed the Meaning and Attentional Components (MAC) model, suggesting that boredom arises from insufficient attentional engagement and a lack of meaning. Specifically, individuals may experience boredom when they are unable to concentrate on the current activity or perceive it as important and valuable.

Further research has demonstrated that boredom is not merely a negative experience but also serves an important adaptive function. Boredom can signal that current activities fail to satisfy individuals' needs and motivate them to seek new goals or alter their current states (Bench & Lench, 2013; Elpidorou, 2017 Elpidorou, , 2023)). Overall, boredom is not simply a state of insufficient stimulation or low arousal; rather, it is a complex psychological state arising from failures in attentional engagement, unmet needs for meaning, and mismatches between individuals and their current activities (Eastwood et al., 2012; Finkielsztein, 2024; Westgate & Wilson, 2018).

However, individuals do not respond to boredom in the same way. When experiencing the same boring situation, some individuals may accept the experience and remain engaged in the current activity, whereas others may experience intense discomfort and become motivated to immediately alter their current state (Bench & Lench, 2019; Pellegrini et al., 2025). These individual differences suggest that examining boredom experiences alone may not be sufficient to explain how boredom contributes to subsequent psychological and behavioral outcomes. Therefore, it is necessary to further investigate individuals' capacity to tolerate boredom experiences.

Distress tolerance theory provides an important framework for understanding how individuals respond to aversive internal experiences. This theory proposes that individuals differ in their ability to tolerate distress, negative emotions, and other aversive states, and that adaptive outcomes are determined not only by the presence of negative experiences but also by individuals' capacity to tolerate them (Zvolensky et al., 2010). Specifically, distress tolerance involves two related but distinguishable aspects: emotional responses when encountering negative experiences and behavioral responses toward these experiences, such as avoidance or termination behaviors (Leyro et al., 2010). Accordingly, individuals with low distress tolerance do not necessarily experience more intense negative emotions; rather, they are more likely to perceive such experiences as unbearable and engage in avoidance behaviors to obtain immediate relief. Although boredom differs from traditional forms of distress, it also involves aversive experiential features and may motivate individuals to alter their current state (Bench & Lench, 2019). Based on distress tolerance theory, boredom intolerance may involve not only difficulties accepting boredom experiences but also tendencies to engage in avoidance or coping behaviors when confronted with boredom.

Therefore, the conceptualization of boredom intolerance should incorporate both emotional and behavioral components.

Accordingly, the present study defines boredom intolerance as a relatively stable tendency characterized by reduced tolerance toward boredom-related states or experiences.

Specifically, individuals with higher boredom intolerance have difficulty accepting the discomfort elicited by low stimulation, low engagement, or lack of meaning and exhibit stronger emotional discomfort as well as tendencies to escape from or regulate such experiences through behavioral responses. Based on distress tolerance theory and previous research, we further propose that boredom intolerance consists of two dimensions: emotional intolerance and behavioral avoidance.

1.2 The Predictive Role of Problematic Short-Video Use in Boredom Intolerance

Problematic short-video use may positively predict boredom intolerance. Problematic short-video use generally refers to excessive and poorly controlled engagement with shortvideo platforms, characterized by prolonged use and difficulties in regulating usage behaviors (Galanis et al., 2024). According to the Incentive Sensitization Theory, repeated exposure to highly rewarding stimuli may gradually sensitize individuals' reward systems, increasing their motivation to seek stimuli with high incentive value while reducing their ability to adapt to low-stimulation states (Robinson & Berridge, 2008). Short-video platforms continuously provide novel, diverse, and instantly rewarding content through personalized recommendation algorithms, allowing individuals to obtain intensive stimulation within a short period (Guerra, 2026). Prolonged reliance on such highly stimulating environments may gradually alter individuals' adaptation patterns toward stimulation, making ordinary daily activities characterized by lower levels of stimulation and novelty less engaging and reducing individuals' tolerance for boredom in everyday contexts (Novanská Škripcová & Viteková, 2025). Therefore, compared with individuals who engage in short-video use without problematic patterns, those with problematic short-video use may be more likely to perceive boredom experiences as aversive and exhibit higher levels of boredom intolerance.

Previous research has also demonstrated that problematic short-video use is closely associated with negative responses to boredom experiences. Specifically, individuals with problematic short-video use are more likely to experience irritability, restlessness, and a tendency to seek external stimulation when encountering low-stimulation situations (Zhou et al., 2025). Based on these theoretical assumptions and empirical findings, the present study hypothesized that problematic short-video use would positively predict boredom intolerance.

1.3 The Predictive Role of Boredom Intolerance in Problematic Short-Video Use

Boredom intolerance may also positively predict problematic short-video use. According to the Compensatory Internet Use Model, problematic digital behaviors do not simply result from individuals' preferences for online activities; rather, they may serve as compensatory strategies that individuals use to alleviate negative experiences in daily life or fulfill unmet psychological needs (Kardefelt-Winther, 2014). For individuals with high levels of boredom intolerance, boredom is not merely a transient unpleasant state but an experience that is difficult to accept and requires immediate regulation or relief (Bench & Lench, 2019). To reduce the discomfort associated with boredom, these individuals may be more likely to use short-video platforms as a readily accessible source of immediate stimulation and mood regulation (Baltacı & Açar, 2025). However, such compensatory patterns of use may increase the risk of developing problematic short-video use.

1.4 The current study

In summary, existing research on boredom has primarily focused on boredom experiences themselves, while paying less attention to individual differences in adapting to boredom. Moreover, existing measures of boredom intolerance have mainly emphasized individuals' negative evaluations and emotional responses toward boredom experiences, with relatively limited attention to behavioral tendencies when facing boredom. Based on distress tolerance theory, which distinguishes between tolerance of subjective experiences and behavioral responses toward distress, the present study conceptualizes boredom intolerance as a relatively stable tendency characterized by difficulties in accepting and regulating boredom experiences. Accordingly, we developed a boredom intolerance scale comprising two dimensions: emotional intolerance and behavioral avoidance, to provide a more comprehensive assessment of individuals' responses to boredom. Furthermore, based on Incentive Sensitization Theory and the Compensatory Internet Use Model, boredom intolerance and problematic short-video use may be reciprocally associated. To examine their potential developmental associations, the present study employed a two-wave longitudinal design and cross-lagged panel models to investigate the bidirectional predictive relationships between boredom intolerance and problematic shortvideo use.

Finally, considering that boredom experiences and short-video use behaviors may fluctuate across daily contexts, examining only stable individual differences may not fully capture how boredom intolerance operates in everyday life. Therefore, the present study employed a 14-day diary design to examine the dynamic association between boredom intolerance and state-level problematic short-video use in naturalistic settings. Compared with retrospective questionnaires, diary methods can reduce recall bias and capture temporal variations in boredom experiences and short-video use behaviors, providing further evidence for the ecological validity of boredom intolerance in predicting problematic short-video use (Bolger et al., 2003).

Together, the present study integrated scale development, longitudinal tracking, and diary-based approaches to examine the conceptual structure of boredom intolerance and its relationship with problematic short-video use. By doing so, this study aims to advance understanding of how individuals respond to boredom in contemporary digital environments and how boredom intolerance may be involved in problematic short-video use.

2.Study 1: Conceptualization and Development of the Boredom Intolerance Scale

Study 1 aimed to clarify the conceptualization and multidimensional characteristics of boredom intolerance using a qualitative approach. Guided by grounded theory, in-depth interviews were conducted to explore individuals' understanding, subjective experiences, and behavioral manifestations related to boredom intolerance. The interview transcripts were systematically coded and analyzed to identify the core components of boredom intolerance and to develop an initial assessment instrument.

2.1 Method

2.1.1 Participants

Maximum variation sampling (Chen, 2000) was used to obtain heterogeneous accounts of boredom intolerance. The interview sample comprised 23 individuals (Mage = 26.04 years, SDage = 5.33), nine of whom were female. Educational attainment ranged from high school to master's level; participants enrolled in undergraduate or postgraduate programs came from six fields, including economics, linguistics, and computer science.

2.1.2 Research Procedure

The interview protocol and questionnaire underwent a two-round Delphi review (Vernon, 2009) by two specialists in qualitative methodology and affective science. In the first round, the specialists commented on the content, wording, and coverage of the questions. The research team revised the materials accordingly and returned them for a second appraisal, after which agreement was reached on the final protocol.

The final protocol was administered in semi-structured interviews with all 23 participants, each lasting 20-40 min. Interviews took place in quiet settings and were audiorecorded and transcribed verbatim. Two psychology doctoral students independently applied grounded-theory coding. A six-member panel-two researchers specializing in affective science and psychological measurement and four psychology doctoral students-then reviewed discrepancies and refined the coding framework.

2.1.3 Results

Participants showed a general consensus that boredom represents a negative experience characterized by an inability to disengage from an uninteresting or meaningless situation or to engage in activities that are interesting or meaningful. Through a multi-stage coding process of the interview transcripts and multiple rounds of discussion within the focus group, a theoretical framework of boredom intolerance was developed. Boredom intolerance was defined as a relatively stable tendency characterized by low tolerance for boredom-related states or experiences. Specifically, individuals with higher levels of boredom intolerance tend to perceive boredom as difficult to accept and show stronger tendencies to regulate or escape from such experiences. Thus, boredom intolerance includes two dimensions: an emotional dimension reflecting negative affective responses to boredom and a behavioral dimension reflecting tendencies to avoid or alleviate boredom through alternative activities.

Based on the interview findings, coding results, and focus group discussions, an initial Chinese-language Boredom Intolerance Scale was developed. The scale consisted of 16 items, with eight items assessing the emotional dimension and eight items assessing the behavioral dimension. All items were originally written in Chinese, and the Chinese version was administered and psychometrically evaluated in the subsequent studies. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater levels of boredom intolerance. English translations of the items are provided in this article solely for presentation purposes and were not administered to participants or psychometrically evaluated.

3. Study 2: Exploratory Factor Analysis of the Boredom Intolerance Scale

The aim of Study 2 was to quantitatively examine the psychometric properties of the initial Boredom Intolerance Scale and explore whether the proposed dimensions of boredom intolerance could be empirically supported.

3.1 Method

3.1.1 Participants

The online survey produced 558 responses. Cases were screened out when completion time was below 200 s, response patterns suggested insufficient engagement, or an attention check was failed. This procedure yielded 444 usable cases (79.57%). Of the retained participants, 8.78% had completed junior college or less, 79.28% held a bachelor's degree, and 11.94% had postgraduate education. Their mean age was 23.99 years (SD = 5.41), and 297 (66.89%) were female. Ethical approval was obtained from the authors' institution, and informed consent was secured from every participant.

3.2 Results

3.2.1 Item Analysis

Item discrimination was examined within a classical test theory framework. Respondents in the upper and lower 27% of the total-score distribution formed the high-and lowintolerance groups, respectively. Critical-ratio tests showed that the two groups differed significantly on each of the 16 candidate items, supporting adequate discrimination.

Each candidate item also showed a significant positive association with the total score.

No item was eliminated at this stage, and all 16 proceeded to exploratory factor analysis.

3.2.2 Exploratory Factor Analysis

Factorability was supported by the KMO value of .94 and Bartlett's test of sphericity, χ²(78) = 3516.77, p < .001. A principal component analysis with Promax rotation was therefore performed. Factor retention was guided by eigenvalues above 1 and the scree plot.

Items were removed iteratively if their largest absolute loading was below .40 or if the gap between their two largest loadings was less than .15; the analysis was rerun after each deletion until neither condition applied.

Following this procedure, three items (Items 5, 7, and 8) were removed. The remaining 13 items loaded onto two factors, corresponding to the emotional and behavioral dimensions of boredom intolerance. These two factors accounted for 64.06% of the total variance, and factor loadings ranged from .51 to .83. Subsequently, five additional items (Items 6, 9, 10, 11, and 15) were removed due to substantial content overlap or redundant wording. The final scale consisted of eight items, with four items representing each dimension. The two factors explained 70.59% of the total variance. All items showed substantial loadings on their corresponding factors, ranging from .62 to .87 (see Table 1).

4. Study 3: Validation and Reliability/Validity Assessment of the Boredom Intolerance Scale

Based on the Boredom Intolerance Scale developed in Study 2, Study 3 aimed to evaluate its psychometric properties. Specifically, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to examine whether the proposed two-dimensional structure of boredom intolerance could be replicated.

To further examine the construct validity of the Boredom Intolerance Questionnaire, we investigated its associations with theoretically related constructs. First, although boredom intolerance is closely related to boredom experiences, the two constructs are conceptually distinct. Boredom proneness reflects an individual's stable tendency to experience boredom across situations, whereas boredom intolerance refers to difficulties in accepting and regulating boredom experiences (Struk et al., 2015). Therefore, we expected boredom intolerance to be positively associated with boredom proneness while representing distinct theoretical constructs. Second, boredom intolerance may reflect difficulties in adapting to low-stimulation states, whereas sensation seeking represents an individual's tendency to actively pursue novel and intense stimulation. Both constructs involve individual differences in the regulation of optimal stimulation levels (X. Chen et al., 2013). Therefore, we further examined the association between boredom intolerance and sensation seeking and expected a positive relationship between them. Moreover, previous research has demonstrated that boredom experiences are closely associated with depression and anxiety (Lee & Zelman, 2019). Accordingly, we further examined the associations of boredom intolerance with depression and anxiety. Finally, considering that individuals with higher boredom intolerance may be more likely to seek external stimulation to regulate their current states, we examined its association with problematic short-video use to further evaluate the external validity of boredom intolerance in the context of digital behaviors.

4.1.1 Participants

Of the 374 online respondents, 347 remained after screening for completion times below 200 s, patterned responding, invalid attention checks, and duplicate submissions. Educational attainment was distributed as follows: junior college or below, 6.91%; bachelor's degree, 83.86%; and master's degree or above, 9.22%.

Responses were given on a five-point scale and coded so that larger values represented greater intolerance. Cronbach's α was .854 for the full scale, .841 for emotional intolerance, and .798 for behavioral avoidance.

7. Discussion

Drawing on distress tolerance theory, the present study reexamined the psychological conceptualization of boredom intolerance in the digital era and systematically investigated its association with problematic short video use through scale development, a longitudinal follow-up study, and an intensive diary study.

First, addressing the limitation that previous research on boredom intolerance has primarily focused on individuals' negative emotional reactions to boredom while paying less attention to their behavioral responses in boredom-related situations, the present study collected individuals' subjective experiences of boredom intolerance through qualitative interviews and used grounded theory analysis to examine its underlying psychological structure. The findings suggest that boredom intolerance consists of two dimensions: emotional intolerance and behavioral tendencies in response to boredom. Building on this conceptualization, Studies 2 and 3 systematically evaluated the two-dimensional Boredom Intolerance Scale using exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and validity assessments. The results demonstrated that the scale showed satisfactory reliability, structural validity, discriminant validity, and incremental validity, providing a reliable tool for further investigating individuals' difficulties in adapting to boredom experiences in the digital era.

Furthermore, Studies 4 and 5 examined the dynamic associations between boredom intolerance and problematic short video use using a two-wave longitudinal design and a 14day diary design, respectively. Study 4 showed that higher levels of problematic short video use were prospectively associated with increased boredom intolerance, suggesting that prolonged reliance on high-frequency and highly stimulating digital media may be associated with reduced adaptability to low-stimulation states. Study 5 further demonstrated that boredom intolerance predicted daily state-level problematic short video use, indicating that individuals who have difficulty tolerating boredom experiences may be more likely to use short videos as a means of seeking immediate stimulation and regulating their current states.

First, the present study explored the psychological conceptualization of boredom intolerance in the digital era through qualitative interviews and developed a two-dimensional framework consisting of emotional intolerance and behavioral tendencies based on grounded theory. The interview findings indicated that individuals' responses to boredom involve not only negative emotional reactions and difficulty accepting boredom experiences, but also behavioral tendencies to actively cope with or escape from boredom-related situations. These findings provided ecological evidence supporting the proposed two-dimensional conceptualization of boredom intolerance. Building on these qualitative findings, Study 2 further developed an eight-item Boredom Intolerance Scale through exploratory factor analysis. The results showed that all retained items demonstrated satisfactory factor loadings and that both dimensions exhibited good internal consistency, indicating that the scale provided a reliable measure of boredom intolerance. Study 3 further validated the factor structure through confirmatory factor analysis. The results showed that both the overall scale and its two dimensions demonstrated satisfactory reliability, and the two-dimensional model showed significantly better model fit than the one-factor model. These findings supported the theoretical proposition that boredom intolerance consists of two related but distinguishable dimensions: emotional intolerance and behavioral tendencies in response to boredom.

Furthermore, the present study further examined the validity of the Boredom Intolerance Scale. The results showed that boredom intolerance was significantly positively correlated with boredom proneness, depression, anxiety, sensation seeking, and problematic short video use, providing evidence for its criterion-related validity. In addition, hierarchical regression analyses, together with convergent and discriminant validity analyses, demonstrated that boredom intolerance accounted for additional variance beyond related psychological constructs, supporting the incremental validity and construct validity of the scale. Taken together, the present study extends previous research on boredom intolerance by broadening its theoretical conceptualization. Specifically, rather than viewing boredom intolerance solely as an emotional reaction to boredom experiences, this study conceptualizes boredom intolerance as a two-dimensional construct comprising both emotional experiences and behavioral regulation tendencies (Pellegrini et al., 2025).

Study 4 examined the bidirectional longitudinal associations between boredom intolerance and problematic short video use using a two-wave longitudinal design and a cross-lagged panel model. The results showed that T1 problematic short video use positively predicted T2 overall boredom intolerance and emotional boredom intolerance, partially supporting our hypotheses and the Incentive Sensitization Theory. This theory proposes that repeated exposure to highly rewarding stimuli may increase the incentive salience of these stimuli, thereby strengthening individuals' motivation to seek experiences with immediate reward value (Robinson & Berridge, 2008). Compared with traditional forms of media, short video platforms are characterized by brief content duration, rapid content switching, and high information density. Prolonged exposure to this pattern of immediate and frequent stimulation may further reinforce individuals' expectations for novel and rapidly changing information, making it more difficult for them to adapt to situations characterized by low stimulation and delayed feedback (Ye et al., 2025). Consequently, individuals who engage in problematic short video use may exhibit greater intolerance toward boredom experiences.

However, the present study found that boredom intolerance did not significantly predict subsequent problematic short video use, which did not support our hypothesis. One possible explanation is that the majority of participants in this study were emerging adults aged 18-25 years who grew up in an environment characterized by rapid digital media development. For this population, short video use has become a common and habitual behavior pattern, and problematic short video use may demonstrate relatively high temporal stability (Zhang et al., 2024). Such stability may reduce the extent to which boredom intolerance can account for subsequent changes in problematic short video use. Consistent with this interpretation, problematic short video use showed a relatively high autoregressive coefficient across the two measurement waves, further suggesting that short video use behavior may exhibit considerable persistence and stability over a relatively short period.

Given that problematic short video use may also exhibit substantial situational variability, the present study further employed a 14-day diary design to examine the association between boredom intolerance and daily state-level problematic short video use.

The results showed that overall boredom intolerance and the emotional dimension of boredom intolerance positively predicted state-level problematic short video use. These findings are consistent with the compensatory internet use model, which suggests that individuals may engage in online activities to alleviate negative experiences in daily life (Kardefelt-Winther, 2014). Accordingly, when individuals with higher levels of boredom intolerance experience boredom in daily situations, they may be more likely to use short videos as a compensatory strategy to shift their attention, obtain immediate stimulation, and regulate their current experiences, thereby increasing the likelihood of state-level problematic short video use (Wan et al., 2025). These findings further suggest that the association between boredom intolerance and problematic short video use may operate through a contextdependent triggering process. Individuals with high boredom intolerance may not necessarily maintain consistently elevated levels of problematic short video use; rather, when they encounter boredom or other aversive states in daily life, they may be more likely to rely on short videos as a means of regulating their current state.

The present study further found that the behavioral dimension of boredom intolerance was not significantly associated with problematic short video use. Specifically, behavioral boredom intolerance did not significantly predict subsequent problematic short video use, and problematic short video use did not significantly predict subsequent behavioral boredom intolerance. Thus, these findings did not support our hypothesis. One possible explanation is that the behavioral dimension of boredom intolerance reflects a general tendency to actively alter one's current state when experiencing boredom, and such state regulation may be achieved through various behavioral pathways rather than necessarily through short video use. For example, individuals may adopt adaptive coping strategies, such as physical exercise, social interaction, or engaging in interest-based activities, or they may engage in maladaptive avoidance behaviors, such as prolonged short video use or other forms of immediate stimulation seeking (Lench et al., 2025). In other words, the behavioral dimension of boredom intolerance reflects a broader tendency to regulate one's internal state, whereas problematic short video use represents only one of the possible behavioral manifestations of this tendency.

Furthermore, the association between behavioral boredom intolerance and problematic short video use may depend on individual characteristics and situational factors. For instance, individuals with higher levels of need for cognition or stronger goal orientations may be more likely to engage in challenging and meaningful activities when experiencing boredom, rather than relying on short videos or other forms of immediate stimulation to regulate their states.

Therefore, even individuals with high behavioral boredom intolerance may not necessarily exhibit higher levels of problematic short video use. Future research should further examine the boundary conditions that determine when behavioral boredom intolerance translates into problematic digital behaviors, thereby providing a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the association between boredom intolerance and problematic short video use.

7.1 Theoretical and Practical Implications

The present study has several important theoretical implications. First, it extends the conceptualization of boredom intolerance based on distress tolerance theory. Previous research has primarily viewed boredom as a negative subjective experience, focusing on individuals' discomfort and negative evaluations of boredom, while paying less attention to how individuals regulate their behaviors when experiencing boredom (Pellegrini et al., 2025).

The present study proposes that boredom intolerance involves not only difficulty accepting the emotional discomfort associated with boredom but also behavioral tendencies to actively alter or escape from boredom states. Through qualitative investigation and scale development, this study identified two dimensions of boredom intolerance: emotional intolerance and behavioral tendencies. This expanded conceptualization provides a more comprehensive understanding of individuals' psychological and behavioral responses to boredom and offers a new perspective for examining how individuals adapt to boredom experiences in the digital era.

Second, by integrating longitudinal and diary designs, this study provides insight into the dynamic association between boredom intolerance and problematic short video use. While previous research has largely focused on stable individual differences, the present study further examined how boredom intolerance operates in daily life contexts. The diary findings showed that boredom intolerance predicted daily state-level problematic short video use, suggesting that boredom intolerance may function not only as a stable individual tendency but also as a situational trigger that increases the likelihood of using short videos for immediate state regulation. These findings highlight the importance of considering both traitlevel and state-level factors when understanding problematic digital behaviors.

This study also has practical implications. First, interventions targeting problematic short video use should not focus solely on reducing usage time or restricting digital media exposure but should also address the underlying psychological regulation processes. Because individuals with high boredom intolerance may be more likely to rely on short videos to escape boredom experiences, improving tolerance of boredom, enhancing acceptance of negative experiences, and developing more diverse state-regulation strategies may help reduce reliance on short videos as an immediate coping tool. Second, the findings suggest that problematic short video use and boredom intolerance may form a mutually reinforcing cycle. Therefore, digital health education and intervention programs should not only increase awareness of the potential risks of excessive short video use but also encourage engagement in low-stimulation and delayed-reward activities, such as reading, exercise, deep learning, and face-to-face social interaction. Reducing reliance on immediate digital stimulation may contribute to healthier patterns of digital media use.

7.2 Limitations and Future Directions

Although the present study extends the conceptualization of boredom intolerance and further examines its dynamic associations with problematic short video use, several limitations should be acknowledged, which also provide directions for future research.

First, the proportions of female participants in Studies 4 and 5 were relatively high.

Although gender was controlled as a covariate in the analyses, the imbalance in gender distribution may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research should recruit samples with more balanced gender distributions and examine whether the associations between boredom intolerance and problematic short video use remain consistent across different gender groups.

Second, the Boredom Intolerance Scale was developed in Chinese and evaluated using samples from the Chinese cultural context. The English item translations provided in this article are for presentation purposes only and have not been psychometrically evaluated.

Therefore, the present findings support the Chinese-language scale, whereas its applicability to other languages and cultural contexts remains to be established. Given that cultural environments may shape individuals' interpretations of boredom experiences and coping strategies (Ohlmeier et al., 2020), future studies should undertake translation and cultural adaptation and examine the generalizability of the two-dimensional structure across languages and cultures.

Finally, the present study primarily relied on self-report measures to assess boredom intolerance and problematic short video use. Although self-report measures can effectively capture individuals' subjective experiences and behavioral tendencies, they may still be influenced by common method bias and social desirability. Future research could integrate multiple approaches, such as behavioral experiments, ecological momentary assessment (EMA), and physiological measures, to further validate the behavioral manifestations of boredom intolerance and clarify how boredom intolerance contributes to problematic short video use. For example, boredom-induction tasks could be used to examine individuals' behavioral choices in objectively low-stimulation situations, providing further insight into how boredom intolerance translates into specific problematic short video use behaviors.

8. Conclusion

Drawing on distress tolerance theory, the present study expanded the conceptualization of boredom intolerance by identifying a two-dimensional structure comprising emotional intolerance and behavioral tendencies. Through scale development, longitudinal tracking, and diary studies, this study further revealed the dynamic associations between boredom intolerance and problematic short video use. The findings showed that problematic short video use positively predicted subsequent overall boredom intolerance and emotional boredom intolerance, whereas boredom intolerance, particularly its emotional dimension, predicted daily state-level problematic short video use.
