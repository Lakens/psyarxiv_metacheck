Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

The social challenges encountered by autistic people are increasingly understood as a double empathy problem: a mutual misunderstanding between autistic and non-autistic individuals, rather than an autistic deficit. It is thus critical to cultivate inclusive spaces that foster mutual understanding, where autistic people can fit in while allowing the general population to experience high-quality neurodiverse contacts. Choral singing offers a promising medium to do so and to facilitate cross-neurotype dynamics, as it is thought to have evolved as a biological mechanism to foster social bonding. Accordingly, this qualitative study explored how 12 general population adult choristers experienced a 6-month neurodiverse choir shared with 8 autistic adolescents and 3 autism specialists. We used a two-stage qualitative design, first capturing individual experiences through a thematic analysis of an open-ended online questionnaire, and subsequently deepening our results through a discussion with a community stakeholder panel. The findings revealed an intrinsically demanding experience, especially emotionally and cognitively, counterbalanced by important benefits, as it was also described as joyful and powerful, encouraging personal growth among the choristers and a shift in their perceptions of autism. These findings support the neurodiversity paradigm and suggest that choral singing may support double empathy experiences, facilitating intergroup contact in the social majority, to reduce prejudice and thereby remove social barriers to the inclusion of autistic voices.

1. Introduction

Autism is a complex neurodevelopmental condition that is conventionally characterised by communication and interaction difficulties (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Such difficulties are a major challenge for autistic people, as they are a strong determinant of impaired social participation and a lower quality of life (Mason et al., 2018; Orsmond et al., 2013). An accurate understanding of these difficulties is thus essential for effectively improving autistic individuals' lives.

The current version of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR) specifies that such difficulties manifest as deficits in social-emotional reciprocity, or in developing, maintaining, and understanding relationships (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). While this traditional definition implies that such difficulties constitute individual deficits, more recent accounts posit that they are instead the product of a mutual misunderstanding between autistic and nonautistic individuals. Indeed, autistic individuals are well-known to struggle to understand non-autistic mental states, but this has most classically been interpreted as a difficulty to read minds in general (the so-called "mindblindness"; Baron-Cohen, 1997); at the same time, it has been neglected that non-autistic individuals also display an evident lack of comprehension or empathy toward autistic individuals. For instance, autistic students are much more likely to be bullied than their peers in the general population (Maïano et al., 2016), which is in turn a strong predictor of emotional problems (Accardo et al., 2025). Challenging this epistemic injustice, Milton (2012) reframed the difficulties experienced by autistic people as reflecting a double empathy problem: autistic and non-autistic individuals tend to struggle to understand each other because of differing cognitive functioning and experiences.

The double empathy account of autistic difficulties is supported by a growing body of empirical data (Milton et al., 2022). For instance, Chen et al. (2021) investigated natural interactions in an inclusive school group of 6 autistic and 6 non-autistic adolescents, aged 12-14 years, over 5 months. Using video recordings, they coded for the frequency and characteristics of communication behaviours of the different participants. In line with the double empathy account, they did not observe an autistic deficit, but instead a marked preference for same-neurotype interaction, for initiatives as well as responses, both for autistic and non-autistic students. Compared to cross-neurotype interactions, same-neurotype interactions were more reciprocal, and served more relational purposes (sharing thoughts and experiences) than functional purposes (requesting help or objects). Importantly, sameneurotype preference, both for autistic and non-autistic adolescents, increased over time, suggesting that merely gathering a neurodiverse group is not sufficient for bringing down the double empathy problem. Accordingly, individuals both with and without experience with autistic people have been found to dehumanise them (Cage et al., 2019), and bullying victimisation risk for autistic students has been shown to be heightened -and not reduced -in inclusive school settings, as opposed to schools exclusively serving children with special needs (Zablotsky et al., 2014). This inclusion paradox may appear counterintuitive at first sight, considering that interactions between members of different social groups can effectively reduce prejudice, stereotypes, and discrimination, as put forward by the intergroup contact theory (Allport, 1954; Pettigrew et al., 2011).

To face this challenge, some specific activities may help bringing together a diverse group and allowing for positive interactions. Choral singing may be one of those. Indeed, choral singing has been shown to promote strong and fast social connections within large groups (Pearce et al., 2015; Weinstein et al., 2016). It implies multi-level synchronization between individuals (Delius & Müller, 2023), and by encouraging a constant representation of the collective intention and goal, joint musicmaking may have specifically evolved in the human species as a biological mechanism to foster social bonding and group cohesion (Kirschner & Tomasello, 2010; Savage et al., 2021).

Applied to autism, music has been shown to enhance the social response in specialized interventions (Ke et al., 2022) and may hold a particular importance in autistic people's lives (Korošec et al., 2024).

More specifically a few inclusive neurodiverse experiences have been reported in the literature. Eilat & Raichel (2016) report the experience of a choir that brought together neurotypical students from a mainstream elementary school and autistic children from a special education school, over 6 years.

They interviewed stakeholders from different perspectives to provide insights on this experience.

They report perceived benefits in terms of emotional regulation and well-being, in encouraging a strength-based perspective on autism, and in promoting empathy and camaraderie between participants. Similarly, we have reported a community based neurodiverse choir, which gathered autistic adolescents, general population adult choristers, and autism specialists (Petit, 2024; Petit & Jurek, 2025). Interviews with the autistic adolescents and their parents revealed important perceived benefits in terms of social connections and mental health. The adult general-population choristers reported through a questionnaire a highly enjoyable and enriching experience, and an improved representation of autism. The different participants described choral singing as a particularly powerful tool for fostering meaningful encounters, potentially by encouraging an open attitude (Petit, 2024). This lends support for the use of choral singing as a tool to facilitate interaction within neurodiverse groups and, by doing so, reducing prejudice and improving empathy between autistic and non-autistic participants. In short, it may be used as a double empathy solution, reducing the disability associated with autism.

In line with this idea, we have more recently reported a preliminary study aiming to confirm and specify the changes described by the general population choristers, using a quantitative single-group pre-post design (Petit et al., 2026). This revealed a measurable shift in participants' perception of autism, which became more accurate, increasingly viewed as a difference rather than as a deficit, and associated with a more open attitude, after this experience. Importantly, the choristers also felt more able and more inclined to interact with other neurodivergent individuals after participating in this choir. This is crucial, because the double empathy account aligns more generally with the social approaches to disability (e.g., Oliver, 1990), stressing how disability is not the sole expression of individual characteristics, but the product of the environment and especially, in this case, the social environment constituted by the general population. Any way to influence the general population's attitudes toward autism should thus be carefully considered by anyone interested in reducing the disablement experienced by autistic people.

Significantly, we developed this initiative in France, a country with a specific history regarding neurodiversity. The uniquely long influence of psychoanalysis on French autism care (Bishop & Swendsen, 2021) necessitated a struggle to adopt the internationally consensual neurodevelopmental model (Chamak, 2008). This struggle, in turn, might explain why the French approach to autism now remains deeply rooted in a biomedical paradigm, while the English-speaking world was seeing the rise of the social model of disability and of the neurodiversity movement more specifically. For example, this biomedical stronghold is visible in the recently released clinical guidelines for paediatric autism, which carefully avoid any mention of the neurodiversity paradigm (Haute Autorité de Santé, 2026).

Hence, this neurodiverse choir experience also offers a unique opportunity to explore how a neurodiversity-informed framework might operate in a society largely unfamiliar with this paradigm.

While previous quantitative data captured measurable changes in participants, it only approximated the underlying dynamics of the encounter. Complementary to this quantitative approach, the current study thus explores the lived, subjective dimensions of this neurodiverse experience from the general population choristers' perspectives, extending the qualitative exploration initiated in our earlier work (Petit, 2024). We deploy an iterative, two-steps qualitative approach, by first using an open-ended online questionnaire with the exact same sample as our previous quantitative research (Petit et al., 2026) to capture individual experience reports (stage 1, elicitation phase). Then, we deepen our findings and enhance their trustworthiness through a group discussion with a community stakeholder panel (stage 2, co-construction phase).

2. Methods

This study was run after the experience described by Petit et al. (2026). In this study, 12 general population adult choristers were recruited to constitute a choir, together with 8 autistic adolescents (7 males and 1 female; age range = [12-15] years), with associated conditions in most cases (including ADHD, intellectual disorder, language disorder); and 3 health professionals specialized in autism. The choir rehearsed 1.5 hours per week for 6 months (18 sessions) under the direction of a professional choir director. It then performed 4 polyphonic a cappella public shows in front of various audiences.

The primary direct aim of the project was artistic, rather than therapeutic or educational, and adult choristers were explicitly told that they were not expected to tutor or educate the autistic adolescents, but to sing and share music with them. Adult choristers took turns at rehearsals, participating in approximately two-thirds of the sessions depending on their availabilities, while the other participants (autistic adolescents, health professionals) were systematically present. Two months after the beginning of rehearsals, an information session on autism was proposed to the adult choristers by one of the autism specialists of the choir, including a 20-minute presentation and a 20-minute Q&A session. This information session was included following a request from participants of an earlier iteration of the project (Petit, 2024). Details of the rehearsals and information session are provided in the supplementary materials (Box S1).

We first captured the immediate, uninfluenced individual reflections from those choristers, using an open-ended online questionnaire (stage 1, elicitation phase). Subsequently, we expanded these baseline findings through a community co-construction panel (stage 2, co-construction phase).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local (French) laws, and each participant provided informed consent to participate.

The research team included the initiator of the neurodiverse choir project, a speech and language therapist trained in cognitive science; two speech and language therapy students trained in qualitative research but not involved in the choir experience; and a child and adolescent psychiatrist working in neurodevelopment, expert in qualitative research and public health research. These differing positions, combining direct involvement with external perspectives, were considered reflexively throughout the analysis. The analyses were held in French. The transcripts were subsequently translated to English for this paper (see table S3 in the supplementary materials).

2.1. Post-experience questionnaire (stage 1)

All general population choristers were French speaking and had previous experience in choral singing. The sample included 4 men, 7 women and 1 participant who identified as another gender.

The sample was relatively diverse in age (age range in years = , mean = 45, SD = 18) with a relatively high education level (12/12 had a Baccalauréat [A-levels] degree, 6/12 had a master's degree). Within the group, 1 participant identified as autistic without medical diagnosis, 9 reported not being autistic, 2 did not know. We purposely did not apply any exclusion criteria for neurodivergent participants to capture a realistic community dynamic, acknowledging that the general population includes neurodivergent individuals. Most participants declared they had previously interacted with autistic individuals through their personal (8/12) and/or their professional lives (4/12). Some of them (4/12) had participated in an earlier neurodiverse choir. The table S1 in the supplementary materials provides individual description of each participant of the sample.

An online open-ended written questionnaire was sent to the participants after the experience, allowing them to report their experience. Although semi-structured interviews typically yield deeper material, this format was preferred for two reasons. First, reflections on neurodiversity and prejudice are very sensitive to social desirability bias, and the written format provided a reflective space that minimized face-to-face performance pressure, encouraging more authentic reports. Second, this approach made it easier to gather qualitative data across the whole original sample from Petit et al. (2026), prioritizing descriptive breadth and sample continuity, over narrative depth which will be developed in stage 2.

This questionnaire first included demographic information and 5-points Likert scales assessing whether they evaluate the experience as (1) pleasurable, (2) enriching, (3) comfortable, and (4) whether it led to greater ease in interacting with autistic individuals. This data was treated descriptively.

Then, participants were asked three open questions on (1) whether they perceived benefits in participating, (2) whether they perceived barriers along this experience, and (3) whether they had anything else to report (see exact questions and their translations to English in the supplementary materials, table S2). These questions were meant to be very general, to capture what was prominent in their experience, without biasing them toward a particular direction. There was no character limit.

The data were collected in June 2025.

These text-based responses were analysed using an inductive, descriptive thematic analysis, to explore the participants' lived experiences. Data analysis followed an iterative, multi-investigator approach to enhance interpretive rigor. First, the first three authors independently immersed themselves in the data through repeated readings and generated initial inductive codes. Following this independent open-coding phase, a collaborative meeting was held with the last author. During this session, we confronted the independent analyses, discussed divergent interpretations, and collectively developed a consensual, hierarchical structure of overarching themes and sub-themes.

2.2. Community panel deepening (stage 2)

After the initial elicitation phase, an analysis of stage 1 material, as well as of the quantitative findings from Petit et al. (2026) were presented to a group of approximately 25 stakeholders, including original study participants, participants of other neurodiverse choir experiences (mostly previous or ulterior iteration of our project), and professionals specializing in inclusive artistic practices. The results were presented during approximately 45 minutes by the first author, followed by a 60-minute facilitated group discussion. The group was asked to discuss whether these results matched their lived experience, and whether any nuance or addition should be made to the analysis.

Priority was first given to the participants of stage 1 analysis, then opened to the rest of the group.

The facilitator actively managed power dynamics and employed targeted prompts to invite input from quieter participants. The panel discussion was held in March 2026. This group discussion was audio-recorded and transcribed for analysis. During transcription, speakers were categorized into three distinct stakeholder groups: (1) original study choristers, (2) choristers from other neurodiverse experiences and (3) professionals. A descriptive content analysis of the transcript was performed by the three first authors to assess the group's level of consensus on the first-stage analysis (in general and for each of the themes in particular), and how the panel elaborated on, critiqued, or deepened those baseline findings. 3.2.1. A powerful and enriching human experience (theme 1)

Stage 1. First, the participants reported a powerful and enriching human experience. This manifested in positive emotions (sub-theme 1.1), especially joy ("joy every week!", P5) and gratitude ("A huge thank you […] for setting up this choir", P2), alongside a desire to continue the project. More specifically, the participants insisted on how enriching the connections they have formed within the group have been (sub-theme 1.2), and notably with the adolescents ("a nice bond with the children we were accompanying", P1), a population they were mostly not used to interact with closely, be it because of their neurotype ("This is the first time I've been able to spend time regularly and closely with neurodiverse teenagers", P9) or because of their age ("[I enjoyed] being able to spend time with people of different age", P12). The participants also reported that this experience led to positive personal changes also outside of the group (sub-theme 1.3), especially in their relations with others ("I adopt a more welcoming approach towards people who seem different from me", P7; "An experience that is very rewarding in helping us learn to communicate our needs more effectively", P10) and with themselves ("I am now more comfortable with who I am, with all my quirks", P4).

Stage 2. The panel insisted on how this experience was meaningful and important to them ("it's not just a hobby […], there's more to it than that; there's something that… really matters", a chorister from another neurodiverse experience), giving it a social and political value in learning how to live together as humans, that generalizes beyond the mere question of autism or neurodiversity ("I can really see the political value in supporting this sort of project; in this particular case, it touches on the issue of autism, but […] it could go further than that […], affecting far more people than that", an original study chorister). The group also suggested an explanation of why they considered this experience successful, judging how important it was that all the participants (adults and adolescents, autistic or not) were equally considered as choristers, working toward a musical/artistic aim rather than in therapeutic or educational purpose ("I was really pleasantly surprised […] because I've never been asked to work with the kids […], it's explained very clearly […] right from the start […], we're actually there to sing together […], to do something fun, and that's […] an approach that works really well […], it makes all the difference", a chorister from another neurodiverse experience). This led to a fluctuating redistribution of disadvantages within the choir, which is also identified as central to the experience, with autistic adolescent occasionally helping neurotypical adults to sing ("It was the other way round: it was the teenager who was teaching the old man!", an original study chorister).

3.2.2. A new perspective on autism (theme 2)

Stage 1. Among the reported benefits, the participants shared that they developed a new perspective on autism through this experience. This means they report a more accurate representation of autism (sub-theme 2.1), encompassing the diversity of the spectrum ("realising the diversity of autism and what the concept of autism spectrum disorders actually entails", P6) but being also more positive ("Their [the autistic adolescents] spontaneity is a real breath of fresh air during the week", P4) and less essentializing ("I simply realised that, in the end, we had a lot in common", P4).

More than additional information, the participants reported that they also let go of false beliefs on autism (sub-theme 2.2), especially of fears they might have had ("it's an experience which can help demystify autism -it's a word that can be frightening, and I haven't experienced anything frightening this year", P9).

Stage 2. The panel endorsed stage 1 findings and especially highlighted how such experience promoted a perspective in which neurodiversity was valuable and not just a challenge to be overcome ("This difference becomes an asset", an original study chorister). The group insisted on how it was important to gain new knowledge, but also to deconstruct old representations ("I've mainly been deconstructing things, rather than confirming what I thought I knew", a chorister from another neurodiverse choir experience). They added that singing facilitated concrete social connections, which was in turn the key to forming more adjusted representation, attitudes and behaviours ("When you take action and experiment with people, that closeness leads you to accept differences", an original study chorister).

3.2.3. A demanding experience (theme 3) Stage 1.

Finally, the participants reported that, balancing for the important benefits described, this experience was also demanding. This includes demands in terms of personal resources, due to the neurodiverse composition of the group (sub-theme 3.1), which demanded important attention and energy ("I sometimes left rehearsals feeling exhausted by the energy it demanded", P7), or induced frustrating incomprehension ("I'd really like to understand their language so that I can have a proper conversation with them", P3). These costs are not isolated and removable but rather described as intrinsic to the experience and to its benefits ("Surprises, yes, emotions, tenderness, curiosity, a puzzling, yet so refreshing frankness", P9). Independently from the neurodiverse composition of the group, participants also reported organisational factors that influenced the experience (sub-theme 3.2), such as the hours of the rehearsals, the imbalance between different vocal sections, or their irregularity in being able to attend the rehearsals ("Irregular attendance: hard to get to know one another", P12).

Stage 2. The panel endorsed the demand induced by this experience and focused on the socioemotional engagement it requires (sub-theme 3.1). They notably acknowledged how communication with the autistic choristers could be difficult not because of the autistic participants, but also because of themselves ("As I participated, I realized that the interaction problems didn't stem from the young [autistic] people themselves, but from me -I was the one putting up barriers […], I was the one trying to overcontrol the situation", a chorister from another neurodiverse choir experience). They reported that they were initially looking for a "user manual" on how to communicate ("The first questions I asked myself were: How should I behave? […] Do I need a user manual?", an original study chorister), which was solved not by finding one, but by realizing there was no user manual ("I don't have a user manual because, well, there isn't one", a chorister from another neurodiverse choir experience) and that one just needed to make an effort trying ("we need to make an effort to build bridges with others", a chorister from another neurodiverse choir experience). Finally, the panel also considered this experience demanding because it led to an effortful and somewhat uncomfortable questioning, which was directly linked to what they learned ("It really makes you think […], and I think that's also what helps you gain knowledge and skills", an original study chorister).

4. Discussion

In this study, we explored how general-population adult choristers experienced a neurodiverse choir, shared with autistic adolescents and few autism specialists, during approximately 6 months.

We first systematically captured the immediate, uninfluenced individual reflections from the 12 choristers from Petit et al. (2026), using an open-ended online questionnaire (stage 1, elicitation phase). After a first stage qualitative analysis, we expanded our findings through a community coconstruction panel (stage 2, co-construction phase). This revealed that this experience was joyful and important for the choristers, characterized by fulfilling social connections and encouraging a personal blossoming. The participants especially reported a shift in their perception of autism, which became more accurate, more positive, leading them to deconstruct false beliefs and fears, and to recognize neurodiversity as valuable. Intrinsic to these benefits, participants also described this experience to be puzzling and demanding, in terms of emotions, energy and cognitive efforts, and in terms of organisational factors.

These results are clearly aligned with previous qualitative findings (Petit, 2024) and with the quantitative study recently run in the very same sample (Petit et al., 2026), showing that this experience is highly satisfying and enriching. It consistently supports the idea that a successful neurodiverse experience is not only beneficial to neurodivergent people, but to any individual and to the group. This in turn supports the idea that neurodiversity itself is valuable ("This difference becomes an asset"), which is the core idea of the neurodiversity paradigm (Chapman, 2021). Of note, not all neurodivergent individuals are aware of their neurodivergence -as reflected by the fact that two of our participants did not know whether they were autistic -and the choir experience also served as a safe space to explore one's own "quirks". Importantly, our findings support a nuanced approach of this paradigm, as it also showed that the benefits of neurodiversity are not automatic, but come with a cost, in terms of energy, efforts, and emotions that can be uncomfortable. This is also manifest in the contrast observed in Likert scales between participants' very high satisfaction and their relatively lower comfort. l Such demand does not seem distinct from the benefits reportshence, not removable -but instead the intrinsic cost of such benefits. For example, a participant reported that this experience was puzzling -eliciting a need for a "user manual" -but that this trouble was also the very source of their enrichment.

Furthermore, this cost can be directedly linked to the double empathy problem (Milton, 2012), as participants acknowledge that it is induced by interaction difficulties they encountered, and that they progressively took responsibility for ("As I participated, I realized that the interaction problems didn't stem from the young [autistic] people themselves, but from me"). To overcome this difficulty, the participants report realizing they had to let go of the idea of any "user manual" and instead experiment, take the time, and build ad hoc solutions. Supporting this idea, a participant of the community panel reported: "I don't have a user manual, because there isn't one anyway. There's a bridge to build with the other person […], as we have [different] ways of communicating (but even between people who aren't concerned [by autism]), […] we need to make an effort to build bridges with others, to engage in the conversation, but it's just a change of perspective. It's nothing more than that". Saying so, they also raised that such efforts were not specific to communicating with autistic individuals, but much more generally to anyone whose communication style is different, for various reason.

As introduced above, the mere juxtaposition of neurodiverse individuals is not sufficient to reduce prejudice and enhance mutual empathy (Cage et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2021; Zablotsky et al., 2014): it is more likely to result in cacophony. It is thus important to examine what led the choristers of this choir to experience a satisfying polyphony instead, as demanding as it appeared. The literature demonstrated how singing together is likely to serve as a biological mechanism to foster social bonding and group cohesion (Delius & Müller, 2023; Kirschner & Tomasello, 2010; Savage et al., 2021; Weinstein et al., 2016). Consistently with previous evidence (Eilat & Raichel, 2016; references 1 and 3, masked for review), our findings suggest that this general consideration also applies more specifically to neurodiverse groups.

Moreover, the participants of our study suggested additional explanations why their experience was successful. They put forward the importance of (1) sharing a concrete experience, and (2) sharing it while being all equally considered as choristers, working together toward a shared artistic aim. As a professional from the community panel puts it: "We're here to make music". Importantly, this echoes the conditions that facilitate prejudice reduction in Allport's (1954) intergroup contact theory. Indeed, this empirically validated theory (Pettigrew et al., 2011) states that mere groups juxtaposition is not sufficient but that that intergroup contact can be beneficial, as facilitated by (1) equal status, (2) common goals, (3) no intergroup competition, and (4) authority support. The participants in our study directly referred to the first three conditions. A choir intrinsically relies on intergroup cooperation, rather than competition. Regarding the fourth condition, this choir is structured to accept and even value atypical behaviours (Petit & Jurek, 2025), which is ensured by the choir director's attitude and the presence of three autism specialists in the choir, who represent an authority within the group. Importantly, these autism specialists sing alongside the other choristers, working toward the collective artistic aim of the initiative, rather than pursuing a therapeutic goal for autistic participants (Petit & Jurek, 2025). Their expertise may nevertheless be important as it puts them in a position to provide support, answer questions, and act as role models, fostering behaviours and attitudes that facilitate social interactions within the choir. In this perspective, the autism information session implemented during the project likely served as a critical cognitive scaffold. This session built directly upon the choristers' ongoing, lived experiences within the rehearsals. By providing context for specific behavioural observations retrospectively, it allowed participants to dismantle the anxieties associated with a missing "user manual".

Altogether, this highlights how choral singing (combined with timely, experiential-led psychoeducation) could support significant double empathy experiences, that could be deployed to push our societies to recognize the value of neurodiversity, for the benefit of all -while also acknowledging its worthwhile cost. Benefits could be expected within such groups, with positive ripple effects reaching much wider. Indeed, in line with previous evidence in the intergroup contact theory (Pettigrew et al., 2011), our participants also report benefits that generalize and apply outside of this group, and toward other differences than neurodivergence, as a participant reports: "I adopt a more welcoming approach towards people who seem different from me".

The specific French sociocultural context in which this project was initiated might contribute to explain why the experience felt "puzzling" to some choristers. However, our findings suggest that, even in a context less familiar with the neurodiversity paradigm (or the double empathy problem) gaining influence in the English-speaking world, such a view can be smoothly adopted through shared experience, supporting its robustness. Of course, it must be acknowledged that the group included in this study is likely not representative of the French general population, as participants had high education levels and prior interest in and experience with autism (and, most likely, specific baseline attitudes and knowledge). The fact that they still report important shifts in their representations makes our conclusion for such a group only more compelling, but it would now be important to study whether different groups experience such an initiative differently.

More generally, the current study presents limitations that demand further research. First, we used open-ended questionnaires rather than semi-structured individual interviews, so the depth of the initial narrative material was inherently constrained, limiting the assessment of data saturation.

Although the subsequent community panel allowed us to deepen the analysis and reach conceptual maturity on the core themes, a single panel could not establish that no additional themes or nuances would have emerged from further data collection. Moreover, the format of our group session limited the depth of individual contributions, and despite careful facilitation to balance power dynamics, we cannot rule out the influence of social conformity or the dominance within this group. Second, it is vital to match general population choristers' experiences to those of the autistic participants (as done in Petit [2024]), in order to develop a comprehensive appreciation of the neurodiverse dynamics. Finally, it would now be relevant to design and implement confirmatory quantitative research to test how such experience could lend the base for effective and socially valid double empathy solutions.

Details on the shared choral practice

Schedule

The choir rehearsed 1.5 hours per week for 6 month (18 sessions) under the direction of a professional choir director. It then performed 4 shows in front of various audiences. Adult choristers took turns at rehearsals, participating in approximately 2/3 rd of the sessions depending on their availabilities, while the other participants (autistic adolescents, health professionals) were systematically present.

Rehearsal content

Each rehearsal included a structured get-to-know-each-other activity (≈ 5-10 minutes), a body then vocal warm-up (≈ 10-15 minutes), vocal listening games (≈ 10 minutes), the teaching of the musical pieces by the director (≈ 45 minutes) and ended with an informal chatting time (≈ 15minutes).

Information session

Two months after the beginning of rehearsals, an information session on autism was proposed to the adult choristers. This included a 20-minute presentation by one of the autism specialists of the choir, on (1) the manifestations of autism, (2) the notion of neurodevelopmental disorder, (3) the concept of disability and (4) the relation between autism, mental health and camouflage, social connexion and stigma. This was followed by a 20-minute Q&A session, during which participants could ask questions about specific observations they had made during rehearsals. The session was not implemented before rehearsals but after few weeks of rehearsals so as not to bias participants' experience but rather make more sense of their concrete observations. This component was added in response to a previous experience, in which choristers expressed challenges due to difficulties in interpreting autistic adolescents' communication (Petit, 2024). This was arguably effective since such challenge was much more rarely expressed in the current experience.

Satisfaction

Likert scales responses (see figure1) indicated that this experience was highly pleasurable (12/12 agree, including 10/12 who strongly agree) and highly enriching (12/12 agree, including 10/12 who strongly agree). It was also mainly judged comfortable though notably with less unanimity, as some participants reported slight discomfort (2/12 somewhat disagree, 10/12 agree, including 4/12 who strongly agree). Finally, participants reported that it made them more comfortable with the idea of interacting with autistic individuals (12/12 agree, including 4/12 who strongly agree).
