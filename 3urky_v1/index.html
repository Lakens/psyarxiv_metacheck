Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Calibrated trust is considered an important factor for human-system interaction in decision-making. However, definitions of calibrated trust are heterogeneous and research often lacks conceptual clarity. In our work, we conducted a theoretical integration to summarize approaches defining and operationalizing calibrated trust in automation and AI.

We then mapped these approaches onto the trust development process that builds on the Trustworthiness Assessment Model (Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025) and the Organizational Trust Model (Mayer et al., 1995). The resulting Trust Calibration Points Framework (TCa-P) offers theoretical clarity and actionable insights for improving trust calibration by identifying six calibration points that can be calibrated to optimize joint human-system performance, and, as a consequence, effective human oversight: maximum joint performance benchmarking, trustworthiness assessment, alignment of individual standards, risk-benefit assessment, dispositions, and behavioral adaptation. The TCa-P emphasizes that trust calibration is not monolithic but instead happens at different points along the trust development process.

While most prior work shares the goal of optimizing joint human-system performance, it often remains unclear which aspects of the human-system interaction should be calibrated, and how. This leaves research and practice with uncertainty in communication about trust calibration and, more importantly, in identifying design and intervention opportunities to achieve calibrated trust and appropriate trusting behavior.

As a result of our analysis, we advocate for a more holistic process-based perspective on trust calibration, and thereby contrast prior monolithic perspectives on trust calibration. We offer an integrative solution by providing six calibration points within the Trust Calibration-Points Framework (TCa-P), which allow to specify which aspects need to be calibrated to optimize joint human-system performance, and allow for a targeted discussion on how they could be calibrated.

Theoretical Background

Our analysis of trust literature builds on the propositions of the Trustworthiness Assessment Model (TrAM; Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025) and the Organizational Trust Model (Mayer et al., 1995). Therein, the trust development process (Figure 1) begins with the trustor's (e.g., a human user's) assessment of a trustee's (e.g., a system's) actual trustworthiness, resulting in the trustor's perceived trustworthiness (Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025). The TrAM proposes that the actual trustworthiness of a system depends on the overlap between a trustor's individual standards and a system's characteristics. Trustworthiness consists of multiple dimensions reflecting the trustee's (actual or perceived) competencies, functionalities, and skills; its positive orientation towards the trustor; and its adherence to principles and values the trustor deems acceptable (J. D. Lee & See, 2004; Mayer et al., 1995). The TrAM (Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025) further proposes that trustors infer the latent actual trustworthiness of the system based on observable cues resulting in their perceived trustworthiness of the system. The accuracy of the trustworthiness assessment depends on the availability and relevance of cues on the system's side, and on the detection and utilization of those cues on the trustor's side.

However, not all observable behavior that superficially looks like trusting behavior stems from trust (Onnasch et al., 2026; Patton & Wickens, 2024; Vereschak et al., 2021). External influences on behavior can include social factors (e.g., social conformity; Asch, 1955), and task demands (e.g., time pressure; Rieger & Manzey, 2022) (for an overview cf. Consequently, behavior in interactions between humans and systems can, but must not necessarily be a manifestation of trust. The trustor's evaluation of the outcome (e.g., a perceived negative performance or process) then feeds back into their perceived trustworthiness of the system.

Calibrated Trust as a Match of Trust with Automation Capabilities 185

Most traditionally, calibrated trust has been conceptualized as the match of a trustor's 186 trust and the actual trustworthiness of a system (J. D. Lee & See, 2004; Muir, 1987). This view is also supported by philosophical approaches that describe trust as well-grounded when it matches trustworthiness (McLeod, 2021). However, when we try to map this conceptualization on the trust development process (Figure 1) conceptual issues arise: 1) it is unclear how to map trust onto trustworthiness, and 2) even if this was clear, its predictive value would be inconclusive.

First, matching trust considered a unidimensional construct (i.e., a "willingness to be vulnerable", Mayer et al., 1995; respectively an attitude, J. D. Lee & See, 2004) directly with actual trustworthiness requires that both constructs are compared on the same latent scale (Merritt, Lee, et al., 2015). As trustworthiness is considered multidimensional (cf. J. D. Lee & See, 2004; Mayer et al., 1995), it requires these dimensions to be condensed for meaningful comparison with the unidimensional trust construct. To overcome this problem system reliability was often used as a proxy for system trustworthiness (de Visser et al., 2016; Rittenberg et al., 2024). However, reducing trustworthiness to system reliability oversimplifies the trustworthiness concept. Most theoretical accounts define trustworthiness of automation as a concept beyond system reliability (Hoff & Bashir, 2015; J. D. Lee & See, 2004; McKnight et al., 2011; Sheridan, 2019). For instance, Lee and See (2004) considered process and purpose as two additional important aspects of trustworthiness beyond performance. As powerful AI systems are increasingly embedded in tasks where system reliability is just one of several key goals, such as value alignment, it is crucial to address the integrity and benevolence dimensions of trustworthiness (Lucas et al., 2024; Manzini, Keeling, Alberts, et al., 2024).

Second, if we find a way to meaningfully compare trust with system trustworthiness, and trust is miscalibrated (i.e., overtrust or undertrust), we will neither know the cause, nor the consequence of this assessment. In the trust development process (Figure 1), we see that this form of miscalibration (i.e., trust compared to actual system trustworthiness) can stem from an inaccurate trustworthiness assessment (e.g., due to missing or misinterpreted cues), misalignment between trustor's and system's standards, and from the trustor's extensively high or low trust propensity. Moreover, we do not know if trust is predictive for the actual trusting behavior, which might be moderated by perceptions of the stakes involved in showing the behavior (Hoesterey & Onnasch, 2023; Mayer et al., 1995; Patton & Wickens, 2024).

Calibrated Trust as Perfect Joint Performance or Ideal Trusting Behavior

Calibrated trust has been conceptualized as trusting behavior and outcome of the trust development process. Calibrated trust, in this sense, reflects a human action or performance measure in comparison to an ideal behavior or outcome (Carter et al., 2024). Some researchers used terms like "appropriate reliance" or "appropriate compliance" to describe this comparison between system performance and joint performance (Eckhardt et al., 2025; J. D. Lee & See, 2004; Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Schoeffer et al., 2025; Vereschak et al., 2021). In this sense, perfect calibration is achieved when users rely on correct system outputs and override incorrect ones, while miscalibration occurs when users over-rely on inaccurate outputs or under-rely on correct ones. Typically, this form of calibration is measured by factors such as decision accuracy (e.g., Schoeffer et al., 2025; Vereschak et al., 2021), and is operationalized by comparing the joint decision accuracy against two benchmarks: Human performance alone and the system performance alone (e.g., Rieger & Manzey, 2022). Others have proposed calibration as achieving some ideal state of monitoring the system (i.e., how frequently a system needs to be validated; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). These approaches evaluate trust calibration post-hoc, after outcomes are known, providing an external diagnostic perspective on the human-system interaction. A challenge arising from this approach is that it neither provides insights into the factors leading to suboptimal performance nor ways to achieve better calibration. For example, it is unclear whether the suboptimal performance was due to an inaccurate trustworthiness assessment or due to an inaccurate risk assessment. Consequently, calibrated trust in this sense only captures the outcome of the trust development process and thus offers a symptomatic description rather than an identification of root causes or a solution for (mis)calibration.

Calibrated Trust as Accurate or Justified Trustworthiness Assessment

Calibrated trust has been conceptualized as an accurate trustworthiness assessment (Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025), i.e., the match between system characteristics and perceived trustworthiness (de Visser et al., 2016; Wischnewski et al., 2023). In this sense, calibration focuses on the correct assessment of an AI and its characteristics. Research often investigated how providing specific cues influences the accuracy to estimate automation reliability or correct system functioning (Cohen et al., 1997; Sheridan, 2019). In the trust development process, trust follows from the perceived trustworthiness. Consequently, an accurate trustworthiness assessment is a prerequisite for (calibrated) trust, but it cannot be calibrated trust itself (Kohn et al., 2021; Mayer et al., 1995; Onnasch et al., 2026). This understanding of calibrated trust thus only focuses on the trustworthiness assessment step of the trust development process neglecting aspects such as a trustor's individual standards that affect a system's actual trustworthiness, their trust propensity and their assessment of stakes in a specific context.

Calibrated trust has also been conceptualized as justified trustworthiness assessment (Ferrario & Loi, 2022; Manzini, Keeling, Marchal, et al., 2024; McLeod, 2021; Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025). A trustor is "calibrated" in this sense, if their trust (or trustworthiness assessment) is rational given the information available to the trustor. In other words, if the trustworthiness assessment is adequate given the cues available to the trustor, the trustworthiness assessment can be considered justified, even if it turns out to be incorrect (Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025) leading, for instance, to negative joint performance consequences. While it is important to distinguish that an assessment of trustworthiness can be justified but inaccurate, this understanding of calibrated trust again focuses only on a single aspect of the trust development process, neglecting that even a perfectly justified trustworthiness assessment can be inaccurate, and hence may not necessarily lead to optimal joint performance.

Calibrated Trust as Alignment

Some researchers used the term trust alignment interchangeably to trust calibration (Kaniarasu et al., 2013), and the term alignment has often been used to specify trust calibration (e.g., de Visser et al., 2020; Lucas et al., 2024; F. Yang et al., 2020). Others introduce alignment as a prerequisite for trustworthy systems (Manzini, Keeling, Marchal, et al., 2024; J. McVay et al., 2025; Summerville et al., 2025). In the AI (value) alignment literature, which is primarily concerned with the problem that human values should be imposed onto AI systems (Gabriel, 2020), the term "calibration problem" was used to describe issues around AI alignment (Firt, 2023). It seems that this cross-use bears potential for future confusion, which we want to prevent at this point. In the trust development process, alignment happens as a part of the trustworthiness assessment. More specifically, therein alignment mainly refers to the degree of overlap between the individual standards of the trustee and the trustor.

Towards a Revised Trust Calibration Framework

The previous section pointed out problems with existing conceptualizations of "calibrated trust", which are fragmented and often narrowly focused on isolated stages of the trust development process (cf. Figure 1, Table 1). While each perspective sheds light on important facets of human-system interaction, none sufficiently captures the complexity required to systematically diagnose or improve joint human-system performance. These partial views limit both theoretical clarity and practical applicability. Notably, what unites all research on calibrated trust is its shared goal: optimizing joint human-system performance, as well as minimizing harmful errors and problematic outcomes (e.g. unfair discrimination) that might be introduced by implementing automated systems into high-risk contexts. We share this goal, and introduce the Trust Calibration Points Framework (TCa-P) that enables researchers to investigate and understand why the joint human-system performance in each context is suboptimal, and how it could be optimized. The TCa-P aims to unify the different conceptualizations of trust calibration along the trust development process, thereby offering a structured basis for diagnosis, measurement, and intervention.

With the TCa-P we propose six calibration points that aim at differentiating and diagnosing the single parts within the trust development process that might hinder optimal joint performance of humans and systems. We consider these calibration points the levers and knobs to tune to calibrate joint human-system performance. It is important to note that we assume a context where the preconditions for trust being relevant are met (Mayer et al., 1995). That means, a) humans need to have the freedom to decide (not) to trust a system (e.g., if the human fears to face punishment when rejecting system advice, following system advice may not reflect trust), and b) humans need to be in appropriate contexts to make this decision (e.g., avoiding excessive time or peer pressure which may render any decision than to follow and use a system impossible), and c) the outcome of trusting or relying on a system needs to be uncertain to a degree (e.g., trust is not an issue if the human knows a system is always right, like a calculator), and d) there needs to be something at stake for the human that provides a perceived benefit of trusting (e.g., higher efficiency), but at the same time puts them at risk, and renders them vulnerable to the outcome of the system's advice or action (e.g., the human is responsible for the outcome). All these factors are prerequisites to talk about trust and thus also for trust calibration. If external factors are determining behavior, trust calibration may not be the primary concern.

Calibration Point 1: Maximum Joint Performance Benchmarking

Calibration Point 1 requires setting realistic expectations regarding the outcome, i.e. the joint performance potential of human-system teams. Without setting realistic expectations it is unclear whether the observed joint human-system performance reflects the best possible, or at least an acceptable joint outcome. Often, when introducing an AI system, it is implicitly assumed that human-system collaboration will result in synergy, where the joint performance exceeds the individual performances of both partners, or at least, augmentation, where the joint performance exceeds one partner's individual capacity. Whereas synergy is rare, augmentation is more commonly observed (Vaccaro et al., 2024). In other words, human-AI teams often tend to perform worse than either the human or AI working alone (but see Cabrera et al., 2023; de Visser & Parasuraman, 2011; Roemer et al., 2026; Wickens & Dixon, 2007).

Maximum joint performance represents the theoretical best performance given the individual contributions of the team members; it does not necessarily equal perfect performance, which is the flawless completion of a task. Instead, maximum joint performance provides a reasonable benchmark on what can be expected from human-system collaboration.

Maximum joint performance depends on complementarity, i.e., the diversity of unique performance contributions of humans and systems (Burton et al., 2024; de Winter & Dodou, 2014; Metcalfe et al., 2021). Figure 3 illustrates the role of overlapping and complementary skills for maximum joint performance benchmarking. This understanding of complementarity differs from that proposed by others (such as Schoeffer et al., 2025), who describe it in terms of post-hoc evaluations where the human relies on AI recommendations in a way that the final decision quality is higher than without human involvement. Note, within the terminology proposed earlier, we refer to Schoeffer et al.'s (2025) definition as augmentation (or synergy), and argue that complementarity is a requirement for augmentation to occur. As such it is critical to consider complementarity a priori, to properly assess the team's potential and limitations and to establish an appropriate benchmark for their joint performance (Meyer & Kuchar, 2021), i.e., to estimate how the observed joint performance deviates from maximum joint performance. For instance, Meyer and Kuchar (2021) provide a way to compute the maximal benefits that can be derived from a binary aid, Wickens and Dixon (2007) suggest that automation should perform accurately in at least 70% of the cases to provide a benefit to joint performance.

Miscalibration: Inadequate benchmarks

For this calibration point, miscalibrations means that expectations about the joint performance of human-system teams exceed or fall short of their joint potential. This might lead to wrongful claims that problems in human-system interaction occurred at other points within the trust development process (Calibration Points 2-6). For example, if a pilot's skills declined after relying on the autopilot for substantial time, their manual skills may no longer enable them to intervene effectively, even if they notice system malfunction and are fully aware of the risks. In this case, the implicitly expected joint human-system performance exceeds their joint potential. Given the human should remain part of the dyad, addressing this miscalibration requires acknowledging the reduced human contribution and providing appropriate training, rather than attempting to improve risk communication or the presentation of system failures.

The key takeaway is that expectations should match the human-AI teams potential based on their capabilities. Neither users nor AI systems can magically increase their own capabilities, and hence their contribution to the joint task, when teaming up. Synergy is only possible if the individual contributions of humans and AI are diverse, complementary and allow for efficient aggregation (Burton et al., 2024). This is also reflected in research showing that peoples' own capabilities in a task are a strong predictor for their joint performance with a system in this task (Vaccaro et al., 2024), as the probability of diversity and complementarity rise within these human-system teams.

Implications

To get a better benchmark of what to expect from teaming up humans with systems we need to develop a clear understanding of the potentials and limits of each team member (e.g., human and AI). This includes identifying performance strengths and weaknesses, as well as the unique contributions of each team member, and their aggregation (Centeio Jorge et al., 2024; McNeese et al., 2023). By clarifying what each member can, want and should do (Centeio Jorge et al., 2026; M. Johnson et al., 2014; M. Johnson & Bradshaw, 2021), it is possible to uncover areas where training or system improvements are required. For example, Vaccaro et al.(2024) report evidence that there might be synergies in tasks that require content generation rather than decision-making, where humans need to choose from a predefined set of options.

Another consideration entails the question whether specific tasks can be fully delegated to a system, or whether human input is still required-the classic problem of function allocation (Kaber, 2018; Onnasch et al., 2014; Parasuraman et al., 2000; Parasuraman & Wickens, 2008). In cases where complete delegation is infeasible, careful thought must be given to which elements of a task can be handled by systems and which require human oversight. Aggregation could be improved by means of relational aspects to improve cooperation within the human-system team and by means of increasing transparency of the system (Chiou & Lee, 2023). Thereby also other factors that contribute to task performance should be considered like time, costs, and the impact of errors (Chiou & Lee, 2023; Vaccaro et al., 2024), as well as hedonic, eudemonic and motivational aspects of the task (Hassenzahl et al., 2010; Helander & Tham, 2003; Sterz et al., 2024). This delegation process will require continuous refinement as both systems and human expertise may change over time.

Calibration Point 2: Trustworthiness Assessment

Calibration Point 2 refers to the accuracy of the trustworthiness assessment, i.e. whether the trustor's perceived trustworthiness matches the system's actual trustworthiness (cf. TrAM, Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025, as well as section Theoretical Background and Figure 4 below). The accuracy of the trustworthiness assessment depends on the availability and relevance of cues on the side of the system, and on the detection and correct utilization of cues on the side of the trustor. Only if relevant cues are available to the trustor, and only if trustors detect those cues and interpret them correctly an accurate trustworthiness assessment is possible. If a trustor utilizes all available cues correctly but there are not enough relevant cues (e.g., due to the system's opaqueness Barredo Arrieta et al., 2020), the trustor's assessment is justified but inaccurate (Funder, 1995). Therein, a trustworthiness assessment is accurate when the actual trustworthiness of a system matches the perceived trustworthiness of the trustor (upper purple connection line). A trustworthiness assessment is justified (lower blue connection line), when available cues are detected and correctly utilized by the trustor, independent of the accuracy of the trustworthiness assessment.

Miscalibration: Inaccurate assessment

The differentiation between an accurate and a justified trustworthiness assessment helps to understand the causes of inaccurate assessments. An inaccurate, but justified assessment shows that relevant system cues are missing which emphasizes necessary changes on the system side. An inaccurate and unjustified assessment shows that trustors cannot detect or correctly utilize the available cues, an issue with the interface or human operator.

An accurate, but unjustified assessment might be the result of lucky guessing, and may lead to "learned carelessness" (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010) posing a risk of future errors. As such, the goal should be to have trustors being able to build justified and accurate assessments.

Inaccurate trustworthiness assessment of the system can lead to tragic consequences, as illustrated by the USS Vincennes incident in 1988 (Rochlin, 1990). Operators of the ship's anti-aircraft system misidentified a commercial aircraft as an attacking military aircraft, and shot it down, because the system provided misleading and poorly designed cues. The operators of the system relied on the available, and seemingly consistent cues, making their assessment to shoot down the aircraft justified from their perspective, while ultimately inaccurate. The result was the downing of the Iranian Flight 655 and the loss of 290 civilian lives.

Implications

Key to achieving accurate and justified trustworthiness assessments is the relevance, availability, detection, and utilization of cues to assess the trustworthiness of a system.

Depending on why a trustworthiness assessment is inaccurate different steps need to be taken to improve its accuracy. If a trustor's assessment is justified but inaccurate, the system needs to be adapted or redesigned, in a way that makes relevant cues available. This could happen by realizing AI systems with metacognitive capabilities (S. G. B. Johnson et al., 2026), e.g., by the means of explainable AI methods (Barredo Arrieta et al., 2020; Gunning & Aha, 2019), or by developing systems that are transparent by design (Cha et al., 2019; J. Y. C. Chen et al., 2018; de Visser et al., 2014; Mercado et al., 2016). For example, likelihood alarm displays, which estimate and display the probability that a signal is accurately detected, improve the availability of relevant cues in monitoring tasks (Rieger, Marx, et al., 2025; Sorkin et al., 1988; Wiczorek & Manzey, 2014; X. J. Yang et al., 2017; Zirk et al., 2020).

If a trustor's assessment is not justified, examining the causes for their assessment will reveal the next course of action (Funder, 1995; Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025). For example, if a trustor did not detect (all) relevant cues, the interface design needs to be adapted to highlight relevant information. Alternatively, user training needs to focus on where relevant information can be retrieved (Jelínek et al., 2025). If a trustor did detect the relevant cues, but interpreted them incorrectly or if they also utilized irrelevant cues, user training needs to focus on the correct interpretation of available relevant cues (Bahner et al., 2008; Harari et al., 2019; Kyriakidis et al., 2019; Walliser et al., 2019 Walliser et al., , 2023)), and system designers might need to search for more ways to unambiguously convey available information, uncertainty, and confidence (Alarcon et al., 2026; Bisantz et al., 2005; de Visser et al., 2014; Endsley, 2017; Kunze et al., 2019; McGuirl & Sarter, 2006; Phillips & de Visser, 2026).

Calibration Point 3: Alignment of Individual Standards

Calibration Point 3 is closely related to the field of value alignment (Bhat et al., 2024; Gabriel, 2020; Pflanzer et al., 2023), which addresses the challenge that AI systems should align with the individual standards of different trustors, while at the same time they need to align with broader ethical principles and legal requirements (Figure 5). We here define alignment as the degree to which the individual standards of at least two parties overlap.

Building on the TrAM (Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025), individual standards reflect the values, norms, moral beliefs, preferences, decision-making styles, and goals that determine what each party considers to be a trustworthy AI system. These standards may differ across parties due to pluralistic moral beliefs (Gabriel, 2020; Hu et al., 2025), varying cultural backgrounds (Awad et al., 2018), motivations (Durán & Jongsma, 2021), and goals (Gabriel & Keeling, 2025). Parties can be trustors (e.g., end-users, or developers; Saffarizadeh et al., 2024), trustees (e.g., an AI system), and third parties (e.g., observing researchers with specific normative standards, more abstract human values or ethics, Gabriel, 2020; or agreed upon standards, Jobin et al., 2019). To clarify, Calibration Point 2 concerns whether trustors accurately assess a system's individual standards, Calibration Point 3 concerns whether the standards of a trustor and a system align. Consequently, alignment and accurate trustworthiness assessments are conceptually independent. Multiple parties may be aligned without being aware of it, or fully aware of their misalignment. Alignment of individual standards is hard to assess (Borders et al., 2025; J. C. McVay & Visser, 2025; Peterson & Gärdenfors, 2024; Shortland et al., 2025) and technically challenging to achieve (Arvan, 2024; Ji et al., 2024; Lyons et al., 2023). Even if technical alignment were possible, agreement on a single ethical framework is unlikely and arguably undesirable in pluralistic societies (Gabriel, 2020). Different moral frameworks may legitimately collide (e.g., Kantian ethics versus utilitarism versus virtue ethics; Gabriel, 2020), and even amongst experts, disagreement with respect to a "correct" decision exists in high-stakes decision-contexts like medical triage (J. McVay et al., 2025; Summerville et al., 2025).

To illustrate, consider a setting with three parties: An AI system (the trustee), a user of the AI system (the trustor), and a third party who evaluates the joint performance of the human-AI team according to ethical standards (Figure 5). This triadic relationship entails three alignment relationships. First, alignment between trustee and trustor means that the AI system aligns with the user's individual standards and thereby foster trust and acceptance (Hu et al., 2025; J. McVay et al., 2025; Rauch et al., 2025; Shortland et al., 2025; Summerville et al., 2025). Second, alignment between trustee and third party concerns whether the AI system adheres to broader ethical, safety, or regulatory standards that aim to ensure societal benefit (for an overview see Jobin et al., 2019), which may conflict with individual user preferences (i.e., third parties; Gabriel & Keeling, 2025). For instance, such conflicts may arise when personal convenience leads to environmental harm, or when speed undermines safety (Phillips & Malle, 2025). Third, alignment between trustor and third party reflects whether trustors share broader normative principles of third parties.

Miscalibration: Misalignment

Problems could arise when parties are misaligned or when they are not aware of their (mis)alignment. Misalignment may especially occur in cases of normative uncertainty, for example when different ethical principles could legitimately guide a system's behavior (e.g., maximizing life expectancy vs. minimizing suffering, Durán & Jongsma, 2021). In such cases of moral uncertainty, it is key that all parties are aware of their degree of alignment, which is reflected in pluralistic alignment approaches (Hu et al., 2024 (Hu et al., , 2025;; Lyons et al., 2023; Pflanzer et al., 2023; Schelble et al., 2026; Sorensen et al., 2024). Unawareness might be especially problematic when people accept a system, but with wrong expectations about its intended purpose or optimization goals (Durán & Jongsma, 2021). Unawareness of misalignment could be enhanced through the false-consensus effect (i.e. one party assumes that their individual standards and those of another party are aligned, Ross et al., 1977), through automation bias and complacency (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Those may lead people to assume that an automated system works perfectly. A further unawareness of misalignment may arise when people use AI agents to act on their behalf. Previous work has highlighted that acting through AI agents to direct other humans can enable unethical behavior (Gratch & Fast, 2022), or improve cooperation (de Melo et al., 2019) depending on how users can program their agent.

Implications

It is important to recognize that trustworthiness assessment (Calibration Point 2) and alignment of standards (Calibration Point 3) require different diagnoses and therefore different interventions. More specifically, when third parties (e.g., researchers) assess the accuracy of a trustor's trustworthiness assessment they need to be aware of the trustor's individual standards. Otherwise, third parties could confuse inaccurate trustworthiness assessment with misalignments, thereby overlooking the actual cause and hindering the application of effective countermeasures. Whereas inaccuracies in trustworthiness assessment may be addressed by improving relevant system cues or by user training, misalignment requires adapting either the trustor's or the system's standards, or deciding not to use the system.

To address misalignment, the standards of a system could be aligned with those of a trustor either during the design of the system or they could also be adapted automatically during interaction with the system (de Visser et al., 2023; Jelínek et al., 2025). However, alignment is not always appropriate, e.g., if the trustor's individual standards conflict with ethical or legal standards. In these cases, interventions could focus on changing trustors' individual standards. In cases of moral uncertainty (Baum & Slavkovik, 2025) or in cases in which multiple legitimate options exist (Deutsch, 1975; Summerville et al., 2025), it may be neither possible nor appropriate to change the trustor's standards, but instead to change the system's standards to match those of the individual trustor (J. McVay et al., 2025; Summerville et al., 2025). In these cases, it is important that individual standards are made transparent, enabling accurate trustworthiness assessments (J. Y. C. Chen et al., 2018; Endsley, 2023).

Calibration Point 4: Risk-Benefit Assessment

Calibration Point 4 refers to the accuracy of the risk-benefit assessment. Risk refers to the likelihood and severity of potential losses, such as the probability and cost of an error (e.g., an accident). Benefits refer to the anticipated gains (e.g., productivity, increased performance). These considerations include what is at stake, how large the anticipated benefit is, and what the consequences of failure would be.

The fact that Mayer et al. (1995) used the term perceived risk highlights that there might be deviances from actual risk associated with a given behavior. This is also highlighted by Slovic (1987) who referred to intuitive risk judgments of lay people (or the public) as risk perceptions. He outlines how risk perceptions differ between experts and lay people, and between people with different backgrounds. Moreover, he depicts that different criteria might be applied when risk is assessed by experts (e.g., annual mortality rates) and lay people (e.g., uncertainty surrounding a risk, and a potential influence on future generations).

Risk-benefit assessments influence the trust development process (Chancey et al., 2017; Lyons & Stokes, 2012) and have been discussed under situational risk (Hoff & Bashir, 2015) and situation-specific trust (Cohen et al., 1997) in the context of automation. Satterfield et al. (2017), for example, support this assumption by showing that in high-risk situations compared to low-risk situations, participants were more likely to intervene manually in a task that was delegated to an automated agent. Participants were thus less likely to show trusting behavior in high-risk situations, although they perceived the automation equally trustworthy respectively trusted the agent similarly in the high-and low-risk conditions. There is further evidence that this relationship inverts with simpler automation (Fahnenstich et al., 2024).

Following from that, if trustors inaccurately assess risks and benefits this may lead to inadequate behavior and potentially also influence the depth of the trustworthiness assessment (Schlicker, Lechner, et al., 2025).

Miscalibration: Inaccurate risk-benefit assessment

Inaccuracies of the risk-benefit assessment can arise out of limited available, relevant information, and due to limited detection and incorrect interpretation of available information, and individual circumstances. In risk-benefit assessments, often only partial information is available on the exact probabilities of both positive and negative outcomes of a decision. Additionally, detection may be limited by people's tendency to search for satisfactory rather than optimal decisions, as outlined in the concept of "bounded rationality" (Gigerenzer & Goldstein, 1996; Gigerenzer & Selten, 2002; Simon, 1990).

With respect to the interpretation of available information, research on cognitive heuristics shows that people are generally poor at assessing risks, even when probabilities are explicit. For instance, the conjunction fallacy illustrates how individuals may judge a combination of characteristics as more likely than a single characteristic (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983). Similarly, "loss aversion" refers to the tendency that people react more sensitive to estimated losses than to equivalent gains (Kahneman et al., 1991). Additionally, affective heuristics are used as a replacement for risk-benefits assessments, especially under time pressure (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007). Finally, individual circumstances and prior experiences can shape the perception of risks and benefits. A technology may appear particularly attractive when alternatives are limited or unattractive (M. K. Lee & Rich, 2021). For example, individuals without access to healthcare might value the benefits of AIgenerated medical advice more highly than the risk of receiving imperfect AI health advice or data (Schlicker, Lechner, et al., 2025).

Implications

If an inaccurate risk-benefit assessment causes suboptimal performance, education and training of the user as well as proper communication of risk and uncertainty in system design can be effective countermeasures. User training should address common fallacies in risk-assessment. If risks are underestimated, it could, for instance, involve exposure to base rates and historical automation accidents and errors to give users a better idea about the probabilities and costs of the consequences (Bahner et al., 2008).

On the communication side Bostrom et al. (2023) denote: "Risk communication research […] shows [...] that the effects of visualizations and other communications of uncertainty on trust are likely to depend on the quality and context of communication [...]. Thus, there is a need for meaningful and useful information rather than just more information." Simple changes in risk framing can influence human behavior, even if the odds are transparent (Kahneman & Tversky, 1984). System design should enable more accurate risk judgements by providing clear risk communication (J. Chen et al., 2023; J. D. Lee et al., 2004; Zipkin et al., 2014).

Calibration Point 5: Dispositions

Calibration Point 5 refers to relatively stable dispositions that moderate the relationship of trust related concepts, including the transitions from perceived trustworthiness to trust and from trust to trusting behavior (Figure 1). This refers to concepts such as trust propensity (Mayer et al., 1995; Scholz et al., 2024), dispositional trust (Hoff & Bashir, 2015; Merritt & Ilgen, 2008), automation expectancy (Pop et al., 2015), risk propensity (Ferronato & Bashir, 2020; Mayer et al., 1995), as well as related concepts like the perfect automation schema (Lyons & Guznov, 2019; Matthews et al., 2020; Merritt, Unnerstall, et al., 2015), and (automation-induced) complacency potential (Merritt et al., 2019; Singh et al., 1993). For instance, "all-or-nothing thinking" was associated with trust reduction after errors occurred (Merritt, Unnerstall, et al., 2015), and trustors with high automation expectancy were more sensitive to changes in automation reliability compared to people with lower expectations (Pop et al., 2015). Other dispositional factors that were found to influence trust in automation include personality (Szalma & Taylor, 2011), working memory (Rovira et al., 2017), and even genetics (Krueger et al., 2012; Parasuraman et al., 2012). Schlicker et al. (2025) discuss that trust propensity (including related dispositions) depicts the trustor's individual threshold to consider becoming vulnerable (i.e., to trust).

Given an accurate trustworthiness assessment, trust propensity influences a) how much evidence for the presence of trustworthiness a trustor needs (as reflected in the item of the Propensity to Trust in Automated Technology Scale [PTT-A]; Scholz et al., 2024: "Even though I may sometimes suffer the consequences of trusting automated technological systems, I still prefer to trust than not to trust them."), b) how much evidence for the absence of trustworthiness they tolerate (PTT-A item: "I believe that I am generally better off when I do not trust automated technological systems than when I trust them."), and c) how much uncertainty they tolerate (PTT-A item "I rarely trust automated technological systems because I can't handle the uncertainty.") to consider trusting. All of this affects the likelihood of engaging in later steps of the trust development process, like considering the risks and benefits of using a system in a specific situation, and also to use or rely on it (i.e., to show trusting behavior). What can be calibrated here is the range of uncertainty and vulnerability that should optimally be tolerated. The extremes might vary from no evidence needed to trust (naïve mindset) to excessive evidence needed to trust (skeptical mindset).

Miscalibration: Extreme Attitudes

Dispositional miscalibration is present if people hold extreme attitudes, i.e., they have strong aversion against or appreciation for systems in general or against a specific system (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Logg et al., 2019). This may lead people to tolerate much uncertainty and evidence for the absence of trustworthiness, and still trust and rely on the system. For instance, this case is reflected in an interpersonal trust-game context where Dunning et al. (2014) found across four studies that about 62% of their participants showed interpersonal trusting behavior, but only 20% of the participants considered their partner to be trustworthy. Conversely, it could also happen that people have substantial evidence for the presence of trustworthiness, but still, they do not consider to trust or use a system that may benefit them (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Longoni et al., 2019).

Defining what degree of trust propensity is advantageous or disadvantageous for optimal joint performance brings up questions like: how much evidence is enough to rightfully trust a system? Even if we cannot provide a satisfying answer to this question, we can conclude that it is beneficial to the joint human-system performance to avoid the extremes, i.e. having trustors who either always or never trust a system, who need excessive or very little evidence of trustworthiness, or who are willing to take any or no risk. In these extreme cases we lose the potential of finding emergent capabilities of humans and systems working together. To frame it differently, a trustor is "calibrated" at this point when they are open to incorporate new cues into their trustworthiness assessment. For example, people with high expectations may develop more accurate trustworthiness assessments when understanding why automation errors occur (Dzindolet et al., 2003; Pop et al., 2015; Walliser et al., 2023).

Implications

If an extreme disposition or attitude leads to suboptimal joint human-system performance, effective countermeasures should focus on training and guiding trustors to stay aware of and potentially counter their own biases. One approach is to enhance users' metacognitive awareness of their own biases, helping them recognize situations in which they are particularly susceptible to over-trusting or under-trusting the system (He et al., 2023).

Training interventions could incorporate scenario-based learning, real-time feedback, and reflection exercises to reinforce awareness of trust dynamics and their implications for performance (He et al., 2023).

Beyond individual training, it is crucial to develop effective methodologies to assess trust propensity and its stability over time. This requires a deeper understanding of the factors that moderate trust dispositions, such as individual differences in cognitive styles, domain expertise, or exposure to technology. It might be worthwhile to investigate certain categories of technologies that inherently elicit stronger trust or skepticism (e.g., in the medical domain, Longoni et al., 2019). Additionally, ways to mitigate general tendencies to reject (AI) technologies may involve social proof mechanisms like user testimonials or expert reviews (Knowles & Richards, 2021; Scharowski et al., 2023), or individually tailored system solutions to counteract persistent presumptions (Costello et al., 2024; Okamura & Yamada, 2020).

Calibration Point 6: Behavioral Adaptation

Calibration Point 6 adopts a behavioral perspective and focuses exclusively on the calibration of observable trusting behavior, such as monitoring behavior or reliance on system advice or alerts. Users are behaviorally calibrated when their actions match a predefined ideal behavior. Once such an ideal behavioral pattern is specified, calibration can be assessed independently of the actual task outcome (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

Nevertheless, the desired outcome implicitly shapes what counts as ideal behavior (Figure 6).

Behavioral adaptation does not necessarily require a deep understanding of the underlying system. Instead, it relies on user compliance with prescribed behavioral rules. The primary challenge at this calibration point lies in defining such rules that determine how much one should ideally monitor automation and how to appropriately rely and comply with it (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010; Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Parasuraman & Wickens, 2008). Determining what constitutes ideal behavior becomes particularly difficult when systems demand improvements in both accuracy and speed simultaneously, or when at least one dimension must be maintained while the other improves (Bahner et al., 2008).

Miscalibration: Suboptimal Behavioral Adaptation

Behavioral miscalibration occurs when users deviate from predefined ideal behavior.

Two well-studied manifestations of such miscalibration are automation complacency and automation bias, which are both considered behavioral manifestations of excessive trust in and reliance on automation (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

Automation complacency reflects insufficient system monitoring taking the form of omission errors, in which system malfunctions or anomalies remain undetected, which leads to decreased joint performance (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). It occurs under multitasking conditions characterized by high workload, when attention is reallocated from automation to concurrent manual tasks (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Within this context, complacency potential is defined "as a behavioral tendency to react in a less attentive manner in interacting with a specific automated system" (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010, p. 403). Complacency has frequently been linked to trust. Similarly, trust has been directly conceptualized as monitoring intention (Loi et al., 2023), where higher trust was associated with reduced monitoring behavior (Patton & Wickens, 2024).

Automation bias emerges rather in collaborative decision-support environments. It was defined as "the tendency to use automated cues as a heuristic replacement for vigilant information seeking and processing" (Mosier & Skitka, 1996, p. 205). Automation bias is often observed when users rely on inaccurate or incomplete system advice (Gaube et al., 2021; Wickens et al., 2015). Automation bias may arise because automated systems are initially attributed higher trust than human sources, based on assumptions about their superior capabilities, reliability, and authority (de Visser et al., 2016; J. D. Lee & See, 2004; Madhavan & Wiegmann, 2007).

Lastly, distrust and relatedly disuse of automation may be caused by frequent false alarms (Huegli et al., 2020; Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Parasuraman & Wickens, 2008).

The frequency of false alarms is related to the challenge of setting appropriate decision thresholds to respond accurately to extremely rare events (Parasuraman et al., 1997).

Recognized under the "cry wolf effect" frequent false alarms may signal to an operator that the system is not reliable, lower trust and consequently cause the operator to eventually ignore accurate system warnings or disable them altogether (Huegli et al., 2020; Parasuraman & Riley, 1997).

Implications

Calibrating behavior at this point requires defining an ideal behavior. For instance, for monitoring tasks ideal behavior may be optimal sampling behavior (i.e., how often a system should be monitored) based on the expected signal frequency (i.e., the Nyquist rate; Moray, 2003; Moray & Inagaki, 2000; Parasuraman et al., 2008). In practice, defining ideal behavior can be difficult (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010), especially when working with self-learning AI systems whose internal processes are opaque and whose errors may occur unpredictably.

When ideal behavior can be defined, training can improve behavioral calibration. For instance, exposing users to automation errors during training can foster more appropriate behavioral strategies, enhance multitasking performance, and reduce the impact of automation complacency (Bahner et al., 2008; Metzger et al., 2000; Walliser et al., 2023). This may also include the correct application of check lists, where behavior is considered optimal when a set of safety critical values is validated, as often apparent in aviation (Clay-Williams & Colligan, 2015; Degani & Wiener, 1993).

Note, once an ideal behavioral pattern is specified, it also becomes necessary to determine who is accountable when users behave in accordance with this ideal behavior, yet errors occur. This issue becomes particularly salient in high-stakes domains like medicine where users are ultimately responsible for an outcome (European Data Protection Supervisor, 2025; Sterz et al., 2024). One would assume that if users comply with a behavioral guideline, they might not be held responsible for later errors. Accordingly, it is important to clarify the accountabilities and responsibilities of different involved stakeholders (Mosier et al., 1997; Skitka et al., 2000).

Behavioral calibration depends on the existence of a normative behavioral standard.

When such standards cannot be established (e.g., when interacting with systems whose behavior is unpredictable, including large language models used for task support), interventions should shift toward earlier calibration points (CP1-CP5) that target system transparency or trustworthiness assessment rather than prescribing specific behavior.

Discussion

Overall, there seems to be agreement on the goal of trust calibration to achieve optimal joint human-system performance. However, there is conceptual and terminological heterogeneity on how this goal should be achieved and which role trust-related concepts play in this endeavor (Benk et al., 2024; Mehrotra et al., 2024). Thus, it is difficult to navigate the research field including its various terminology and operationalizations and to understand what and how to calibrate in order to optimize joint human-system performance. Within the Trust Calibration-Points Framework (TCa-P) we integrate different approaches of trust calibration and consider trust calibration an umbrella term that reflects six distinct calibration points along the trust development process (Mayer et al., 1995; Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025). We argue that each of these points can and need to be calibrated to optimize joint human-system performance. The TCa-P highlights what trust-related constructs can be calibrated, and provides a basis for how to calibrate these aspects.

The TCa-P advances the field by grounding calibration in a trust-process model, by improving terminological and conceptual clarity, and by providing a diagnostic tool for a long-standing challenge in human factors research and application: Identifying where miscalibration occurs in human-system interaction and countering it by the respective means.

In the following, we will elaborate on the theoretical and practical implications of the TCa-P on a broader level, and discuss limitations of the current approach as well as directions for future work.

Getting Closer to the Gist of Calibration

One key question motivating this paper was: What is calibrated trust? Our answer is that calibrated trust is a deceptively intuitive term that lacks precision. The term "calibrated trust" disguises the actual goal of this research, as it suggests that the objective is to calibrate trust itself (J. D. Lee & See, 2004; Muir, 1987), whereas the actual underlying goal is optimal joint human-system performance (by means of trust-related factors). Our analysis of prior work showed that the problem with prior monolithic perspectives on calibrated trust was that they lacked at least one of the aspects necessary for calibration: a clear goal and a tolerance range (answering the question: Towards what should be calibrated?), relevant influencing factors (What can be calibrated?), and means to influence these factors (How can these factors be adjusted?).

We want to stress here that the TCa-P is not just an integration of prior work, but also an invitation to shift the perspective on how we think about trust calibration in general. To clarify the holistic perspective on trust calibration as proposed in the TCa-P, let us think through calibration in another context: the case of boiling a perfect egg for breakfast (Chakraborty et al., 2025; Figure 7). First, the goal must be defined, e.g., a soft-boiled vs.

hard-boiled egg. Alongside the goal, a tolerance range must be specified, i.e., what is considered an acceptable egg, because not every deviation from the optimum necessarily constitutes failure. Second, the relevant influencing factors must be identified, such as egg size and type, temperature, and altitude (cf. Chakraborty et al., 2025). Third, one must determine how to actually influence these egg-related factors, e.g., by changing the heat or the time in the water, by chosing smaller or larger eggs, or by climbing a hill. To which degree one influencing factor needs to be adapted depends on the other factors, e.g., how long the egg should remain in the water depends on the temperature and the altitude. In any case the resulting configuration should reflect for each influencing factor an ideal value ("the ought") to which the current value ("the is") should be adjusted in order to achieve the set goal, e.g., if the current temperature is 30° it needs to be adjusted to reach the ought-value of 100°.

This example should illustrate a more general principle of calibration. Calibration is most likely not a one-shot adjustment of a single variable, but a process in which multiple interacting factors are adjusted towards a defined goal. In this sense, the described process is closely connected to kibernetic systems, in which each factor can influence the others, and in which the calibration of one factor alone may not suffice to reach a specific goal (Carver & Scheier, 2000; Gaube et al., 2026; Schrills, 2025). To meaningfully move forward with "trust calibration", we suggest using the term as an umbrella for the process of calibrating trust-related factors to achieve whatever is considered optimal performance. The TCa-P offers a framework that enables specificity in determining a goal, the influencing factors to achieve this goal, and a structure to determine how these factors can be meaningfully influenced towards this goal. Additionally, the TCa-P offers a more precise terminology supporting this specification. Instead of referring to general terms like calibrated trust (and the often-used but vague associated terms of over-or undertrust), we suggest to refer clearly to a specific calibration point, i.e., expectations regarding the optimal joint human-system performance (CP1), the degree of accuracy (respectively justification) of trustworthiness assessments (CP2), the (mis)alignment between users, systems and normative standards (CP3), the degree of accuracy of risk-benefit assessments (CP4), variations of trust dispositions (CP5), and the degree of adaptation to a behavioral ideal (CP6), or a combination of those (see Table 1).

Practical Application of the TCa-P: Effective Human Oversight

Beyond providing a conceptual framework for understanding trust calibration and the practical implications outlined at each calibration point, the TCa-P framework can also support practical challenges in the design and governance of human-AI interaction. One example is effective human oversight, which has become a central requirement for the deployment of AI in high-risk domains (European Data Protection Supervisor, 2025).

Effective human oversight provides a useful example of the practical relevance of the TCa-P because many of the challenges associated with effective oversight are, at their core, challenges of trust calibration. For example, Article 14 of the European AI Act requires that human operators understand the capabilities and limitations of AI systems, appropriately monitor and interpret their outputs, remain aware of their potential (automation) bias, decide when to rely on or override AI recommendations, and intervene when necessary (European Data Protection Supervisor, 2025). Similar to issues around calibrated trust as outlined in the beginning of this paper, it is currently not clear how effective human oversight should be achieved and evaluated, i.e., what the standards are to consider oversight effective (or at least acceptable) (Sterz et al., 2024; van Voorst, 2024).

The TCa-P provides a structured framework for identifying the normative questions that need to be answered to achieve an appropriate reference standard that could help in determining when human oversight is considered effective. More specifically, the TCa-P can guide this process, by supporting to ask the right questions when defining an ideal reference or calibration standard (Figure 8). Not only "what should be calibrated?", and "how should be calibrated?" are important questions, but also "towards what should be calibrated?"

"Towards what?" refers to normative questions that need to be discussed when implementing AI systems into high-risk contexts. For example, Calibration Point 1 requires defining the goal of human oversight, i.e., when oversight can be considered fulfilled.

Calibration Points 2-4 require specifying what counts as accurate and justified trustworthiness and risk-benefit assessments and to which standards users and systems should be aligned. Calibration Point 5 requires defining unacceptable dispositions. Calibration Point 6 requires specifying the behavioral standards that users are expected to follow in each context (e.g., monitoring strategies that define adequate human oversight). Together, these normative reference standards establish a calibration blueprint that enables systematic comparison between the desired ("ought") and the observed ("is") state of factors influencing human-AI interaction (Figure 8) (Schlicker, 2026). Such reference standards may also provide a basis for questions and discussions of accountability. If users comply with predefined calibration standards, occurring errors may rather indicate limitations of the AI system than failures of human oversight. For example, if users are instructed to verify AI recommendations only below a predefined confidence threshold expressed by the AI system, errors occurring despite compliance with this behavioral standard may point to miscalibrated confidence estimates within the system rather than inadequate human oversight (Hunsicker et al., 2026). In this way, the TCa-P may help clarify when human oversight obligations have been fulfilled and when responsibility more appropriately lies with the AI system respectively its provider or deployer (Haftenberger & Dierks, 2023). The same reference standards may also provide concrete learning objectives for AI literacy and human oversight training (Art. 4, European Data Protection Supervisor, 2025) by making explicit which judgments, attitudes, and behaviors users are expected to acquire.

Limitations and Future Work

The TCa-P should be regarded as an initial conceptual framework rather than a final taxonomy. Trust calibration is a complex and dynamic process, and future research may reveal further nuances that refine or expand the model. For example, signal detection theory has been proposed as a different perspective on trust calibration (Langer et al., 2024; Sheridan, 2019), while other trust models emphasize learned, temporal, specificity or delegation components that may introduce additional calibration mechanisms (Castelfranchi & Falcone, 2010; Hoff & Bashir, 2015; J. D. Lee & See, 2004). Moreover, we presented the six calibration points at a high level to provide an overview and synthesis of the TCa-P idea, and hence diving deeper into each calibration point was out of scope for this manuscript, e.g., with respect to implications for larger human-AI teams (Duan et al., 2025 (Duan et al., , 2026;; O'Neill et al., 2022; Walliser et al., 2023). Consequently, some further nuances at each calibration point could be described and throughout the paper we have tried to refer to additional theoretical work that underpins each calibration point (e.g., Schlicker, Baum, et al., 2025 for CP2).

A second limitation concerns the empirical operationalization of the framework.

While the TCa-P is theoretically grounded, it remains an open question to what extent individual calibration points can be measured distinctively and reliably (Kohn et al., 2021; Tenhundfeld et al., 2022). Several calibration points require normative reference standards that may be difficult to define in practice. For instance, defining maximum joint performance in complex tasks, determining what counts as accurate or justified trustworthiness assessment of non-deterministic AI systems, or defining ideal behavioral adaptation may not always be straightforward. Nevertheless, we believe that a proper understanding and definition of the problem and potential solutions on a conceptual level are a necessary requirement for the similarly grand challenge of developing methods to achieve these solutions. If we do not specify the expectation regarding the outcome we might achieve the goal without noticing, and potentially call it inappropriate reliance (implicitly blaming a user who might have justifiably followed system advice [CP2], or has legitimately different individual standards [CP3]). While the TCa-P adds complexity to the issue of calibrating trust-related phenomena to optimize the joint human-system performance, we advocate to embrace this complexity rather than to oversimplify the concept up to the degree that it is neither diagnostic nor useful.

A valuable step for future research might be to define normative reference standards against which calibration can be validated, and to develop ways to operationalize the validation of calibration points, and means to adequately influence these points towards a defined standard. Building on this work, future research could develop targeted methods to diagnose miscalibrations at each point. For example, interdependency analysis, which assesses the relative strengths of humans and machines within a team, may be particularly suited for Calibration Point 1 (Centeio Jorge et al., 2024; M. Johnson et al., 2014; M. Johnson & Bradshaw, 2021), whereas human error identification methods that aim to categorize human errors (e.g., detection vs. utilization errors) may be suitable for Calibration Points 2 and 4 (Cheng & Hwang, 2015; Kirwan & Ainsworth, 1992; Stanton, 2017). Novel and integrated multi-modal measurement of trust process components could help determine the causes of behavioral consequences of trust described in Calibration Point 6 (de Visser, Beatty, et al., 2018; Feldmann-Wüstefeld & Wiese, 2026; Topoglu et al., 2026; Wiese & Feldmann-Wüstefeld, 2026). Such work could establish a methodological toolbox that links diagnostic methods to specific calibration points across various application contexts. Conclusion "Trust calibration" was defined and operationalized heterogeneously, often focusing on separate aspects of the trust development process while sharing the common goal of optimizing joint human-system performance. The Trust Calibration-Points Framework (TCa-P) integrates these different approaches by conceptualizing trust calibration as a process comprising six calibration points that can be calibrated to achieve optimal joint humansystem performance. Thereby, the TCa-P advances conceptual and terminological clarity, and enables a more holistic and diagnostic understanding of trust calibration. Rather than asking whether "trust is calibrated", researchers and practitioners can identify which component of the trust development process is not where it ought to be and select interventions that specifically target this factor. This perspective supports more targeted research, more specific system design and user training, and may also provide a structured framework for evaluating the effectiveness of human-system interaction, which can be especially valuable for highstakes applications of AI requiring human oversight. Furthermore, our perspective on trust calibration may inspire novel ways for research and practice, and can support the safe integration of AI systems into human decision-making.
