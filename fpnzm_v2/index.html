Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Abstract

Work-life balance policies can redistribute time without transferring responsibility for anticipating and coordinating care. This conceptual article asks when changes in time and task allocation permit caregivers to stop monitoring care needs. Integrating research on cognitive household labor, work-family resources, psychological recovery, and social care, it develops a model linking responsibility, authority, and the opportunity to disengage. The model proposes that continued answerability sustains monitoring and anticipatory vigilance even when another person performs the associated tasks. A dependable successor, understood as a person or institution able to assume responsibility for a defined care need, is proposed as one relational condition supporting disengagement. Gender, socioeconomic conditions, family composition, institutional recognition, and workplace arrangements shape access to this condition. The contribution is to specify how responsibility is retained or transferred within broader accounts of demands and resources, rather than to treat those accounts as exclusively concerned with time. Testable propositions distinguish changes in task performance from changes in monitoring, identify boundary conditions, and guide research across care arrangements. Using European work-life balance provisions as an illustrative policy context, the article proposes indicators for evaluating whether leave, flexibility, and services redistribute cognitive responsibility and support recovery, alongside their effects on participation in employment.

Introduction

One parent takes a child to a medical appointment; the other has noticed the symptoms, arranged the visit, prepared the information, and remains responsible for checking the outcome.

The first parent performs the appointment task, while the second continues to monitor it. If a workplace policy gives the first parent time off, task time may change hands without an equivalent transfer of coordinating responsibility. This example raises the central question of this article: under what conditions does redistributing time and tasks also transfer responsibility for anticipating, organizing, and monitoring care? Research on cognitive household labor establishes why this distinction matters: the activities that keep care functioning are not adequately represented by counts of visible tasks or hours (Daminger, 2019; Dean et al., 2022).

The problem extends beyond the household because work-life balance policy acts predominantly through time. In the European Union, Directive (EU) 2019/1158 establishes minimum rights to paternity, parental, and carers' leave and provides for requests for flexible working arrangements (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2019). These instruments widen access to employment and create conditions for sharing care. Whether the time they make available also transfers responsibility for care is a separate question, and it lies beyond the reach of common indicators, which record hours and leave uptake. If responsibility can remain in place while time is redistributed, predominantly temporal instruments may be insufficient on their own to reduce the strain that responsibility produces.

Existing research gives reasons to expect such persistence. Cognitive labor persists when physical household labor declines: women's employment and earnings are associated with less physical household work and with no comparable reduction in cognitive labor (Weeks et al., 2025). Responsibility for anticipating and monitoring care is often held apart from the power to decide (Daminger, 2019), and responsibility appears more consequential for well-being than the performance of particular tasks (Ciciolla & Luthar, 2019). Time spent in other activities does not reliably release caregivers from family concerns either, since thinking about family matters continues during the working day and carries costs that differ between mothers and fathers (Offer, 2014). Taken together, these findings describe a demand that is held through answerability, that moves independently of task time, and that follows the caregiver into paid work.

Established theories provide substantial resources for answering this question. Job demandsresources theory (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017) and the work-home resources model (ten Brummelhuis & Bakker, 2012) encompass psychological demands, contextual resources, and conditions that shape their consequences. The stressor-detachment model explains why continuing engagement with demands can impede recovery (Sonnentag & Fritz, 2015). These approaches can accommodate mental load and do not imply that equal hours produce equal strain. The more specific task pursued here is to explain how responsibility remains attached to one caregiver when tasks change hands, and which arrangements allow that person to relinquish monitoring. This requires specifying the allocation and transfer of a demand, alongside its intensity and the resources available to meet it.

The article develops a three-level model of care-related mental load. Structural antecedents include social position, institutional and workplace resources, and the distribution of responsibility, authority, and the opportunity to disengage. Psychological mechanisms include persistent monitoring, anticipatory vigilance, and difficulty detaching. Proposed consequences include distress, exhaustion, work-family interference, and insufficient recovery. The focal contribution is a relational account of transfer: a dependable successor is a person or institution that credibly assumes responsibility for a defined care need, including follow-up and foreseeable contingencies. This is proposed as one condition supporting disengagement, rather than as a new name for all forms of social support or the only possible route to recovery.

Workplaces and public services help determine whether such transfers are possible. Schedule control, supervisor support, institutional contact routines, and reliable care provision can change who is reachable, who can make decisions, and who must respond when an arrangement fails.

The model therefore connects household coordination to organizations and policy. Five propositions, with subpropositions, identify testable pathways, and an evaluation matrix translates them into questions about leave, flexibility, workplace practices, and support services.

The argument is conceptual, and its proposed mechanisms require empirical evaluation.

European work-life balance policy provides an illustrative institutional context. The model concerns the design of care arrangements; it does not estimate the effects of the European directive or establish that its provisions have failed. Applications elsewhere should examine the relevant local institutions and entitlements.

Conceptual Approach

This article develops a conceptual model through a targeted integration of published scholarship. Its starting point is a distinction established in research on household labor: performing a task and retaining responsibility for its completion are not necessarily the same activity. The argument brings that distinction into conversation with resource, boundary, recovery, and social care theories to specify how monitoring can persist or be relinquished (Daly & Lewis, 2000; Daminger, 2019; Dean et al., 2022; ten Brummelhuis & Bakker, 2012; Sonnentag & Fritz, 2015).

The analysis proceeds by defining the care relations at issue, comparing them with neighboring constructs, and deriving propositions about changes in task performance, answerability, and monitoring. The cited studies supply evidence about relevant associations and conceptual distinctions; they do not constitute a systematically searched or pooled body of evidence. Where a proposed mechanism extends findings from another setting, that extension is identified as a hypothesis. European leave and flexibility provisions are used to illustrate policy implications, not as data from an evaluation of implementation. The resulting framework is judged by conceptual clarity, consistency with existing evidence, and the availability of empirical comparisons that could support or challenge its added value.

Invisible Responsibility, Cognitive Overload, and the Organization of Care Cognitive Labor and Invisible Responsibility

Daminger (2019) identified cognitive household labor in four activities: anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding between them, and monitoring outcomes. Subsequent work has extended this description. Robertson et al. (2019) define mental labor as thinking activity performed for the sake of accomplishing family goals and, from focus groups with mothers of young children, identify six forms: planning and strategizing, monitoring and anticipating needs, metaparenting, knowing, managerial thinking, and self-regulation. Dean et al. (2022) describe the combination of cognitive and emotional labor as invisible, persistent, and able to extend across work and non-work settings, and Wayne et al. (2023) distinguish managerial, cognitive, and emotional dimensions of invisible family load and report both costs and benefits across them. A systematic review of 31 studies concluded that women carry the larger share of this work, particularly when they are not employed or work part time, and that the imbalance is associated with emotional strain, dissatisfaction, and career disadvantages (Reich-Stiebert et al., 2023).

Evidence on the consequences of this work points to responsibility as the component that matters most for well-being. In a sample of 393 partnered mothers, Ciciolla and Luthar (2019) found that nearly nine in 10 reported being solely responsible for organizing family schedules, and that responsibility for children's well-being was the strongest predictor of feelings of emptiness and of lower satisfaction with life and with the partner relationship. Major financial decisions, which were usually shared, were associated with lower partner satisfaction, which suggests that decision-making power can carry demands of its own. In Daminger's (2019) study, women undertook more anticipation and monitoring, while decision-making was more evenly shared. Holding responsibility for care therefore does not automatically confer equivalent power over how it is organized, and the two can be configured in several ways within the same household.

The distinction between task execution and coordinating responsibility becomes especially consequential when delegation requires reminders, contingency plans, or continued responses to institutional queries. In such arrangements, sharing visible activity can leave the obligation to ensure completion concentrated on one person. The question is therefore which components of a care need change hands and who remains answerable if it is not met.

Answering these questions requires keeping three terms apart. In this article, cognitive labor refers to the activities involved in organizing care, mental load to the continuing cognitive and emotional demand that this work imposes, and distress to a possible psychological consequence.

The distinction prevents the demand from being defined by its adverse outcome, since planning can be valued, performed competently, and experienced as rewarding. The concern here is the unequal and insufficiently supported obligation to remain responsible.

Gender Inequality and the Persistence of Responsibility

This obligation falls unequally on women, and its allocation is notably resistant to changes in time and resources. Yavorsky et al. (2015) followed highly educated dual-earner couples who had shared housework relatively equally before the birth of their first child. After the birth, mothers' daily workload rose by about two hours and fathers' by about 40 minutes, while both partners perceived their additional work as similar. Weeks et al. (2025), in a survey of 2,133 partnered heterosexual parents in the United States, found that women's employment and earnings were associated with less physical household labor and with no comparable reduction in cognitive labor, a pattern they describe as gendered cognitive stickiness. Offer (2014) showed that mental labor about family extends into the working day and that it was associated with negative emotions and stress among employed mothers, with no comparable association among fathers.

These findings concern partnered parents, mostly in the United States, which limits generalization. Taken together, they suggest that responsibility attaches to persons through expectations of competence and accountability that persist when the resources of the household change. Invisibility contributes to this persistence. Planning can be described, documented, and discussed, so it is open to inquiry; what often goes unrecognized is its standing as work for which others should share responsibility. When attentiveness is treated as a personal trait, its unequal allocation can appear natural, and the questions of whose competence is taken for granted and whose withdrawal is judged unacceptable remain unasked.

Cognitive Overload: From Responsibility to Persistent Monitoring

Expectations of accountability become a continuing psychological demand through processes that research on cognition and stress has described. The perseverative cognition hypothesis proposes that worry and rumination prolong stress-related physiological activation beyond the duration of the stressor itself, linking stressors to health outcomes through repetitive thought (Brosschot et al., 2006). Research on goal pursuit shows that unfulfilled goals produce intrusive thoughts that interfere with unrelated tasks, and that forming a specific plan for completing the goal eliminates these effects (Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011). In working life, unfinished tasks at the end of the week have been linked to impaired sleep over the weekend through rumination (Syrek et al., 2017).

The present model extends this reasoning to care as a hypothesis, rather than treating laboratory findings on goals as direct evidence about caregiving. An unresolved care need may remain cognitively active while the caregiver expects to be answerable for its completion. A specific plan can itself reduce intrusive thought (Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011); another person is not necessarily required. For recurring or unpredictable needs, however, a dependable person or service may make the completion arrangement credible without requiring continued personal monitoring. The model proposes that this arrangement can reduce persistent monitoring, anticipatory vigilance, and difficulty detaching, while allowing that these processes may continue because of worry, mistrust, preference, or previous failures of care.

The Organization of Care

Whether a successor exists depends on how care is organized beyond the household. Daly and Lewis (2000) conceptualized social care as labor carried out within normative frameworks of obligation and responsibility and with financial and emotional costs, distributed across the state, the market, the family, and the community. This perspective places schools, health services, employers, and care providers among the actors that assign responsibility, hold authority over resources, and supply or withhold successors. Purchasing help can redistribute work within an employing household while placing demands on paid workers whose own caring arrangements may remain unsupported, so a reduction in one person's burden leaves open whether the wider arrangement is equitable. The model therefore treats institutional resources as structural antecedents alongside the household.

The caregiving stress process model (Pearlin et al., 1990) describes how such arrangements become stress for family caregivers. It distinguishes primary stressors, the hardships anchored directly in caregiving, from secondary stressors, which include strains in roles and activities outside caregiving and intrapsychic strains involving diminished self-concepts, with coping and social support intervening at several points. The present model shares this process view. Within it, the model specifies the arrangements that determine whether a caregiver can relinquish monitoring, and it locates part of the spillover into other roles in the working day.

The Work-Family Interface and What It Leaves Open

When responsibility for care follows a caregiver into paid work, it becomes a source of workfamily conflict, which Greenhaus and Beutell (1985) distinguished into three forms: time-based conflict, in which time devoted to one role makes it difficult to meet the demands of another; strain-based conflict, in which strain produced in one role affects performance in another; and behavior-based conflict, in which behaviors required in one role are incompatible with those expected in another. Time-based policy is designed to relieve time-based conflict. Mental load corresponds more closely to strain-based conflict, which conflict theory treats as the product of role demands in general. The present model specifies one source of that strain, responsibility held across roles, and the conditions under which it persists after time-based conflict has eased.

Ten Brummelhuis and Bakker (2012) describe work-home conflict through depletion of personal resources and enrichment through resource accumulation. Their model includes contextual demands and resources and recognizes conditional influences beyond the individual.

Continuing responsibility can therefore be represented as a demand, and reliable care provision as a contextual resource. The present model specifies the relation between these elements: practical help transfers responsibility only to the extent that it also changes who must monitor, arrange contingencies, and account for unmet needs. This is a proposed elaboration of resource processes, whose value depends on whether it improves explanation and measurement beyond existing constructs.

The same resource logic allows for positive transfers. Greenhaus and Powell (2006) proposed that experiences in one role can improve the quality of life in another through the transfer of resources such as skills, perspectives, and positive affect. Care coordination can build such resources, including planning skill and a sense of competence. This possibility is one reason the model keeps mental load distinct from distress, and it suggests that coordinating work is more likely to generate transferable resources when the person who holds responsibility also holds the power to shape the arrangement.

How far care responsibilities reach into paid work also depends on boundaries between domains. Ashforth et al. (2000) describe role boundaries on a continuum from segmentation to integration and the transitions people make across them. Clark (2000) emphasizes permeability, flexibility, and border-keepers such as supervisors and partners. These theories already recognize that boundaries are relational and constrained. The present model focuses on one such constraint: when institutions continue to hold a caregiver answerable, a preference for segmentation may not translate into an opportunity to stop monitoring. The proposed contribution is to identify the care arrangement that maintains or relaxes this constraint. Supervisors are among the border-keepers whose behavior has been measured directly. Hammer et al. (2009) developed a measure of family-supportive supervisor behaviors comprising emotional support, instrumental support, role modeling, and creative work-family management. Such behaviors can widen a caregiver's authority over schedules and can provide the cover that makes disengagement possible. Recovery research, for its part, distinguishes psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, and control as recovery experiences measured at the level of the individual (Sonnentag & Fritz, 2007). The model draws on this work and adds a structural precondition: detachment is an experience, and the opportunity to disengage is a property of the arrangement that determines whether that experience is available for a given care need.

Structural Antecedents

At the first layer of antecedents, social position covers gender, socioeconomic condition, family composition, and institutional recognition, and institutional resources cover public care provision, employment conditions, workplace practices, leave and flexibility provisions, and the administrative routines of schools, health services, and other providers. An intersectional approach examines how these positions jointly shape the relations that follow. Crenshaw (1991) shows that frameworks organized around a single axis can miss inequalities that do not fit their categories, and Collins (2019) develops intersectionality as critical inquiry into connected relations of power. In the model, each position is read as a set of arrangements that produce particular responsibilities and possibilities for action, and none serves as a general index of psychological weakness.

These positions and resources shape three relations among the people and institutions involved in a care need. Responsibility means being answerable for anticipating needs, ensuring they are met, and accounting for outcomes when arrangements fail. Authority is the power to define adequate care, revise decisions, and command the money, schedules, information, and cooperation needed to respond. The opportunity to disengage is an arrangement in which a caregiver can cease personal monitoring of a specified need because responsibility is dependably assumed elsewhere. It is bounded by the need, the period, and the agreed contingencies, rather than requiring the permanent transfer of a caring relationship.

Responsibility and authority correspond to the two terms of the demand-control model, in which Karasek (1979) proposed that mental strain arises when job demands are high and decision latitude is low. Responsibility for outcomes functions as a demand that accumulates through unmet needs, deadlines, and the prospect of being held to account, and authority corresponds to latitude over how that demand is met. Care coordination differs from the paid job the model describes in two respects. Authority over care is relational: it is negotiated with partners, relatives, schools, employers, and service providers, and it is rarely written into any role. The demand is also open ended, since needs arise at unpredictable times and across several settings at once, with no shift that formally ends.

The opportunity to disengage identifies a proposed structural support for the experience of psychological detachment described by Sonnentag and Fritz (2015). Finishing paid work does not automatically end its cognitive demands, and care has no universally defined endpoint either.

A dependable successor is expected to support release from monitoring where ongoing needs require somebody to remain answerable. This successor must have sufficient information, authority, and capacity to act, including when circumstances change. Assistance with a task may leave all of these responsibilities with the original caregiver. A reduction in hours can therefore leave the arrangement unchanged, while a limited period of reliable succession may permit a meaningful interval of disengagement.

The Workplace as a Site of Authority and Succession

During the working day, schedule control influences whether a caregiver can respond to a need without negotiating each interruption. Supervisors act as border-keepers, and familysupportive behaviors can supply practical assistance and workable adjustments (Clark, 2000; Hammer et al., 2009). Cover for an employee's paid duties can reduce the simultaneous demands of work and care. It does not by itself provide a successor for the care need. That requires another caregiver, an employer-supported service, or another provider to assume the relevant responsibility. Conversely, flexibility can make one worker the default contact for schools and services. The model distinguishes these pathways so that organizational support is evaluated by what it actually changes: control of paid work, responsibility for care, or both.

Psychological Mechanisms

The model connects these relations to persistent monitoring of known needs, anticipatory vigilance for needs that have not yet arisen, and difficulty setting that engagement aside during paid work, rest, or sleep. Together, they describe the sustained experience of care-related mental load examined here. The formulation builds on perseverative cognition and goal-pursuit research (Brosschot et al., 2006; Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011), but does not establish a distinct cognitive process merely by specifying its object as care. Difficulty detaching corresponds to low psychological detachment (Sonnentag & Fritz, 2007). The added explanatory question concerns how the arrangement of answerability sustains these experiences, alongside individual tendencies and the objective unpredictability of needs.

Consequences

Persistent monitoring and vigilance are proposed to contribute to distress, exhaustion, workfamily interference, and insufficient recovery. Research on perseverative cognition provides a rationale for associations with sustained activation and health (Brosschot et al., 2006); workfamily conflict and recovery research identify related pathways across roles and into rest (Greenhaus & Beutell, 1985; Sonnentag & Fritz, 2015; Syrek et al., 2017). These sources support the proposed links without establishing every pathway for care coordination. The same coordinating activities can generate skills, meaning, and positive transfers under supportive conditions (Greenhaus & Powell, 2006; Wayne et al., 2023), so mental load should not be defined solely by adverse outcomes.

Distinguishing the Constructs

The model combines established ideas with more specific relational definitions. It must therefore demonstrate added value without treating different terminology as evidence of a new construct. Empirical redundancy is a documented concern in organizational research (Le et al., 2010). Responsibility is treated as a particular demand defined by answerability, which may persist at low levels of personal task performance. Authority is a care-specific expression of control distributed across household members and institutions. The opportunity to disengage describes the arrangement supporting detachment, whereas detachment is the person's experience. These distinctions do not establish independence from demands, control, support, or boundary management. Their value depends on whether indicators of answerability and reliable succession explain variation beyond established measures, including the dimensions of invisible family load described by Wayne et al. (2023).

Alternative Roles of Authority and the Opportunity to Disengage

Authority and the opportunity to disengage can act as antecedents, mediators, or moderators, depending on the arrangement of care, and the conditions under which each role applies can be stated in advance.

The opportunity to disengage can have a direct association with monitoring where responsibility is otherwise similar but reliable succession differs. When succession varies across days, it may also condition the association between mental load and recovery. A mediated pathway is proposed where institutions repeatedly direct information and follow-up to the caregiver recognized as responsible: concentrated contact may reduce opportunities to hand over monitoring, contributing to difficulty detaching. These are distinct hypotheses requiring temporal ordering and appropriate comparison groups. A single cross-sectional association cannot distinguish them, and studies should specify the intended pathway before analysis.

Authority acts as a moderator in the sense of the demand-control model: when the responsible person also holds authority over the resources that determine responses, responsibility is expected to translate less strongly into persistent monitoring and vigilance, because problems can be resolved and closed. Evidence for this buffering interaction in the demand-control literature is weak overall, while additive effects of demands and control are consistent; buffering appears mainly when demands and control are matched on the same dimension (Häusser et al., 2010). The model therefore specifies authority over the resources relevant to the particular care need, which corresponds to this matching principle, and it allows for additive effects of authority as well. Authority also acts as a mediator. Income, secure employment, and institutional recognition confer authority over money, schedules, and dealings with services, so part of the association between social position and the psychological mechanisms is expected to run through authority. Finally, authority can act as an antecedent of vigilance under one condition, for which the available evidence is indirect. When authority over consequential decisions is concentrated in the same person who holds responsibility, decisionmaking may add demands of its own. Ciciolla and Luthar (2019) found that financial decisionmaking was associated with lower partner satisfaction, a finding consistent with this possibility, although it concerns relationship satisfaction and leaves vigilance unmeasured. Authority is therefore expected to buffer strain when it matches responsibility and may add to it when it concentrates consequential decisions on a person who already carries the coordinating role.

Proposition 4b.

When provisions transfer responsibility for identifying, responding to, and following up care needs, they are associated with reduced monitoring and difficulty detaching for the previous holder of responsibility. Individual leave taken alone, reliable services, and shared institutional contact are candidate arrangements; their effects depend on whether the transfer actually occurs.

Proposition 4c.

Flexibility provisions that increase a caregiver's availability without transferring responsibility can increase that caregiver's responsibility and reduce the opportunity to disengage, particularly when availability makes the caregiver the default contact for schools and services.

Proposition 4d. Schedule control and dependable cover for paid duties reduce competing work demands and widen caregivers' authority over their working time. A further reduction in personal monitoring of care is expected when workplace support also enables a dependable person or service to assume the relevant care responsibility.

Proposition 5a. Gendered expectations of competence and accountability allocate responsibility for monitoring to women more than authority over resources, widening the gap between responsibility and authority (Ciciolla & Luthar, 2019; Daminger, 2019; Weeks et al., 2025).

Proposition 5b. Higher income and secure employment expand authority and the opportunity to disengage through purchased services, schedule control, and the capacity to use paid and unpaid entitlements, while low income and precarious employment restrict them; part of the association between socioeconomic condition and the psychological mechanisms is therefore expected to run through authority (Chieregato, 2020; Chung, 2022).

Proposition 5c. Lone caregivers and caregivers of dependents with complex or continuing needs have fewer potential successors and face more open-ended responsibility, which lowers their opportunity to disengage.

Proposition 5d.

When institutions do not recognize a caregiver's relationship or family form, the caregiver holds less authority in dealings with those institutions and assumes added responsibility for securing recognition, a demand that minority stress research links to the psychological work of navigating unsupportive environments (Frost & Meyer, 2023; Meyer, 2003; Rivas-Koehl et al., 2023).

The propositions hold within boundary conditions. Authority can bring demanding decisions, and close monitoring can reflect a chosen commitment. Shared responsibility generates coordination costs, and added services can generate administrative work. Continuing involvement in care can be rewarding under supportive conditions. Evidence should therefore examine how obligations are negotiated and experienced, and should avoid inferring strain from the presence of care or ease from the language of choice.

Theoretical Contribution and Empirical Tests The Contribution to Existing Accounts

Job demands-resources theory, the work-home resources model, and conservation of resources theory can accommodate cognitive demands, social support, control, and unequal access to resources (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; Hobfoll, 1989; ten Brummelhuis & Bakker, 2012). They do not require strain to fall whenever physical task time falls, because responsibility can remain a demand. Likewise, recovery theory does not guarantee detachment merely because time away from paid work is available (Sonnentag & Fritz, 2015). The contribution proposed here is therefore one of specification: identifying when an intervention changes task execution, the distribution of answerability, or the opportunity to cease monitoring, and how those changes are connected.

Research on cognitive labor has already established its invisibility, persistence, and unequal allocation (Daminger, 2019; Dean et al., 2022). Wayne et al. (2023) further distinguish managerial, cognitive, and emotional dimensions and show that their implications need not be uniformly negative. The present model builds on these contributions by making the transfer of responsibility a focal object of explanation. It asks whether one caregiver can reduce monitoring because another actor assumes identification, follow-up, and contingencies, and which institutional arrangements make that handover credible. The novelty claim concerns this proposed mechanism and its evaluation in policy, rather than the discovery that care extends beyond visible tasks.

Mechanisms Specified by the Model

Three linked mechanisms organize the account. First, continued answerability may maintain monitoring after task delegation. Second, credible succession may reduce monitoring by giving the caregiver grounds to expect that needs and contingencies will be addressed. Third, authority over relevant resources may help convert formal responsibility into an actionable arrangement.

Succession is narrower than general support: a supportive person may provide comfort or practical help while leaving the original caregiver answerable for every outcome. It is also distinguishable from trust alone, because confidence without sufficient capacity or authority may fail when a need changes. Whether these distinctions add explanatory value must be tested against established measures and plausible alternatives.

Evidence That Would Challenge the Model

The central mechanism would be weakened if reliable measures of answerability and succession were indistinguishable from existing constructs and added no explanatory value. It would also be challenged if adequately powered longitudinal or experimental studies showed comparable reductions in monitoring after task-only redistribution and after a credible transfer of responsibility, while accounting for care needs and relevant resources. Differences between leave taken alone and concurrently are informative only if the arrangements differ in responsibility transfer; leave configuration alone is an imperfect proxy. A null result for a conditional pathway, such as the proposed cost of concentrated decision authority, would challenge that pathway rather than automatically refute the whole model. Conversely, monitoring that persists after a well-supported transfer would require examination of worry, mistrust, care preferences, and previous service failures. Tests should specify meaningful effect sizes and measurement assumptions in advance; statistical non-significance alone does not establish equivalence.

Time-Based Instruments and the Transfer of Responsibility

The policy implications of the model follow from Propositions 4a to 4c. European work-life balance policy combines labor market objectives with commitments to gender equality, and its provisions set minimum entitlements to time and to requests for flexibility. The analysis addresses sufficiency, a question distinct from what the instrument presupposes: predominantly temporal instruments may be insufficient, on their own, to transfer responsibility, and the three propositions specify the conditions under which they contribute to such a transfer. In the terms of Greenhaus and Beutell (1985), time-based provisions address time-based conflict, and their reach into strain-based interference depends on whether a grant of time is accompanied by a transfer of monitoring, by authority over the resources that meet care needs, or by a dependable successor.

Evidence on fathers' leave illustrates both outcomes. Pizarro and Gartzia's (2024) systematic review identifies associations between leave-taking and later involvement in childcare and household work, together with wide variation in the conditions surrounding uptake and outcomes. Cross-national research on fathers who take leave alone, while the mother has returned to paid work, indicates that sole charge of care during leave can support fathers' assumption of responsibility (O'Brien & Wall, 2017). In the model's terms, leave taken alone is a route to a dependable successor for the other caregiver, and leave taken concurrently can leave the existing allocation of monitoring unchanged.

Whether an entitlement transfers responsibility depends on who can use it and how.

Chieregato (2020) identifies limits to the inclusiveness of the European framework. Chung (2022) shows how flexible working can reproduce gendered inequalities even when it increases discretion. Flexibility may increase boundary permeability and make a caregiver the default contact, but its effects depend on work demands, household practices, and institutional routines (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000). The model therefore treats flexibility as an arrangement with conditional effects, rather than assuming either benefit or harm. Cover for paid duties and succession in care should be measured separately when evaluating workplace support.

Outside the workplace, administrative routines can keep responsibility in place. A school or health service may record one person as the default contact and direct information, reminders, and follow-up to that person. A grant of leave leaves that standing in place, and the burden of being the permanent point of contact eases when institutional routines distribute information and responsibility to more than one responsible person.

Implications for Research and Practice

Research

Testing begins with measurement. Wayne et al. (2023) provide an established measure of managerial, cognitive, and emotional family load against which the proposed indicators should be evaluated. Measures need to separate task performance from answerability, identify authority over relevant resources, and record whether another actor assumes follow-up and contingencies for a specified need and period. General social support, perceived reliability, control, boundary management, and care-related worry are essential comparators. Psychological outcomes can draw on adapted measures of perseverative cognition and detachment. The opportunity to disengage should be assessed through the arrangement itself, so it is not defined by the reduction in monitoring that it is intended to explain. Dyadic or service records could supplement individual perceptions.

With measures in place, each proposition calls for a design matched to its form. Daily diary designs with multilevel analysis suit Propositions 2a and 3b, since moderation can be examined within persons across days that differ in authority and in the availability of a successor. Dyadic designs suit Propositions 1, 2a, and 2b, since responsibility and authority can be held by different members of a household. Mediation designs with repeated measures suit Propositions 3c and 5b.

Multilevel designs with employees nested in work units suit Proposition 4d, since schedule control and supervisor support vary across supervisors and teams. Longitudinal and quasi-experimental designs around leave uptake, leave taken alone or concurrently, changes in working arrangements, and changes in service procedures suit Propositions 4a to 4c, because they allow task time and the three relations to be tracked separately before and after a change. Comparisons across gender, socioeconomic condition, family composition, and recognition status suit Propositions 5a to 5d. Designs should attend to prior distress and changing care needs, which shape how work is organized and how its burden is reported.

The positional propositions also call for an intersectional research agenda that involves caregivers in deciding what to measure and how to interpret findings. Recruitment should encompass varied household arrangements and relationships between paid and unpaid care, with attention to who is routinely excluded by language, time, or access requirements. Participatory work could identify administrative or relational labor that researchers would otherwise miss, and compensating participants keeps knowledge about invisible labor from being produced through another unrecognized demand.

Policy and Practice

For policy, the model implies evaluation criteria for work-life balance measures that extend beyond hours and uptake. A measure can be assessed by whether it transfers monitoring along with time, whether it widens the authority of the person who holds responsibility, and whether it provides a dependable successor for the needs that caregivers monitor. The indicators in Table 3 translate these criteria into observable quantities. Dependable public provision, accessible information, predictable employment arrangements, and workable leave entitlements are conditions that may reduce the unequal concentration of responsibility, and each requires evaluation, since added provision and coordination can themselves generate administrative work.

In service design, procedures that allow households to name more than one responsible contact, distribute information directly, and obtain coordination support address the relation that leave entitlements leave untouched. Such procedures must accommodate lone caregivers, chosen or extended family networks, and people who cannot safely share access with another adult, so joint access is best offered as an option for households that want it.

Workplaces can combine predictable schedules, manageable workloads, and familysupportive supervision with explicit cover for paid duties (Hammer et al., 2009). Where employers support access to care services, their continuity and ability to manage contingencies should also be evaluated. An employee's ability to hand over a work task is different from the ability to stop monitoring a family need, and neither should be inferred from a generic flexibility entitlement. Psychologists and organizations can combine individual support with attention to these material and relational arrangements. Boundary-setting advice may have limited effects where disengagement carries an unresolved obligation to respond, while reliable succession may still require support for trust, communication, and adjustment.

Conclusion

Redistributing the time spent on care tasks need not redistribute responsibility for ensuring that care happens. The model specifies how answerability, authority, and dependable succession may connect care arrangements to persistent monitoring, vigilance, and difficulty detaching. It builds on existing theories of demands, resources, boundaries, and cognitive labor by identifying the transfer of responsibility as a mechanism to measure and test. For policy and practice, the implication is to evaluate who anticipates needs, who follows them through, and who can temporarily disengage, alongside hours and leave uptake. A grant of time may support recovery more effectively when households, workplaces, and services also make a credible transfer of responsibility possible.
