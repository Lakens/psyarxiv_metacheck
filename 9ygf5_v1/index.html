How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Sustained effort and perseverance are essential for successful language learning, particularly in contexts where learners face linguistically demanding target languages such as Finnish, taught in L2 English. This study examined the role of linguistic background, language exposure, and emotional factors in explaining variation in L2 grit among 82 early-stage learners of Finnish as a foreign language. Participants completed an online survey assessing linguistic background, exposure to Finnish, language learning enjoyment, anxiety, and L2 grit. Hierarchical linear regression analyses showed that greater linguistic distance to English was consistently negatively associated with lower Finnish L2 grit, while linguistic distance to Finnish became a negative predictor after controlling for exposure. The number of previously learned languages was positively associated with L2 grit. Among exposure-related variables, Finnish media use, but not time spent in Finland, was associated with Finnish L2 grit. Language learning enjoyment emerged as a strong positive predictor, whereas anxiety was not. The findings suggest that perseverance in early-stage Finnish learning is shaped not only by the target language itself but also by multilingual experience, instructional language accessibility, and positive emotional engagement.

Introduction

Proficiency in the majority language is a key factor in the social and economic integration of international students and migrants (Capstick, 2021; Esser, 2006). In the Finnish context, knowledge of Finnish is often essential for accessing the labour market, participating in society, and establishing long-term residence (OECD, 2025; Ryökkynen et al., 2023). However, acquiring functional proficiency in Finnish is often challenging, and many learners struggle to make sufficient progress in the early stages of learning, which may limit their ability to participate fully in educational, professional, and social domains (OECD, 2018) (Mustonen et al., 2023; Ryökkynen et al., 2023). This highlights the importance of understanding not only how well learners perform but also whether they persist in learning over time. In this regard, learners' perseverance and determination in learning Finnish, as measured by L2 grit, may play a central role in their success.

How language learners think, feel, and behave in learning environments, and how these processes influence language development, has long been a central concern in second language acquisition (SLA) research (Credé et al., 2017; Dewaele et al., 2025; Dewaele & MacIntyre, 2014; Teimouri et al., 2022). One construct that captures these dynamics is L2 grit, defined as the combination of perseverance and sustained passion for long-term learning goals. Originally introduced by Duckworth et al. (2007), grit refers to the tendency to maintain effort and interest over extended periods despite challenges, setbacks, and slow progress. Research in educational psychology has consistently shown that grit contributes to academic success beyond innate ability, predicting achievement outcomes such as test performance and course grades (Duckworth et al., 2007; Duckworth & Quinn, 2009).

In SLA, L2 grit captures learners' sustained effort and interest in pursuing long-term language learning goals (Teimouri et al., 2022). Higher L2 grit was associated with greater investment of effort in language learning, indicating that learners who persist and maintain long-term interest are more likely to invest effort in language learning (Pawlak et al., 2024). In contrast to shortterm motivation, L2 grit reflects learners' capacity to persist in the face of difficulty, which is particularly relevant in demanding language learning contexts. Learning Finnish as a foreign language may represent such a context, particularly when English serves as the language of instruction and may itself be an additional language for many learners. Finnish is often perceived as challenging due to its typological characteristics, including agglutinative morphology, complex inflectional systems, and its distance from Indo-European languages (Karlsson, 2006).

For many learners, acquiring Finnish requires sustained long-term effort, making perseverance a key factor in successful language development. Despite its clear relevance, however, relatively little is known about what shapes L2 grit in multilingual learning contexts, particularly during the early stages of foreign language learning.

L2 grit and affective factors of language learning

L2 grit, foreign language enjoyment (FLE), and foreign language anxiety (FLA) interact and jointly influence learners' experiences in second language learning. These variables do not operate independently but form a complex, interrelated system of individual differences that shape learners' engagement (Pawlak et al., 2024). More recent research has also demonstrated that L2 grit is positively associated with psychological resources, including self-efficacy, optimism, hope, and resilience, as well as mindfulness (Pawlak et al., 2025). Among the affective variables impacting language learning, FLE and FLA have recently received particular attention.

Meta-analyses of FLE (Botes et al., 2022) and FLA (Teimouri et al., 2019) have shown that positive learner emotions are linked to better performance or self-reported academic achievement, while negative emotions have the opposite effect. In comparing enjoyment and anxiety, it was found that FLE is a stronger predictor of long-term language development than FLA, which tends to affect performance more situationally (Dewaele et al., 2025).

Moderate positive correlations were found between FLE and willingness to communicate, academic achievement, and self-perceived achievement (Botes et al., 2022). FLE has been linked to positive classroom experiences and greater engagement in language learning and has been positively associated with both dimensions of L2 grit, perseverance of effort and consistency of interest (Dewaele & MacIntyre, 2014; Pawlak et al., 2024). Furthermore, Pawlak et al. (2024) found that enjoyment was positively associated with motivated learning behaviour, both directly and indirectly through perseverance of effort and consistency of interest, highlighting the potential role of positive emotions in sustaining learners' engagement with language learning. FLA has been consistently identified as a negative predictor of language learning success, influencing performance, participation, and self-confidence (Dewaele et al., 2018; Dewaele & MacIntyre, 2014; Teimouri et al., 2019). FLA has also been negatively associated with the consistency-of-interest dimension of L2 grit and with self-perceived competence, although its relationship with perseverance of effort was not significant (Pawlak et al., 2024).

These two emotional dimensions are not simply opposites but can co-occur, reflecting the complex emotional landscape of language learning (Dewaele et al., 2018; Dewaele & MacIntyre, 2014). From this perspective, FLE and FLA can be understood as proximal factors that shape learners' persistence in language learning. Recent findings further indicate that L2 grit may mediate associations between psychological resources and language achievement (Pawlak et al., 2025). However, while the relationships between emotions and grit have been explored in second language contexts, less is known about how these dynamics unfold in multilingual settings where learners acquire an additional language under more complex conditions.

L2 grit and linguistic background

In addition to emotional factors, learners' linguistic backgrounds may also shape L2 grit in foreign language learning, particularly in contexts where learners navigate both the target language and a language of instruction as L2s.

In multilingual settings, learners can draw on previously acquired languages when learning an additional language (Cenoz & Gorter, 2022). Non-native speakers of English studying an additional foreign language in an English-medium university perceived their previous languagelearning experience as an advantage, suggesting that multilingual experience may provide resources that facilitate the learning of further languages (Bruen & Kelly, 2017).

Furthermore, the relationship between previously acquired languages and the target language can influence language learning processes (Schepens et al., 2016).

A substantial body of research has operationalized linguistic background through measures of linguistic distance (LD), which quantify similarities and differences between languages based on their linguistic features. Research has shown that greater LD is generally associated with greater difficulty in acquiring an additional language (Schepens et al., 2016; Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026). For example, Schepens, van der Slik, and van Hout (2016) showed that both L1-L3 and L2-L3 LD predict success in learning Dutch, with greater distance increasing learning difficulty.

While this research focuses on linguistic outcomes, it suggests that LD may also affect learners' subjective experiences.

Empirical evidence linking LD to emotional variables remains limited. However, Tao et al.

(2024) found higher levels of classroom anxiety among learners whose L1 was more distant from the target language, suggesting that LD may influence emotional experiences through increased difficulty and reduced perceived competence. In contexts where a second language such as English serves as the language of instruction, LD to both the instructional and the target language may therefore shape learners' anxiety, enjoyment, and ultimately their L2 grit. Despite this, the relationship between LD to the instructional language and to the learned language, as well as its association with L2 grit, remains a research gap.

English as a language of instruction

In many higher-education contexts, English is used as a language of instruction. When learners study an additional language through English, and English itself is an L2, this creates a distinct multilingual learning situation in which learners engage with the target language through another additional language. This differs from both conventional foreign language instruction and conventional English-medium instruction (EMI), where English is typically used to teach academic content rather than another language. In the Finnish university context, learning Finnish may also be linked to expectations of linguistic integration and participation in the academic and wider social environment, particularly for international doctoral researchers and university staff.

Research specifically examining learners' experiences of learning an additional language through L2 English remains limited. One of the few studies addressing this context investigated Chinese international students learning French as an L3, with L2S in both English and French (Lin, 2022). Learners reported moderate to high levels of foreign language anxiety, while some perceived similarities between English and French as facilitating their learning and reducing anxiety. These findings indicate that the instructional language itself may form part of learners' affective experience when acquiring an additional language.

Related evidence from conventional EMI contexts further demonstrates that studying through English can involve both positive and negative emotional experiences. For example, students learning academic content through English reported both FLE and FLA, with enjoyment associated with opportunities for authentic English use and interaction, and anxiety arising from linguistic difficulties such as challenging texts, listening comprehension, and speaking in English (Sun & Yang, 2025). Another study found that language learning anxiety, self-regulation and English proficiency skills affect EMI students' proficiency across various academic disciplines (Yuksel et al., 2023).

Taken together, the limited existing evidence suggests that learners' emotional experiences may be shaped not only by the target language itself but also by the language through which they learn. However, little is known about affective factors in contexts where an additional language is learned through L2 English, and the role of L2 grit in such multilingual instructional settings remains largely unexplored.

Language exposure

In addition to linguistic factors, language exposure has been shown to shape L2 grit. Research has consistently demonstrated that increased exposure to a target language, through residence in the target-language environment or study abroad, facilitates language development and enhances learners' engagement and motivation persistence (e.g. Sanz & Morales-Front, 2018).

Additionally, language exposure to the target language through television, streaming, and online media has been found to facilitate language learning and increase engagement in adult contexts (Dressman, 2020; Sockett, 2014; Sundqvist, 2024).

Although these studies primarily focus on linguistic outcomes and general motivation, they suggest that greater exposure may support sustained effort and engagement. As L2 grit reflects learners' perseverance and long-term commitment, exposure may indirectly contribute to grit by shaping emotional experiences and opportunities for language use. However, empirical evidence directly linking exposure to L2 grit remains limited.

Current study

In the current study, we examined the extent to which LD to the instructional language (English) and the target language (Finnish), exposure-related factors, and affective factors are associated with L2 grit in early-stage learners of Finnish as an additional foreign language, and addressed the three hypotheses: • H1: Greater linguistic distance to the instructional language (English) and the target language (Finnish) will be associated with lower levels of L2 grit in early-stage L3 learners, whereas a higher number of previously acquired languages will be positively associated with L2 grit.

• H2: Greater language exposure, operationalised as longer residence in Finland and higher levels of Finnish media use, will be positively associated with L2 grit.

• H3: Higher levels of language learning enjoyment will be positively associated with L2 grit, whereas higher levels of language learning anxiety will be negatively associated with L2 grit.

Methods

Participants

The participants were 82 adult early-stage learners of Finnish as an additional language, including international students and staff, enrolled in a beginner-level Finnish language course at a university in Finland. English served as the language of instruction. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 41 years (M = 25.77, SD = 5.61).

Participation in this study was voluntary. Participants were recruited through announcements made by course instructors during Finnish language classes, and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Finnish National Board on Research Integrity (TENK). Under the TENK guidelines, the study did not meet the criteria requiring prior ethical review and therefore did not require approval from an ethics committee.

Data Collection Procedure

Data were collected anonymously through an online survey during the first four weeks of the beginner-level Finnish language course. The survey was administered in English and took approximately 15 minutes to complete.

The survey collected demographic information, including age, gender, and educational background, linguistic backgrounds, and exposure-related variables, including time spent in Finland and engagement with Finnish-language media, FLE, FLA, and L2 grit. Educational attainment was coded according to the International Standard Classification of Education (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2011).

Instruments

The survey included standardised self-report measures assessing FLE, FLA, and L2 grit, adapted to the context of Finnish language learning (Appendix A).

FLE and FLA were measured using standardised FLE and FLA scales (Dewaele & MacIntyre, 2014). The original FLE survey was shortened from 21 items to twelve items and assessed learners' positive emotional experiences in the Finnish classroom (e.g., enjoyment, classroom atmosphere, and sense of accomplishment). To measure FLA, learners' feelings of tension, nervousness, and apprehension, all eight items from the original survey were used. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. L2 grit was measured using the L2 grit scale (Teimouri et al., 2022), consisting of nine items and captures perseverance and consistency of interest in Finnish language learning. Responses were recorded on a 5-point scale ranging from not like me at all to very much like me. Reverse-coded items were recoded prior to scale score computation.

Language exposure was assessed using two indicators: time spent in Finland, measured as the number of months from arrival to survey completion, and Finnish media use, assessed via four self-report items capturing informal daily exposure to Finnish (e.g., apps, videos, radio, and everyday interactions; Appendix B). Finnish media use was rated on a 5-point scale reflecting approximate daily engagement, ranging from never to more than three hours per day, with higher scores indicating greater engagement with Finnish outside formal instruction.

Linguistic Distance (LD) was measured using lexical distance measures derived from the Automated Similarity Judgment Program (ASJP; Wichmann et al., 2025). The ASJP method computes lexical distances between languages based on standardised comparisons of a 40-word Swadesh list containing culturally independent core vocabulary items (Swadesh, 1952). Distance scores are derived using the normalised Levenshtein distance (Levenshtein, 1966), which quantifies the number of insertions, deletions, or substitutions required to transform the phonetic representation of a word in one language into its equivalent in another. Higher LD values indicate greater linguistic dissimilarity with Finnish. Although based on a limited lexical sample, ASJP-based LD measures have been shown to reliably predict language learning outcomes and are widely used in multilingual and educational research (Berthelé et al., 2022; Jaekel et al., 2025; Schepens et al., 2016). In the present study, the lexical LDs (Appendix C) were calculated between participants' first language (L1) and English (LDtoEN) as the instructional and the target language, Finnish (LDtoFI).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (Posit Team, 2024; R Core Team, 2024) using the base stats package for linear regression analyses and the broom package for extracting model estimates and confidence intervals for reporting. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables prior to inferential analyses.

To examine the predictors of L2 grit, a series of hierarchical linear regression models were estimated using the lm() function (R Core Team, 2024). Hierarchical regression was used to assess the incremental contribution of theoretically distinct sets of predictors to the explained variance in L2 grit. Four models were specified. The base model included demographic control variables (age, gender, and educational level). Model 1 added linguistic predictors, including LDtoEN, and LDtoFI, and the number of languages spoken. Model 2 further included exposurerelated variables, operationalized as months spent in Finland and Finnish media use. Model 3 (full model) additionally incorporated FLE and FLA.

Model fit was evaluated using R² and adjusted R² to assess the proportion of variance explained by each model and the incremental contribution of additional predictor blocks. Regression coefficients, standard errors, and 95% confidence intervals were extracted for interpretation.

Results

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for participant characteristics, exposure variables, emotional measures, and LD indicators. The participants (N = 82) had a mean age of 25.77 years (SD = 5.61), with educational attainment corresponding on average to ISCED level 6 (SD = 0.71). Participants had spent an average of 6.08 months in Finland (SD = 15.00), although the wide range (0-85 months) suggests substantial variation in exposure to the Finnish language environment. Participants reported relatively high levels of FLE (M = 3.98, SD = 0.60) and moderate L2 grit (M = 3.54, SD = 0.52), whereas FLA was comparatively lower (M = 2.77, SD = 0.96). Finnish media use was moderate overall (M = 7.33, SD = 2.37). Regarding linguistic background, participants generally spoke more than two languages. LDtoFI was consistently high (M = 99.39, SD = 2.75), whereas LDtoEN showed substantially greater variation (M = 81.76, SD = 32.24). The baseline model, including only demographic control variables (age, gender, and educational level), explained little variance in L2 grit (R² = .055, adjusted R² = .017), and the overall model was not statistically significant (Appendix D).

The results of the hierarchical linear regression models testing the three hypotheses are summarised in Table 2. Hypothesis one predicted that smaller LD to English and Finnish, and a greater number of spoken languages, would be associated with higher levels of L2 grit. Model 1 shows that hypothesis 1 was partly confirmed. Higher LDtoEN (b = -0.005, SE = 0.002, 95% CI [-0.010, -0.000]) and a lower number of languages (b = 0.304, SE = 0.082, 95% CI [0.140, 0.469]) were associated with lower L2 grit, whereas LDtoFI was not a significant predictor.

Although the regression coefficient for LDtoEN is numerically small, the effect becomes more meaningful when considered across the observed variation in the sample. Each 1-point increase in LDtoEN was associated with a 0.005-point decrease in L2 grit. For example, a native English speaker and a Mandarin speaker, whose languages differ by approximately 102 points in lexical distance to English, would be expected to differ by approximately 0.51 points in L2 grit, with the Mandarin speaker showing the lower predicted grit score. Given the 1-5 response scale, this indicates that differences in LD can correspond to meaningful differences in predicted L2 grit.

The positive effect of multilingual experience was substantial: each additional language spoken was associated with an increase of approximately 0.30 points in L2 grit, meaning that a learner speaking four languages would be expected to score about 0.61 points higher in grit than a learner speaking two languages. The explained variance was 24.0% for L2 grit (adjusted R² = .178), including predictors and control variables. Among the control variables, neither gender nor educational level emerged as a predictor in the baseline model (Appendix D), but both became significant after the inclusion of linguistic background variables in Model 1.

Specifically, educational level (b = 0.236, SE = 0.098, 95% CI [0.041, 0.432]) and gender were positively associated with L2 grit (b = 0.307, SE = 0.107, 95% CI [0.094, 0.520]), with male learners reporting higher grit, whereas age remained non-significant across models.

Model 2 tested the associations between exposure and L2 grit and partly confirms the second hypothesis. Finnish media use was positively associated with L2 grit (b = 0.054, SE = 0.023, 95% CI [0.007, 0.100]), whereas time spent in Finland was not.

Each additional point on the Finnish media use scale was associated with an increase of approximately 0.05 points in L2 grit, meaning that a learner scoring 10 points higher on Finnish media use would be expected to score about 0.54 points higher in grit. The inclusion of exposure-related variables did not substantially alter the association between LDtoEN and L2 grit. In contrast, LDtoFI, which was not significant in the linguistic model, emerged as a significant negative predictor after exposure variables were added (b = -0.068, SE = 0.029, 95% CI [-0.125, -0.011]). Adding exposure-related variables in Model 2 increased the explained variance in L2 grit from 24.0% to 31.0% (adjusted R² = .231).

In Model 3, FLE and FLA were added to test hypothesis three. FLE was positively associated with L2 grit (b = 0.325, SE = 0.100, 95% CI [0.126, 0.524]), whereas FLA was not a predictor.

Each one-point increase in FLE corresponded to an estimated 0.33-point increase in L2 grit. The effects of LDtoEN and the number of languages remained similar, while the associations with LDtoFI and Finnish media use were no longer significant. In the full model, including affective factors explained 40.8% of the variance in L2 grit (adjusted R² = .321).

Discussion

The present study examined the associations among learners' linguistic backgrounds, language exposure, emotional experiences, and L2 grit in a university-level Finnish as a foreign language context. Overall, the findings suggest that L2 grit is associated with a combination of linguistic and affective factors, although these associations vary across different aspects of learners' backgrounds and experiences. In particular, the results point to the relevance of prior languagelearning experience, linguistic distance to the language of instruction, and positive emotional experiences, while suggesting that mere exposure to the target-language environment and language anxiety may play a less prominent role.

Linguistic background and L2 grit

To explore individual differences in L2 grit in Finnish learners, we first addressed participants' linguistic background. We hypothesised that greater linguistic distance to both English and Finnish would be associated with lower L2 grit, whereas experience with more languages would be associated with higher grit. This hypothesis was partly supported. As expected, linguistic background variables contributed to explaining individual differences in L2 grit, with the linguistic model accounting for a meaningful proportion of variance in learners' perseverance and long-term commitment toward learning Finnish. However, the effects of LD differed depending on whether the distance concerned the instructional language (English) or the target language (Finnish), and these relationships changed across models.

A particularly robust finding was the negative association between LD to English and L2 grit.

This effect remained relatively stable across models, even after accounting for exposure-related and emotional variables, suggesting that the accessibility of the instructional language plays a consistent role in shaping learners' perseverance. Although the unit-level coefficient was small, the association was meaningful across larger differences in LD. For example, the approximately 102-point difference in LDtoEN between a native English speaker and a Mandarin speaker corresponds to an estimated 0.51-point difference in L2 grit across the models, with greater LD associated with lower grit. This finding extends previous research on English-medium instruction, which has shown that studying through a non-native instructional language can create additional cognitive and emotional demands and shape learners' classroom experiences (Sun & Yang, 2025), particularly when proficiency in the instructional language is limited (Yuksel et al., 2023). In the present context, learners with a greater LD to English may have faced a double challenge, navigating both the instructional language and the target language simultaneously. These findings suggest that persistence in learning an additional language may depend not only on the difficulty of the target language itself, but also on learners' access to the language through which learning is mediated.

The pattern for LDtoFI was less straightforward. In the initial linguistic model, lexical distance to Finnish was not significantly associated with L2 grit, but it emerged as a significant negative predictor once exposure-related variables were included. One possible explanation is that the relationship between target-language distance and perseverance becomes more apparent when differences in learners' opportunities for engagement are considered. At the same time, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as the present study relied on lexical distance only. LD is a multidimensional construct, and different operationalisations, including lexical, phonological, typological, and morphosyntactic measures, may capture distinct aspects of crosslinguistic similarity and differ in their explanatory power depending on the learning outcome (Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026). In the case of Finnish, lexical similarity may capture only part of the challenge, as learners often encounter difficulties stemming from morphosyntactic complexity rather than from vocabulary overlap alone. Given the relatively restricted range of LDtoFI values in the present sample, future research should examine whether broader LD measures yield similar patterns.

The positive association between the number of languages spoken and L2 grit was particularly notable. Each additional language spoken was associated with an increase of approximately 0.30 points in L2 grit, meaning that a learner speaking four languages would be expected to score 0.61 points higher on the five-point L2 grit scale than a learner speaking two languages. Given the observed variability in grit scores, this represents a substantial effect and suggests that the more languages a learner speaks, the greater their L2 grit in learning Finnish. These findings align well with previous research, which suggests that learners with broader multilingual repertoires have accumulated prior experiences of successful language learning, which may strengthen confidence, metalinguistic awareness, strategic flexibility, and persistence when approaching an additional language (Bruen & Kelly, 2017; Cenoz & Gorter, 2022; Schepens et al., 2016).

Language exposure and L2 grit

In the next step, the effect of language exposure was examined. The second hypothesis predicted that greater language exposure, reflected in both longer residence in Finland and more frequent Finnish media use, would be associated with higher L2 grit. The results showed that only Finnish media use was positively associated with L2 grit, whereas length of residence in Finland was not.

Finnish media use contributed additionally to explaining variation in L2 grit beyond linguistic background factors, improving the explained variance from 24% to 31%. The positive association of Finnish media use and L2 grit suggests that informal engagement with the target language supports learners' perseverance and long-term commitment. The effect was meaningful in practical terms, and learners with substantially greater engagement with Finnish-language media showed noticeably higher levels of perseverance. This finding aligns with previous research showing that informal language exposure through media, including television, online content, and digital interaction, can support language learning, vocabulary development, and learner motivation (Dressman, 2020; Peters & Webb, 2018; Sundqvist, 2024). While these studies have focused primarily on linguistic development rather than grit specifically, the present findings suggest that regular voluntary engagement with the target language may also positively affect L2 grit. One possible explanation is that media-based exposure creates low-pressure opportunities for meaningful contact with Finnish, increasing familiarity and reducing the psychological distance to the language.

In contrast, time spent in Finland was not significantly associated with L2 grit. This suggests that mere physical presence in the target-language environment may not be sufficient to strengthen perseverance in language learning, even for those participants who have spent years in the country. Simply residing in Finland does not necessarily guarantee meaningful interaction with Finnish, particularly in university and international contexts where English is widely used for communication, study, social life, and daily functioning. In such environments, learners may have extensive residence in the country while experiencing relatively limited authentic engagement with Finnish itself. This distinction between passive exposure and active engagement may help explain why Finnish media use, but not time in Finland, was associated with L2 grit. The lack of an association between length of residence and L2 grit may also reflect differences in learners' opportunities to engage with Finnish during their time in Finland.

Participants had different institutional roles and responsibilities: while, for instance, exchange students may receive study credits for Finnish language courses, university staff and researchers must balance language learning with research, teaching, and administrative responsibilities.

Opportunities to devote work time to language learning may also vary across professional contexts. Thus, a longer period of residence in Finland did not necessarily correspond to greater opportunities to invest time in learning Finnish.

Taken together, these findings suggest that not all forms of exposure contribute equally to sustained motivation in language learning. What appears to matter is not simply access to the target-language environment, but the extent to which learners actively engage with the language in ways that create meaningful and self-directed learning opportunities.

Emotional factors and L2 grit

Turning to emotional factors, we expected FLE and FLA to show contrasting associations with L2 grit: higher FLE was hypothesised to be associated with higher L2 grit, whereas higher FLA was expected to be associated with lower L2 grit. This hypothesis was only partly supported. As expected, emotional factors contributed substantially to explaining L2 grit, with the inclusion of enjoyment and anxiety increasing the explained variance from 31% to over 40%. However, only FLE was associated with L2 grit, whereas FLA was not.

As expected, FLE showed a strong positive association with L2 grit, suggesting that learners who experience greater positive engagement in the language learning process are also more likely to demonstrate perseverance and long-term commitment. The effect was substantial: each one-point increase in FLE was associated with an estimated 0.33-point increase in L2 grit, indicating that emotional engagement may be a central factor in sustaining motivation during early-stage Finnish learning. The positive association between FLE and L2 grit may partly reflect shared features of the two constructs, as both involve positive engagement with language learning. Nevertheless, they capture conceptually distinct aspects of the learning experience: FLE represents a positive emotional response to language learning, whereas L2 grit concerns sustained perseverance and interest in pursuing long-term language-learning goals. The significant association between the two therefore suggests that learners who experience greater enjoyment in learning Finnish also tend to report greater persistence and sustained interest.

This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating positive associations between foreign language enjoyment and L2 grit (e.g., Pawlak et al., 2024; Teimouri et al., 2019). FLE may foster persistence by increasing learners' willingness to invest effort, remain engaged despite setbacks, and perceive the learning process as rewarding rather than burdensome.

By contrast, FLA was not associated with L2 grit. Rather than contradicting previous research, this pattern may reflect the different roles that positive and negative emotions play in language learning, particularly in the initial stage. While anxiety has been consistently linked to reduced performance and situational classroom difficulties (Teimouri et al., 2019), enjoyment appears to be more strongly associated with sustained engagement and longer-term developmental outcomes (Botes et al., 2022; Dewaele et al., 2025). Because L2 grit reflects a relatively stable orientation toward perseverance and commitment rather than immediate performance or momentary classroom reactions (Teimouri et al., 2022), positive emotional engagement may be more relevant than anxiety in explaining individual differences in grit.

Taken together, these findings suggest that positive emotional experiences may be more important for sustaining L2 grit than the mere absence of anxiety. In the context of early-stage Finnish learning, fostering enjoyment may therefore be particularly relevant for supporting learners' long-term commitment to continued language study.

Conclusion

This study contributes to research on L2 grit by showing that perseverance in early-stage Finnish language learning is shaped not only by individual engagement but also by learners' broader linguistic and learning environment. Access to the instructional language, prior multilingual experience, meaningful opportunities to engage with Finnish, and positive emotional experiences appear to create conditions that support sustained commitment.

These findings have practical implications for language teaching in multilingual highereducation settings. Instructors should pay attention to the fact that learners may face challenges not only with the target language but also with the instructional language. Providing linguistically accessible support and scaffolding may help reduce barriers to persistence.

Encouraging self-directed engagement with Finnish beyond the classroom, such as through media, digital resources, everyday interaction, or awareness raising may further strengthen learners' long-term commitment. The strong role of enjoyment also underscores the importance of fostering positive, engaging classroom environments. Future longitudinal research should examine how linguistic and emotional factors shape L2 grit over time as learners progress beyond the earliest stages of acquisition.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the current study.

First, the relatively small sample and recruitment from a single beginner university-level Finnish course limit the generalizability of the findings to other learner populations and instructional contexts. Second, the cross-sectional design does not allow conclusions about the direction of the observed associations. In particular, while enjoyment was associated with greater L2 grit, greater perseverance and sustained interest may also contribute to more positive language-learning experiences. Third, L2 grit, emotional experiences, and language exposure were assessed through self-report, which may introduce shared-method and response biases. Fourth, LD was operationalised as lexical distance based on ASJP; consequently, the findings cannot be generalised to other dimensions of LD, such as morphological or syntactic distance. Finally, exposure was represented by length of residence and Finnish media use, which provide only broad indicators of learners' actual quantity and quality of contact with Finnish. The universitybased sample also limits generalizability to learners from other educational and socioeconomic backgrounds, such as refugees and other migrant populations. Differences in socioeconomic circumstances and motivations for learning Finnish may also be relevant to L2 grit. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples, longitudinal designs, and more fine-grained measures of LD and language exposure would help establish the robustness of these findings.
