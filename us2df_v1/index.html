Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Living in poorer economic circumstances is routinely associated with poorer mental health throughout the life course. In adolescents, explanatory theories have almost exclusively focused on the role that economic circumstances have on parenting behaviors, and the resources families can provide for their children, which in turn influence adolescents' mental health outcomes. Through a systematic search of mediation studies and expert consultation we propose candidate causal mechanisms through which economic circumstances might impact adolescents' internalizing mental health outcomes such as anxiety and depression.

Most of the candidate causal mechanisms we identified have not been considered in adolescent-specific theories of health inequalities to date and many have not been empirically investigated within mediation studies, indicating clear limits in current awareness of the processes through which economic circumstances affect adolescents' mental health.

We propose a conceptual model that highlights how economic circumstances may shape environmental and material factors, which we theorize in turn influences an interconnected set of behavioral, biological, psychological and social mechanisms linked to mental health outcomes.

Symptoms of internalizing mental health problems, such as anxiety and depression, are typically more common in adolescents that live in poorer economic circumstances compared to wealthier peers (Reiss, 2013; Steare et al., 2024). Along with other health inequalities, this is widely considered unfair and avoidable (McCartney et al., 2013). Mental health problems in adolescence limit social and educational opportunities with knock-on effects for health, relationships and economic prosperity (Clayborne et al., 2019; Copeland et al., 2015; Fletcher, 2013). These effects accumulate, cluster and reinforce each other over time, meaning mental health inequalities in adolescence can persist throughout the course of life (Evans-Lacko et al., 2024). Understanding how economic inequalities in mental health develop can help inform actions to prevent them developing in the first place, benefiting individuals and societies.

How health inequalities develop in a population has been of significant interest and debate, with numerous theories and frameworks developed. Across different theories, two key conditions are regularly proposed. The first condition is social stratification, which leads to a hierarchy of social positions with differing levels of privilege and status (Diderichsen et al., 2001; Grusky, 2001; Krieger, 2001; Solar & Irwin, 2010). In other words, health inequalities are a product of structural inequality that exists in societies. In the context of economic circumstances, this can be interpreted as stratification within societies according to differing levels of personal income, wealth and other economic resources, which may confer multiple social advantages and privileges.

The second condition is that there are one or more causal mechanisms through which an individual's social position impacts their health (Diderichsen et al., 2001; Solar & Irwin, 2010). These mechanisms are commonly categorized into two types. The first type, differential exposure, refers to how people of different social positions differ to the extent that they are exposed to factors that are positive or detrimental to health. The second type, differential vulnerability, refers to differing susceptibility to the negative impact of determinants of ill health across social groups even if the determinants are distributed evenly. Accordingly, adolescents' economic circumstances can (a) predispose them to experience certain factors that are causes of ill or good health, and (b) influence the extent that these certain factors are beneficial or harmful for their health.

Two theories of economic inequalities in health have been developed specifically for children and adolescents: the Family Stress Model of Economic Hardship and the Family Investment Model (Conger & Donnellan, 2007; Duncan et al., 2017; Masarik & Conger, 2017). These theories focus on the second condition of health inequalities; causal mechanisms. The Family Stress Model outlines how economic hardship within a family might affect? adolescents' outcomes. These outcomes include positive adjustment, such as cognitive ability, social skills and academic achievement, as well as negative outcomes including internalizing or externalizing mental health problems (Masarik & Conger, 2017).

The experience of household economic pressures, such as unmet material needs and cutting back on expenses, is proposed to cause stress and emotional difficulties for adolescents' parents which negatively affects their relationship with one another. Conflict and hostility consequently increase between parents limiting their ability to provide consistently warm and nurturing parenting to their children. This in turn is theorized to lead to a greater risk of adverse outcomes for children.

The Family Investment Model proposes mechanisms linking household financial resources to children's and adolescents' outcomes through specific investments that parents make. According to the model, wealthier families can invest in more material and time resources that foster healthy adolescent development (Becker, 1991). Material resources include healthy and sufficient food, cognitively stimulating resources (e.g., schooling), housing within a safe neighborhood and access to health care (Duncan et al., 2017). The model also proposes that experiencing economic deprivation captures parents' attention and cognitive resources, meaning parents in poorer households potentially have less time and attention to dedicate towards child-rearing compared to parents with fewer economic stressors.

Whilst there are multiple theories of health inequalities, they might accurately explain how adolescents' economic circumstances affect their internalizing mental health for a number of reasons. First, many theories of health inequalities typically view health as a single concept and do not differentiate between physical or mental health. The causal mechanisms underlying health inequalities are likely to differ for specific health outcomes, therefore limiting the validity of these theories. For instance, health-related behaviors that vary across social positions, including diet, exercise and smoking, have historically been proposed as drivers of physical health inequalities due to their importance for physical morbidity and mortality (Department of Health and Social Security, 1980). These healthrelated behaviors have fewer clearly established pathways through which they affect mental health and there are likely to be less salient casual mechanisms that link social position to mental health than to physical health. This issue is even more apparent in adolescentspecific theories of health inequalities (the Family Stress Model and the Family Investment Model), which propose that health, educational and social outcomes share the same causal mechanisms (Conger & Donnellan, 2007; Duncan et al., 2017; Masarik & Conger, 2017).

Second, theories have primarily focused on health inequalities according to social position.

However, the structural determinants of health inequalities and their causal mechanisms might differ if inequalities are defined according to social position or economic circumstances (Braveman et al., 2005). Third, health inequality theories primarily focus on adults. The causal mechanisms that impact health in adulthood might also be apparent in adolescence, but specific mechanisms (e.g., family, peers, school) potentially more relevant to adolescents are not captured in these theories. The Family Stress Model and the Family Investment Model propose mechanisms that are likely to be relevant for adolescents, but these are narrow in scope and overwhelmingly focused on factors inside adolescents' immediate family or household. Theories of mental health inequalities need to consider contexts relevant to adolescents, given that 52% of anxiety disorders and 13% of depressive disorders have their onset by age 18 (Solmi et al., 2022). Consequently, existing theories disregard the likely complexity and multitude of causal mechanisms that might link adolescents' economic circumstances to their internalizing mental health, and their validity might not extend to settings that are contextually different from those in which the theories originated. Finally, most, if not all, theories have been developed by researchers from and based on observations in high-income countries in Europe and North America. The validity of health inequality theories might not generalize across countries that are contextually and culturally different to these given significant cross-country differences in wealth, living standards and health profiles (Patton et al., 2016). For instance, markers of social position differ between countries and cultures, and therefore the causal mechanisms linking social position to health might not be universal across settings (Diderichsen et al., 2001; Grusky, 2001). Therefore, theories primarily developed in European and North American countries might not translate to other settings, such as low-and middle-income countries where 90% of the world's adolescents live (Blum & Boyden, 2018).

This paper aims to address the lack of specific theory concerning causal mechanisms that link adolescents' economic circumstances to internalizing mental health outcomes. We achieve this through two complimentary approaches. First, we ran a systematic search to identify studies designed to investigate causal mechanisms of an association, known as mediation studies. This enabled us to identify factors that have been hypothesized as potential mechanisms linking economic circumstances to adolescents' internalizing mental health. A previous systematic review, identified family-, psychologicaland social-level factors as potential mechanisms through which adolescents' socioeconomic status might affect different psychosocial outcomes, but the review included potential mechanisms related to a broad range of outcomes including behavioral, educational and mental health problems, and hence not all may be relevant to internalizing mental health problems (Devenish et al., 2017). More specific literature reviews have explored the potential mechanisms that link economic circumstances to physical and mental health outcomes in adults only, but (a) the proposed mechanisms might not apply to adolescents, and (b) the reviews do not consider salient factors that are specific to childhood, such as school or parenting behaviors of parents. Second, we consulted experts in mental health inequalities from a variety of academic disciplines who proposed further potential mechanisms that were not identified in our search.

Methods

We used a systematic search to identify mediation studies that met our inclusion criteria. We did not conduct aspects of systematic reviews such as risk of bias assessment or the use of a second reviewer, as our aim was to scope mechanisms that have been studied to date. The protocol and search strategy were pre-registered (https://osf.io/jhx4z).

Search Strategy

We searched six bibliographic databases: MEDLINE, Global Health, PsycINFO, EconLit, Child Development and Adolescent Studies and Web of Science (Science Citation Index Expanded, Social Sciences Citation Index). Studies were eligible from database inception to 26 th March 2024 (the date of the search). Search terms were related to adolescents, economic circumstances, internalizing mental health and mediation analyses.

Full search terms are provided in the supplementary material.

Following the searches, we uploaded the records into Endnote software and identified duplicates. We then uploaded titles and abstracts to Rayyan, an online screening tool (Ouzzani et al., 2016). TS screened all records at title and abstract-level and at full-text.

Our aim was to identify mediation studies that investigated candidate causal mechanisms that might link adolescents' economic circumstances to their internalizing mental health. We included studies where the outcome (internalizing mental health) was assessed between the ages of 10 and 21. The exposure (economic circumstances) and mediators could be measured at any age if they were measured before or at the same time as the outcome. We included observational and experimental studies, such as randomized trials. We excluded dissertations, conference abstracts and study protocols. We only included studies published in English. Full inclusion criteria are presented in the supplementary material.

Data Extraction

We extracted key study data using an Excel-based form. Extracted data included the age of participants, variables representing the exposure, mediator and outcome and how they were measured, study design, analytic approach and main results.

Creation of Mechanism Domains

We identified candidate causal mechanisms from the mediation studies and categorized them into different domains and sub-domains. Domains were chosen by TS and PP based on their conceptual coherence and distinctiveness. Some mechanisms span multiple domains, but all were categorized into singular domains for simplicity. We then used a non-systematic approach to search for studies (in Google Scholar and Web of Science) that provided evidence that specific variables were associated with both adolescents' economic circumstances and internalizing mental health, thus representing a candidate causal mechanism despite not being formally tested in a mediation analysis (Braveman et al., 2011). These candidate causal mechanisms were added to the existing list of candidate causal mechanisms found in the systematic search. The creation of the domains and the running of the non-systemic searches were iterative and took place alongside data extraction and expert consultation.

Expert Consultation

Following the initial creation of domains and categorization of the candidate causal mechanisms, we sought input from experts to identify candidate causal mechanisms yet to be investigated in mediation studies. First, the domains and mechanisms were presented to a group of six mental health researchers based at University College London and were revised according to their feedback. We then contacted, via email, 34 academic experts from a variety of disciplines from different world regions. We randomly selected half of these experts to contact first and asked them to identify any candidate causal mechanisms we had missed. Seven experts suggested further mechanisms. We then updated the domains and candidate causal mechanisms based on the expert input. We then emailed and sought feedback from the remaining experts three weeks later. Six experts suggested further mechanisms in the second stage.

Data Synthesis

We conducted a narrative synthesis to describe and summarize candidate causal mechanisms through which adolescents' economic circumstances might impact their internalizing mental health. To aid the synthesis, we categorized mediation studies according to the mediating variable investigated. Summaries were grouped according to six broad domains: social, psychological, material, biological, environmental and behavioral factors.

We present and include candidate causal mechanisms regardless of whether there is statistical evidence of mediation, as our aim is to map the range of plausible mechanisms rather than summarize the strength of existing evidence.

Results

We first present the results of the systematic search, summarizing key details of the included studies. We then summarize the results of mediation studies across different domains and sub-domains of candidate causal mechanisms. In our summaries, we prioritize the discussion of (a) high-quality evidence where available and (b) the wide variety of candidate causal mechanisms. For each domain, we present a list of candidate causal mechanisms that have been investigated in mediation studies to date or were proposed by experts as part of our consultation. Finally, we present a theoretical model of how adolescents' economic circumstances might impact their internalizing mental health.

Social Mechanisms

Family

Most studies investigated the potential mediating role of factors related to adolescents' family, particularly their parents (n=51). Nine studies investigated the Family Stress Model in full, including two that used longitudinal data (Landers-Potts et al., 2015).

One notable cross-country study investigated the Family Stress Model across 10 cultural groups (Zietz et al., 2022). There was support for the Family Stress Model, and therefore mediation through parenting practices in eight of the ten cultural groups. Many other studies tested single mechanisms within the Family Stress Model, but not the full model. These were namely parenting factors such as the level of authoritative practices, parents' disciplinary styles, or parent-child conflict or parental stress.

Peer relationships

Economic disadvantage can limit adolescents' opportunity to develop and maintain friendships, potentially by increasing financial and practical barriers to access social activities, or from stigma or low peer acceptance from peers due to being of lower social status (Attree, 2006; Bukowski et al., 2020). Adolescents living in poorer households are therefore more likely to experience social isolation, have fewer close friends and experience bullying (Gibb et al., 2016; Hjalmarsson & Mood, 2015; Olsson, 2007); all of which affect internalizing mental health (Alsarrani et al., 2022).

Five studies, all cross-sectional, investigated whether aspects regarding peer relations mediated the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health. Three found that living in poorer economic circumstances was associated with a greater likelihood of peer conflict or victimization, which in turn was associated with poorer internalizing mental health (Jiang, 2020; Lee et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). In two further studies, a single variable comprising social support from classmates, parents and friends mediated the association between economic circumstances and internalizing symptoms in Chinese adolescents (Li & Zhang, 2023; Zou et al., 2020).

School and education

Four studies investigated the mediating role of school-related factors, including one longitudinal study. In the longitudinal study, the association between living in a household under the poverty line and higher levels of internalizing symptoms was not mediated by adolescents' perceived satisfaction with their school (Keegan Eamon, 2002). Results from the three cross-sectional studies found that adolescents' feelings regarding the atmosphere of their school and their academic performance might mediate the association between their economic circumstances and internalizing mental health (Gu et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022).

Adverse childhood experiences and stressful life events

Adverse childhood experiences are extreme stressors, such as maltreatment, or family adversities, such as violence or substance abuse (Finkelhor et al., 2013). Five studies investigated whether maltreatment mediated the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health. Of which, three longitudinal studies found evidence of mediation in US adolescents. Adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances were more likely to experience maltreatment, which in turn was associated with higher levels of internalizing symptoms (Barnhart et al., 2022; Weissman et al., 2022; L. Zhang et al., 2024).

Four studies investigated factors classed as stressful life events -more transient and less severe experiences than adverse childhood experiences (Amone-P 'Olak et al., 2009; Bøe et al., 2018; Lengua et al., 2022; Tracy et al., 2008). In all studies, these were measured with a composite assessing multiple stressors, including those at the adolescent-(e.g., problems at school or family conflict), and household-level (e.g., as parental unemployment or a serious illness in the family). The single longitudinal study was carried out in the US during the COVID-19 pandemic. The association between lower household income and increased internalizing symptoms was partially mediated by a greater level of stressful life events experienced by poorer adolescents during lockdown restrictions (Lengua et al., 2022).

Expert consultation, categorization and summary of social mechanisms

We grouped social mechanisms into eight secondary domains, four of which were added following the expert consultation (Table 4). The first additional secondary domain refers to discrimination and stigma from others in the community or from institutions that adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances might experience (Inglis et al., 2023).

The second refers to adolescents' employment status. Adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances are more likely to leave education early to help financially support their family (Putnick & Bornstein, 2015). This work can be precarious or in dangerous conditions that harm their mental health (Benjet, 2010; Benjet et al., 2012). The third refers to the greater likelihood of contact with state systems and services, such as the criminal justice system, social care and welfare services in adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances.

Contact with these services might increase internal or external stigma (Lapham & Martinson, 2022). The final additional secondary domain, migration and mobility, concerns the greater risk of residential instability for adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances, as well as parental migration for employment, which can result in left-behind child status (Fellmeth et al., 2018; Ihrke & Faber, 2012; Kohlbeck et al., 2024).

Over 70% of mediation studies investigated social mechanisms. In most cases, these concerned factors linked to adolescents' parents or family, as also identified in the review by Devenish and colleagues (2017). This is most likely due to the influence of the Family Stress Model on research in this field (Masarik & Conger, 2017). Several studies, including those with robust longitudinal designs, found evidence that adolescents' parental mental health and parenting practices mediated the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health. However, the expert consultation highlighted a wide range of potential variables that (a) could be caused by adolescents' economic circumstances, and (b) affect adolescents internalizing mental health that have not been investigated in mediation studies to date. Family-level mechanisms are of clear importance, but further investigation of mechanisms relating to other social factors (such as those presented in Table 4) is warranted.

Psychological Mechanisms

Twenty-four studies investigated whether different psychological factors mediate the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health.

Autonomy

Autonomy is the perception of being self-governed or instead controlled by external forces or factors (Legault, 2016). Adolescents with high levels of autonomy can make their own decisions and pursue their goals, which facilitates healthy development and positive wellbeing (Ryan & Deci, 2000). In comparison to wealthier peers, adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances are less likely to believe their actions determine their own outcome (Lachman & Weaver, 1998). This lower sense of control is likely caused by having fewer material and social resources that facilitate control over their circumstances (Bosma et al., 2014). A reduced sense of control might lead to feeling trapped or frustrated leading to internalizing mental health problems. Two longitudinal studies found evidence that adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances had a reduced sense of control, which in turn was associated with future internalizing symptoms (Conger et al., 1999; Weinberg, Stevens, Peeters, Visser, Frankenhuis, et al., 2023).

Beliefs and attitudes

We found one study that focused on adolescents' beliefs and attitudes, specifically belief in a just world, as a potential mediator. Individuals that believe in a just world consider success and status to be a result of hard work, whilst those that do not believe in a just world see success and status as products of luck and the unfairness in society (Lerner, 1980).

Believing in a just world is likely to increase optimism and trust in others, which may improve adolescents' internalizing mental health (Furnham, 2003). Adolescents that live in poorer economic circumstances are less likely to believe in a just world, potentially due to first-hand experiences of adversity and injustice (Thomas & Mucherah, 2016).

A cross-sectional study found that adolescents living in wealthier subjective economic circumstances were more likely to believe in a just world, which in turn was associated with decreased internalizing symptoms. However, there was no evidence of mediation when an objective measure was used to assess economic circumstances (Weinberg, Stevens, Peeters, Visser, Tigchelaar, et al., 2023).

Personality and emotional development

Emotions can be intense and variable in adolescence, but the ability to regulate them improves over time (McLaughlin et al., 2015). Three studies found evidence that the ability to (a) recover from negative emotional experiences (emotional resilience) (Davidson, 2000), (b) recognize, comprehend and control emotions (emotional intelligence) (Cheung et al., 2018), and (c) manage and respond to different emotional experiences (emotional regulation) (Palacios-Barrios & Hanson, 2019) were greater in wealthier adolescents (Barch et al., 2020; M. Cao et al., 2021; Cheung et al., 2018). These abilities were associated with fewer internalizing symptoms.

Adolescence is also a critical period for the development of personality and sense of self. Several studies investigated self-esteem -the evaluation of your own worth or value, which is commonly lower in adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1991; Twenge & Campbell, 2002). There was evidence in two longitudinal studies that self-esteem partially mediated the association between poorer economic circumstances and increased internalizing symptoms, but there was no evidence in two other longitudinal studies (J. Cao et al., 2024; J. Kim, 2021; Weinberg, Stevens, Peeters, Visser, Frankenhuis, et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2023).

Differences in two types of coping strategies might mediate the association between economic circumstances and internalizing mental health. The first type, disengagement coping strategies, are actions and efforts, such as avoidance or inaction, that orient attention away from stressors or from thoughts and emotions that accompany stressors. The second type, engagement coping strategies, are efforts that purposely orient attention towards stressors through direct action, problem-solving or seeking support. In a longitudinal study, US adolescents who lived in poverty for longer used disengagement coping strategies more frequently, which in turn were associated with experiencing increased internalizing symptoms (P. Kim et al., 2016). There was no mediation through engagement coping strategies.

Several studies investigated optimism, a generalised feeling of confidence in positive outcomes in the future, which is strongly associated with the risk of mental health problems (Carver et al., 2010; Hitlin et al., 2015; Rincón Uribe et al., 2022). Adolescents that live in poor economic circumstances might have reduced optimism as they can face multiple financial and practical barriers to first overcome before achieving their future goals (Segerstrom, 2007). Adolescents' optimism did not mediate the association between their economic circumstances and internalizing mental health in the sole longitudinal study (Weinberg, Stevens, Peeters, Visser, Frankenhuis, et al., 2023), but there was support for mediation in two cross-sectional studies (Piko et al., 2013; Zou et al., 2020).

Whether adolescents are mindful, present and aware might mediate the association between economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health. Greater trait mindfulness might foster objective perceptions of moment-to-moment experiences and adaptive responses to negative situations or threats (Shapiro et al., 2006). In a longitudinal study of Chinese students, living in wealthier economic circumstances was associated with increased trait mindfulness which was associated with decreased internalizing symptoms one year later (Yuan et al., 2023).

Expert consultation, categorization and summary of psychological mechanisms

We categorized psychological mechanisms into four secondary domains (Table 5).

Only one secondary domain (cognition) was added based on experts' feedback. An example of a cognitive tertiary mechanism is bandwidth tax, which refers to the reduced enjoyment gained from activities by adolescents living in poor economic circumstances (Schofield & Venkataramani, 2021) . This might be because their attention and cognitive resources are focused on the material or economic stressors in their lives rather than on the activity itself (Schofield & Venkataramani, 2021).

Mediation studies that assessed psychological mechanisms primarily investigated adolescents' personality and emotional development. No mediation studies investigated aspects relating to adolescents' cognition. Over half of the studies analyzed cross-sectional data, which are at even further risk of bias due to the conceptual overlap and natural correlation between many of the psychological mechanisms investigated and the symptoms of internalizing mental health problems (e.g., self-esteem). Furthermore, many of the tertiary psychological mechanisms are conceptually similar to one-another and likely to be highly correlated. The evidence for mediation through these tertiary mechanisms could be driven by the higher-order secondary domain they stem from (e.g., personality and emotional development), and the individual tertiary mechanisms might have little relevance beyond being indicators of the secondary domain. This is highlighted by a longitudinal study, where the evidence of mediation for three psychological mechanisms was reduced when they were modelled together compared to when modelled separately, suggesting that the observed mediation of the individual mechanisms is partly a result of their shared variance from a higher-order domain (Weinberg, Stevens, Peeters, Visser, Frankenhuis, et al., 2023).

Material Resources

This domain consists of material resources that are found in adolescents' households or land and are typically provided by their caregivers. Eleven studies, six of which were longitudinal, investigated the mediating role of variables concerning material resources.

Access to Essential Consumables

Essential consumables are basic goods and resources essential for everyday life (e.g. food, water, clothing), that are needed to maintain good health. In one longitudinal study in the US, greater food insecurity in poorer households partially mediated the association between reduced household income and increased internalizing symptoms (J. H. Chen et al., 2024). In a trial in Uganda, an economic intervention increased adolescents' food security, but food security was not associated with adolescents' internalizing symptoms (Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2021). In a cross-sectional study in Hong Kong, reduced household income was associated with fewer material necessities owned by adolescents such as warm clothes and school uniforms, which was associated with increased internalizing symptoms (J.-K. Chen et al., 2021).

Material Assets

Material assets are substantial resources that make up a significant proportion of families' total wealth, such as housing. Households with larger incomes can afford assets that are greater in quantity and quality compared to less wealthy households.

In a trial of Ugandan adolescents (Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2021) receiving an economic intervention led to fewer internalizing symptoms, partially by increasing the quantity or quality of assets that were owned by the adolescents' family, such as livestock, transport and land.

In a cross-sectional study, poorer economic circumstances were associated with living in an unsuitable home environment, characterized by issues with cleanliness, structural safety, or over-crowding. which in turn was associated with increased internalizing symptoms (Martinez et al., 2015).

Cognitive Stimulation and Parental Involvement

In two longitudinal studies in the US, the level of cognitive stimulation in adolescents' homes partially mediated the association between reduced household income and increased internalizing symptoms (Weissman et al., 2022; L. Zhang & Han, 2021). Cognitive stimulation (measured according to the amount of cognitively stimulating materials or assessed in a composite alongside different material resources) was lower in poorer adolescents' households. Lower levels of cognitive stimulation were associated with increased internalizing symptoms. In a cross-sectional study in China, adolescents in wealthier households took part in more organised activities with their parents, which in turn was associated with fewer internalizing symptoms (Y. Zhang, 2021).

Biological Mechanisms

Six studies investigated biological mechanisms.

Neurological Factors

Adolescents' economic circumstances can impact numerous brain functions and structures. Living in poverty is associated with reduced volume in some brain regions, potentially due to the negative effects of chronic stress on adolescents (Luby et al., 2013).

This might lead to an increased risk of mental health problems (Hackman et al., 2010). Six studies, all conducted in North America or Europe, investigated whether neurological factors mediated the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and internalizing mental health.

Mediation studies have investigated structural and functional differences in the prefrontal cortex -a brain region that controls many higher-order functions, including emotion regulation. A longitudinal study of adolescent girls in the US found that functional differences in the medial prefrontal cortex partially mediates the association between economic circumstances and internalizing symptoms (Romens et al., 2015). In another study in the US, adolescents with higher household income at age 10 displayed increased activity in the subgenual anterior cingulate during a reward learning and decision-making task conducted at age 15. Increased subgenual anterior cingulate activity was associated with fewer internalizing symptoms a year later (Palacios-Barrios et al., 2021).

Three studies investigated the function or structure of the amygdala -a brain region proposed to be involved in the etiology of internalizing mental health problems (Groenewold et al., 2013; Swartz et al., 2015). In one longitudinal study, resting state functional connectivity between the amygdala and the prefrontal cortex did not mediate the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing symptoms (Ip et al., 2022). There was also no evidence that volume of the amygdala mediated the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing symptoms in a crosssectional study across France, Germany, Ireland and the UK (Stepanous et al., 2023).

However, in a longitudinal study, there was evidence of partial mediation via increased threat-related amygdala reactivity (Swartz et al., 2017).

One study investigated a brain region outside of the prefrontal cortex. In a study of US adolescents, household income from ages 3 to 5 was associated with the magnitude of change in adolescents' hippocampal volume from ages 8 to 15, which in turn was associated with internalizing symptoms at age 15 (Barch et al., 2020).

Epigenetics

One study investigated the potential role of epigenetics as a mediator. The study investigated changes in the expression of the serotonin transporter gene (SLC6A4) caused by environmental effects, in this case adolescents' economic circumstances. The longitudinal study found that living in poorer economic circumstances was associated with greater change in the expression of the proximal promoter region of SLC6A4, which in turn was associated with increased amygdala reactivity in response to threats. Greater threat-related amygdala reactivity was associated with increased internalizing symptoms (Swartz et al., 2017). Evidence of mediation was found only for adolescents that had a parent with a history of depression.

Hormones

One study investigated whether hormones, specifically testosterone, might mediate the association between adolescents' economic circumstance and their internalizing mental health. Testosterone regulation might be impacted by early adversity. Furthermore, testosterone dysregulation might negatively impact the development of the hippocampus, which has a role in stress reactivity (van Bodegom et al., 2017). The longitudinal study found that adolescents' economic circumstances might affect their internalizing symptoms through influencing their testosterone levels (Barch et al., 2020).

Expert consultation and categorization of biological mechanisms

We grouped biological mechanisms into seven secondary domains (Table 7). These consisted of many candidate causal mechanisms that were proposed by experts. The first secondary domain refers to epigenetics. The second consists of hormones, with cortisol, estrogen, and testosterone proposed as candidate causal mechanisms. The third secondary domain refers to obstetric factors. Complications in pregnancy and birth are more common in mothers living in poorer economic circumstances and can have long-term impacts on children's mental health (M. K. Kim et al., 2018; Robinson et al., 2020). The fourth features brain regions and neurotransmitters that have key functions in mood and emotion. The fifth secondary domain refers to the pace and timing of physical development. As an example, the onset of puberty is often earlier in girls living in poorer economic circumstances, which in turn is associated with an increased risk of internalizing mental health problems (Colich et al., 2020; Ullsperger & Nikolas, 2017). The sixth secondary domain refers to adolescents' physical health. Severe injuries, illnesses, and health conditions are more common in adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances and are also associated with increased internalizing symptoms (Arrondo et al., 2022). The final secondary domain refers to different physiological states, systems and functioning. Tertiary mechanisms include inflammation, the microbiome and biological sensitivity to context which is the theory that some adolescents are more biologically sensitive to stressors than others, which might include adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances (Armstrong-Carter & Telzer, 2022).

Evidence from the mediation studies that economic circumstances can impact adolescents' biology and in turn their mental health is in line with the ecosocial theory of health inequalities. According to the theory, people accumulate and biologically express their experiences of economic and social inequality, which leads to the unjust differences in health outcomes between economic and social groups (Krieger, 2021). However, only a few studies investigated biological mechanisms, potentially due to (a) the financial costs and logistical challenges of measuring biological factors, particularly through neuroimaging, and (b) the limited feasibility of targeting many of these factors through intervention as means to reduce inequalities in adolescents' mental health.

The Wider Environment

Environmental mechanisms concern factors linked to the area where adolescents live.

Neighborhood and the Local Community

In a longitudinal study in the US, adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances were more likely to live in neighborhoods with less social cohesion and trust, which in turn was associated with increased depressive symptoms (Oh & Thomas, 2024). In a cross-sectional study in China, neighborhood cohesion partially mediated the association between lower family income and increased internalizing symptoms. There was no evidence of mediation by neighborhood safety or trust (Gu et al., 2023). In another cross-sectional study in China, higher self-reported wealth was associated with living in safer and less polluted neighborhoods, which in turn was associated with fewer internalizing symptoms (Y.

Zhang, 2021).

Expert consultation, categorization and summary of environmental mechanisms

We categorized environmental mechanisms into three secondary domains (Table 8). The first refers to the accessibility and quality of services that are important for adolescents' development. The second concerns environmental events and hazards of which adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances are more at risk. The third domain refers to the quality and safety of adolescents' neighborhoods.

Despite significant research interest on the effects adolescents' environment and neighborhood can have on mental health, only five mediation studies investigated these as causal mechanisms; all of which investigated the role of variables concerning the neighborhood and the local community (Visser et al., 2021). This interest is potentially drawn from the Family Investment Model, which hypothesizes that wealthier economic circumstances enable families to afford housing in safer and more suitable neighborhoods that foster healthy development in their children and adolescents (Duncan et al., 2017). The lack of mediation studies investigating other environmental factors limits understanding of how adolescents' environment might be affected by their economic circumstances, and in turn affect their internalizing mental health.

Behaviors

Adolescents' economic circumstances might impact their internalizing mental health via their health-related behaviors (Pampel et al., 2010). We found a single mediation study that investigated behavior.

Physical Activity:

There was no evidence that the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing symptoms was mediated through their level of physical activity in a longitudinal study of UK adolescents (Chigogora et al., 2020).

Expert consultation, categorization and summary of behavioral mechanisms

We categorized behavioral mechanisms into two secondary domains: health-related behaviors and help-seeking (Table 9). Only one study investigated behavioral mechanisms.

This may be due to criticisms regarding behavioral explanations of health inequalities. These explanations can put the focus on the individual and their behaviors, placing blame on their actions for their poorer health whilst disregarding that these health behaviors are structurally determined and influenced by many social determinants of health (Pearce et al., 2019).

There is however evidence that diet, substance use, screen time and sleep, among other health-related behaviors, all might be linked to both adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health as well other health outcomes (da Costa et al., 2024; Marshall et al., 2014; M. A. Russell & Odgers, 2020; Song et al., 2020).

Regarding help-seeking, there might be differences between wealthier and poorer adolescents regarding how much they seek clinical or non-clinical help in response to experiencing internalizing symptoms. Adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances might be less likely to seek help due to practical or financial barriers or because they might be less optimistic that help would be given or be effective (Radez et al., 2021).

In summary, there are several health-related behaviors that have been shown in separate studies to be associated with adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health. Theoretical model

In this section, we present our theoretical model of how adolescents' economic circumstances might impact their internalizing mental health, informed by the systematic search of mediation studies and expert consultation. In our model (Figure 4a), we propose a potential temporal ordering and relationship between the different domains of candidate casual mechanisms. We provide examples of candidate causal mechanisms within the domains (Figure 4b).

The model highlights the interconnectedness of different causal mechanisms and the complex process through which economic circumstances might impact adolescents' internalizing mental health. The model contains key pathways between different domains that represent the most plausible relationships between different mechanisms. The absence of a connection between any two domains does not necessarily indicate the absence of a direct relationship. Furthermore, the broad domains, and candidate causal mechanisms within them, should not be considered wholly separate to one another. The mechanisms are likely to be descendants of others, and in turn a cause of other mechanisms within the same and also different domains.

There is likely to be a complex interplay between these mechanisms from birth and throughout childhood and adolescence, as shown in a number of mediation studies that modelled sequential mediators measured at different time-points (Barch et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2020). In the following section, we describe the different pathways within our model, providing examples of how one mechanism within a domain might impact a mechanism from a different domain.

In the first part of the model, adolescents' economic circumstances directly impact (a) the quantity and quality of material resources and essentials within adolescents' household, and (b) adolescents' neighborhood and the wider environment within which they live (Duncan et al., 2017).

In the second part of the model, adolescents' material resources and their wider environment impact their social world, biology, behavior and how they think and feel.

Adolescents that live in poverty or in a less affluent neighborhood might have negative social experiences such as stigma or bullying because of their poorer economic circumstances (Attree, 2006). Furthermore, experiencing food or water insecurity can cause physical health problems, therefore affecting adolescents' biology (Moradi et al., 2019). Living in an overcrowded house in a noisy neighborhood could impact adolescents' behaviors such as sleep habits (Mayne et al., 2021). Finally, owning fewer or lower-quality possessions compared to peers might lead to adolescents experiencing negative psychological feelings of shame or low self-esteem (Isaksen & Roper, 2012). Poorer economic circumstances could also directly impact these mechanistic domains (e.g., economic circumstances could directly impact parental stress and parenting (social mechanisms; (Masarik & Conger, 2017)), lead to obstetric complications (biological mechanism; (M. K. Kim et al., 2018)), the affordability of health services and therefore help-seeking (behavioral mechanism; (Radez et al., 2021)), and adolescents' ability to enjoy activities (psychological mechanism; (Schofield & Venkataramani, 2021)).

The third part of the model theorizes bidirectional relationships between social, biological, psychological and behavioral mechanisms. As highlighted in Figure 4b, experiencing a social stressor, such as peer victimization, could impact mechanisms from biological (e.g., cortisol levels), psychological (e.g. self-esteem), and behavioral (e.g. sleep quality) domains (Kliewer et al., 2019; Levková et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2021). Whilst those biological, psychological and behavioral mechanisms also all have the capacity to influence outcomes across all other domains within the third part of the model.

In the final part of the model, we propose that social, biological, behavioral and psychological mechanisms impact adolescents' internalizing mental health. These different mechanisms might impact adolescents' internalizing mental health through different pathways. For instance, psychological mechanisms will impact adolescents' mental health through influencing how they perceive themselves, their life situation, their future and the wider world (Beck, 1967 (Beck, , 1976)). Alternatively, biological mechanisms are likely to alter the functioning of different biological processes implicated in mental health problems (Spenrath et al., 2011). Accordingly, our model proposes that adolescents' economic circumstances, their material assets and the wider environment they live in primarily indirectly impact adolescents' internalizing mental health through influencing their social world, biological states and processes, behaviors and psychological factors.

Discussion

Our study is the first attempt to identify candidate causal mechanisms for the association between adolescents' economic circumstances with internalizing mental health problems or symptoms through a systematic search of mediation studies.

Other reviews have focused on adults, broader wellbeing outcomes, or socioeconomic status rather than economic circumstances (Benzeval et al., 2014; Devenish et al., 2017; Ridley et al., 2020). Our broad list of candidate causal mechanisms was informed by 87 mediation studies and consultation with experts from different academic disciplines and world regions. We identified numerous candidate causal mechanisms, including many that have not previously been considered in theories of health inequalities.

Most mediation studies focused on causal mechanisms concerning adolescents' parents, potentially due to the influence of the Family Stress Model on current understanding of adolescent development during economic hardship (Masarik & Conger, 2017). Many mediation studies also investigated psychological mechanisms, mainly factors relating to adolescents' personality and emotional development. Fewer studies investigated material, biological, environmental and behavioral mechanisms. We proposed numerous candidate causal mechanisms through which adolescents' economic circumstances might affect their internalizing mental health that have not been formally tested in mediation studies and warrant investigation. Mediation studies were overwhelmingly carried out in China, Europe and North America. The lack of research in Africa, Central America, Oceania, South America and numerous countries in Asia means there is limited understanding of causal mechanisms in these contexts. That candidate causal mechanisms may not operate similarly across settings is suggested in a cross-country study that investigated the Family Stress Model, where there was support for the model in most, but not all countries (Zietz et al., 2022).

We did not conduct any formal risk of bias assessments of the mediation studies, owing to the lack of rigorously developed and validated quality assessment tools for mediation studies (Vo et al., 2022). Regardless, many of the mediation studies were of low quality. Most analyzed cross-sectional data which is subject to significant threats to validity, as any of the exposure-mediator, exposure-outcome, or mediator-outcome associations can be partially or wholly driven by reverse causation (Maxwell & Cole, 2007). Therefore, most of the mediation studies we identified might overestimate the extent of mediation by any candidate causal mechanism. Twenty-seven studies did not adjust for any confounders meaning their results are likely to biased, whilst adjustment for intermediate confounders (variables that are caused by the exposure but confound the mediator-outcome association) was rare too. Finally, eight studies assessed mediation using an approach pioneered by Baron and Kenny, but this approach rests on numerous hard-to-meet assumptions that affect these studies' validity and findings may be biased (Baron & Kenny, 1986; Zhao et al., 2010).

Our list of candidate causal mechanisms is therefore informed by mediation studies, that in most cases are likely to over-estimate the extent of mediation. Studies using more robust designs, such as the use of longitudinal data and confounder adjustment, are needed to ascertain whether the candidate causal mechanisms are plausible causal mechanisms rather than a product of bias.

The search strategy and our approach had several key limitations. To identify as many studies as possible, and therefore candidate causal mechanisms, we included studies that used different measures to capture economic circumstances and did not synthesize mediation studies by the measure used to assess economic circumstances. Different indicators of economic circumstances (e.g., income, wealth, or socioeconomic status) are only moderately correlated with one another, and the magnitude of their association with adolescents' internalizing mental health differs (Braveman et al., 2005; Hazell et al., 2022). Whether a variable mediates the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health might depend on how economic circumstances are measured (Braveman et al., 2005). We also included studies that featured a broad age range of adolescents, but we did not investigate if causal mechanisms vary across early-, middle-and late-adolescence. The different causal mechanisms we have identified and proposed therefore might only be relevant for certain measures of economic circumstances, or for certain adolescent age groups.

We focused on internalizing mental health, due to the significant global burden of disease caused by anxiety and depression, particularly in adolescence where approximately two-thirds of mental health problems are one of depression or anxiety (Polanczyk et al., 2015). Externalizing disorders, such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), are also more common in adolescents living in poorer economic circumstances, but causal mechanisms might differ to that of internalizing disorders (A. E. Russell et al., 2016).

Based on our systematic search we developed our own theoretical model which highlights the interconnectedness of different causal mechanisms and the complex process through which economic circumstances might impact adolescents' internalizing mental health. The model is intended to aid intervention development and help inform the design of mediation studies and further theories of health inequalities.

We propose multiple pathways between different domains that could be targeted and modified to weaken the association between adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health. Actions that directly improve adolescents' economic circumstances and tackle the structural causes of inequality are preferable from an ethical perspective, as this could greatly benefit multiple health, economic and social outcomes (World Report on Social Determinants of Health Equity, 2025). This is also in line with the Fundamental Cause Theory which emphasizes that strategies should focus on distal factors, such as economic circumstances, that drive exposure to proximal causes of illness (i.e., specific causal mechanisms), rather than focuses on changing the proximal causes to improve population health (Link & Phelan, 1995). However, actions intended to alleviate poverty, such as cash transfers, are not always feasible or effective in improving adolescents' mental health (Baird et al., 2025; Garman et al., 2022; Lund, 2023; Zaneva et al., 2022). Targeting upstream causal mechanisms, such as adolescents' material resources and their wider environment, alongside poverty alleviation strategies might hold promise in improving adolescents' economic circumstances and their internalizing mental health and subsequently reduce economic inequalities in adolescents' mental health. These upstream causal mechanisms are key determinants of the downstream causal mechanisms (e.g., social, biological, behavioral and psychological mechanisms) that more directly influence mental health.

The model outlines the importance of confounder selection when estimating economic inequalities in adolescents' internalizing mental health. Researchers need to consider whether variables they are adjusting for might be one of the many candidate causal mechanisms that link economic circumstances to mental health. Adjustment for variables on the causal pathway could inadvertently bias the association between economic circumstances and adolescents' internalizing mental health towards the null, therefore underestimating the magnitude of health inequalities (Schisterman et al., 2009).

Our model could be tested in future mediation studies, to an extent, assuming sufficient longitudinal data on a wide variety of casual mechanisms. Our model proposes bidirectional relationships between different causal mechanisms that cannot be modelled within mediation analysis without repeated measurements of mediators, which is uncommon in many datasets. The bidirectionality of different causal mechanisms could be explored through random intercept cross-lagged panel models applied to repeated measures of different mechanisms (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). However this approach is best suited for a small number of variables of interest, and it might be unable to sufficiently model the comprehensive set of causal mechanisms we present, as well as the complex relationships between them. Agent-based modelling approaches could be suitable for testing our model as they can incorporate feedback loops and therefore account for the bidirectional relationship between different causal mechanisms (Cerdá et al., 2015).

Conclusion

Our review suggests that the candidate mechanisms through which adolescents' economic circumstances might influence their internalizing mental health are numerous and interrelated. We propose that these mechanism are likely to operate across six interconnected domains: material, environmental, social, biological, behavioral and psychological. These candidate mechanisms are not fully captured in existing theories of economic inequalities in adolescents' outcomes, and most have not been investigated in empirical investigations of mechanisms.

Participants

We included studies of adolescents where internalising mental health was measured between the ages of 10 and 21. We determined adolescents' age based on the mean age of the sample when internalising mental health was measured. If the mean age was not reported, we determined study eligibility from the age range or school year of the sample.

Exposure

We included any study that measured adolescents' economic circumstances, including, but not limited to, measures wealth, income, or subjective assessments.

Outcomes

We included studies which measured the symptoms or caseness of internalising mental health problems including anxiety and depression, We also included non-diagnostic specific measures of psychological distress, internalising or emotional symptoms, or psychosomatic symptoms. We included studies that had a measure of internalising mental health regardless of the informant (i.e., adolescents themselves, their parents, caregivers or teachers).

Study design

We included studies of any design which investigated whether any variable(s) mediates the association between economic circumstances and internalising mental health, including both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies. We excluded dissertations, conference abstracts and study protocols. Included studies were published in English.
