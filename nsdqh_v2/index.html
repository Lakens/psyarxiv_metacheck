Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .089 Although the correlation between MARQ-19 and arithmetic fluency did not reach statistical significance (r = -.23, p = .089), both the MARQ-3 and MARQ-5 subsets showed moderate negative associations with arithmetic fluency (MARQ-3: r = -.29, p = .027; MARQ-5: r = -.33, p = .011).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

From a clinical perspective, identifying individuals with mathematical difficulties is essential for offering support. However, these individuals often avoid mathematical tests due to co-occurring mathematical anxiety. From a research perspective, this leads to systematic data missingness.

Moreover, in large-scale research, direct testing is often not feasible because of time and resource constraints. Here, we develop a self-report scale for screening for mathematical difficulties in adults as a cost-effective and time-efficient alternative to mathematical tests. Our mathematical ability rating questionnaire (MARQ) is available in full (19 items) and brief (3 items) versions. The MARQ was developed using a sample of 278 Finnish adults. Participants completed four mathematical tests and filled out a 30-item questionnaire, developed based on extensive literature review. Regression analysis, item response theory, and confirmatory factor analysis were used to identify the items most strongly associated with directly assessed mathematical skills. The full and brief versions of the MARQ had good internal consistency (α = 0.94 and 0.86, respectively) and correlated with mathematical skills (-0.70 and -0.63), mathematical anxiety (0.72 and 0.69), mathematical enjoyment (-0.58 and 0.50), and their children's mathematical skills (-0.24 and -0.22). The full MARQ accurately identified 93% of adults with higher mathematical skills (test scores above -1 SD) and 52% with lower skills (test scores below -1 SD). A preliminary Dutch replication among university students demonstrated strong internal consistency (α = .86) of the full MARQ but weaker associations with arithmetic fluency (-.23). Overall, the findings support the MARQ as a reliable and scalable tool for assessing self-perceived mathematical ability, particularly in Finnish-speaking populations.

MARQ: VALIDATING A NEW SELF-REPORT MEASURE

Mathematical Ability Rating Questionnaire (MARQ) for Adults:

Validating a New Self-Report Measure Mathematics is a foundational skill that permeates various aspects of daily life. A stronger ability to grasp and apply mathematical concepts is associated with less risky behavior (Jasper et al., 2013), higher socioeconomic status (Ritchie & Bates, 2013), and an increased capacity to pursue a career with high societal impact (Lubinski et al., 2014). Mathematical difficulties, in contrast, predict various health problems, unemployment, and even the withdrawal from participation in democratic processes (Aro et al., 2019; Garcia-Retamero et al., 2015; OECD, 2016). Accordingly, understanding how mathematical skills develop and how their development can be supported across different life stages is crucial. To gain this understanding, both practitioners and researchers require valid and reliable tools for assessing individuals' mathematical ability across its entire spectrum. In this study, we report the construction and validation of the mathematical ability rating questionnaire (MARQ) for adults with 19 and 3 items (the full and brief versions, respectively).

Compared with other specific learning difficulties/disorders (e.g., dyslexia/reading difficulties, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and speech and language disorder), mathematical difficulties have received less research attention (Mishra & Khan, 2023), resulting in limited understanding and a lack of effective identification methods.

Consequently, many cases of mathematical difficulties may go unnoticed and without remediation, ultimately undermining the educational and professional opportunities of those affected.

Brief but reliable self-report measures can benefit large-scale research endeavors that often employ questionnaire-based assessments owing to their high time-and cost-effectiveness relative to test-based assessments. The use of questionnaires allows for scaling up studies focused particularly on mathematical development as well as those encompassing broader epidemiological cohorts with diverse sets of measurements. In such general cohort studies, only a limited number of items must measure each trait. For example, consider genome-wide association studies (GWASs) that find genes linked to specific traits. One extremely laborintensive GWAS that aimed to identify genes associated with reading skills individually tested 34,000 children across 22 distinct cohorts (Eising et al., 2022). Another GWAS, where efforts were directed at data collection with a single yes-or-no questionnaire item ("Have you been diagnosed with dyslexia?"), reached an astonishing sample size of 1 million participants (Doust et al., 2022). To propel fields such as gene finding, which necessitate enormous sample sizes, the imperative for rigorously validated items cannot be overstated. An equivalent question ("Have you been diagnosed with dyscalculia?") might not work as well for identifying genes associated with mathematical skills because many individuals with significant mathematical difficulties have never received a formal dyscalculia diagnosis.

Moreover, questions with ordinal scales (that can be aggregated into a continuous sum score) capture more nuanced information and provide greater variability than binary (yes-or-no) questions (Ceyisakar et al., 2021). Thus, the use of ordinal scales can lead to more precise estimates and more sensitive analysis.

Reliable self-reports can also benefit research that specifically examines the role of the home environment in children's mathematical development. Incorporating data on parental mathematical skills as a proxy for genetic confounding can enhance the rigor of such research, ultimately elucidating which home-learning activities are truly beneficial for mathematical development (van Bergen et al., 2017). A practical, but not perfect, way to control for genetic confounding in research predicting children's mathematical skills based on the home environment is to include parental mathematical skills as covariates.

Importantly, if parental skills can be accurately assessed through either mathematical tests or self-report measures, the use of reliable self-report measures can save resources, increase sample sizes, and reduce participation bias. Previous research on the intergenerational transmission of reading difficulties indicated that parents with difficulties, compared with those without difficulties, were significantly less likely to participate in a direct testing session but just as likely to complete a self-report measure (Khanolainen et al., 2023a). It is thus reasonable to expect similar patterns in the mathematical domain, given the evidence showing that mathematical difficulties are often comorbid with mathematical anxiety (Zhang et al., 2019) and that higher mathematical anxiety is associated with higher avoidance of mathematical tasks (Choe et al., 2019). This should be considered if larger and more representative samples are to be collected.

Because mathematical skills and difficulties run in families (Desoete et al., 2019; Torppa et al., 2025), by identifying adults with low mathematical skills, we can simultaneously identify children at familial risk of mathematical difficulties, allowing for close monitoring and the provision of early support when needed. Existing intervention research has promisingly shown that early preventive measures can indeed benefit different groups of children at risk of mathematical difficulties (Fuchs et al., 2012), which makes their early identification (including that based on parental self-reports of their own difficulties) a worthwhile avenue for further development.

In summary, effective methods for assessing adult mathematical skills and identifying adults with mathematical difficulties are needed in various educational, clinical, and research contexts. The main goal of the present study is to develop and validate a self-report measure (the MARQ) that can effectively screen for adults with lower mathematical skills.

Conceptual Foundations of the MARQ

In developing the MARQ, we were guided by both theoretical insights in the field of mathematical development and existing scales designed to measure various mathematical constructs. We first compiled a base scale of 30 items (referred to as the MARQ-30), from which we excluded items with poor performance during our validation process. The MARQ-30 was designed to capture mathematical ability through three complementary domains: childhood mathematics-related experiences (11 items), mathematical self-concept (4 items), and experiences of mathematical difficulties in everyday life in adulthood ( 15items). The rationale for including each of these three domains is presented below.

The first group of questions in the MARQ-30 was focused on childhood experiences.

Mathematical difficulties are often stable across development (Nelson & Powell, 2018), and adults with mathematical difficulties frequently report negative experiences with mathematics during childhood. This is likely because one of the most common reasons for mathematical difficulties is developmental dyscalculia, a chronic neurocognitive condition that becomes apparent in childhood and often persists into adulthood (APA, 2013).

The second group of questions in the MARQ-30 pertains to the participants' current mathematical self-concept. Because difficulties often persist into adulthood, people's perception of their own current abilities can be a valid indicator of difficulties. Research has shown that domain-specific academic self-concepts are related to attainment in their respective domains (Marsh et al., 2011; Möller et al., 2020). Supported by empirical evidence from longitudinal studies, the reciprocal effects model, a prominent theory in the field, explains the link between a person's self-concept and their achievement in the corresponding domain through bidirectional influences (Arens et al., 2017; Marsh & Scalas, 2011).

The third group of questions in the MARQ-30 was about the use of mathematical skills in daily life situations. Previous research has revealed that adults with a history of learning difficulties (identified in childhood) experience significantly more difficulties in daily mathrelated situations than their peers without such history (Rämö et al., 2024; OECD, 2013).

Indeed, considering that numeric information is ubiquitous in the modern world, mathematical difficulties can profoundly affect adults' everyday lives (Vigna et al., 2022).

Importantly, the inclusion of all three domains in the MARQ was important because each domain is subject to its own potential sources of bias. Childhood experiences rely on retrospective recall and may be affected by memory inaccuracies, self-concept ratings may reflect inadequate social comparisons rather than actual mathematical ability, and reports of everyday mathematical difficulties may partly stem from occupational demands or opportunities to engage with numerical tasks on a daily basis. By sampling across multiple domains rather than relying on a single type of self-report, the MARQ seeks to reduce dependence on any one source of bias and provide a more robust assessment of mathematical strengths and difficulties.

Existing Questionnaires That Informed the MARQ

The first two groups of questions in the MARQ-30 (items about childhood and mathematical self-concept) were formulated akin to the adult mathematical history questionnaire (AMHQ; Nguyen et al., 2022; Stefansson et al., 2014) and the adult arithmetic history questionnaire (AAHQ; Sury & Gaab, 2020). These scales have similar questions and were modeled after the adult reading history questionnaire (ARHQ; Lefly & Pennington, 2000). However, the main drawback of the AMHQ and AAHQ is the lack of validation studies.

The AMHQ is a six-item measure created and first used by Stefansson et al. (2014) in a study on schizophrenia and autism. The items in this measure ask about childhood difficulties and the current skill self-concept. Stefansson et al. (2014) reported the AMHQ's internal consistency as 0.90 (N = 2,757). No psychometric properties of the measure were noted, other than the reported Cronbach's alpha, as the development of the AMHQ was not the focus of their study. This original AMHQ only includes six items, presumably to facilitate large-scale data collection. Nguyen et al. (2022) expanded Stefansson et al.'s (2014) measure into a more comprehensive version comprising 17 items. These items focused on childhood experiences and the current skill self-concept. Their study (N = 198) did not focus on validating this extended version of the AMHQ, but they did report that the 17-item AMHQ had a Cronbach's alpha of 0.93.

Another scale that inspired the MARQ-30 was the AAHQ created by Sury and Gaab (2020).

The researchers, however, preprinted a list of 26 items without offering an accompanying methodological investigation to validate their measure and establish its psychometric properties and factor structure. The AAHQ mainly comprises questions related to childhood experiences and the skill self-concept, but it has one item that enquires about daily life situations: "such as handling a budget, balance checkbooks, or figuring out the percentage of a discount?" Several different situations are all listed in this single item, which may be confusing for participants who experience difficulties in only one of these situations.

Therefore, we included a wider range of situations in the MARQ-30 and presented them as separate items. These situational items were constructed based on previous literature (Semenza et al., 2014) and clinical work with adults who described how their mathematical difficulties affected their everyday lives (Author et al., in press).

Overall, when designing the MARQ-30, we thoroughly reviewed both relevant theory and various original measures as well as their derivatives. We designed our scale to thus be comprehensive, capturing diverse aspects of how mathematical difficulties are experienced by different people.

Present Study

The introductory section of this article has detailed how the MARQ-30 was developed based on theory and existing questionnaires. Next, we determine which of the 30 items of the MARQ-30 have the strongest correlation with tested skills and thus could be effectively used as a screening tool for identifying adults who may have mathematical difficulties. In other words, using various statistical approaches, we aim to validate the new self-report measure by carefully examining the associations of all 30 items with tested skills and by excluding the items that are not sufficiently well-functioning. We expect that the removal of less informative items will enhance the measure's efficiency, making it a more precise and streamlined screening tool applicable in clinical and research settings. At the same time, we do not aim to set a specific cut-off point for our scale that would indicate where mathematical difficulties begin, as the scores on the scale are to be used as a continuous variable.

The study also aims to identify the three best (most discriminating) items for creating a brief version of the scale. A scale of only three items is more likely to be employed in large-scale (epidemiological) studies than a lengthy measure. We selected three items for the brief version because this represents the optimal number of variables required for constructing latent factors in structural equation modeling (Little, 2024). Although the validated versions of the MARQ are the 19-item (MARQ-19) and 3-item (MARQ-3) scales, we also report the performance of the scale with 1 to 5 best items to facilitate future research and potential scale adaptation. This information illustrates how the inclusion or omission of specific items affects the questionnaire's psychometric properties but should not be interpreted as validating alternative versions of the MARQ. Any modified version would require an independent psychometric evaluation before use.

Additionally, to establish the construct validity of the MARQ, the study examines its associations with variables that are theoretically expected to relate to mathematical ability. Specifically, we assess associations with mathematical anxiety, given its robust negative association with mathematical performance (Barroso et al., 2021); with math enjoyment, as positive attitudes toward mathematics consistently accompany stronger mathematical competence (Wen & Dubé, 2022); and with participants' children's mathematical skills (tested in Grades 1 and 2), as mathematical abilities show substantial intergenerational transmission through both genetic and environmental pathways (Torppa et al., 2025).

Finally, this study provides a replication across two different countries. The 19 items identified as best-performing in the Finnish sample were translated into Dutch and evaluated in an adult Dutch sample to examine their internal consistency and associations with timed arithmetic fluency in a different linguistic and cultural context. This replication in the Netherlands highlights the importance of a separate study dedicated to a proper cross-cultural evaluation of the MARQ with explicit cross-cultural hypotheses. All questionnaire items used in this study and their translations are available in Appendices B and C.

Methods

The study data were derived from a larger, ongoing project that has been systematically collecting information on the development of children's reading and mathematical skills from the age of 2 years, VUOKKO (Lerkkanen & Salminen, 2015 -2019; Salminen et al., 2021 Salminen et al., -2023)). At its initiation in 2015, this project involved the participation of approximately 200 families with young children. The families were recruited through day-care centers. As the participating children progressed into school, the recruitment efforts were extended by engaging the classmates of those who had participated in earlier phases of the project. This yielded a sample of 600-700 children in Grades 1 and 2. As part of the school-age data collection, children's parents were invited to the university to participate in adult mathematical skill assessments and to report on their mathematical strengths and difficulties via a 30-item questionnaire (278 adults contributed their data through both assessments and self-reports; however, only 7 out of 30 self-report items were included in the initial data collection round, and the adults rated the remaining 23 items at a later time point, resulting in different sample sizes for different items (see Table 1 for details)). The data used in this study were collected in person. Four adults in the sample were not the biological parents of the participating children. Their data were included in all steps of the analysis except when calculating correlations between parental and children's skills.

Participating adults were between the ages of 26 and 64 years (M = 40.35 years, SD = 5.52).

Most of them were female (57.9 %), monolingual Finns (93%), and well-educated (37% completed a degree at a university of applied sciences, 34% completed a degree at a traditional university, and 3% had a licentiate or doctorate).

MARQ-30

Adult participants completed the MARQ-30 using a five-point scale. The items of this measure have already been described in detail in the introduction, and the full list of items is available in Table 2.

Adult Skill Assessments

The battery of adult mathematical skill assessments included four tasks. The first was the arithmetic fluency task (Aunola & Räsänen, 2007), which comprised 28 items with additions, subtractions, multiplications, and divisions. The participants were asked to solve as many arithmetic tasks as possible in 3 min. The score was the number of correct responses given in 3 min (the maximum score = 28). The second task was the mathematical achievement test (RMAT; Räsänen et al., 2008) containing 56 items, which included arithmetic operations (e.g., 6000 -627; 204 × 12), fractions and decimals (e.g., 3/10; 5 × 0.3), measurement (e.g., 9

The score was the sum of correct answers (the maximum score = 56). The third task was the Finnish standardized test of mathematics skills (KTLT; Räsänen & Leino, 2005), which is designed to assess the mathematical skills achieved during the first nine grades of comprehensive school. This test is used in Finland as a screening tool to identify students with mathematics difficulties. It contains 40 items assessing basic arithmetic skills (addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division tasks) as well as skills in word problem solving, algebra, geometry, and unit conversion. The score was the sum of correct answers, with a maximum possible score of 40, achieved within a 30-min time limit. The fourth task was the 2-min multiplication fluency test (Koponen & Mononen, 2010a) that included 120 multiplication problems with numbers from one to nine.

Participants were given 2 min to solve as many problems as possible with a maximum score of 120. They were also instructed to move on to the next problem if a problem seemed too challenging. The Cronbach alpha for the four skill assessments was .87.

Mathematical Anxiety

Mathematical anxiety was assessed using a questionnaire adapted from Edwards et al. (2023), originally designed for assessing reading-related anxiety. The questionnaire was translated into Finnish and reworded into statements. One item (related to studying) in the original questionnaire was deemed irrelevant for the Finnish respondents and was thus excluded. The final questionnaire administered to the participants comprised nine statements: 1) "I feel embarrassed about my math skills," 2) "I find it uncomfortable to calculate in front of others," 3) "I fear people will correct my mistakes when I do math calculations," 4) "I worry that I won't get the correct result in math calculations," 5) "I avoid doing math," 6) "Doing math calculations feels unpleasant to me," 7) "Calculating makes me nervous," 8) "Solving math problems feels overwhelming," and 9) "Mathematics causes me anxiety."

Participants responded to these items on a five-point Likert scale: 1 (does not describe me at all), 2-4 (unlabeled points), and 5 (describes me very well). The Cronbach alpha for the scale was .97. Moreover, given the extent to which this questionnaire was adapted, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis and reported the results in Appendix (Figure A1).

Mathematical Enjoyment

Three items were developed in this study to measure mathematical enjoyment: 1) "I find it fascinating to come up with new, creative ways of solving mathematical puzzles," 2) "I like it when I come across situations in everyday life where I can use mathematics as a way of solving problems (e.g., sewing, woodwork, and making financial decisions)," and 3) "I don't like math." Each of these items had the following scale: 1 (does not describe me at all), 2-4 (unlabeled points), and 5 (describes me very well). The Cronbach alpha for the scale was .80.

Child Skill Assessments

Children's arithmetic fluency was assessed in Grades 1 and 2 using two tasks-one for addition and another for subtraction fluency. The 2-min addition fluency test (Koponen & Mononen, 2010b) comprised 120 simple tasks with addends smaller than 10 (the maximum possible score = 120). Tasks were listed in three columns, and the participants were instructed to complete the task from up to down and from left to right. The 2-min subtraction fluency test (Koponen & Mononen, 2010c) comprised 120 simple tasks with outcomes within the range of 1-9 (the maximum possible score = 120). These tasks were organized on the page similarly to the addition fluency test. Children were asked to solve as many addition tasks as possible in 2 min. The number of correct answers was the sum score of the two tasks.

The Cronbach alpha for the four variables (two skill assessments measured twice -in Grades 1 and 2) was .91.

Statistical Analysis

The study analysis was preregistered (Khanolainen et al., 2023b). Our analysis plan included a combination of confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and item response theory (IRT). As preliminary analysis, we examined the descriptive statistics of the four available mathematical skill assessments and tested if these variables contained any outliers. Then, we conducted CFA using these four variables to determine if they collectively formed a single latent mathematical skill factor.

Next, we evaluated the distributions and associations of all items of the MARQ-30 with mathematical test scores. The associations were examined by regressing the mathematical skill latent factor onto each MARQ item separately. Items showing standardized associations below .40 with the latent mathematical skill factor were considered insufficiently related to the target construct and were not retained for subsequent psychometric analyses. The .40 threshold was selected as a pragmatic criterion to retain items demonstrating at least a moderate association with directly assessed mathematical skills while excluding items that contributed relatively little to the construct of interest.

Subsequently, using the 19 retained questionnaire items, we conducted EFA to explore the factor structure of the questionnaire. In EFA, the items were treated as ordinal and the weighted least squares estimator with mean-and variance-adjusted test statistics (ESTIMATOR = WLSMV) was applied. After completing EFA, we conducted IRT analysis to identify the combination of items that was the most efficient in discriminating along the continuum of mathematical skills and had the strongest association with the latent factor of the tested skills. To this end, we constructed a model that combined CFA and IRT analysis.

The CFA part of this model was specified the same way as in the previous analysis step, and the IRT part was specified as a graded response model. Whereas regular graded response models are built using the information of questionnaire items only, our combined model additionally allowed each questionnaire item to load onto the latent factor of the tested mathematical skills. Figure 1 illustrates this model. The combined model suited our study better than the more common IRT models because the factor loadings of the questionnaire items were fixed to the values estimating the latent factor of the tested mathematical skills.

As the latent scale of the questionnaire items came from the latent factor of the tested mathematical skills, all questionnaire items' difficulty and discrimination parameter estimates had a fixed scale. This analysis step aimed to create a questionnaire that could be effectively used among general population samples for identifying mathematical difficulties without running test-based assessments.

All analysis steps were performed in MPLUS 8.10. The models were run using the maximum likelihood robust estimation method. Because some of the participants were spouses (the sample included 39 married couples), we ran the models utilizing the complex option to account for the nested structure of the data (i.e., married couples were nested in families).

The use of the complex option only slightly shifted the results and did not lead to any changes worth reporting.

After the brief versions of the scale (the MARQ-3 along with the versions that included 1, 2, 4, and 5 items) were created, we conducted logistic regressions in SPSS 28 (one logistic regression for each MARQ version). The MARQ score was used as a predictor, and the tested skills were used as an outcome. Because the purpose of this analysis was to evaluate the MARQ's ability to discriminate between relatively low-and higher-performing individuals within the study sample, tested mathematical skills were dichotomized using a cut-off of one standard deviation below the sample mean (Devine et al., 2013) (higher mathematical performance, score above -1 SD; lower mathematical performance, score below -1 SD).

Although this step was not included in our preregistered analysis plan, we later decided that in addition to pursuing our primary research goal (i.e., to investigate how strongly different versions of the MARQ are associated with adult foundational mathematical skills), exploring how accurately different versions of the MARQ would identify low mathematical performance in adults is worthwhile.

Finally, we conducted correlation analysis by constructing a correlation table that included all versions of the MARQ, tested skills, mathematical anxiety scores, mathematical enjoyment scores, and children's scores on mathematical tests completed in Grades 1-2.

The replication sample was a convenience sample that consisted of 61 Dutch university students, primarily majoring in Psychology and Educational Sciences. The participants were between the ages of 17 and 26 (M = 19.38, SD = 1.55), and the majority was female (95%). Reliability of MARQ-19, MARQ-3, and MARQ-5 was assessed with Cronbach's alpha. In addition, Pearson's correlations were calculated between these subsets and the total score on the Tempo Test Arithmetic. More details can be found in a report on OSF (Kuijper et al., 2025).

The Finnish Replication Sample (Second Validation Sample)

The Finnish replication sample was collected as part of another longitudinal sample (Torppa et al., 2026) following children's numerical development from age 2 onwards. The data reported here are of their parents. The participants were between the age 22 and 54 (M = 36.17, SD = 5.26) and 54% were female. The education level was rather high (26.6% had completed a degree at a university of applied sciences, 22% completed a degree at university, and 4.4% had a licentiate or doctorate). Of the MARQ-19 items, 18 were collected in this sample when the children were 2.5 years (items 1, 2, 29), 3.5 years (items 3, 4, 5, 6) and 4.5 years (items 8, 9, 11,12, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18, 23, 26). The sample size for the MARQ-18 was 221, and for the MARQ-3 and MARQ-5 was 232. Both MARQ item data and tested mathematical skill data was available for 162 participants. The mathematical skills were evaluated with three same tasks as in the main validation sample (the first Finnish sample): arithmetic fluency, KTLTA, and multiplication fluency. Results

Mathematical Skills Latent Factor

As a preliminary analysis step, we examined the descriptive statistics of the four variables with adult mathematical test scores (Table 1). Inspection of their descriptive statistics indicated some variability (variances ranging from 16.85 to 494.99) and relatively low levels of skewness (-0.66 to 0.73). The Cronbach's alpha of the four variables was 0.87. We then evaluated these variables for outliers and noted the following: 1) the scores of arithmetic fluency did not contain any outliers, 2) RMAT had two outliers, 3) KTLT had four outliers, and 4) multiplication fluency had two outliers. All eight outliers performed three standard deviations (SD) below the mean and were windsorized.

In Step 1, we conducted CFA to determine whether the four mathematical test scores formed a single latent factor. We observed that the initial CFA model fit could be improved (CFI = 0.97; TLI = 0.91; RMSEA = 0.15; SRMR = 0.02). After examining the modification indices, we added a single fixed negative residual correlation between multiplication fluency and KTLT; this addition sufficiently improved the model fit (CFI = 0.99; TLI = 0.99; RMSEA = 0.04; SRMR = 0.01).

MARQ Items Pre-Selection

In Step 2, the distributional characteristics of the MARQ-30 items were examined (Table 1).

One item (Item 7) showed very low variance (0.25) and was excluded from further analysis.

In addition, two items (Items 21 and 24) had relatively high skewness (slightly above 3). We did not exclude any items based on their skewness but to account for potential violations of normality assumptions, our models were estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR).

Next, to test if the remaining 29 items were significantly associated with the tested skills, we regressed the latent factor of mathematical skills onto each questionnaire item (a separate regression model was run for each item).

Table 2 (the third column) shows the standardized path of each item with the latent factor of mathematical skills. All 29 items had significant associations with the skills. Some items, however, demonstrated only weak associations with directly assessed mathematical skills. Because a 29-item measure would likely capture multiple dimensions (which violates the assumption of unidimensionality required for the subsequent IRT analysis) we reduced the item pool at this stage by excluding items that were only weakly related to directly assessed mathematical skills. The cut-off was set at a standardized association of .40, resulting in the retention of 19 of the 29 items for the IRT analyses (Step 4).

In Step 3, we conducted EFA twice: 1) with 29 items and 2) with 19 pre-selected items to examine the unidimensionality of each set of items. In the one-factor EFA model with 29 items, the eigenvalue ratio (the ratio of the first to the second eigenvalue) was 2.91. A ratio above 3, in general, indicates unidimensionality (Embretson & Reise, 2000). In the one-factor EFA model with 19 items, the eigenvalue ratio was 4.48, which further reinforced our view that the MARQ-19, rather than the MARQ-29, should be used in the next analysis step (unidimensionality is an important assumption for IRT analysis). We then performed CFA with the 19 items loaded to one factor, where the factor loadings were 0.52-0.82, indicating that all 19 items shared a large amount of common variance and thus can be used in IRT analysis (see the results in the following section). For the measure with all 29 items, a threefactor model had the best fit to the data, and a separate IRT analysis was conducted for each of the three factors (see Appendix A for both the EFA and IRT results obtained for the MARQ-29).

Results from the Dutch and the Second Finnish Samples

In the Dutch replication sample, all MARQ versions demonstrated strong internal consistency (MARQ-19: α = .86, MARQ-3: α = .83, and MARQ-5: α = .82). Although the correlation between MARQ-19 and arithmetic fluency did not reach statistical significance (r = -.23, p = .089), both the MARQ-3 and MARQ-5 subsets showed moderate negative associations with arithmetic fluency (MARQ-3: r = -.29, p = .027; MARQ-5: r = -.33, p = .011). Additional details are available in a dedicated report on OSF (Kuijper et al., 2025).

In the second Finnish sample (where all but one of the 19 items were included) the internal consistency was strong across all MARQ versions (MARQ-18: α = .94, MARQ-3: α = .86, and MARQ-5: α = .90). Mathematical skills (z-scored mean composite scores) were strongly correlated with MARQ-18 (r = -.63, p < .001), MARQ-3 (r = -.62, p < .001), and MARQ-5 (r = -.66, p < .001). To facilitate adequate comparison with the Dutch replication sample, we also examined associations between the MARQ and the arithmetic fluency task (the arithmetic fluency scores were taken separately from the broader mathematics test battery).

The results closely mirrored those obtained in the initial Finnish sample and were substantially stronger than those observed in the Dutch sample: MARQ-18 (r = -.54, p < .001), MARQ-3 (r = -.57, p < .001), and MARQ-5 (r = -.60, p < .001).

Discussion

The goal of the present methodological study was to develop and validate the MARQ for adults. In this study, multiple versions of the MARQ (with 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 19 items) were created, and their psychometric properties were reported. The full version of the MARQ with 19 items included 9 items about childhood experiences, 4 items about mathematical selfconcept, and 6 items about everyday life situations. When developing the MARQ-19, all selfconcept items and most of the childhood-related items in the original MARQ-30 were retained, whereas most of the removed items were those about everyday situations (9 items).

This is mainly because most of the situations presented in these items were easily navigated by most participants, and the data distributions on these items were the furthest from being normal. Reassuringly, this indicates that most people, even if they have mathematical difficulties, do not struggle severely and feel that they can effectively navigate most daily situations that require mathematical skills (e.g., situations where they need to understand bus/train timetables, use clocks/watches, or count 2 euros and 75 cents using different coins).

However, the six items about everyday situations that were retained represent more challenging situations (e.g., calculating a discount on a product and the amount of salary after tax deduction). Note here that the ability to deal with specific everyday situations requiring basic math skills does not represent a mathematical strength. Rather, it indicates the absence of difficulties in that situation. A person who does not struggle in such a situation might still have rather low mathematical skills. When designing the MARQ-30, we anticipated that many of the selected everyday situations would not pose a problem for most participants, resulting in items with non-normal distributions. For questionnaires aimed at diagnosing or screening for certain conditions or difficulties -especially heterogeneous ones (conditions experienced differently by those with the same condition / type of difficulty) -individual items commonly have limited informativeness. However, when these items are combined, their aggregate scores can reveal the presence of a condition or difficulties. In our study, the identification of difficulties through only situational items was not the primary research objective; further analysis of these items is thus warranted.

Among all the versions of the MARQ, the 19-item version proved to be the most reliable. It effectively differentiated between different levels of mathematical skills across nearly the entire skill spectrum. The MARQ-19 demonstrated the lowest specificity (93%) but the highest sensitivity (51.9%) compared with the other questionnaire versions. However, considering that the differences in specificity among the different versions of the MARQ were minimal (their specificity ranged between 93% and 97%), we conclude that the MARQ-19 provided the most accurate classifications overall, as it correctly identified both those with and without difficulties most often.

The Dutch translation of the MARQ-19 showed strong internal consistency (α > .80 for both versions of the MARQ), but weak-to-moderate associations with timed arithmetic fluency (r = -.33 for the Dutch MARQ-5), suggesting broadly comparable reliability but more modest convergence in this specific cohort and context. A plausible reason for the weaker convergence in the Dutch sample is that the MARQ was designed to reflect a broad range of self-perceived mathematical strengths and difficulties, whereas the available Dutch criterion measure captured only timed arithmetic fluency, which represents a narrower construct.

Additional factors may include subtle differences introduced during translation, cultural differences in self-evaluation of mathematics, and restricted variance in the relatively young, high-performing university sample. Nevertheless, the Dutch MARQ versions demonstrated strong internal consistency, and the Dutch MARQ-5 showed the highest convergence with arithmetic fluency, suggesting that this short form may be best suited for future large-scale (Dutch) data collections. However, broader mathematics test batteries are needed to more fully evaluate cross-cultural validity, ideally also beyond Europe.

Returning to the main Finnish validation, the brief versions of the MARQ had similar psychometric properties. Importantly, the versions with 2, 3, 4, 5, and 19 items correlated rather strongly with tested skills (-.76 to -.72), whereas the MARQ-1 and MARQ-29 had only moderate correlations with test scores (-.63 to -.62). The main brief version with three items demonstrated high specificity (95% of those without mathematical difficulties were identified as negatives) but rather low sensitivity (37% of those with mathematical difficulties were identified as positives). However, despite its low sensitivity, the MARQ-3 is a valuable screening tool that can be applied as the first step for identifying adults with difficulties and children at familial risk for difficulties. It can be particularly useful in future GWASs, which require enormous sample sizes and therefore can only accommodate brief and easy-to-use measures (see Doust et al., 2022).

Our findings thus indicate that most people's reflections on childhood experiences are accurate and often represent a unique source of information. These findings are in line with those of earlier studies that developed and validated short versions of the ARHQ (Feng et al., 2022; Khanolainen et al., 2024a), which also showed that childhoodrelated self-report items were most predictive of reading skills tested in adulthood. This suggests that both reading and mathematical difficulties are relatively stable and often persist over time (Aunola et al., 2004; Eklund et al., 2015). Extant research on the stability of mathematical difficulties and their possible distinct developmental trajectories is rather limited (Nelson & Powell, 2018). However, in the field of reading development, research has shown that difficulties can follow resolving, late-emerging, and persistent trajectories (Torppa et al., 2015) and that children whose parents have resolved reading difficulties are more likely to follow a resolving/steep trajectory themselves compared with the children whose parents have persistent difficulties (Khanolainen et al., 2024b). Similar crossgenerational and long-term longitudinal research is needed in the field of mathematical development.

The newly developed MARQ (all versions, especially if used in combination with mathematical tests) can facilitate such research by providing the means for identifying adults with resolved mathematical difficulties. Indeed, the MARQ and mathematical tests can complement each other to explain unique variances in children's skills, and the combined information from the two methods can provide more accurate predictions. This hypothesis, however, must be tested in a future dedicated study. The accuracy of the prediction each method and their combination can provide remains to be determined. Promisingly, our correlation analysis revealed that parental scores on the MARQ-19 correlated with children's skills measured in Grade 1 (r = 0.28) almost as equally as parental mathematical test scores correlated with children's skills (r = 0.24). This demonstrates that the MARQ-19 can be used to control for genetic confounding when the familial control method is applied (van Bergen et al., 2017). In other words, the new self-report measure can improve the rigor of studies aiming to discern the true influences of the home numeracy environment. Emerging research has already begun using parental self-reports of mathematical difficulties for this purpose (Salminen et al., 2021). Nevertheless, more reliable and validated research tools are needed.

Although mathematical tests are often considered the gold standard, they are not a perfect measure. However, in the absence of a better measure, we assumed that mathematical tests allow correct identification. At the same time, we are fully aware that those adults who had childhood learning difficulties but then followed a resolving trajectory might have been wrongly identified with adult mathematical tests but correctly identified with the MARQ.

Determining the size of this group of people in adulthood is impossible owing to the lack of prior research, but evidence from existing studies with child samples on the stability of mathematical skills (Nelson & Powell, 2018) suggests that this group of people exists. More research involving children and parents with both resolved and persistent difficulties is thus needed in the mathematical domain, as it could potentially reveal factors that contribute to faster mathematical development.

Finally, in our study, mathematical anxiety and mathematical enjoyment scores were more strongly correlated with self-reported skills (the MARQ-19) (0.72 and -0.58) than with tested skills (-0.46 and 0.34). Moreover, self-reported skills correlated with mathematical anxiety as strongly as with tested skills (0.72 and 0.70). These findings could be explained by the mechanisms of how mathematical anxiety, enjoyment, and skills develop. According to the deficit theory (Hembree, 1990), mathematical anxiety emerges from mathematical difficulties, and in later life, the unpleasant memories of earlier failures lead to feelings of anxiety around mathematical tasks faced in the present. Recent empirical evidence has shown that the relationship between mathematical skills and mathematical anxiety is likely bidirectional, meaning that the deficit theory is partially correct (Carey et al., 2016).

Furthermore, mathematical anxiety tends to increase with years at school, often peaking in high school and leveling off in later years (Ramirez et al., 2018). Considering that mathematical anxiety does not cease to exist after high school graduation, even if a person's mathematical difficulties gradually resolve to the point of being undetectable with mathematical tests in adulthood, the scars of early negative experiences may still be visible in adulthood and manifest themselves through high mathematical anxiety. This would explain the stronger correlation of mathematical anxiety with self-reported skills and its weaker correlation with tested skills considering the emphasis on childhood experiences in the selfreport questions.

Mathematical anxiety can also be experienced by people with a moderate or high level of mathematical skills who do not have a history of mathematical difficulties (Devine et al., 2018). In addition, research has shown that people with higher mathematical anxiety tend to underestimate their mathematical skills (Ahmed et al., 2012). The possible presence of people with both moderate/high skills and high anxiety in our sample would also drive the correlation between self-reported skills and self-reported anxiety. Finally, we cannot exclude the possibility of some people not understanding the difference between difficulties and anxiety-such conceptual confusion is not surprising if a person equates having negative emotions and experiencing struggles. However, the actual reason behind the discrepancy in the identified correlations remains to be investigated in future studies.

Limitations

Our main results may not be generalizable to contexts significantly different from Finland.

Finland is a highly homogenous country in terms of its ethnic and cultural composition. It is also a western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) country (Henrich et al., 2010). Although our recruitment efforts in the Finnish context followed structured procedures, neither sample was probability-based. Nevertheless, we aimed to collect samples representative of the general population in Finland by recruiting adults of varying ages. Our samples also nearly balanced in terms of gender, with a slight overrepresentation of women.

However, a significant limitation affecting the generalizability of our findings is the samples' high education level, which is above the Finnish average. Individuals with the lowest education levels were underrepresented, likely indicating that people with the most severe mathematical difficulties were not included in the recruited sample. Underrepresentation of individuals with low socioeconomic status is a common issue in scientific research, as they are less likely to be recruited, consent to participate, and remain in studies (Jang & Vorderstrasse, 2019; Stone, 2003).

Notably, our findings indicate high specificity but low sensitivity across all tested versions of the MARQ, with the highest sensitivity being 51.9% for the MARQ-19. This suggests that although the measure distinguishes between low and high skills, it does not effectively differentiate between varying levels of low skills (skills below the mean). This limitation could be attributable to our sample's underrepresentation of individuals with severe mathematical difficulties. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that future studies, inclusive of such individuals in sufficient numbers, might identify a different set of MARQ items as most efficient.

In the logistic regression analyses examining how well the MARQ identified individuals with relatively low mathematical performance on direct mathematical assessments, mathematical performance was dichotomized using a cut-off of -1 SD. Because this threshold was defined relative to the present sample rather than population norms, it should not be interpreted as indicating clinically significant mathematical difficulties. Instead, it served as a research criterion for distinguishing lower-and higher-performing participants within the sample.

Future validation studies should establish clinically meaningful cut-off scores using representative normative samples and standardized diagnostic criteria.

A further limitation is that the present study does not establish a clinically meaningful cut-off score on the MARQ for identifying individuals with mathematical difficulties. Because the current validation phase treated MARQ scores as continuous variables to maximize statistical sensitivity and information retention, the findings do not provide empirically derived thresholds for clinical decision-making. This limits the instrument's current use as a standalone diagnostic tool. At present, the MARQ is best viewed as a supplementary screening instrument that can be used alongside other assessments to identify individuals who may benefit from further evaluation or support. Future research should establish normative data and clinically meaningful cut-off scores in large, representative samples and evaluate the sensitivity, specificity, and predictive validity of the MARQ for identifying individuals with diagnosed mathematical difficulties. Such work is necessary before the instrument can be recommended as an independent clinical screening or diagnostic tool.

Another limitation is that not all MARQ-30 items were simultaneously presented to the participants. The initial questionnaire included only 7 items (n = 148), and the follow-up questionnaire, conducted a year later, included the remaining 23 items (n = 278). The smaller sample size for the seven items may have resulted in lower statistical power, potentially affecting the process of selecting the most effective items. We recommend future studies to administer all questionnaire items at once to ensure consistency and reliability.

What is also important to mention is that mathematical anxiety and mathematical enjoyment were measured using adapted rather than standardized instruments. Because these variables served only as supplementary indicators of construct validity rather than primary outcomes, we do not expect this to substantially affect the main conclusions of the present study.

Nevertheless, future validation studies should replicate these findings using well-established measures of mathematical anxiety and enjoyment.

Finally, cross-cultural applicability also requires further validation: although the Dutch translation showed strong internal consistency, convergence with timed arithmetic fluency was modest, underscoring the need for broader mathematics assessments and more diverse samples. Importantly, the Dutch sample was recruited through convenience sampling, and findings may differ in a sample more representative of the general Dutch population.

Conclusion

The newly developed tool demonstrated strong psychometric properties in the Finnish sample, indicating its effectiveness for screening large groups of adults and offering a costeffective and user-friendly (i.e., not inducing mathematical anxiety) method to collect research data and identify individuals in need of interventions and support. For example, the tool can identify children at familial risk of mathematical difficulties, so they can receive timely intervention to prevent the development of difficulties (like Zijlstra et al. (2021) did for children at familial risk for reading difficulties). The tool can also help adults whose difficulties went unnoticed earlier in life. This is particularly relevant in prison settings, where mathematical difficulties are widespread and often undiagnosed. Incorporating a brief screening tool such as the MARQ into routine assessments may help to identify support needs and inform targeted interventions with potential benefits for both the individuals concerned and society more broadly (Patterson, 2022). In conclusion, the MARQ offers a reliable, flexible self-report tool for assessing mathematical strengths and difficulties in adults, with the brief version suitable for large-scale studies. Future work should extend validation across cultures and broader mathematics outcomes.
