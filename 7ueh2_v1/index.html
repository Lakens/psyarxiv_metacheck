Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

This study aims to discover how visual representations of common word sequences in language can increase or decrease a viewer's gullibility and trust based on how well the ordering of the stimuli matches the common ordering in the viewer's culture. My primary research question is "How does the cultural congruence of the horizontal arrangement of objects in an image with its respective language's normative binomial collocation sequences affect the viewer's processing of subsequent questions?" This paper hypothesizes that, when shown images that are in a culturally fluent arrangement, participants will be in a state of greater gullibility.

Alternatively, when shown images arranged in a culturally disfluent manner, the viewer will engage in higher-level reasoning and be more skeptical. We randomly assigned participants to one of three groups -the control group, the culturally fluent group, and the culturally disfluent group. In the culturally fluent group, participants were shown visual representations of binomial collocation sequences arranged left to right, as they are commonly spoken in English. In the culturally disfluent group, participants were shown collocation sequences arranged opposite to the cultural norm. After viewing the images, participants were tested on tasks that assess intuitive vs. systematic reasoning and measure their gullibility. The data do not support our hypotheses that if the participants are shown culturally fluent visual representations of collocation sequences, then they exhibit more intuitive reasoning, and that those shown culturally disfluent stimuli exhibit more systematic reasoning, nor does it support that this change prompts more face-value acceptance of inherence in culture. The data do indicate, however, that participants prefer viewing two associated stimuli rather than individual pictures.

Within the context of marketing, advertising, and engagement of consumers, it is important to be able to have an intended audience trust the content one puts out. One's trust in a stimulus is often based on its familiarity and its cultural norms. Language serves as a basis for how one perceives the world around them, as it is how they rationalize tangible objects and abstract concepts into something understandable. Even though there are established rules for grammar within language, developed norms cultivate a paradigm of visual arrangement as well.

Across many contexts, we learn to group related words, meaning the words are collocated. As these relationships between words develop, we learn the conventional phrasing of how natives of a certain language often portray these phrases in conversation. The cultural norms and use of related words lead to conventional orderings of words called "binomial collocation sequences." Even though one ordering of certain words may not be more grammatically correct than another, we become familiar with the order of words and collocation sequences of our native language. We often refer to "fruits and vegetables," "father and son," or "fire and ice." Very rarely do we hear people talk about "vegetables and fruits," "son and father," and "ice and fire." The prior list displays culturally fluent sequences, while the latter presents culturally disfluent sequences.

Our brains are wired and trained to distrust a stimulus when it is unfamiliar, as, from an evolutionary perspective, if we don't understand or recognize something, it can be dangerous (Lin et al., 2019). Alternatively, if something is familiar to us, we can let our guard down and trust more (Whittlesea et al., 1993;Oyserman, 2019). We will explore how differences in visual representations of binomial collocation sequences can cause one to be more gullible or more skeptical based on the cultural fluency of the sequences.

As a fundamental principle, we human beings have evolved to distrust the unknown.

While it is not impossible to trust people we just met, generally, people and concepts earn our trust through continuous proof that leads us to a more positive state. With different cultures, this level of trust varies greatly with different stimuli.

1. Processing Fluency

"Processing fluency" -the ease with which one can process a stimulus -is heavily influenced by the access to an established schema. A person's familiarity with an idea or stimulus prompts them to have an easier, more pleasant, and more trusting experience with the stimulus (Schwarz et al., 2021). When a stimulus is easier to process, it motivates the viewer to see something as more believable. Okuhara et al. (2017) discuss processing fluency as subjective to the individual; it refers to one's experience of a stimulus as easy to process rather than the stimulus itself being "objectively simple." Results found that with greater processing fluency of language-based stimuli -"lexical fluency" -a person judges a stimulus more positively (Okuhara et al., 2017). In turn, this raises the question of how lexical fluency may affect trustworthiness and language-based familiarity. Processing fluency relies not just on the person or the stimulus, but rather on the interaction between the two that may differ from person to person or throughout different cultures.

2. Cultural Fluency

The idea of processing fluency is inseparable from its cultural context. With language serving as a fundamental part of a person's manner of thinking, it is important to discuss language's ties to fluency. A sentence structure where one is more easily able to predict a noun allows for easier processing, as shown by how subjects could more easily pronounce a word when the prior context of the sentence fits with the word (Whittlesea et al., 1993). This shows how people process stimuli more quickly and easily when they align with a visual structure with which they are more familiar. While familiar stimuli are easier to process, the reverse remains true as well, as people remember words more easily if they are presented in a contextually predictive structure (Whittlesea et al., 1993). This is due to the "mere exposure effect" -the idea that people prefer a stimulus that they have been exposed to before; recurring experience makes a stimulus easier to process, driving memory to retain a certain stimulus (Whittlesea et al., 1993).

The mere exposure effect and processing fluency lead us to discuss the concept of "cultural fluency" and its inverse, "cultural disfluency." Every culture develops an implicit set of rules for the supposed correct way to present or perceive a stimulus, and, thus, every stimulus carries a specific cultural fluency -the ease with which one processes a stimulus due to the perceiver's culturally rooted expectations (Oyserman, 2019). Culture acts as a network of teaching implicit knowledge, so people set expectations of how a stimulus would be presented.

3. Collocation Sequences

One specific aspect of language that contributes to cultural and processing fluency is the specific ways of phrasing ideas that are normative within specific cultures. Some words are more frequently "collocated" with others -meaning they are often associated together and are perceived closely together by a native speaker. When something has lower processing fluency, and one must go beyond the immediate sensory experience, then the stimulus triggers higher levels of processing (Flusberg et al., 2024). The semantic frames of a sentence greatly affect a person's perception of the statement, even if it holds the same meaning (Flusberg et al., 2024).

Despite what one may initially believe, much of language processing is not through complex thought but rather an immediate sensory processing. In a certain language, one expects a set of other words in conjunction with the original. The authors assert that there is a "normatively conditioned combination of a given word with another word." The meaning of a word is affected by the collocating words used in the context, serving as a paradigm that one uses to approach certain words and their language due to their culture (Arkhipova & Vlavatskaya, 2019).

We can see this change in framing when examining how different cultures have different sequences of words despite the same meaning. Different languages have different "binomial collocation sequences," referring to the order in which a two-noun phrase may present the ideas in a specific succession. For example, native English speakers generally refer to produce as "fruits and vegetables." Although saying "vegetables and fruits" is not grammatically incorrect, it simply does not align with the sequence that most native English speakers are used to. The more natural-sounding translation of "fruits and vegetables" in Korean, however, places vegetables first. In general, studies have shown that participants can read information more quickly when shown a binomial collocation sequence that aligns with their language's normative structure as opposed to the reverse order (Siyanova-Chanturia et al., 2011). We can understand how this reverse order and cultural disfluency contribute to reduced processing fluency.

4. Visual Metaphors

With writing, language then translates into a visual stimulus, relying on specific placement. This leads us to examine "conceptual metaphors" -adding an associated visual stimulus to an abstract concept. For example, people perceive more rational ideas as being placed vertically above emotional concepts; rationality involves "higher-level thinking," and so, people perceive it as being placed literally higher (Cian et al., 2015). Similarly, this applies to temporal ideas as well. Multiple experiments have examined the effect of the order of written language in conjunction with one's perception of past and present. In the English language, since we write left to right, people often perceive the past as on the left, and the present or future on the right; Zhang et al. (2022) found that people prefer pictures of objects from the past to the left of modern objects, as opposed to the visual-temporal reversal. Native Farsi speakers, on the other hand, showed no such preference, as Farsi's language is written from right to left (Zhang et al., 2022). Different languages contribute to different perceptions of visual stimuli.

5. Trust

Re-examining the mere exposure effect, one's ease of processing information directly affects how much they trust the stimulus. When a stimulus is congruent with a given culture's norms, then the viewer does not need to go into higher-level reasoning, stopping a person from being skeptical of the stimulus, making them more likely to trust whatever the stimulus is (Oyserman 2019). Oyserman (2019) discusses how when a stimulus defies expectations and is more culturally disfluent, people perceive it as dangerous and untrustworthy. This reaction is due to a basic survival instinct that if a person sees something they are not used to, then something must be wrong, and so they use higher levels of cognition to handle the situation. We can see this by examining how, when shown advertisements with a stimulus that aligns or goes against expectations of a certain culture, participants rated the statement as less inherently true when it was culturally disfluent (Lin et al., 2019). Additionally, when lower-bound modifiers and upperbound modifiers were used in contexts that were different from the cultural norm -for example, saying "more than 5%" compared to "less than 5%" -participants believed the associated claims at a much lower rate (Zhang and Schwarz, 2020). Seemingly insignificant changes in a sentence or the structure of a phrase may change one's tendency to believe a sentence. Even though there is nothing grammatically incorrect about how the sentence is portrayed, its deviation from what one in a specific culture expects prompts skepticism.

When asked questions that contained incorrect information, participants were able to detect discrepancies at a much higher rate when the questions were shown in a more difficult-toread font, as people already triggered higher cognitive functioning processing due to the initial difficulty in processing (Song & Schwarz, 2008). We can see how stimuli that are more difficult to process prompt a person to use higher-level thinking and lead to lower levels of gullibility.

Gullibility correlates more with higher levels of perceptual fluency, as opposed to culturally incongruent stimuli that prompt lower fluency. Similarly, when personal questions are in a larger and easier-to-read font, people tend to reveal more negative information about themselves, revealing that when a stimulus is disfluent, people tend to be less trusting and disclose less personal information (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009).

On top of visual stimuli being physically easier to see, studies have found that people tend to have greater trust in strangers when the name presented is easier to pronounce or more common in a person's culture, stressing the importance of cultural congruence in trust (Newman et al., 2014). Similarly, participants have also been found to place greater trust in online strangers' testimonials and product descriptions if their username is easier to pronounce (Silva et al., 2017). Once again, this echoes the idea that greater processing fluency results in greater levels of trustworthiness, and lower processing fluency aligns with greater skepticism.

6. Positive Affect

By examining studies on how processing fluency influences positive affect, we can understand how stimuli that are easier to process correlate with more pleasant emotions. For example, the mere exposure effect demonstrates this positive effect from familiarity. When participants were asked to rate how much they liked a person in a photo, subjects were more likely to rate the person higher if they were shown a picture of them before (Harmon-Jones & Allen, 2001). This shows how people generally have more positive associations with stimuli that they are used to, as opposed to those with which they are unfamiliar. Additionally, "the Fluency-Affect Link" theorizes how high levels of fluency of stimuli lead to more positive emotions (Winkielman et al., 2003). On a similar note, the "hedonic marking thesis" argues that fluency of a stimulus elicits a positive response; this can be seen as Winkielman et al. (2003) examine a wide range of studies to argue how perceptual and conceptual fluency results in a more positive affective state. In general, people tend to have more positive reactions to familiar and easier-toprocess stimuli; often, the two qualities intersect.

7. Aesthetics

Similar to preference, experiments have also shown that people find easier-to-process stimuli more aesthetically pleasing. Zhang et al. (2022)also explored how participants differed in finding visual stimuli more aesthetically pleasing, with results finding that people found objects more aesthetic when they fit the language congruence of a person's cultural background. Reber et al. (2004) assessed a variety of papers to argue that when people expect a stimulus to appear a certain way, and the stimulus aligns with their expectations, then they will find it more aesthetically pleasing and of greater beauty. The "Keats heuristic" asserts that people find aesthetically pleasing expressions to be more accurate than mundane expressions. Specifically, Kara-Yakoubian et al. (2022) found that statements with an A-B-B-A lexical repetition pattern (often used in poetry) were found to be more accurate by participants than sentences phrased abnormally. This shows how aesthetics within language, such as collocation sequences, can influence people's trustworthiness. In total, the established literature shows how people find visual stimuli that are more familiar as more aesthetically pleasing, and people find stimuli that are more aesthetically pleasing as more trustworthy.

8. Consumer Interaction

Using this knowledge of processing fluency, aesthetics, and trustworthiness, we can examine how it can be practically applied to a real-world setting within product engagement and consumer interaction. The literature shows that consumers are more likely to trust and prefer objects from an advertisement that contains a stimulus that aligns with their culture's expected norms and possibly linguistic sequences, as opposed to culturally disfluent stimuli. Studies have shown that people are more easily able to recall and process less complex syntax from advertisements; additionally, people differentiate between strong and weak more frequently when syntax is complex in an advertisement, showing how complex language in ads triggers higher-level reasoning (Lowrey, 1998). For a company to make their claims as believable as possible, it is helpful to make them as easy to process and aesthetically pleasing as possible to limit a person from resorting to higher-level thinking and skepticism of a product. This works in conjunction with cultural fluency. In experiments by Mourey et al. (2015), participants frequently take food from plates that use normative imagery with what the celebrated holiday was, as opposed to iconography from another holiday, extending cultural fluency into consumer behavior. People are more likely to interact with a product that reflects their schema and normative iconography. Furthermore, participants rated themselves as more likely to purchase a product when shown iconography that aligned with their cultural norms (such as a white wedding dress), as opposed to when shown a picture that was culturally disfluent (a green wedding dress) -even when the product being sold had no relation to the pictures shown (Mourey et al., 2015). This echoes back to the mere exposure effect, as people prefer and trust what they are used to. When examining consumer behavior over their preference in eyeglasses, data reveal that people prefer glasses when shown a picture of their mirrorswapped self (what they're used to seeing) significantly more than a picture of themself from an outside viewpoint (Cho & Schwarz, 2012).

By seeing that people prefer the products in the more familiar photo, we can discuss that people prefer products when the stimulus presented with it is more familiar. Although I do not aim to study consumer behavior in my study, I believe that cultural fluency can help manipulate consumer purchasing behavior.

Research Questions & Hypotheses

These ideas prompt me to examine how the visual representation of concepts and expectations of language may jointly influence processing fluency with downstream effects on gullibility. This leads me to two central research questions.

Research Question 1: How does the arrangement of images' adherence to their respective language's normative binomial collocation sequences affect the viewer's gullibility?

Methods

Design Overview

In this study, I tested how the cultural fluency of visually represented binomial collocation sequences may affect one's gullibility. "Binomial collocation sequences" refer to terms commonly related and placed next to one another in language. For native speakers of English, these sequences are culturally fluent or congruent when they align with the order in which they most commonly appear in the English language; for example, "peanut butter and jelly." Alternatively, this sequence is incongruent when one says, "jelly and peanut butter." I assessed how one's exposure to a visual representation of binomial collocation sequences may prompt one to be more or less gullible based on the images' cultural fluency.

For this study, I collected all my data through a Qualtrics survey posted on USC's SONA. I did not require or use any funding, as I was able to collect a sufficient sample size through the psychology department's subject pool.

For this, I used an experimental, between-subjects design. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of three groups. For the control group, participants were shown a series of singular images and were measured based on their inherence and cognitive reflection performance. In the "culturally fluent group," participants were shown an image-pair that depicts binomial collocation sequences in the conventional order of the English language (peanut butter on the left, jelly on the right). In the third group, the "culturally disfluent group," participants were shown the same images in reverse order (jelly on the left, peanut butter on the right).

Participants in all three groups were assessed using the same measures.

This study procedure includes one singular survey at one time, with the median time and mean time (excluding outliers) being roughly eight minutes to complete.

Participants

For recruitment, I posted my study on SONA. Participants were limited to only students at the University of Southern California who are taking a set number of psychology courses, where they are required to participate in studies on SONA. This limits my sample to only undergraduate students at USC. Furthermore, to be included in the study, participants must be fluent in English and have grown up speaking English. Although English does not necessarily have to be their first language, it must be one of their native languages, so they have adopted the language's conventional binomial collocation sequences as part of their natural way of thinking.

Due to my subject pool being from SONA, participants did not receive any form of financial compensation, but rather, they received SONA research credits for their psychology class. Provided that they are students raised speaking English and are in one of the necessary psychology courses, there is no exclusion criterion based on gender, age, ethnic background, socioeconomic level, sexual orientation, or anything of the sort. However, I still collected these demographic details for analysis purposes. Participants were also eliminated from the pool if they did not pass our attention check.

To reach a power level of 0.80 with an effect size of F = 0.2 using ANOVA, G-Power analysis reveals that I needed a total sample size of at least n = 246. This means that among the three groups, I needed a sample size of at least n = 82 for each.

Our final sample included 319 participants (218 female). The average age was M = 20.038 (SD = 1.503). 168 of the participants spoke a foreign language as well as English; only 7 of those participants spoke a language that is read right-to-left instead of left-to-right as the English language is read.

In terms of ethnic distribution, (participants selected multiple ethnicities, so the distribution does not add up to a perfect 100%) roughly 37.93% (n = 121) identify as Asian or Pacific Islaner, 12.54% (n = 40) identify as Black or African American, 19.12% identify as Hispanic (n = 61), 6.27% as Middle Eastern (n = 20), 1.25% as Native American or Alaska Native (n = 4), 41.07% (n = 131) as White/Caucasian, 1.57%% (n = 5) listed themselves as "Other," while eight participants declined to answer.

Of the participants surveyed and analyzed, roughly 41.07% (n = 131) are studying a brain science such as psychology, cognitive science, neuroscience, or behavioral economics as their major.

Measures

Participants' gullibility was assessed with two dependent variables -their inherence score and their cognitive reasoning.

Inherence

One operationalization of gullibility was one's tendency to simply accept norms and cultural conventions -how inherent and "correct" their culture feels. The more one feels that their environment reflects the natural order of the way things should be, the higher they are on gullibility. In past studies, when exposed to culturally disfluent stimuli, participants experienced decreased inherent beliefs. To measure inherence, I used a modified version of the 15-item "Inherence Scale" created by Salomon & Cimpian (2014) and later refined and used by Lin et al. (2019). The inherence scale includes fifteen statements about culturally accepted norms with no actual logical value (See Appendix A). Participants rated their agreement with each statement, ranging from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree."

All these items assess one's acceptance of cultural norms as a "given." Each item starts with "It seems natural…," "It seems right that…," or "There are good reasons why…," followed by a cultural norm with little logical basis outside of our culture. For example, one item is "There are good reasons why orange juice is typically consumed for breakfast." The more one agrees with this statement, the more it reveals their inherent acceptance of cultural norms and lack of skepticism. Additionally, there was one attention question to check subjects' focus on the items (See Appendix A). This attention check item simply read, "Please choose 'strongly disagree' for this question so that we know you are paying attention."

Intuitive vs. Systematic Reasoning

In past studies, experiencing cultural disfluency prompted higher levels of cognitive reasoning and suspicion, while culturally fluent stimuli put one at ease and limited higher cognitive reasoning. I tested cognitive reasoning with the Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT), created by Frederick (2005), and I used the modified version from Mourey et al. (2015). These include three free-response questions that are designed for one to give a quick and intuitive answer when in a state of gullibility and for one to give a different answer when truly focusing on and thinking about the question (See Appendix B).

For example, one question was, "A fishing rod and fishing bait cost $11 in total. The fishing rod costs $10 more than the bait. How much does the bait cost?" When looking at the fishing rod and bait question, for example, someone who is not using higher cognitive thinking will answer $1. When thinking critically, however, one can pick up on the fact that the price is only "0.50." If one answers the questions intuitively, then this will be counted and marked as a "1," indicating a tendency to use intuitive thinking. If they answer more questions with the correct answer, then their response will be marked as a "0," and this would indicate higher cognitive reflection. I used this measure to see if one is relying on intuitive reasoning or higher-level cognitive reasoning. Relying on intuitive reasoning without cognitive reasoning demonstrates to us that participants are in a state of higher gullibility and lower skepticism.

Procedures

Upon beginning the survey, participants were directed to an informed consent form.

Participants were then randomly assigned to one of the three groups. After this random assignment, participants were asked to rate a series of fifteen visual stimuli, which differed by condition. In the "control group," all fifteen of the images presented were a singular image that shows an individual object of attention. All images were presented in a random order. Below, participants indicated how much they like the image by using a slider between two endpoints, with the lowest being "Do not like at all" and the highest "Like very much" (Figure 1)."

For participants randomly assigned to the "culturally fluent group," ten out of the fifteen items shown were pairs of images that reflect culturally fluent binomial collocation sequences rather than singular images. These image pairs included ten images from the control group, coupled with one that fits into a binomial collocation sequence. The control group would have just shown a picture of something like "fruits," but the "culturally fluent" group was shown two pictures, one that shows "fruits" next to one that shows "vegetables." Similar to the structure of image pairs in Schwarz et al. (2021). Each image pair shows two images that are associated in English side-by-side, with the image on the left containing the object commonly said first in the English language (Figure 2). For the other five, we included them as neutral stimuli that do not show binomial collocation pairings. We paired them with a slightly related image that is not part of a collocation sequence. For example, the control group showed a picture of a boat, while the "culturally fluent" group showed a boat and a coral reef next to each other. Instead of participants being asked to rate how much they like the image, we asked them to rate how much they like the arrangement of images to ensure that participants are given a task and process the images.

With the "culturally disfluent group," I followed the same procedure as the "culturally fluent group," only the image pairs showed a visual representation of the opposite of the culturally congruent binomial collocation sequences. While in the "culturally fluent group," I showed ten image pairs that may have shown "fruits and vegetables" or "burger and fries," the image pairs in the cultural disfluency group show image pairs such as "vegetables and fruits" and "fries and burger" (Figure 3). The five neutral stimuli here, however, were the same as those in the "culturally fluent group." For example, the "boat and coral reef" arrangement is still "boat and coral reef" in the "culturally disfluent group."

After rating the fifteen images, participants read through and provided their opinions on the statements from the inherence scale. I asked them to rate their agreement with each item from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree." I presented all sixteen of these questions (fifteen actual items and one attention check) on the same page.

After the inherence scale, I then showed the participants a series of eight more visual stimuli and asked them to rate each one. This was in the same structure and procedure as the first fifteen stimuli. Five of the presented stimuli indicated binomial collocation sequences for their respective group, and the other three were neutral stimuli. I chose to repeat the visual stimuli after measuring subjects' inherence because, despite my hypothesis that a sequence of culturally fluent image pairs will increase gullibility and culturally disfluent image pairs will decrease gullibility, I did not want to risk that these effects could wear off in the minute or so that it would take to rate the items on the inherence scale.

Subsequently, after rating the quality of the eight images, participants completed the Cognitive Reflection Tasks. All three of these questions were separated into different pages. At the top, instructions were presented, simply reading, "You will now be asked a series of 3 questions.

Please answer them to the best of your ability." Participants then filled in the blank for the three CRT questions. These questions included a simple math problem and a blank space underneath.

Participants then answered a series of demographic questions; this finished the survey. Just before gathering demographic data, participants were asked on a separate page if there were any obvious patterns that they had detected when taking the study. This was to determine whether they detected the arrangement of binomial collocation sequences.

Discussion

Inherence

Due to the very minimal differences in the inherence score among the different groups, we can identify that our manipulation had practically no effect on the participants' endorsement of inherence beliefs. For this reason, we can infer that viewing objects in an order that follows or reverses the common collocation sequence does not affect the endorsements of the cultural expectations reflected in the inherence scale. The visual registering of "peanut butter and jelly" as opposed to "jelly and peanut butter" does not prompt one to be more skeptical of their environment. This is likely because they are still seeing two concepts that are associated with each other. There is no cultural disfluency when seeing jelly placed to the left of peanut butter, possibly because they are used to seeing peanut butter next to jelly anyway and have never thought of the directional positioning of the jars relative to each other.

It is also important to note that there is no difference between the treatment groups and the control group as well. We cannot assume that people are more inclined to blindly accept their cultural norms more when presented with stimuli that go together, because participants displayed similar reactions to seeing a collocation sequence pairing than they did for individual objects.

The high Cronbach's Alpha score here indicates that we were able to achieve relatively consistent results throughout the test and that all questions elicited a similar reaction. The results indicate that we cannot reject the null hypothesis, and that the visual ordering of collocation sequences likely has no effect on how inherent one's culture feels to them. There are many ways to explain this; possibly nobody registered that the ordering of collocation sequences was disfluent to cultural norms, or even if they did, even subconsciously, it did not affect how they perceive their own culture.

Cognitive Reflection Test

Additionally, when considering the cognitive reflection test, we found very little difference between the different groups. The only question that showed any significant effect was the second question, and so there is a possibility that participants are more inclined to gullibility in the fluent group, specifically for the question about making toys. However, that result needs to be adjusted for multiple tests, which renders it nonsignificant as well. Looking at this data, it becomes apparent that showing culturally incongruent visual representations of collocation sequences did not prompt participants to engage in higher levels of cognitive reasoning. There is no indication that being shown culturally congruent sequenced stimuli increased gullibility or lack of ability to engage in higher cognitive functioning. The rates of whether one answered the cognitive reasoning questions with either intuitive thinking or careful cognitive functioning were not determined by the arrangement of the stimuli.

Our analyses testing moderation by gender remain inconclusive because the sample size fell under the sample size required for adequate statistical power. The PROCESS analysis did indicate that gender does serve as a moderator for the effect. Perhaps with a larger sample size, we could understand the interaction effect of gender on the order of collocation sequences triggering higher cognitive reasoning, but the data are too inconclusive to make any assumptions now. It is worth mentioning that although our data indicate different results for male and female participants, our sample size was not large enough for either gender to make any conclusions.

Liking Stimuli

Although we predicted that participants would be more inclined to like both the groupspecific and neutral stimuli when in the fluent group as opposed to the other two, we found a different significant effect: participants prefer the paired stimuli over the individual, non-paired stimuli. With the fluent and disfluent group rating the paired stimuli significantly higher than the control rating the individual similar stimuli, we understand that people are more inclined to like images when showing a pair that seems natural. To use the "peanut butter and jelly" example, people find an image more appealing when shown peanut butter and jelly as opposed to peanut butter on its own. The minimal difference between the treatment groups provides us insight into how people may perceive collocation sequences visually. As previously discussed, the visual order does not matter, but the pairing of associated ideas itself is more appealing than individual stimuli.

Our hypothesis predicted that showing participants these paired, culturally fluent stimuli would prompt them to be in a state where they would be more inclined to like all stimuli, even when it does not contain a culturally fluent pairing. The data do not support this, as neutral stimuli were given similar ratings as group-specific stimuli. Strangely enough, the opposite predicted effect takes place for male participants rating the neutral stimuli lower in the fluent group, but it is a small enough difference with a small enough sample size that it is likely due to chance.

One could make the argument that the paired arrangements are not more appealing, but rather simply that there are two images instead of one. I would argue against this since the control group means for the neutral stimuli are higher than the mean rating for the group-specific stimuli. The participants would have no way of knowing that there is another group that shows two images together, so it is possible that the images I chose for the neutral stimuli were simply more appealing.

The directionality of the means is interesting here. We would have expected all ratings for the control group (group-specific and the neutral stimuli) to be relatively consistent, but with the group-specific stimuli in the fluent group and the disfluent group to be higher, and so this would indicate a higher liking of the paired arrangements. Instead, the data reveal that there is a consistent rating for the group-specific stimuli in the treatment groups as there is for the neutral stimuli, but that the group-specific stimuli for the control group are rated lower. This provides the information, not necessarily that participants like the paired arrangements more, but that they like the individual images that otherwise would have been in a paired arrangement less when they are alone.

I would like to entertain the idea that when participants saw an object that usually comes in a pair or is associated with another concept, then they find the image less appealing when it is on its own because it does not appear to be whole, or that, subconsciously, one feels it is missing the other image. It is possible that when one is shown a picture or a boat, they may think "that is a nice boat," but when shown a picture of peanut butter, they think "where is jelly?"

To put it simply, we can infer that people find images of associated items appealing when they are presented together.

Limitations

One limitation with internal validity is that we have no way of knowing whether the presence of the stimuli in the control group (such as a lone picture of a burger) may have an adverse effect, making a subject more or less gullible when presented with the scale items. I tried to alleviate this problem by making sure that the stimuli in the control group were shown in the experimental groups as part of image pairs as well. On top of that, the time difference and lack of visual stimuli when comparing the control group and experimental groups may cause one to sway more to one way of gullibility than the other due to lack of fatigue in the control group, as image pairs require one to look at more stimuli, which may tire a participant out more.

The external validity of my study is limited solely to undergraduate students who are native English speakers. While it is possible to generalize this to other ages, I feel that the generation currently in college has been raised with much more visual stimulation and advertising due to social media than other generations, and so a study about visual stimuli and advertising may not generalize much further.

One of the most significant limitations in this study, I believe, is the smaller sample size when splitting up the data into male and female. Due to the sample falling below the n = 82 required by the G-power analysis, we were unable to make any reasonable inferences when analyzing how males and females interacted with the experiment differently.

In terms of construct validity, one limitation may be that none of my measures of gullibility necessarily mean one's ability to be tricked or believe false information, but rather they demonstrate gullibility in the sense of lack of critical thought and ease of accepting answers.

In this sense, my measures provide strong internal validity for how the different groups may affect one's inherent acceptance and conclusions of information without critical thought, but they have very little internal validity when it comes to assessing how one can believe a deception, and least of all, the tendency to purchase goods based on an advertisement.

The CRT is often used in literature as more of a diagnostic measure of tendencies towards cognitive reasoning, acting as more of an independent variable than a dependent. This study is slightly different because it uses CRT to change it with group manipulation. It is possible that we could test the hypothesis with a scale that is less frequently used to diagnose a person's individual tendency to lean towards gut-based or high-level cognitive reasoning, and it could be more effective to use a scale that is created with the intention of being malleable when put into different situations.

Since 168 of the participants also speak a foreign language other than English, we have some insight into the fact that many of the participants, though being native English speakers, have interacted with other cultures through their family, and so, many of the items on the inherent list may not be relevant to the norms with which they are aware. It is possible that this test would have been more relevant in a setting away from a university, since so many participants could have been international students who were raised speaking English.

It is worth mentioning, however, that due to the inherence scale, questions about green being the color for money, pink being the color for girls, and aliens having two arms and legs scoring much lower on average than the other twelve, we should reassess how inherent these are to our culture. With changing gender norms, increased globalization, and the popularity of science fiction, these three questions may be obsolete in terms of identifying how inherent one's culture feels to them.

Finally, another limitation here is that participants may have perceived some of the pairings as chronological rather than verbally sequenced. For example, although "fries and burger" may be incongruent in terms of reading a collocation sequence left to right, if someone perceives time in terms of left to right, then they would often imagine that the fries would come first, and so, it would be on the left. This study had no indication or priming to make people think specifically in terms of visual reading sequences for associated words, and so our design assumed that it is always part of one's paradigm if they are experiencing stimuli that they perceive as having nothing to do with written words.

I think the general theme of limitations surrounding this study is that the stimuli were not strong enough to prime the participants to think any differently.

Conclusion & Future Implications

With the null results, we are not able to support any of our hypotheses that presenting collocation sequences in a culturally fluent order makes someone more susceptible to accepting cultural notions as inherent, nor does presenting the sequences in a culturally disfluent order prompt one to be more suspicious of culturally dominant notions or trigger higher levels of cognitive reasoning.

The results do demonstrate, however, that people find stimuli paired in associated arrangements more appealing than the same stimuli without the associated pairing. Although this does not offer guidance on how advertising may portray the directionality of stimuli, it does encourage advertisers to portray images with culturally associated objects because it would appeal to the viewer.

In the future, I would like to be able to apply these same principles and hypotheses to an experiment with stronger (or different) manipulation. For example, I would like to see if the susceptibility/gullibility paradigm would be effective if participants were to pronounce the stimuli, they saw in the order presented, or how the results would change if participants were to write out the objects they saw. Additionally, I am interested in the results may change if the pairings were presented in a timed sequence instead of right to left, as the screen shows only a burger, and then only fries, so the brain must register them in an individual order.

Although this experiment does not support that any form of collocation sequence directionality primes someone to think differently, I think there is still some potential in how this might help us discover how people's visual representations of language may change the way they perceive stimuli and how that prompts them to process information.
