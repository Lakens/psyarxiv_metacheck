Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate). This validation was made with an earlier, looser pattern; the pattern now requires “significant” or “significance” to follow the trigger word closely (#695).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. Full text

Abstract

Emotional prosody perception is vulnerable in later adulthood, yet the contributions of central aging and peripheral hearing loss remain unclear. Across two experiments, we combined eventrelated potentials (ERPs) , time-frequency analyses and behavioral measures to examine how aging and hearing sensitvity shape emotional prosody processing. Experiment 1 used acoustically matched speech and nonspeech stimuli in a passive oddball paradigm to assess preattentive processing. Experiment 2 examined explicit emotion identification and implicit stimulus judgment to evaluate goal-directed performance and its associations with neural responses. Aging was associated with reduced neural differentiation between emotions and lower behavioral efficiency, alongside enhanced P3a amplitude. Older adults also responded more slowly than younger adults during emotion identification. Hearing sensitivity showed no significant main effect on accuracy or reaction time, despite associations with delayed early neural processing and stronger theta-and alpha-band activity. Hearing-related neural effects were most evident in older adults and varied across emotions and stimulus types. Brain-behavior associations were selective: reduced mismatch negativity (MMN) was associated with slower responses during stimulus identification, whereas longer MMN latencies were associated with lower accuracy and slower responses during emotion identification. These findings indicate that aging and hearing loss have distinct yet interacting associations with emotional prosody processing, with neural responses not necessarily corresponding to behavioral performance. We propose an "asymmetric adaptation" account in which neural responses to degraded auditory input reorganize selectively across processing stages and contexts, potentially explaining this neural-behavioral divergence and informing more sensitive assessment of socioemotional communication in later adulthood.

Introduction

In daily communication, emotion is conveyed not only by what speakers say but also by how they say it, through variations in pitch, intensity, and speaking rate, which are collectively known as emotional prosody. Decoding these vocal cues is essential for effective social communication, yet this ability becomes increasingly vulnerable in later adulthood (Dupuis & Pichora-Fuller, 2015; Ruffman et al., 2008). Understanding this age-related vulnerability is important for explaining how socioemotional communication is maintained or disrupted in later life. A central challenge is that emotional prosody perception is shaped by both age-related changes in central neural processing and declines in peripheral hearing. While often studied in isolation, these factors exert partly distinct, yet dynamically interacting, effects on vocal emotion perception (Goy et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2026). How these central and peripheral factors interact across successive processing stages, and how their effects vary across individuals and contexts, remains insufficiently understood. Here, we addressed this gap by examing the joint influence of central processing resources and peripheral auditory ability on emotional prosody perception, with a particular focus on their dynamic interaction and adaptation across neural and behavioral levels.

Temporal Dynamics of Emotional Prosody Perception: From Neural Decoding to Behavior

Emotional prosody perception unfolds through multiple stages that progressively transform acoustic cues into emotional meaning and behavioral responses. In the multistage model proposed by Schirmer and Kotz (2006), processing begins with the automatic extraction of acoustic features, followed by the derivation of emotional significance and higher-order evaluation (Kotz & Paulmann, 2011). Subsequent work has further supported this view, showing that vocal emotion processing engages a distributed network spanning auditory and superior temporal regions as well as frontal and limbic systems, with different components contributing at different stages of analysis (Kotz & Paulmann, 2011; Liebenthal et al., 2016; Themistocleous, 2025).

Event-related potentials (ERPs) provide a precise window into this temporal architecture. In auditory oddball paradigms, mismatch negativity (MMN) indexes the automatic, preattentive detection of deviations from regular auditory patterns whereas the subsequent P3a reflects involuntary orienting toward novel or salient events (Näätänen et al., 2007). Both components have been shown to be reliably elicited by emotional prosodic changes, indicating that listeners can detect and orient toward emotionally relevant vocal information even when attention is not explicitly directed to emotion (Pakarinen et al., 2014). More recent work using naturally varying spoken materials further demonstrates that MMN and P3a can track emotional prosody beyond simple acoustic repetition, supporting their use as markers of successive stages of emotional speech processing (Kao & Zhang, 2023). Complementing waveform results, time-frequency analyses characterize the oscillatory dynamics that accompany these stages. Theta activity has been linked to the encoding and integration of emotionally salient information, whereas alpha activity is associated with attentional selection and controlled access to task-relevant representations (Symons et al., 2016). These oscillatory measures capture changes in neural engagement and processing effort that may not be fully reflected in ERP component amplitude or latency measures.

A related yet unresolved question is how these early neural processes relate to later behavioral performance. While early auditory responses predict speech perception outcomes in non-emotional domains (Koerner & Zhang, 2018), it remains unclear whether similar brain-behavior associations characterize emotional prosody, particularly across varying task contexts and considering individual differences in aging and hearing (Kao & Zhang, 2024; Lin et al., 2022).

Age-and Hearing-Related Changes in Emotional Prosody Perception

Age-related difficulties in emotional prosody perception likely reflect the combined effects of central aging and peripheral hearing decline. Older adults generally perform worse than younger adults in vocal emotion recognition (Dupuis & Pichora-Fuller, 2010 , 2015; Lima et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2024; Ruffman et al., 2008), while age-related hearing loss can further degrade the acoustic input available for processing (Peelle & Wingfield, 2016).

At the central level, aging is associated with structural and functional changes in frontal and temporal systems, slower information processing, and reduced executive f efficiency including working memory and cognitive control (Dexter & Ossmy, 2023; Raz et al., 2005). These changes may constrain the extraction, integration, and evaluation of emotional information, particularly under increased processing demands. However, neural aging does not necessarily entail uniformly reduced neural activity. The scaffolding theory of aging and cognition proposes that older adults recruit additional neural circuitry to support performance when primary systems become less efficient (Reuter-Lorenz & Park, 2014). Similarly, the compensation-related utilization of neural circuits hypothesis suggests that neural recruitment increases with task demand until available resources reach a limit, or "crunch point" (Reuter-Lorenz & Cappell, 2008). Consistent with these accounts, older adults exhibit greater or broader frontal recruitment across perceptual and cognitive tasks (Davis et al., 2007; Du et al., 2016). Thus, age-related neural differences may reflect both reduced processing efficiency and adaptive reorganization, depending on processing stage and task demands.

Peripheral hearing losss introduces an additional constraint. According to information degradation accounts, diminished sensory input reduces the fidelity of representations available for subsequent processing (Lindenberger & Baltes, 1994; Schneider & Pichora-Fuller, 2000). Age-related hearing loss can impair spectral and temporal resolution, potentially disrupting emotional prosody perception, which relies on variations in acoustic cues such as fundamental frequency (F0), intensity, and duration (Mitchell & Kingston, 2014; Peelle & Wingfield, 2016). The Framework for Understanding Effortful Listening (FUEL) further proposes that degraded auditory input increases the cognitive resources required to extract meaningful information (Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016). Consequently, hearing loss may increase neural processing demands even when overt performance remains relatively preserved.

Critically, central aging and peripheral hearing decline are unlikely to operate independently.

Sensory-cognitive coupling accounts propose that the consequences of sensory degradation

depend partly on the central resources available to compensate for reduced input quality (Lindenberger & Baltes, 1994). As auditory input becomes less reliable, successful perception may require greater reliance on attentional and cognitive resources (Peelle & Wingfield, 2016; Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016). Thus equivalent degrees of hearing decline may have different neural and behavioral consequences acress age groups. This interaction may help explain why hearing sensitivity accounts for some, but not all, age-related variance in vocal emotion perception (Goy et al., 2018; Lambrecht et al., 2012; Lin et al., 2026).

These perspectives point to an important theoretical distinction. A simple decline account would predict broadly reduced neural activity and behavioral performance with advancing aging and hearing loss. An adaptation account, in contrast, allows degraded sensory input or reduced central efficiency to be accompanied by altered or enhanced neural engagement, with behavioral consequences depending on whether such adjustment is sufficient under a given level of demand.

Examining age and hearing jointly across successive processing stages is therefore essential for determining when decline predominates, when adaptive neural responses emerge, and under what conditions these processes remain effective.

Contextual Modulation of Age-and Hearing-Related Effects

The magnitude and form of age-and hearing-related changes are further modulated by contextual factors, which define the conditions under which decline or adaptation becomes most apparent.

First, emotion category is a key source of modulation. According to the threat-advantage account, negative signals such as anger receive rapid, bottom-up priority (Grandjean et al., 2005; Schirmer & Escoffier, 2010). By contrast, socioemotional selectivity theory proposes that older adults increasingly prioritize positive information to optimize emotional experience (Carstensen & DeLiema, 2018; Carstensen & Mikels, 2005; Reed et al., 2014). Recent electrophysiological evidence suggests that aging and hearing loss may enhance early preattentive responses to anger while attenuating attentional orienting to happiness (Zhang et al., 2024). Behavioral findings likewise show pronounced age-related difficulty in discriminating angry and happy prosody, with age-related changes contributing substantially to this decline (Irino et al., 2024), highlighting the need to examine whether age-and hearing-related differences in neural and behavioral measures are specific to emotion categories.

Second, stimulus type provides a critical boundary condition. Although emotion perception of both speech and nonspeech signals declines with age (Amorim et al., 2021; Picou, 2016), the two stimulus classes rely on partly distinct processing mechanisms (Ekberg et al., 2022; Kamiloglu et al., 2021). Nonspeech signals depend more on global acoustic cues and automatic procesing, whereas speech contains rapid spectrotemporal variation and lexical-semantic information, placing greater demands on both peripheral hearing and higher-order central processing (Holt, 2025). A key limitation for a comparative analysis is that emotional speech and nonspeech stimuli generally show large differences in F0, duration, intensity, spectral envelope, and other low-level acoustic properties (Lin et al., 2025a; Lin et al., 2025b), which makes it difficult to isolate the relative contributions of peripheral and central processing. Direct comparison under closely matched acoustic conditions is therefore critical.

Third, task demands provide a further source of modulation. Explicit emotion judgments require direct evaluation of affective meaning, whereas implicit tasks shift attention toward nonemotional properties, engaging different attentional and decisional demands (Fruhholz et al., 2012).

Under high-demand explicit conditions, aging and hearing loss can independently and additively reduce accuracy and prolong response times (Christensen et al., 2019), while early automatic sensory responses may remain relatively preserved (Maltezou-Papastylianou et al., 2022). This distinction is especially relevant in Mandarin, where F0 serves dual lexical-tone and emotional functions.

The Present Study

The present study examined how central processing resources (indexed by age), and peripheral input quality (indexed by hearing sensitivity) jointly shape Mandarin emotional prosody perception across neural and behavioral levels. Moving beyond single-factor accounts, we tested how this central-peripheral interplay unfolds across processing stages and whether it is further modulated by emotion category, stimulus type, and task demands.

A key strength of the design was the integration of multiple contextual factors under tightly controlled stimulus conditions. In Experiment 1, angry and happy speech and nonspeech stimuli were closely matched on basic acoustic properties (Lin et al., 2025b) and presented in a doubleoddball paradigm (Koerner & Zhang, 2018). We combined ERP temporal and time-frequency analyses to characterize preattentive processing (MMN) and subsequent involuntary attentional orienting (P3a) in younger and older adults, alongside with theta-and alpha-band activity indexing processing demands and attentional allocation (Symons et al., 2016). Experiment 2 complemented these neural measures with explicit emotion classification and implicit stimulus judgment, allowing us to assess how age, hearing, and contextual factors shape behavior under different attentional demands and whether early neural responses predict later behavioral performance. We tested four specific hypotheses. First, advancing age was expected to be associated with a neural-behavioral dissociation, characterized by poorer behavioral performance alongside enhanced or prolonged neural responses, potentially reflecting compensatory recruitment, particularly under higher task demands (Ruffman et al., 2008). Second, poorer hearing sensitivity was expected to be associated with reduced processing efficiency and increased neural processing demands, reflected in delayed mismatch negativity (MMN) latency and stronger theta-and alphaband activity (Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016; Schneider & Pichora-Fuller, 2000). Third, age and hearing were expected to interact (Lindenberger & Baltes, 1994), with their combined effects varying across emotion categories (Carstensen & DeLiema, 2018; Grandjean et al., 2005), stimulus types (Lin et al., 2025a; Lin et al., 2025b), and task demands (Maltezou-Papastylianou et al., 2022).

Fourth, early preattentive neural responses were expected to be associated with later behavioral performance (Koerner & Zhang, 2018). We further explored whether these associations varied by task, considering the possibility that explicit emotion identification might yield stronger neuralbehavioral associations than implicit stimulus identification.

Experiment 1: Effects of Age and Hearing on Early Emotional Prosody Perception: Neurophysiological Evidence from Preattentive Processing

Experiment 1 employed neurophysiological techniques to examine how age-related central changes and peripheral hearing loss independently and interactively influence the early automatic processing of emotional prosody under passive listening.

Methods

Transparency and Openness

To promote transparency and openness, we report participant recruitment, sample size determination, experimental procedures, measures, preprocessing procedures, and data exclusion criteria for Experiments 1 and 2. The study design and analyses were not preregistered. EEG data were preprocessed in MATLAB (Version R2016a) using EEGLAB (Version 14.1.2), and ERP measures were derived using ERPLAB (Version 7.0). Statistical analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.4.3), using the lme4, brms, emmeans, and buildmer packages, as described below. Study materials, data and analysis codes are available through the OSF repository: https://osf.io/pnm5k/overview?view_only=a30aca05342345a2b7d37034644157b5

Participants

The study was approved by XXX Ethics Committee. All participants provided written informed consent before testing. The final sample included 62 native Mandarin Chinese speakers: 36 younger adults (18 women; Mage = 22.08 years, SD = 3.81) and 26 older adults (17 women; Mage = 65.63 years, SD = 7.77) recruited from the local community. Data were collected between 2023 and 2025.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using the simr package in R (Green & MacLeod, 2016). Based on pilot data from three younger and three older participants, Monte Carlo simulations (1,000 iterations per step) indicated that 20 participants provided 80.9% power (95% CI [78.3%, 83.3%]) to detect the highest-order four-way (Stimulus Type × Emotion × Group × Hearing) interaction, whereas a total sample of 40 was estimated to provide more than 95% power under the assumptions of the pilot-based model. Because these estimates were derived from a small pilot sample, they are subject to substantial uncertainty and may not generalize to the final sample or all effects of interest. We therefore recruited 62 participants to increase the sample size beyond the initial estimates.

All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, reported no history of speech, language, hearing, or psychiatric disorders, and had fewer than five years of formal musical training. None were professional musicians. Hearing sensitivity was assessed using standard audiometry and indexed by the mean pure-tone thresholds at 1 and 2 kHz (Koerner & Zhang, 2018; Oates et al., 2002). Mean PTA was 3.64 dB in younger adults and 19.05 dB in older adults. Because this measure sampled only two frequencies, it was used as an index of hearing sensitivity at those frequencies rather than a comprehensive measure of hearing across the audiometric range.

Demographic and hearing characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Stimuli

The stimuli were adapted from previous studies with younger adults and consisted of emotional speech and acoustically matched nonspeech sounds (Lin et al., 2025a; Lin et al., 2025b).

A young female native Mandarin speaker produced the semantically neutral syllable "bo" with neutral, angry, and happy prosody in a quiet booth (44.1 kHz, 16-bit). All tokens were edited to 300 ms and normalized to 75 dB SPL.

Twelve tokens were recorded per emotion and validated by 14 younger adults who did not participate in the main experiment (7 women; Mage = 23.71, SD = 2.12). Participants identified each emotion and rated its intensity on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not intense,5 = very intense).

Tokens achieving at least 90% recognition accuracy were retained, and the highest-rated token from each category was selected. Mean intensity ratings were 4.86 (SD = 0.35) for anger, 4.6 (SD = 0.48) for happiness, and 3.21 (SD = 0.77) for neutral prosody. Anger and happiness were rated as more intense than neutral prosody (both p values < .001), and the two emotional categories did did not differ significantly in intensity (p = .189). Nonspeech stimuli were generated by extracting the fundamental frequency contours of the selected speech tokens and converting them into hums, thereby preserving F0, amplitude, and duration. Root-mean-square intensity was normalized across all stimuli to equate sound intensity.

Procedures

EEG was recorded in a sound-attenuated, electrically shielded booth using a NeuroScan system at 1000 Hz. Participants wore a 64-electrode cap arranged according to the international 10-20 system, with AFz as ground, an online reference between Cz and CPz, four EOG electrodes for monitoring eye movements, and bilateral mastoid electrodes for offline rereferencing.

Electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ. During Experiment 1, participants watched a muted documentary on a laptop positioned 70 cm away while ignoring auditory stimuli presented binaurally through E-A-R TONE™ 3A insert earphones at 75 dB SPL.

Speech and nonspeech stimuli were presented in separate conditions using a double-oddball paradigm (Koerner et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2005) implemented in E-Prime 3.0.

In the speech condition (Figure 1), the neutral "bo" served as the standard stimulus (70%), whereas angry and happy tokens served as deviants (15% each). Across 10 blocks of 100 trials, this yielded 700 standard trials and 150 trials for each deviant. The angry and happy tokens were then presented as standards in alternating blocks, yielding 150 repetitions of each stimulus. This allowed responses to physically identical stimuli presented as deviants and standards to be contrasted, thereby minimizing acoustic confounds (Kraus et al., 1995). The nonspeech condition followed the same procedure with acoustically matched hums. Each stimulus lasted 300 ms, with a randomly jittered interstimulus interval of 600-700 ms. Participants rested for 10 s between blocks and 30 s between conditions. The full EEG session lasted approximately 50 min.

EEG Preprocessing

EEG data were preprocessed in MATLAB (Version R2016a) using EEGLAB (Version 14.1.2) (Delorme & Makeig, 2004). Data were down-sampled to 250 Hz, band-pass filtered at 0.1-40 Hz, and cleaned using Clean-rawdata and independent component analysis. Epochs (-100 to 700 ms) relative to stimulus onset were extracted and baseline-corrected. Data were then re-referenced to the average of the mastoid electrodes. Residual artifacts were rejected if voltage changes exceeded ±30 μV within a 200-ms sliding window (50-ms steps) or if absolute amplitudes exceeded ±100 μV. This procedure resulted in a 3.26% trial rejection rate on average across all participants and conditions combined.

ERPs were averaged using ERPLAB (Version 7.0). Identity difference waves were computed to avoid physical acoustic confounds by subtracting standard from deviant waveforms for each emotion and stimulus type (Koerner et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2005). MMN and P3a were analyzed across nine frontocentral electrodes (F3, Fz, F4, FC3, FCz, FC4, C3, Cz, C4). MMN and P3a latencies were defined as the most negative peak at 100-250 ms and the most positive peak at 200-350 ms, respectively (Duncan et al., 2009; Lin et al., 2025a; Paris et al., 2018). Mean amplitudes were extracted within ±40 ms of the corresponding peak.

Time-frequency analysis used EEGLAB's newtimef function to compute event-related spectral perturbations (ERSPs) over epochs extending from -100 to 700 ms. The analysis covered frequencies from 0.1 to 40 Hz, with 0.5-Hz steps, using short-time Fourier transforms. Maximum theta-band (4-7.9 Hz) and alpha-band (8-11.9 Hz). Maximum theta (4-7.9 Hz) and alpha (8-11.9 Hz) ERSP values were extracted at Fz within the MMN (100-250 ms) and P3a (200-350 ms) windows.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio using linear mixed-effects (LME) models fitted with lme4 (Bates et al., 2015). Separate models were specified for MMN and P3a amplitude and latency and for theta-and alpha-band ERSP. Fixed effects included Stimulus Type (speech vs. nonspeech), Emotion (happy vs. angry), Group (older vs. younger), and Hearing (meanPTA), with participant as a random intercept. Hearing sensitivity was modeled as a continuous predictor to examine its association with neural measures beyond categorical age-group differences. Because hearing sensitivity differed between age groups, we evaluated the potential for collinearity between age group and PTA using using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The VIF was 1.68, below conventional thresholds for problematic multicollinearity. Significant effects were followed by Tukey-adjusted comparisons using emmeans for categorical predictors and emtrends for Hearing (Lenth, 2020). Where interactions involving hearing sensitivity were supported, simple slopes were examined at the relevant levels of the categorical predictors.

Bayesian models with identical structures were fitted using brms (Bürkner, 2021). Each model was run with four chains and 10,000 iterations per chain, including 5,000 warm-up iterations.

Fixed-effect priors were Normal(0, 3) for waveform measures and Normal(0, 5) for timefrequency measures. Half-Cauchy priors were specified for random-effect and residual standard deviations (scales 5 and 2.5, respectively for waveform measures; scales of 5 for both parameters in time-frequency measures) (Baldwin & Larson, 2017). Convergence was confirmed by Rhat values below 1.1 and bulk and tail effective sample sizes above 200.

Bayes factors (BF10) were obtained through model comparison, with values above 3 indicating evidence for the alternative hypothesis and below 1/3 favoring the null (Lee & Wagenmakers, 2013). Prior sensitivity analyses using half and twice the original widths yielded consistent evidence and conclusions. Given the study focus, we mainly reported Group and Hearing main effects and the highest-order interactions involving these predictors in the following Results section; post hoc effects are interpreted when supported by both frequentist significance and sufficient Bayesian evidence. Where frequentist and Bayesian evidence diverged, this discrepancy was considered in interpreting the findings rather than treating statistical significance alone as sufficient evidence for an effect.

Results

ERP Waveform Results

Mixed-effects model results for the ERP waveform measures are reported in Table S1. Grandaverage ERP waveforms for the younger and older groups are shown in Figure 2.

MMN Amplitude

There was no main effect of Group on MMN amplitude (χ² (1) = 0.02, p = 0.891, BF10 = 0.07). The main effect of Hearing was significant (χ² (1) = 4.61, p = 0.032, BF10 = 0.38), but lacked sufficient Bayesian support.

The Emotion × Hearing interaction was significant (χ² (1) = 24.58, p < 0.001, BF10 >1000).

The Emotion × Group interaction was significant in the frequentist analysis, but the Bayesian evidence favored the null hypothesis to some extent (χ² (1) = 4.37, p = 0.037, BF10 = 0.24).

Stimulus × Group, Stimulus × Hearing, and Group × Hearing were not significant. Significant three-way interactions were observed for Stimulus × Emotion × Hearing (χ² (1) = 22.45, p < 0.001, BF10 >1000) and Stimulus × Group × Hearing (χ² (1) = 29.02, p < 0.001, BF10 >1000), whereas Stimulus × Emotion × Group and Emotion × Group × Hearing were not significant.

The four-way Stimulus × Emotion × Group × Hearing interaction was also significant (Figure 3(a); Table S2; χ² (1) = 8.72, p = 0.003, BF10 = 6.31). In older adults, lower hearing thresholds were associated with more negative MMN amplitudes for angry nonspeech stimuli (t(74) = 2.69, p = 0.009, d = 0.62), happy nonspeech stimuli (t(74) = 2.90, p = 0.005, d = 0.67), and angry speech stimuli (t(74) = 4.32, p < 0.001, d = 1.00), but not for happy speech stimuli (p = 0.927). No significant hearing effects were observed in younger adults. Hearing slopes differed between age groups for angry (p = 0.027) and happy (p = 0.005) nonspeech stimuli, but not for either speech condition.

MMN Latency

For MMN latency (Table S3; Figure 3(b)), the main effect of Group was significant (χ² (1) = 5.47, p = 0.019), although Bayesian support was weak (BF10 = 1.72). Experiment 2: Effects of Age and Hearing on Emotional Prosody Judgments: Behavioral Evidence from Explicit and Implicit Emotion Perception

Experiment 1 investigated the preattentive neural processing of emotional prosody. In Experiment 2, we extended this analysis to later, goal-directed behavior by examining how age and hearing interact with task demands, emotion category, and stimulus type, and whether early neural responses are associated with subsequent behavioral performance.

Methods

The transparency and openness statement provided in Experiment 1 also applies to Experiment 2.

Participants and Stimuli

Participants were drawn from the same sample as in Experiment 1. One older adult was excluded from behavioral and brain-behavior analyses due to incomplete behavioral data.

Including this participant did not alter the behavioral results. The final sample comprised 61 participants (36 younger and 25 older adults).

The auditory stimuli were taken from those in Experiment 1, except that Experiment 2 included only angry and happy speech and acoustically matched nonspeech stimuli.

Procedure

The behavioral experiment was conducted after the ERP session in a sound-attenuated room.

Stimuli were presented binaurally through Sennheiser HD280 PRO headphones at 70 dB SPL using PsychoPy (Version 2022.2.4). Participants completed two tasks: an emotion identification task and a stimulus identification task. In the emotion identification task, they classified each auditory stimulus as angry or happy; in the stimulus identification task, they classified each stimulus as speech or nonspeech. Each task contained 100 randomized trials, with 25 trials in each of four conditions: angry speech, happy speech, angry nonspeech, and happy nonspeech. This task structure followed previous work examining explicit emotion and stimulus-focused judgments (Lin et al., 2025b).

Each trial began with a 500-ms fixation cross, followed by the auditory stimulus (Figure 6).

Participants responded according to the current task while accuracy and reaction time were recorded. In the emotion task, "V" and "M" corresponded to anger and happiness; in the stimulus task, "B" and "N" corresponded to speech and nonspeech. Response mappings were counterbalanced across participants. A 500-ms blank screen followed each response. Before each task, participants completed four practice trials and were required to reach 100% accuracy before proceeding. A 30-s break separated the two tasks.

Data Analysis

Accuracy and reaction time were analyzed using LME models. Accuracy was converted to rationalized arcsine units (RAUs) to reduce ceiling effects (Studebaker, 1985). Reaction-time analyses included only correct trials, and responses falling more than 2 SDs from each participant's mean reaction time were excluded (Baayen & Milin, 2010). This procedure excluded 17.36% of trials from older adults and 6.48% from younger adults. Reaction times were log-transformed to reduce positive skewness.

In the LME analyses, fixed effects included Task (emotion identification vs. stimulus identification), Stimulus Type (speech vs. nonspeech), Emotion (anger vs. happiness), Group (older vs. younger), and Hearing (mean pure-tone threshold at 1 and 2 kHz), together with their interactions. Participant was included as a random intercept. Models were fitted using the automated procedure in buildmer (Version 2.12) (Voeten, 2025). Effects were added based on likelihood-ratio contributions until convergence limits were reached, followed by backward elimination to obtain a parsimonious model. Significant Group and Hearing effects and their highest-order interactions were examined using Tukey-adjusted emmeans and emtrends, respectively.

Bayesian analyses followed the procedures and criteria described in Experiment 1. Fixedeffect priors were Normal(0, 3) for accuracy and Normal(0, 1) for reaction time, with Half-Cauchy priors for random-effect and residual standard deviations (scale 5 for accuracy and scale 1 for reaction time). Bayes factors (BF₁₀) were used to quantify evidence for the alternative hypothesis relative to the null hypothesis.

For brain-behavior analyses, mean MMN and P3a amplitudes and latencies were calculated for each Stimulus Type × Emotion condition and paired with task-specific accuracy and reaction time. Pearson correlations were computed between ERP and behavioral measures, with false discovery rate correction applied to account for multiple comparisons.

General Discussion

The present study examined how age and peripheral auditory sensitivity jointly relate to emotional prosody processing across neural and behavioral stages. The findings revealed a differentiated pattern rather than a uniform decline. Aging was associated with reduced temporal differentiation between anger and happiness, greater emotion-related differentiation in P3a amplitude, and subpar behavioral performance, especially during explicit emotion identification.

Hearing loss was also associated with delayed early auditory processing and stronger theta-and alpha-band activity, despite no clear evidence of hearing-related behavioral impairment. Hearingrelated neural effects also varied across age groups, emotions, stimulus types, and processing stages, although some higher-order effects warrant cautious interpretation. Finally, MMN measures were associated with later stages of emotional judgments in a task-dependent manner.

Together, these findings suggest that age and auditory sensitivity are associated with partially distinct but interacting patterns of neural and behavioral variation. We interpret this pattern through a proposed asymmetric adaptation framework, in which neural responses to changes in auditory input vary across processing stages, individuals, and contexts rather than following a uniform pattern of enhancement or decline.

Aging Reshapes Emotional Differentiation Across Processing Stages

The first hypothesis received partial support. Age effects were evident across neural timing, response magnitude, and behavioral performance, but these effects did not follow a concistent direction. MMN latency showed an age-related difference in the temporal differentiation of anger and happiness: the difference between emotions was larger in younger than older adults. This pattern is consistent with accounts proposing that negative vocal signals can receive preferential processing because of their potential biological and social significance (Charpentier et al., 2018; Grandjean et al., 2005; Kao & Zhang, 2024; Vuilleumier, 2005). The reduced emotion-related latency difference in older adults suggests that this temporal this temporal differentiation may become less pronounced with age. However, the present findings do not establish whether this change reflects reduced sensitivity to anger, altered processing of happiness, or a broader change in the temporal organization of emotional processing.

Socioemotional selectivity theory offers one possible perspective on these findings. It proposes that age-related changes in motivational priorities may influence how emotional information is attended to and evaluated, including a greater prioritization of positive information in some contexts (Carstensen & DeLiema, 2018; Carstensen & Mikels, 2005). Nevertheless, the present study did not directly measure emotional goals or motivational priorities, and a reduced temporal difference between anger and happiness does not by itself demonstrate a positivity effect.

The findings are therefore better understood as evidence that age-related changes in emotional prosody processing can vary by emotion and processing measure, consistent with previous research showing that vocal emotion perception does not necessarily deteriorate uniformly across emotion categories (Amorim et al., 2021; Ruffman et al., 2008).

At the same time, older adults showed greater differentiation between anger and happiness in P3a amplitude, with larger P3a responses to anger than happiness; this emotion difference was not observed in younger adults. The coexistence of reduced temporal differentiation and enhanced emotion-related amplitude modulation suggests that age effects depend on the neural measure and processing stage. In particular, the results do not support a simple account in which aging uniformly attenuates neural responses to emotional information.

One possible interpretation is that age-related changes in processing efficiency are accompanied by altered allocation of neural resources. Neurocognitive aging frameworks such as the Compensation-Related Utilization of Neural Circuits Hypothesis (CRUNCH) and the Scaffolding Theory of Aging and Cognition (STAC) propose that older adults may recruit neural resources differently to meet processing demands (Reuter-Lorenz & Cappell, 2008; Reuter-Lorenz & Park, 2014). The greater emotion-related P3a differentiation observed here is compatible with this possibility. However, enhanced neural differentiation is not necessarily evidence of successful compensation. Because the present study did not directly manipulate processing demands or demonstrate that greater P3a amplitude predicted better performance, it remains unclear whether the observed modulation reflects adaptive recruitment, altered attentional orienting, or another agerelated change in neural processing.

The behavioral findings further define the limits of this adaptation. Older adults were less accurate overall, and responded more slowly than younger adults in the emotion identification task across all four stimulus conditions. In contrast, age-group differences in reaction time were not observed in the stimulus identification task. This pattern suggests that age-related behavioral differences were particularly evident when participants had to map auditory cues onto emotional categories. Emotion identification may place greater demands on affective evaluation and decision-making than judgments about whether a stimulus is speech or nonspeech. This interpretation is consistent with CRUNCH, under which additional neural recruitment may support performance in some conditions with lower levels of demand but may be insufficient to prevent behavioral costs as task demands increase (Reuter-Lorenz & Cappell, 2008).

Importantly, the present results do not establish that emotion identification is universally more demanding than stimulus identification. The tasks differed in their decision criteria as well as their perceptual goals, and task difficulty was not independently manipulated. Nevertheless, the selective reaction-time disadvantage in emotion identification suggests that age-related behavioral differences depend on the nature of the judgment required. Taken together, the neural and behavioral findings indicate that aging is associated with changes in the temporal differentiation, attentional modulation, and explicit interpretation of emotional prosody, rather than a uniform reduction in emotional processing.

Hearing Sensitivity Is Associated With Neural Processing Demands Before Clear Behavioral Impairment

The second hypothesis was also partially supported. Higher PTA values, indicating poorer hearing sensitivity at 1-2 kHz, were associated with longer MMN latency and stronger theta-and alpha-band activity, but not with clear differences in behavioral accuracy or reaction time. Thus, hearing-related variation was more consistently expressed in neural measures than in overt task performance. The MMN latency findings suggest that auditory sensitivity was associated with the timing of early change detection. Better hearing sensitivity was associated with shorter MMN latency, particularly for angry stimuli. This emotion-specific relationship indicates that hearingrelated variation did not affect all emotional categories in the same way (Zhang et al., 2024).

However, the absence of a significant hearing association for happiness should not be interpreted as evidence that happy prosody is unaffected by hearing decline; differences in cue availability, measurement sensitivity, and statistical uncertainty remain possible explanations.

The time-frequency results provide complementary evidence. During the MMN window, higher PTA values were associated with stronger theta and alpha activity for speech stimuli, but not nonspeech stimuli. During the P3a window, poorer hearing sensitivity was associated with stronger activity in both frequency bands. These findings suggest that hearing-related neural variation may extend beyond early auditory change detection to later stages associated with attentional processing. Nevertheless, stronger oscillatory activity cannot be equated directly with greater cognitive effort or successful compensation. The functional significance of theta and alpha activity depends on the task, time window, and underlying neural processes, and the present design does not isolate these mechanisms.

Predictive-processing and effortful-listening accounts provide possible explanations for the observed pattern. Predictive coding proposes that perception involves generating expectations about sensory input and updating internal representations when incoming signals differ from those expectations (Heilbron & Chait, 2018). Reduced auditory fidelity could increase uncertainty about incoming signals and alter the neural responses associated with detecting or resolving discrepancies (Schilling et al., 2023). Likewise, FUEL proposes that degraded auditory input requires greater attentional and cognitive investment to maintain successful perception (Peelle & Wingfield, 2016; Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016). The present findings are compatible with these accounts insofar as poorer hearing sensitivity was associated with altered neural responses despite relatively preserved behavioral performance.

However, preserved performance does not demonstrate that additional neural activity successfully compensated for degraded input. Behavioral tasks may not have been sufficiently sensitive to reveal hearing-related differences, and participants may have relied on acoustic cues that remained available despite variation in hearing sensitivity. Moreover, hearing was indexed by thresholds at 1 and 2 kHz rather than by a comprehensive assessment of auditory function. The results should therefore be interpreted as associations between hearing sensitivity in the measured frequency range and neural processing, rather than as evidence that hearing loss generally produces increased neural effort without behavioral consequences.

Age and Hearing Interact in a Context-Dependent Pattern of Neural Responses

The third hypothesis concerned whether age and hearing sensitivity would jointly relate to emotional prosody processing. The findings provide evidence for such an interaction, particularly for MMN amplitude, although the pattern was not uniform across neural measures.

For MMN amplitude, the four-way interaction among stimulus type, emotion, age group, and hearing sensitivity was statistically significant and received Bayesian support. Follow-up analyses indicated that, among older adults, hearing sensitivity was associated with MMN amplitude for angry nonspeech, happy nonspeech, and angry speech stimuli, but not happy speech stimuli.

Comparable hearing associations were not observed in younger adults. These results suggest that the relationship between hearing sensitivity and early neural responses differed across age groups and stimulus conditions.

One possible interpretation is that age-related changes in central auditory processing alter the relationship between peripheral input and neural responses to auditory change (Slade et al., 2020). . This interpretation is broadly consistent with the sensory-cognitive coupling account, which proposes that sensory and cognitive functions become more closely interdependent with advancing age (Lindenberger & Baltes, 1994; Lindenberger & Ghisletta, 2009). Under this account, variation in auditory input may be associated with more pronounced differences in neural processing when central processing resources or sensory representations have also changed with age. However, the present study cannot establish that age-related central changes caused the observed hearing associations. Age group and hearing sensitivity were not experimentally manipulated, and the groups differed in their hearing profiles. The interaction may reflect differences in central processing, differences in the distribution of hearing sensitivity, or other age-related factors. Furthermore, the lack of significant hearing effects in younger adults does not establish that their auditory processing is unaffected by hearing variation. The evidence supports an age-dependent association in the measured neural responses, rather than a definitive mechanism of age-specific compensation.

This contextual variation in MMN amplitude also merits attention. Hearing-related associations differed between speech and nonspeech stimuli and between anger and happiness.

One possibility is that the neural consequences of reduced auditory fidelity depend on the acoustic information available in each condition. In Mandarin, fundamental frequency (F0) contributes to both lexical tone and emotional prosody, making the interpretation of pitch variation particularly important. Recent work suggests that lexical tone, hearing sensitivity, and age jointly shape Mandarin emotional prosody perception (Fan et al., 2025), and that aging alters the weighting of pitch and other acoustic cues rather than simply reducing overall sensitivity (Chatterjee et al., 2026). Happy prosody relies heavily on F0 variation, whereas anger can additionally draw on intensity and spectral cues (Nussbaum et al., 2022), potentially providing older listeners with a broader cue set when auditory input is degraded. Hearing-related adaptation may therefore depend not only on auditory sensitivity, but also on the language-specific distribution and diagnostic values of acoustic cues. These considerations offer plausible explanations for the observed stimulus-and emotiondependent patterns, but the present study did not independently manipulate acoustic cues or quantify their diagnostic value. In addition, because hearing was assessed only at 1-2 kHz, the study could not comprehensively characterize sensitivity to the full range of frequencies relevant to emotional prosody. The proposed role of cue availability should therefore be regarded as a hypothesis for future testing rather than a demonstrated explanation of the current findings.

The four-way interactions observed for P3a amplitude and latency require additional caution.

Although both interactions reached frequentist significance, the P3a latency interaction received weak Bayesian support, and the significant P3a amplitude interaction was not accompanied by significant condition-specific hearing slopes or between-group slope differences. These findings do not provide strong evidence for a specific pattern of hearing-related adaptation at the P3a stage. Instead, they suggest that higher-order effects may vary across measures and that individual follow-up comparisons are important when interpreting omnibus interactions.

Together, the findings motivate an asymmetric adaptation hypothesis: neural responses to degradation in auditory input are not uniformly enhanced or diminished. Instead, the relationship between hearing sensitivity and neural processing varies across processing stages, age groups, and stimulus contexts. The clearest evidence emerged in early neural processing, where hearing-related associations differed across age groups and stimulus conditions. Oscillatory activity also varied with hearing sensitivity, whereas behavioral measures showed little direct association with hearing. This framework extends simple sensory degradation accounts by emphasizing that the consequences of reduced auditory fidelity may depend on the interaction between sensory input, age-related processing differences, and the characteristics of the information being processed.

Nevertheless, the present findings establish selective patterns of neural association, not a direct causal process of adaptation. Future studies that manipulate auditory degradation and processing demands will be needed to determine whether these patterns reflect compensatory recruitment, altered sensory encoding, or other mechanisms.

From Early Auditory Encoding to Behavior: Task-Dependent Associations

The fourth hypothesis proposed that early neural responses would be associated with subsequent behavioral performance, potentially more strongly during explicit emotion identification. The findings partially supported this prediction and revealed a task-dependent pattern. MMN amplitude was positively associated with reaction time in the stimulus identification task, such that less negative MMN amplitudes were associated with slower responses. In contrast, MMN latency was associated with both accuracy and reaction time during emotion identification: longer latency was associated with lower accuracy and slower responses. These associations were not observed in the corresponding alternative tasks. No significant associations were found between P3a measures and behavioral performance.

The task-specific findings suggest that different properties of early auditory processing may be relevant to different behavioral judgments. During stimulus identification, participants judged whether a sound was speech or nonspeech. The association between MMN amplitude and reaction time is consistent with the possibility that the strength of the neural response to auditory change is related to the efficiency of subsequent stimulus classification. During emotion identification, participants had to interpret the auditory signal in affective terms. The associations between MMN latency and both accuracy and reaction time suggest that the timing of early neural processing may be related to the efficiency of later emotional judgments.

These interpretations remain tentative. The correlational design does not establish that MMN responses determine behavioral performance, and the absence of significant associations in the alternative tasks does not necessarily imply that the correlations differ significantly between tasks. Furthermore, because multiple brain-behavior associations were examined, their interpretation should take account of the correction for multiple comparisons and the magnitude and precision of the observed associations.

The absence of significant P3a-behavior associations also suggests that not every neural measure of preattentive processing is directly related to later task performance. P3a may reflect processes that are less directly associated with the specific accuracy and reaction-time measures used here, or the observed associations may have been too small to detect reliably. These findings are consistent with accounts in which early auditory encoding provides a foundation for higherorder socioemotional interpretation, while the relationship between neural processing and behavior depends on the processes indexed and the demands of the task (Fruhholz et al., 2012; Grandjean, 2020; Koerner & Zhang, 2018). They also reinforce the importance of examining multiple neural measures and behavioral tasks rather than assuming a single, uniform relationship between preattentive processing and emotional judgments.

Theoretical and Practical Implications

The present study contributes to theoretical accounts of auditory and socioemotional aging in three ways. First, the findings indicate that age and hearing sensitivity are associated with distinct but interacting patterns of neural processing, with effects varying across neural measures, stimulus types, and emotions. Second, the divergence between neural and behavioral findings challenges a simple linear account in which poorer hearing sensitivity or older age necessarily produces parallel reductions in neural responses and behavioral performance. Third, the task-specific MMNbehavior associations suggest that early auditory processing measures may provide information about later emotional judgments that is not captured by behavioral measures alone.

We propose an asymmetric adaptation hypothesis to integrate these findings by emphasizing that neural responses to variation in auditory input may depend on processing stage, individual characteristics, and context. It complements sensory-degradation and neurocognitive aging frameworks by drawing attention to the heterogeneous patterns that can emerge when peripheral auditory sensitivity and age-related changes in processing are considered together (Peelle & Wingfield, 2016; Reuter-Lorenz & Cappell, 2008; Schneider & Pichora-Fuller, 2000). At this stage, however, the framework should be viewed as a theoretical proposal that organizes the observed findings, rather than as an established model of the mechanisms underlying auditory aging.

The results also have potential implications for assessment and rehabilitation. Neural measures such as MMN latency and oscillatory activity were associated with hearing sensitivity even when clear behavioral associations were absent. This raises the possibility that neural measures could complement behavioral assessments when evaluating auditory-emotional processing in later adulthood (Chow et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). However, the present study did not evaluate diagnostic accuracy, clinical utility, or responsiveness to intervention. Further work is therefore needed before these measures can be recommended as clinical indicators. More broadly, the findings support investigating whether auditory rehabilitation can improve not only speech intelligibility but also the perception of emotional information that contributes to everyday social communication (Picou et al., 2018).

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow us to distinguish age-related change from cohort differences or to establish how auditory processing changes within individuals over time. The older group also showed greater variability in age and hearing sensitivity, and the study did not include a middle-aged group. Longitudinal studies and samples spanning a wider age range would help clarify the trajectories of auditory and socioemotional processing across adulthood. In addition, age group and hearing sensitivity were associated in the present sample, limiting the extent to which their contributions can be fully separated. Future research should recruit participants with greater overlap in hearing sensitivity across age groups and incorporate direct measures of cognitive functioning, such as processing speed and working memory, to better characterize the central processes associated with age and auditory sensitivity (Amorim et al., 2021).

Second, hearing sensitivity was indexed by mean pure-tone thresholds at 1 and 2 kHz.

Although these frequencies provide information about sensitivity within a speech-relevant portion of the audiometric range, they do not capture the full frequency profile of hearing or all auditory abilities relevant to emotional prosody. In particular, the study did not comprehensively assess sensitivity to higher-frequency cues or other aspects of auditory processing that may contribute to vocal emotion perception. The findings should therefore be interpreted in relation to the measured PTA rather than generalized to hearing loss in all its forms. Future studies should include fullrange audiometry and, where appropriate, measures of suprathreshold auditory function to determine how different aspects of hearing contribute to emotional prosody processing.

Third, the stimuli were restricted to angry and happy syllables and their acoustically matched nonspeech counterparts. Although this design facilitated comparisons across stimulus types, it limits generalization to natural speech, other emotional categories, and everyday communication.

Future work could incorporate words, sentences, a wider range of basic and complex emotions, and systematic manipulations of acoustic features, including F0, temporal envelope, and temporal fine structure (Moore, 2019). Such designs would help establish whether the observed contextdependent associations reflect differences in acoustic cue availability, emotion-related processing, or their interaction.

Fourth, the EEG measures provide high temporal resolution but limited spatial information about the neural systems involved. Future studies combining EEG with fMRI or fNIRS could help identify the networks contributing to age-and hearing-related variation in emotional prosody processing. In addition, more naturalistic listening conditions, including background noise and audiovisual emotional cues, would help establish whether the present effects generalize to communication settings with greater perceptual and cognitive demands (Dor et al., 2025).

Finally, the findings should be interpreted in light of the study's sample size and the number of interactions examined. Although the sample permitted the analysis of age, hearing sensitivity, and their interactions, higher-order effects and condition-specific associations may be estimated with limited precision. Future studies with larger samples, preregistered hypotheses, and independent replication will be important for establishing the robustness of the proposed asymmetric adaptation framework. Such studies should also test alternative explanations for enhanced neural activity, including compensatory recruitment, altered sensory encoding, and differences in attentional allocation.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that age and hearing sensitivity are associated with emotional prosody processing in distinct but interacting ways across neural and behavioral stages. Aging was associated with reduced temporal differentiation between emotions, enhanced emotion-related P3a modulation, and lower behavioral accuracy, whereas poorer hearing sensitivity was associated with delayed MMN responses and stronger theta-and alpha-band activity, without clear behavioral impairment. Hearing-related neural effects varied across age groups, emotions, and stimulus types, and MMN-behavior associations were task dependent. These findings argue against a uniform decline account and motivate an asymmetric adaptation framework in which neural responses to auditory input vary across processing stages, individuals, and contexts.
