Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

A large developmental literature finds that children often fail to compute scalar implicatures, accepting underinformative descriptions such as, "Some of the horses jumped" when all of them did, or, "The bear took a cup or a plate" when he took both. Here, we argue that past studies find such failures because they do not evaluate children's spontaneous interpretation of utterances, but instead ask how they interpret language when provided proffered "worlds" that children would not consider otherwise. We show that when children are allowed the chance to act upon their own spontaneous interpretation of utterances, they compute scalar implicatures in an adult-like fashion. Specifically, we tested children in two conditions: a standard Truth Value Judgment (TVJ) condition, similar to past studies, and a novel, two-phase Act-Out+TVJ condition in which children first acted on the utterance by coloring pictures before judging a puppet's action. In the act-out task children almost never colored both disjuncts after hearing "or" (Experiment 1) and rarely colored all four objects after hearing "some" (Experiment 2). More importantly, after acting out these interpretations, children computed scalar implicatures in an adult-like fashion, and significantly more often than children in the TVJ-only condition. These findings suggest that preschoolers spontaneously access relevant alternative utterances and compute scalar implicatures when allowed to generate their own interpretations before evaluating experimenter-provided worlds, and that previous studies underestimate this ability by artificially proffering irrelevant worlds.

[div-01]

Speakers often intend to communicate information that goes beyond the literal meanings of the utterances that they use. For example, upon hearing a sentence like, "Lucy fed some of the horses in the field," adult listeners typically infer that Lucy did not feed all of them, even though the literal meaning of "some" is compatible with feeding every horse in the field. Similarly, upon hearing a sentence like, "Lucy fed the cow or the horse," adult listeners typically derive an exclusive interpretation, inferring that Lucy fed one of the two animals, but not both, although the literal meaning of disjunction is compatible with a situation in which Lucy fed both animals.

In both cases, listeners assume that a knowledgeable speaker would have used a stronger alternative, "Lucy fed all of the horses" or "Lucy fed the cow and the horse," if either were true.

These inferences, called scalar implicatures, pose an interesting problem, because they require children to infer the speaker's intended meaning by reasoning about statements that were not actually uttered, to rule out alternative possible meanings and corresponding possible worlds.

Interestingly, a large developmental literature finds that children struggle to compute such pragmatic inferences well into their elementary school years. Often, these studies test participants using Truth Value Judgment or Felicity Judgment tasks, in which children are presented with a scene and an underinformative utterance, and are asked to judge if the utterance is a good description. For example, in one study Papafragou and Musolino (2003) presented children with a scene in which three horses jumped over a fence, and children were asked to evaluate the felicity of the description, "Some of the horses jumped over the fence." They found that, unlike adults, who generally rejected such statements, children almost always accepted them (see also Gualmini et al., 2001; Noveck, 2001; Guasti et al., 2005; Barner et al., 2011; Huang & Snedeker, 2009; Foppolo et al., 2012; Papafragou & Tantalou, 2004). Similarly, studies of disjunction report that children accept underinformative disjunctive statements like, "The bear took a cup or a plate," when both disjuncts are true, whereas adults generally do not (e.g., Chierchia et al., 2001; Hochstein et al., 2016; Skordos et al., 2020; Huang & Crain, 2020; Jasbi & Frank, 2021).

Multiple accounts of children's failure to compute implicatures have been proposed, pointing to limited pragmatic reasoning abilities (Noveck, 2001), processing limits (Chierchia et al., 2001; Reinhart, 2006; Pouscoulous et al., 2007; Huang & Snedeker, 2009), difficulties understanding the communicative goals of speakers (Papafragou & Musolino, 2003; Papafragou & Tantalou, 2004), a generalized tolerance for pragmatic infelicity on the part of children (Katsos & Bishop, 2011), incomplete knowledge of quantifier meanings (Horowitz et al., 2018).

These accounts also differ in whether they treat implicatures as the product of reasoning about a speaker's intentions or as derived in the grammar (Chierchia et al., 2012), by an operator that negates stronger alternatives. On all views, however, implicatures depend on which alternatives are considered relevant in context (Fox & Katzir, 2011). Related to this, many studies have argued that children may fail to compute implicatures because they struggle to access relevant alternative utterances, for example failing to spontaneously consider a statement containing "and" as an alternative to a disjunctive utterance, either because scales such as ⟨some, all⟩ are not yet lexicalized (Foppolo et al., 2012; Foppolo et al., 2021) or because alternatives are not recognized as relevant and retrieved in context (Barner & Bachrach, 2010; Barner et al., 2011; Barner et al., 2018; Bleotu et al., 2025; Chierchia et al., 2001; Gotzner et al., 2020; Horowitz et al., 2018; Pagliarini et al., 2018; Stiller et al., 2015; Tieu et al., 2016 Tieu et al., , 2017;; Skordos & Papafragou, 2016). As evidence for this, children readily compute ad hoc implicatures, in which alternatives are contextually salient (Barner et al., 2011; Barner et al., 2018; Papafragou & Tantalou, 2004; Rees et al., 2023; Yoon & Frank, 2019), including in picture-choice paradigms, where alternatives can be generated from visible stimuli (Foppolo et al., 2021; Kampa & Papafragou, 2020; Horowitz et al., 2018; Stiller et al., 2015), or in felicity and truth value tasks when the preceding context includes mention of relevant alternative statements (Skordos & Papafragou, 2016). Crucially, however, although children often compute adult-like inferences when relevant alternatives are contextually salient, no study has found comparable success in truth value judgment tasks when relevant alternatives are not provided. 1 Given this, a natural explanation of past findings is that children fail to compute implicatures because they lack the ability to spontaneously generate relevant alternatives.

While the access-to-alternatives hypothesis provides a natural explanation of why children often fail to compute implicatures in felicity and truth value judgment tasks, and why contextual cues to alternatives help, these findings are also compatible with a somewhat opposite analysis. Specifically, rather than being neutral with respect to alternatives, judgment tasks may actually provide highly salient and highly misleading alternatives, which cause otherwise competent pragmatic reasoners to derive unintended inferences, and to ostensibly fail tests of implicature (see also Crain et al., 1996; Skordos & Papafragou, 2016). On this view, when children hear scalar expressions, they may spontaneously generate relevant alternatives and compute implicatures. However, when they are first shown a scene that proffers possible meanings that they would otherwise never consider -as is typical in truth value and felicity judgment tasks -this may impair children's ability to generate their own, spontaneous meanings, producing apparent failures.

In the present study, we explore this logic and present evidence that children may actually have adult-like abilities to compute implicatures when allowed to spontaneously generate their own meanings of utterances prior to a truth value judgment. In particular, we explore the possibility that children only fail in truth value judgment or felicity tasks when they are tested with scenes that proffer "worlds" that they would otherwise never consider when spontaneously interpreting language, that is, scenes that the experimenter presents before children have acted on their own interpretation of an utterance. In most past studies of implicature that find failures, including those described above, children are asked to evaluate the felicity of a statement given a scene that is created by the experimenter. For example, in a study of disjunction by Hochstein et al. (2016), children were asked to judge whether the statement, "The bear took a cup or a plate," was a good description of a proffered scene in which a bear had taken both objects (by choosing whether it was uttered by a silly puppet or a smart puppet). In order to ascribe this utterance to a "silly" speaker, participants were required to recognize a mismatch between the utterance and the proffered scene (see Gordon, 1996, for discussion). On this "Proffered Worlds" hypothesis, when an experimenter presents a scene that is incompatible with an implicature, this scene competes with the child's own preferred model of the utterance. This irrelevant scene not only interferes with the child's spontaneous meaning, but it also leads them to conclude that the experimenter must have an alternative "question" in mind, which is not compatible with an implicature.

The Proffered Worlds hypothesis takes inspiration from studies of quantifier spreading, where previous studies have also argued that proffering dispreferred worlds to children may conceal adult-like competence. In this literature, multiple studies report that when preschool-aged children are presented with a scene in which a universally quantified expression like, "Every girl is riding an elephant," is true, they nevertheless reject the utterance if, e.g., extra elephants are present (e.g., Aravind et al., 2017; Brooks & Sekerina, 2006; Philip, 1995; Skordos et al., 2022; Crain et al., 1996; Minai et al., 2012). On one analysis, provided by Crain et al. (1996), such cases may be pragmatically confusing to children both because the utterances are trivially true, but also because there are salient additional items that the children might assume are relevant, given that they have been proffered by the experimenter. According to Crain et al. (1996), to override these misleading contextual features, children require the ability to access "circumstances" in which it would be "appropriate to deny the test sentence" (p. 93). Compatible with this idea, Skordos et al. (2022) found that children readily accepted such statements if they were first presented a previous warm-up trial in which they saw 2 out of 3 girls riding elephants and were asked the identical question. In other words, when children were first tested with a scene that matched an adult-like understanding of the universally quantified sentence, they were no longer swayed by the presence of extra elephants in subsequent test trials.

How are these results from quantifier spreading relevant to understanding children's difficulties with scalar implicature? On the analysis provided by both Crain et al. (1996) and Skordos et al. (2022), when experimenters proffer worlds that render utterances trivially true, uninformative, or otherwise vacuous, children assume that the speaker must have intended some other, more informative meaning that corresponds to salient aspects of the proffered scene.

According to Crain et al. (1996) children in previous studies of quantifier spreading were "led to suppose that another interpretation was intended" because the "circumstances were inappropriate" on the adult interpretation (p. 93). In other words, when a misleading world is proffered, children may infer an unintended "Question Under Discussion" or QUD (Hamblin, 1976; Karttunen, 1977; Roberts, 2012), because listeners infer the QUD from context, and a scene presented by the experimenter serves as evidence about what the speaker is asking. The notion of QUD is central to implicature, because formally questions can be thought of as determining "the set of propositions which are the possible answers … to the question; these are the proffered alternatives" (Roberts, 2012, p. 65). Thus, if the QUD is modified contextually, the set of linguistic alternatives that is considered to be relevant should also change accordingly.

Consequently, children may fail to access alternatives that are required for scalar implicature when misleading worlds are proffered, because these worlds trigger unintended QUDs that activate distinct and irrelevant alternatives. Our contention is that otherwise children may not have difficulty accessing relevant alternatives, particularly when they are allowed to spontaneously compute their own meanings for scalar expressions before any such scene is presented, as they typically do in natural conversation.

Lending support to this idea, studies using act-out paradigms have found that when children are asked to act on "some" of the objects in a scene, they almost never act on all of them, and that when they are asked to act on one of the objects with "or", they seldom act on both (e.g., Pouscoulous et al., 2007; Miller et al., 2005; Bleotu et al., 2025). For example, in one early study Miller et al. (2005) asked preschoolers to "Make SOME faces happy", by drawing smiling mouths on pictures of faces, and found that they only drew smiles on all faces around 10% of the time. Using a similar paradigm, Bleotu et al. (2025) presented Romanian-speaking 5-year-olds with a puppet who said, "I would like either the triangle or the circle to have color,"

and were asked to respond by coloring objects, erasing color, or taking no action. In a 0-Object scenario, in which neither object was already colored, most children colored exactly one object (86%) -suggesting that they rarely generated mental representations in which both disjuncts were true. Similarly, in a 1-Object scenario, just over half of the responses involved doing nothing (52.2%), rather than coloring the second object. Finally -and relevant to the present hypothesis -children's performance was worst when they were initially proffered a scene in which both disjuncts were true. Here, in the 2-Object scenario, children only removed one of the two objects on less than half of trials (44.1%), unlike adults who almost always did so.

These results from act-out studies indicate that when children hear a statement like, "The bear took the cup or the plate," they are unlikely to spontaneously generate a mental representation in which both disjuncts are true. Crucially, however, such evidence falls short of demonstrating that children therefore compute scalar implicatures. This is because while these studies reveal the nature of the world that children most strongly associate with the meaning of an utterance, they do not test children's beliefs about other, stronger utterances, and whether they infer that the speaker believes such alternatives to be false. For example, in an act-out task involving disjunction, a child who colors a single shape in response to, "I would like to color the triangle or the circle," produces an action that is compatible with an exclusive interpretation, but also with an inclusive one, since they might also believe that coloring both shapes would be appropriate (but that only one response is possible). In this sense, act-out tasks capture the world that children most strongly associate with an expression, but not whether they strengthen the utterance by negating more informative alternatives to reach that interpretation. A useful analogy comes from work on typicality effects: when participants are asked to "name a bird" they spontaneously generate prototypical exemplars like "robin" but rarely mention atypical instances like "penguin," yet readily judge that penguins are birds when asked (Rosch, 1973). In the same way, act-out tasks reveal which worlds children most readily associate with a sentence, but not whether they would reject other worlds as incompatible with the utterance.

In the present study, we hypothesized that children may only fail on tests of implicature when first provided with proffered worlds, which preclude their ability to spontaneously generate their own models of utterances. Based on this, we reasoned that the contrary may also be true: that children may successfully compute implicatures when first allowed to generate their own models of utterances, because the activation of these preferred worlds precludes the adoption of proffered worlds. In particular, we reasoned that if children are first tested in an act-out task, which allows them to generate their own model of an utterance, they may subsequently compute implicatures for that utterance when tested with a felicity judgment task, because they have already converged upon their own preferred meaning prior to being proffered irrelevant scenes.

To test this idea, we adopted a two-step design in which each trial contained two phases.

In the first phase, children completed an act-out task, which allowed them to interpret the utterance and construct their own representation of its meaning. In this phase, children received no feedback, heard no statement containing a stronger alternative, and saw only a page of uncolored objects that was compatible with any response. In the second phase, children completed a standard truth value judgment task. They saw a puppet, Mr. Monkey, who was given the same instruction as the child, and were asked to judge whether Mr. Monkey carried out the instruction correctly. This judgment phase did not require a new interpretation of the utterance.

Instead, it tested whether the interpretation children had already formed excluded worlds in which the stronger alternative was true. In Experiment 1, children heard a disjunctive statement (e.g., "Lucy colored the cow or the horse") and were asked to color a new page to match it. We measured whether children spontaneously colored one or both of the mentioned items. Crucially, the way in which Mr. Monkey then colored depended on the child's initial response. If the child colored only one of the mentioned objects, Mr. Monkey colored either one object or both, allowing us to test whether children treated the statement as exclusive. If the child colored both objects, Mr. Monkey always colored just one, allowing us to test whether children interpreted disjunction conjunctively (by preferring to color both items but rejecting the coloring of only one). We also included a TVJ-only condition in which children did not color based on the statement themselves, but proceeded directly to evaluating Mr. Monkey's coloring. In this condition, we expected children to give literal responses and accept both colorings.

To broaden the empirical scope of the paper, we sought converging evidence for children's ability to compute scalar implicatures with "some" in Experiment 2. Following previous act-out studies (e.g., Miller et al., 2005), children heard a sentence containing the quantifier "some" (e.g., "Lucy colored some of the cows") and were asked to color a new page with four cows to match this behavior. This phase measured whether children spontaneously colored one, two, three, or all four of the available items. After completing the act-out phase, children saw Mr. Monkey attempt the same task. Mr. Monkey was given the same instruction and colored the page himself. Children were then asked to judge whether Mr. Monkey's coloring matched the statement. Crucially, the way Mr. Monkey colored depended on the child's initial response. If the child colored a subset of the cows, Mr. Monkey either colored three cows or all four cows. This allowed us to test whether children computed the implicature associated with "some." If children interpreted "some" pragmatically as excluding "all", they should reject the case in which Mr. Monkey colored all four cows. However, if the child initially colored all four cows, Mr. Monkey always colored three cows. This allowed us to test whether children interpreted "some" literally. In this case, children who treated "some" as compatible with "all" should reject Mr. Monkey's coloring of three cows. As in Experiment 1, we included a TVJ-only condition in which children did not color based on the statement themselves, but did a truth value judgment task. In this condition, we expected children to give literal responses and accept Mr.

Monkey's coloring regardless of whether he colored some or all of the items, compatible with a literal interpretation of "some."

Participants

We recruited a total of 84 children between the ages of 4;0 and 5;11 (M = 4;11) from preschools and a children's museum in [redacted]. All participants spoke English as their primary language. As per our preregistered exclusion criteria, two children were excluded for failing at least three attention check trials, and two were excluded due to consistent errors in repeating the critical sentence. Our final sample included 80 participants.

Materials & Procedure

Participants were tested individually in-person using an interactive touch-based tablet task framed as a coloring game. The game featured three characters: Lucy, Percy, and Mr. Monkey. Children were told that Lucy loved coloring and had already finished coloring her own coloring book. Mr. Monkey had an uncolored copy of the same book, as did children in the Act-Out+TVJ condition, and the goal was to make each copy look like Lucy's. Because Lucy's book was not shown, a puppet, Percy, guessed what Lucy had colored on each page. Children and Mr. Monkey then colored their own copies according to Percy's guess. Each page showed uncolored objects against a colored background, which was part of every copy of the book.

Children were asked whether Mr. Monkey got it right or wrong, that is, whether he colored his page the way Percy said. Percy's guesses were modeled on the guessing-game method (Skordos et al., 2020; Tieu et al., 2017), although in the present task children judged Mr. Monkey's coloring rather than Percy's guess.

Children were assigned to one of two between-subjects conditions: an Act-Out+TVJ condition and a TVJ-only condition. The two conditions shared the same characters, trial structure, and stimuli; they differed in whether children actively colored the pictures themselves prior to completing the TVJ task. In the Act-Out+TVJ condition, the child was an active participant in the game and was asked to color based on Percy's statement prior to watching Mr.

Monkey do the same, whereas in the TVJ-only condition children did not get the opportunity to color based on Percy's statements, but only watched Mr. Monkey do so. In the Act-Out+TVJ condition, on each page children saw three uncolored objects of different types (e.g., a cow, a horse, and a duck) which they were given the opportunity to color (see Figure 1). Children first completed a short familiarization phase to learn the game mechanics. They were told that to color objects, they first had to tap the items they wished to paint (which caused a paint bucket to appear next to each selected item), and then tap a paintbrush to apply color to their selections. This two-step procedure ensured that children had clearly committed to their intended selection before any coloring happened.

After the familiarization phase, children completed two practice trials before the experiment began. In these trials, Percy's statement did not contain disjunction. For example, in a scene showing a seahorse alongside other sea animals, Percy said, "Lucy colored the seahorse."

Children first colored the picture according to Percy's statement and then judged Mr. Monkey's coloring. On one trial, Mr. Monkey colored the named object (e.g., seahorse) and was therefore correct. On the other, he colored an unnamed object and was therefore incorrect. During these practice trials, children received explicit feedback on their judgments and repeated the trial if necessary.

Following the practice trials, children completed 10 experimental trials: six critical disjunction trials and four attention check trials. For descriptive purposes, we refer to trials in which only one of the two mentioned objects was colored as 1-Disjunct-True (1DT) trials, and trials in which both mentioned objects were colored as 2-Disjunct-True (2DT) trials. Because Mr.

Monkey's response was contingent on the child's earlier action, children who consistently colored both objects did not contribute to trials in which both mentioned objects were colored in the judgment phase, whereas children who colored a single object on at least some trials contributed both types of trials.

In the Act-Out+TVJ condition experimental trials consisted of two parts: an Act-Out phase followed by a Truth Value Judgment phase. In the Act-Out phase, the experimenter addressed Percy and asked, "Percy, what did Lucy color on this page?", inviting him to make a guess about the current page. Percy then appeared in a video window and, following the logic of previous studies using a guessing-game method (e.g., Tieu et al., 2017; Skordos et al., 2020), made a guess about what Lucy had colored using a disjunctive statement -for example, "Lucy colored the cow or the horse." Before coloring, children were asked to repeat Percy's statement back to the experimenter. Children were then asked to color the picture just like Percy said ("Can you color it just like Percy said?"). 2 In the Truth Value Judgment phase, which was present in both the Act-Out+TVJ and TVJ-only conditions, children heard Percy's disjunctive statement once more, after which Mr. Monkey colored the picture. Children were then asked whether Mr.

Monkey got it right or got it wrong. In the Act-Out+TVJ condition children always acted on Percy's statement themselves before making a truth value judgment. Crucially, Mr. Monkey's coloring differed depending on the child's earlier response in the act-out phase. If a child colored only one of the two mentioned objects (e.g., the cow), Mr. Monkey colored the other mentioned object on half of the trials (e.g., only the horse) and both mentioned objects on the other half (e.g., the cow and the horse). This allowed us to test whether children treated the statement as exclusive. If a child colored both mentioned objects in the act-out phase, Mr. Monkey colored only one of the mentioned objects in the judgment phase, allowing us to test whether children interpreted disjunction conjunctively (by preferring to color both items but rejecting the coloring of only one) (see Figure 1).

There were also four attention check trials, two of which were single-target trials that resembled practice trials and did not include disjunction. In these trials, Percy made a simple statement (e.g., "Lucy colored the robot"), and Mr. Monkey colored the mentioned object on one trial and an unmentioned object on the other. The remaining two were two-target attention check trials, which appeared at the end of the experiment. In these trials, children saw displays containing two objects from the same category (e.g., two animals or two fruits) and one unrelated object (e.g., a tractor), and Percy said that Lucy colored the two matching objects. Mr. Monkey colored one matching object on one trial and both matching objects on the other. These trials confirmed that children were willing and able to select two objects when the statement explicitly required it, ruling out the possibility that selecting a single object in the critical disjunction trials reflected a general bias toward minimal responding or inattention.

Trials were presented in one of two pseudo-randomized orders, counterbalanced across participants. In one list, early critical trials were arranged such that, contingent on the child producing a 1DT response in the act-out phase, Mr. Monkey produced two consecutive 2DT trials before alternating with 1DT trials. In the other list, children instead first encountered two consecutive 1DT trials before alternation began. This counterbalancing ensured that any potential order effects (e.g., children anchoring on the first response type they observe) do not systematically bias the results. The spatial positions of the mentioned objects were also counterbalanced across trials, such that the two disjuncts appeared in different pairings (left-middle, middle-right, left-right). The entire session lasted approximately 10 minutes.

Results

Our primary analyses followed our pre-registered plan. For children in the Act-Out+TVJ condition, we distinguished trials on which children produced 1DT responses from those on which they produced 2DT responses. This was important because children's initial act-out responses determined which Mr. Monkey outcome they saw in the subsequent judgment phase.

In particular, following a 2DT response in the Act-Out phase, children only ever saw a 1DT outcome in the TVJ phase from Mr. Monkey. The resulting data were therefore not directly comparable across trial types and were analyzed separately. When there were too few observations of a given trial type to support a mixed-effects analysis, we report those data descriptively rather than fitting a full model.

To assess whether children understood the task, we examined performance on the attention check trials. In the Act-Out+TVJ condition, these trials required both carrying out the coloring step correctly and judging Mr. Monkey's response accurately, whereas in the TVJ-only condition they required only correct judgment of Mr. Monkey's responses. On the single-target attention check trials of the Act-Out+TVJ condition, children always colored the mentioned object and were highly accurate in judging Mr. Monkey's response on these trials (M = .96, SD = .19). Children in the TVJ-only condition were similarly accurate (M = .92, SD = .26). On the two-target attention check trials in the Act-Out+TVJ condition, children typically selected both target objects on these trials (M = .85, SD = .35), showing that they were able to carry out instructions that explicitly required coloring two objects. Across conditions, children were also highly accurate in judging Mr. Monkey's response on these trials (M = .88, SD = .32). Taken together, these findings show that children understood the task and responded as expected on the attention check trials, including correctly selecting one or two target objects when required.

Discussion

In Experiment 1, we found evidence that children compute implicatures for disjunction when first allowed to act on their own spontaneous interpretation of utterances. When children were asked to act out their interpretation of disjunctive statements by coloring images, children almost always produced 1DT responses, suggesting that they almost never spontaneously represented scenes in which both disjuncts were true. However, because act-out data are potentially compatible with either an exclusive or an inclusive interpretation, evidence from children's truth-value judgments was crucial. After producing a 1DT response in the act-out phase, children in the Act-Out+TVJ condition were significantly less likely to accept Mr.

Monkey's coloring on 2DT trials than on 1DT trials, and were also significantly less likely to accept 2DT trials than children in the TVJ-only condition. In contrast, children in the two conditions accepted 1DT trials at similar rates, indicating that acting out the utterance did not lead children to reject Mr. Monkey's coloring in general, but specifically when both disjuncts were true. Children in the TVJ-only condition accepted 1DT and 2DT trials at similarly high rates, a pattern compatible with an inclusive interpretation of disjunction. These findings provide strong evidence that children can compute the exclusive interpretation of disjunction, and that this ability is revealed when they first have the opportunity to construct the relevant scene for themselves, but not in standard TVJ tasks.

Experiment 2

In Experiment 2, we asked whether the pattern observed for disjunction would generalize to "some". As in Experiment 1, our goal was to compare a condition in which children first constructed their own interpretation of the utterance (Act-Out+TVJ) with one in which they judged an experimenter-provided scene without first doing so (TVJ-only). To do so, we presented children with the same tasks as in Experiment 1, but in which children were asked to either color "some" of 4 items presented on a page (in the Act-Out task), or to judge how Mr.

Monkey colored items when he colored only some (i.e., 3 items) or all of them (i.e., 4).

Participants

We recruited a new set of 84 children between the ages of 4;0 and 5;11 (M = 4;11) from preschools and a children's museum in [redacted]. All participants spoke English as their primary language. As per our preregistered exclusion criteria, four children were excluded for failing at least three attention check trials. Our final sample included 80 participants.

Materials & Procedure

We used the same two-step design as Experiment 1, but tested implicatures associated with the quantifier "some". Children again completed either an Act-Out+TVJ condition or a TVJ-only condition in the same tablet coloring game with Lucy, Percy, and Mr. Monkey. Unless noted below, the procedure was the same as in Experiment 1. In the Act-Out task, on each "page" of the electronic coloring book children saw four uncolored objects of the exact same type (e.g., four cows) which they were given the opportunity to color (see Figure 4).

Children first completed the same familiarization procedure to learn game logistics, and then completed two practice trials. Similar to Experiment 1, these trials did not contain the scalar expression, and children received explicit feedback on their judgments. To match the visual structure of the critical trials as closely as possible, the practice trials again involved multiple similar objects, but only one target matched Percy's description. For example, children might see two bears that differed by a salient property, such as whether they were wearing hats, and hear Percy say, "Lucy colored the bear with a hat." Children in the Act-Out+TVJ condition first colored the target object themselves and then judged Mr. Monkey's response. On one practice trial, Mr. Monkey colored the correct one (e.g., bear with a hat); on the other, he colored the incorrect one (e.g., bear without a hat).

Following practice, children completed 10 experimental trials: six critical "some" trials and four attention check trials. On each critical trial in the Act-Out+TVJ condition, Percy described what Lucy had colored using "some" (e.g., "Lucy colored some of the cows"), and children were asked to color the picture just as Percy said ("Can you color it just like Percy said?"). In the subsequent Truth Value Judgment phase, Percy's statement was repeated, Mr.

Monkey colored the picture, and children judged whether he got it right or wrong. In the act-out phase, children could color one, two, three, or all four objects, all of which are compatible with the standard analysis of the logical meaning of "some" (Barwise & Cooper, 1981). As in Experiment 1, Mr. Monkey's response depended on the child's earlier act-out response. If a child colored one, two, or three of the four objects in the Act-Out phase, Mr. Monkey colored three objects on half of the trials and all four objects on the other half in the Truth Value Judgment phase. 3 This allowed us to test whether children treated "some" as excluding "all": children who computed the implicature should reject trials on which Mr. Monkey colored all four objects, whereas children who interpreted "some" literally should accept both response types. If a child colored all four objects in the Act-Out phase, Mr. Monkey colored only three in the Truth Value Judgment phase. For descriptive purposes, we refer to trials in which fewer than four objects were colored as "Some-True" trials (satisfying the implicature), and trials in which all four objects were colored as "Some-False" trials (violating the implicature) (see Figure 4). As in Experiment 1, attention check trials were designed to parallel the structure of the critical trials. Two were single-target attention check trials that resembled the practice trials. In these trials, children again saw multiple similar objects, but only one matched Percy's description. For example, children might see two pigs, only one of which was wearing boots, and hear Percy say, "Lucy colored the pig with boots." Or they might see two bears, only one of which was wearing a hat, and hear Percy say, "Lucy colored the bear with a hat." On these trials, Mr. Monkey colored either the correct object or the contrasting one. The remaining two attention check trials contained "all" and appeared at the end of the experiment, since previous studies find that access to statements including "all" may increase the likelihood of implicature in young children (e.g., Skordos & Papafragou, 2016). In these trials, Percy said that Lucy had colored all four objects (e.g., "Lucy colored all the dolphins", "Lucy colored all the aliens"), and Mr.

Monkey colored either all four or only three. These trials ensured that children were willing and able to color all four objects when the utterance explicitly required it.

Similar to Experiment 1, trials were presented in one of two pseudo-randomized orders, counterbalanced across participants. Half the children first encountered critical trials where Mr.

Monkey produced "Some-False" responses (coloring all four items), while the other half first encountered "Some-True" responses (coloring three items). The entire session lasted approximately 10 minutes.

Discussion

In Experiment 2, we found that children reliably computed scalar implicatures in a truth value judgment task when this was preceded by an act-out phase that allowed them to express their own spontaneous interpretation of "some". In the act-out phase, children colored a subset of the four available objects on almost all trials (one, two, or three objects), replicating previous act-out findings (Pouscoulous et al., 2007; Miller et al., 2005). Critically, children who first acted out the utterance were much more likely to subsequently reject Mr. Monkey when he colored all four objects relative to children in the TVJ-only condition. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that when children are able to construct and act on a spontaneous interpretation of an utterance, they readily compute implicatures when subsequently presented with alternative scenes. By contrast, children in the TVJ-only condition were much less likely to compute implicatures, at rates consistent with past studies that used TVJ or comparable tasks (e.g., Foppolo et al., 2012; Foppolo et al., 2021). These results are compatible with those of Experiment 1, and suggest that (1) children almost never spontaneously interpret "some" as compatible with "all," and (2) compute scalar implicatures at adult-like levels in a truth value judgment task if they are first allowed to express their own, spontaneous meaning prior to being proffered alternative worlds.

General Discussion

We asked whether preschoolers spontaneously compute scalar implicatures when they are first allowed to construct their own models of an utterance before being proffered alternative worlds by the experimenter -as they might in naturalistic conversation. Across two experiments, we tested children in a condition in which they first acted out their interpretation before making a truth value judgment and compared this to a condition in which they only made truth value judgments for experimenter proffered scenes. We found two main results. First, children almost never colored both disjuncts after hearing "or" (Experiment 1) and rarely colored all four objects after hearing "some" (Experiment 2). Second, when children made truth value judgments after first acting out their spontaneous interpretations, they rejected outcomes incompatible with strengthened meanings at significantly higher rates than children who made truth value judgments without first acting out their own meanings. This was true for both "some" and disjunction. Taken together, these results provide evidence that when children are allowed to generate their own models of an utterance before evaluating experimenter-provided worlds, they readily arrive at adult-like strengthened meanings and compute scalar implicatures.

In the Introduction, we raised the possibility that children only fail to compute implicatures when they are tested with stimuli that proffer "worlds" that they would otherwise never spontaneously consider when interpreting language. On this "Proffered Worlds" hypothesis, children fail to compute implicatures when they are shown scenes that do not correspond to their own spontaneous interpretation of an utterance. In particular, proffering worlds interferes with the child's ability to spontaneously generate their own model of how the world should look, and, as argued by Crain et al. (1996) and Skordos et al. (2022), may lead children to assume that the speaker has some other meaning in mind that corresponds to salient aspects of the provided scene. Adults may not struggle in this way because, unlike young children, they are better able to simultaneously represent multiple competing representations of the world and flexibly move between them -a capacity which is slow to develop in childhood, and impacts a range of behaviors including counterfactual reasoning (Rafetseder et al., 2010), theory of mind (Perner & Lang, 1999), rule switching (Doebel & Zelazo, 2015), and the representation of mutually exclusive possible worlds (Gauffroy & Barrouillet, 2011; Sophian & Somerville, 1988).

In most naturalistic conversations, by contrast, children are free to generate their own spontaneous models of sentence meanings, without being led to consider worlds that might otherwise be strongly dispreferred. The results of Experiments 1 and 2 are compatible with this idea. For example, whereas most past studies of disjunction find that children interpret "or" inclusively in truth value judgment tasks -often endorsing scenes in which both disjuncts are true -we found that children almost never preferred scenes where both disjuncts were true when first allowed to act on their own spontaneous interpretation. We also found no evidence for so-called "conjunctive" responding (e.g., Singh et al., 2016), in which children only accept disjunction when both disjuncts are true. Similarly, whereas many past studies find that children often accept "some" when "all" is true (with rates typically ranging from 45-50% of the time, e.g., Foppolo et al., 2012; Foppolo et al., 2021 to 80-90%, e.g., Noveck, 2001), we found that when children first acted out their preferred meaning, they almost never colored all available objects, and were subsequently much more likely to compute implicatures and reject scenes where "all" was true.

In the Introduction, we noted that the Proffered Worlds hypothesis might explain not only children's difficulties with scalar implicature, but also other phenomena where children fail to make adult-like judgments, like Piagetian class inclusion tasks. In studies of these phenomena, children are also proffered worlds that they would otherwise be highly unlikely to spontaneously generate themselves. For example, in classic studies, Inhelder and Piaget (1964) presented children with questions like, "Are there more flowers or roses?" in displays with four roses and two tulips. In such cases, a child might answer "roses" not because they fail to understand class inclusion, but instead because the question asked is pragmatically infelicitous, and the scene that is proffered is more compatible with a more pragmatically sensible question -i.e., "Are there more roses or tulips?" Compatible with this, even adults show significant processing costs when presented such items (Borst et al., 2013), suggesting that they also struggle to accommodate the provided scene with the test sentence. Similarly, children famously judge that the sentence, "Every rose is a flower," to be false when presented with a mix of roses and tulips. Here, too, children may infer that, given the scene presented, the experimenter's intended question must be whether every flower is a rose, both because this contrast is salient in the presented scene, but also because this is the only sensible and potentially informative question that could be intended in the context.

As alluded to in the Introduction, the Proffered Worlds hypothesis suggests a revision of theories which argue that children struggle with implicature due to difficulties understanding the QUD or accessing alternatives. On such accounts, children fail to compute implicatures because they do not spontaneously access relevant alternatives, and can be made to succeed when alternatives are explicitly provided, made salient by manipulation of the Question Under Discussion (Skordos & Papafragou, 2016), or via visual cues (e.g., as in ad hoc implicature; Barner et al., 2011; Barner et al., 2018; Foppolo et al., 2021; Franchin et al., 2024; Porrini et al., 2026; Stiller et al., 2015; Yoon & Frank, 2019). Common to all of these accounts, however, is that without these cues, children should fail to spontaneously identify the QUD and generate stronger alternatives. In contrast, the present results suggest that children may be able to access stronger lexical alternatives spontaneously, even when they are not directly provided. In the Act-Out+TVJ conditions of Experiments 1 and 2, no statements containing "and" or "all" preceded the critical trials, and the experimenter did not make these alternatives salient in any other way, yet children rejected outcomes in which the stronger alternatives were true. This suggests that children may not actually struggle to access relevant alternative utterances in naturalistic conversations, but may only struggle to do so after they have been presented with irrelevant worlds, which trigger unintended QUDs (as in standard truth value judgment tasks).

Because formally a question can be defined as the set of alternative propositions that express answers to it, the act of contextually triggering an unintended QUD should also trigger irrelevant alternatives, while simultaneously eliminating relevant alternatives from consideration. For example, as noted by Skordos and Papafragou (2016), many past studies of implicature present children with sentences like, "Some of the people have shovels," in scenes where "all" is obviously true, but where the identity of objects varies systematically across trials, such that sometimes people have shovels, sometimes scarves, etc. Children may therefore assume that the QUD relates to the kinds of things some people have, rather than to the number of people that have shovels, such that, "Some of the people have scarves," is a relevant alternative, whereas, "All of the people have shovels," is not. Under these conditions, explicitly providing them with relevant alternatives involving "all" may be important because it directly activates the speaker's intended meaning, allowing them to "repair" their understanding of the intended QUD. By contrast, such repair -and being explicitly provided with linguistic alternatives -should not be necessary if the child is not presented with irrelevant worlds, and they spontaneously converge on the speaker's intended QUD and its concomitant alternatives.

Our analysis proposes that acting out a meaning allows children to build a mental model of an utterance, which allows them to use this initial model as a basis for informing subsequent judgments in the TVJ task (see Figure 7). Still, alternative interpretations for our findings might be possible. For example, one possibility is that performing the Act-Out task made it easier for children to infer the speaker's goals, improving their performance relative to children tested only with the TVJ. However, this possibility is compatible with the Proffered Worlds hypothesis.

Inferring a speaker's goal can be understood as identifying their intended QUD, which standard TVJ tasks may make this harder, by presenting an irrelevant world before children interpret the utterance. A second possibility is that performing the Act-Out task may make children less tolerant of pragmatic violations, in the sense of Katsos and Bishop (2011), because they are less willing to accept a coloring that differs from their own. However, our data do not support this possibility. Children who colored first frequently accepted colorings unlike their own as long as they were compatible with the strengthened meaning. When they had colored one object (e.g., the cow), they often accepted Mr. Monkey coloring the other object (e.g., the horse) in Experiment 1, and when many had colored only one or two cows, they often accepted Mr.

Monkey coloring three in Experiment 2, in both cases as often as TVJ-only children did. A final possibility is that acting out the utterances may also have given children time to work out the goal of the task before the TVJ phase. However, in previous studies that explicitly trained children on the goal of the task, children's rejection rates increased slightly but remained relatively low, and well below adult levels (e.g., Guasti et al., 2005; Papafragou & Musolino, 2003). Children in the present study were not instructed about the goal of the task, yet rejected worlds in which the stronger alternative was true at adult-like rates.

Our study raises the possibility that proffered worlds also contribute to children's difficulties in other domains that use truth value judgment tasks. In such tasks, children have been reported to accept violations of Principle B, such as, "Mama Bear is touching her," when Mama Bear touches herself (Chien & Wexler, 1990), to misinterpret object clefts such as, "It's the cat that the dog is chasing," as meaning that the cat is chasing the dog (see Aravind et al., 2018), to resist inverse scope readings of sentences like, "Every horse didn't jump over the fence" (Musolino et al., 2000), to accept plural sentences such as, "Emily fed giraffes" when only one giraffe was fed (Tieu et al., 2014 (Tieu et al., , 2020)), and to overaccept necessity modals, for example accepting, "Cora has to take the yellow road," when another road is also possible (Cournane et al., 2025; Dieuleveut et al., 2022; Öztürk & Papafragou, 2015). In at least some of these cases, the worlds proffered by the experimenter may imply questions not intended by the experimenter (for related arguments about the role of context in truth value judgment tasks, see Aravind et al., 2018; Conroy et al., 2009; Gualmini et al., 2008). If so, children might succeed when allowed to generate their own interpretation of an utterance before any world is proffered.

A related open question is what role, if any, the QUD raised by a task plays in children's broader logical reasoning, and how much such reasoning depends on the ability to mentally represent and entertain alternative possibilities. Drawing a disjunctive inference, for instance, requires representing two states of affairs as mutually exclusive possibilities (e.g., "A or B", A is ruled out, therefore B), and similar demands on representing and selecting among alternatives arise in reasoning about modals, counterfactuals and possibility reasoning more broadly.

Although infants and toddlers appear to succeed in simple reasoning-by-elimination tasks where they infer the location of a hidden object after seeing that one of the two possible locations is empty, preschoolers often fail in related tasks (see Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2018; Cesana-Arlotti & Halberda, 2024; Feiman et al., 2022; Gautam et al., 2021; Mody & Carey, 2016). Notably, 2.5-year-olds who fail this kind of task succeed when the relevant premise (i.e., not "A") is conveyed linguistically (e.g., "There is no ball in the red cup"; Grigoroglou et al., 2019), perhaps because the linguistic cue makes clearer what the experimenter intends in showing the empty cup, and thus which possibility is meant to be ruled out. Preschoolers also have difficulty reasoning counterfactually about alternative antecedents, often failing the "muddy boots" task when asked whether the floor would be clean if one character had taken his shoes off, even though another character had also tracked in mud (Rafetseder et al., 2013), though they perform better when the two muddy events are clearly separated in time and motive (Nyhout et al., 2019), perhaps because each antecedent then becomes more salient as a distinct alternative the question could be about. Future work should build on the findings we report here by exploring whether children's struggles across these domains reflect difficulties in recovering what is being asked when contextual features trigger an unintended QUD and activate irrelevant alternatives, and reasoning about possibilities more broadly (e.g., Leahy & Carey, 2020; Turan-Küçük & Kibbe, 2024).

In summary, our results suggest that when children are allowed to construct their own models of utterances, they successfully identify the speaker's intended Question Under Discussion, and compute strengthened interpretations that closely approximate adult performance. Methodologically, these results suggest that TVJ tasks risk underestimating children's knowledge, with implications across a broad range of literature both in semantics and pragmatics and beyond. Theoretically, they suggest that, despite decades of studies documenting children's failures, children may compute scalar implicatures. Given the chance to construct their own models of scalar expressions, children seem to have little difficulty inferring the speaker's meaning, accessing relevant alternatives, and rejecting interpretations that are incompatible with a scalar implicature. The critical challenge for children may not be computing implicatures or accessing lexical alternatives per se, but recovering the intended interpretation when visual context has already cued them toward an unintended discourse structure.
