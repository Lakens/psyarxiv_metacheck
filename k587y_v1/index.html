To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 21 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Hearing difficulties (HD) in childhood can be associated with emotional and behavioural difficulties (EBD, including attention, peer relations, internalising symptoms, anxiety and externalising symptoms) and can have diverse impacts on educational outcomes, including mathematics. This study has two aims: firstly, to analyse if children with HD differ from typically developing children (TD) in maths and EBD and secondly, to examine the longitudinal bidirectional relationships between maths and EBD in children with HD and TD children. The analyses, t-test and structural equation modelling, are applied on the data of children from 5 to 11 years of age from four large longitudinal datasets from Australia (n = 2,340), the United Kingdom (n = 5,254) and two from the United States (n = 1,537 and 6,343). The findings indicate that children with HD present with weaker maths performance, and more symptoms of EBD than TD children (Aim 1). As for the second aim, the findings regarding the relationships between EBD at age 5 and maths at age 7/8 are inconsistent.

Attentional difficulties were consistently predictive of lower maths performance in both HD and TD children, whereas externalising symptoms were consistently unrelated to maths performance. However, findings regarding peer relations are inconsistent for both groups of children, while higher internalising symptoms were consistently related to poorer maths only for TD children, and higher anxiety related to weaker maths performance only for HD children. In contrast, lower performance in maths at age 7/8 was consistently related to a higher presence of EBD symptoms at age 11 for both groups.

Educational impact and implications

1. Maths difficulties and emotional and behavioural difficulties are closely interconnected across development. Educators and schools should therefore consider monitoring both academic performance and emotional and behavioural indicators to better identify children who may require additional classroom support or referral for further assessment.

2. Attentional difficulties are closely associated with maths performance across childhood, underscoring the need for educators to consider students' attentional functioning when delivering maths instruction and support.

3. The findings further suggest that children with hearing difficulties may be at elevated risk of experiencing both maths and emotional or behavioural difficulties, underscoring the importance of holistic educational monitoring and support across primary school.

Developmental Trajectories of Maths Ability and Socio-Emotional Health in Children with and without Hearing Difficulties

In any given classroom, one can expect children with a broad range of abilities, including those with sensory and learning difficulties. The Centre for Disease Control and Prevention from the United Stated estimated that 15% of school-aged children live with some degree of hearing loss (Center for Disease Control Prevention, 2020) and recent populationbased studies reported slight hearing loss (at the 6-dB to 25-dB hearing level) in 15.2% to 22.6% of school aged children (Le Clercq et al., 2017; Su & Chan, 2017; Wang et al., 2018).

There is evidence to suggest that even mild cases of hearing loss could have a profound impact on children's educational outcomes and socio-emotional health (e.g., Khairi Md Daud et al., 2010; Le Clercq et al., 2017; Lieu et al., 2020; Moore et al., 2020; Stevenson, 2015), and the reported lifetime educational cost of childhood hearing loss in the United States alone is around $115,600 per child (Grosse, 2007).

Turning to learning difficulties, data published by the National Centre for Learning Disabilities reported that in 2017, 1 in 5 primary and secondary school children in the United States struggled with learning difficulties and attention problems. Learning difficulties are associated with adverse outcomes later in life in education, employment, and psychological well-being. Specifically, maths difficulties (MD) have been found to be more likely than reading difficulties to be associated with lower education outcomes, higher likelihood of antidepressant use, higher likelihood of unemployment (Aro et al., 2019; Torppa et al., 2023) and higher likelihood of dropout from upper-secondary education (Hakkarainen et al., 2015). It is estimated that 3-11% of school-age children struggle with maths difficulties (Morsanyi et al., 2018; Reeve, 2019; Shalev et al., 2000) in the United States, United Kingdom, and Australia.

These countries also report concerningly high mental health disorders in children. The Centre for Disease Control and Prevention estimated that 9.4% children in the United States were diagnosed with anxiety, 8.9% with behavioural problems, and 4.4.% with depression between 2016-19 (Bitsko et al., 2022). A survey conducted by the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) in 2014-15 found that in individuals aged 4-17 years, 6.9% experienced anxiety disorders, and 2.8% experienced major depressive disorder in the 12 months before the survey. About 12.8% of 5-18-year-olds in England were diagnosed with at least one mental health disorder in 2017 (Vizard et al., 2020). Childhood emotional and behavioural difficulties (EBD) have been linked with failures in achieving social and educational milestones (e.g., Reid et al., 2018) Taken together, it is not implausible to expect that MD, EBD, and other challenges, such as hearing loss, can interact in ways that can be detrimental to long-term educational and psychosocial outcomes. Gaining an understanding of these developmental trajectories is imperative for designing effective support programs for primary-school-age children with learning and/or sensory difficulties.

Meta-analytical evidence has found a consistent positive association between general academic achievement and two indicators of social relationships (peer social acceptance and friendships) for both primary and secondary school students (Wentzel et al., 2021). A recent study by Aro et al. (2024) examined the association between inattention, hyperactivity and other EBD measures in Finnish primary school children (n=362) with MD.

They found that 20-40% of children presented with inattention, hyperactivity, and anxiety problems. Additionally, inattention explained 2-20% of internalising symptoms, while hyperactivity directly contributed to externalising symptoms, explaining 20-22% of externalising symptoms in children with MD. A few studies have examined the developmental trajectories of inattention/hyperactivity and maths ability. For instance, a systematic review by Tosto et al. (2015) revealed a negative association between Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) symptoms and maths ability, with inattention being more prominently associated with poor maths skills. Furthermore, Salla et al. (2016) found that early childhood inattention, but not hyperactivity, was significantly associated with lower teacher rated maths performance at age 12. In addition, Mauer et al. (2023) based on a sample of Chinese American first, second and third graders (n=258) found that calculation skills were not predictive of sustained attentional focusing almost two years later, and conversely, sustained attentional focusing at the first timepoint was not predictive of calculation skills almost two years later, after controlling for autocorrelations and other relevant variables.

Finally, in the case of teenagers, Park et al. (2025) using a path modelling approach (n=1735) on the KCYPS dataset (Korea) found that self-reported attention, aggressiveness and depression were statistically significant predictors of maths achievement a year later, whereas experiencing difficulties in maths achievement was a statistically significant predictor of aggressiveness and depression, but not attention difficulties a year later. In both directions, the relationship between maths and depression was moderated by gender, being stronger for females than males.

In sum, there is evidence to suggest that maths difficulties are associated with increased EBD, with strong evidence for anxiety, and some evidence of internalising and, externalising symptoms, and difficulties with peer relationships and attention difficulties.. While the co-occurrence of maths and EBD is supported by fairly consistent evidence, the research on directionality of these associations reports mixed findings and must be explored further as they can have implications for intervention and long-term outcomes in education (e.g., Benz & Powell, 2021).

Maths Achievement in Children with Hearing Difficulties

Studies from around the world have reported lower maths achievement for children with HD compared to their hearing peers in primary school (e.g., Foisack et al., 2013; Traxler, 2000; Van Der Straaten et al., 2021). A large proportion of studies on maths achievement and hearing loss have been conducted in children with severe-profound permanent sensorineural hearing loss. These students were found to have lower maths achievement compared with their hearing peers in skills such as in symbolic number concepts (counting, arithmetic, and fractions) (Kritzer, 2009; Leybaert & Van Cutsem, 2002; Pagliaro & Kritzer, 2010 , 2013; Titus, 1995), and these difficulties are evident from as early as 3 years of age (Pagliaro & Kritzer, 2013). On maths achievement tasks, children with HD have shown delays of up to 3.5 years (Bull et al., 2005; Nunes & Moreno, 2022), and the achievement gap to their hearing peers persists to adulthood (Qi & Mitchell, 2012). Similar results have been found in some studies of children with severe-to-profound hearing loss who use cochlear implants. For instance, Sarant et al. (2015) found below average maths performance in 8-9-year-old cochlear implant users (n=44) compared to their hearing peers.

Studies of children with non-permanent HD, such as otitis media (OM)-related conditions, have also reported maths difficulties. Su et al. (2020) found that children with OM-related moderate-severe hearing loss performed poorly on national maths assessments compared to their hearing peers. This supports previous findings of lower maths ability in children with a history of OM in early years of childhood (e.g., Luotonen et al., 1998; the population. Students who have been exposed to sign language early in development have been found to perform better than those without access to fluent sign language (e.g., Hrastinski & Wilbur, 2016; Kritzer, 2009; Mousley & Kelly, 2017) and similarly to hearing signers and hearing controls in arithmetical accuracy (Buyle & Crollen, 2022). Furthermore, Wang et al. (2019) found that students with slight/mild unilateral or bilateral hearing loss had similar scores on NAPLAN-numeracy (the Australian nationwide school-based learning assessment), and mathematical thinking (as reported by teachers) compared with their hearing peers. Similarly, Bess et al. (1998) and Wake et al. (2006) did not find lower academic achievement scores in late childhood for students who had minimal hearing loss.

Additionally, a large cohort study (n=35946) found no association between OM (even with as many as 7 episodes) and maths ability in Danish children (Fougner et al., 2017). Despite these mixed findings, the existing evidence suggests that children with HD differ in their maths abilities and may be at higher risk of MD than their hearing peers.

Emotional and Behavioural Difficulties in Children with Hearing Difficulties

As reported earlier, there is rising concern for the mental health of all primary school children. This concern further intensifies when examining the association between HD and mental health (Moeller, 2007) with the prevalence of mental health and behavioural problems estimated at between 20-40% for children with hearing loss. Stevenson et al. (2015) conducted two separate meta-analyses of studies that used (a) parent/teacher self-ratings on the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; number of studies= 12) and (b) different measures of emotional and behavioural functioning (non-SDQ; number of studies= 33) to examine whether children/adolescents with hearing loss were more likely to present with EBD than those without hearing loss. They found higher scores for children with hearing loss on teacher-and parent-rated total difficulty (emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity, and peer problems), but not on self-rated SDQ scores. Children with hearing loss presented with consistently higher ratings for peer problems across parent (Hedge's g= 0.27; n=10), teacher (Hedge's g= 0.35; n=6), and self-reports (Hedge's g= 0.41; n=4). Only teacher-rated SDQ showed increased conduct problems, and only parent-rated SDQ showed increased emotional problems. In a subsequent study, Stevenson et al. (2017) found that adolescents with hearing problems experienced significantly more self-reported peer problems compared to their hearing peers. This was further supported by a qualitative study that interviewed adolescents with hearing loss and found that girls, in particular, reported peer problems, with greater barriers among individuals with moderate hearing loss than among those with severe to profound hearing loss (Terlektsi et al., 2020).

Finally, in a study of Australian 5-year-olds who were deaf or hard of hearing, Wong et al. (2020) reported that the SDQ total difficulties score fell 0.42 and 0.20 SD below the normative mean for parent and teacher ratings, respectively. Overall, these findings suggest greater EBD in children with hearing loss compared to their hearing peers. A recent study of 3-11-year-olds (n=7241) found increased odds of depressive symptoms and self-harm in children with parent/self-reported hearing loss than those without (Butcher et al., 2022).

Children with hearing loss are also at higher risk of developing anxiety problems than their hearing peers (Long et al., 2021; Theunissen et al., 2012 Theunissen et al., , 2014)). Further, Hancock et al. (2017) found that 12-17-year-olds with hearing problems were 3-4 times more likely to present with internalising symptoms (social phobia and generalised anxiety) than those without. However, this association was not statistically significant in 4-11-year-old children when other developmental factors (e.g., comorbid communication problems, poor health) were controlled for.

There is also some evidence to suggest that children with conductive hearing losses also experience greater EBD. For instance, Niclasen et al. (2016) examined the relationship between parent-reported episodes of otitis media and teacher/parent-reported SDQ scores in 9-11-year-old children (n=7599). They found that boys with four or more episodes of otitis media presented with higher internalising symptoms (parent-reports) than those without otitis media. No such relationship was observed for the other SDQ subscales. However, girls with four or more episodes of otitis media presented with increased internalising and externalising symptoms on both parent and teacher reports, and higher concentration and peer problems compared to those without a history of otitis media.

Several narrative reviews also support this finding, suggesting that children with mildto-profound hearing loss experience more internalising symptoms, social problems, attention deficits, and behavioural and conduct problems than children without hearing loss (Bigler et al., 2019). The children also interacted less frequently and for shorter durations with their peers. This, combined with communication and self-regulation difficulties, may make it challenging to form peer relationships during childhood (Antia et al., 2012; Calderon & Greenberg, 2012). Similarly, Hogan et al. (2014) found that children with a history of ear infections at age 4/5 had increased hyperactivity, emotional problems, and peer problems at 4/5, but these did not persist into later years. However, children with reported HD at 0/1 or 4/5 years presented with higher hyperactivity, emotional problems, and peer problems, which persisted until 10 years of age.

Taken together, there is mixed evidence on the relationship between specific aspects of EBD and HD, but the overall findings suggest that children with HD are at higher risk of EBD compared to their hearing peers. EBD difficulties may be most evident for children with HD who live in a predominantly hearing environment where access to linguistic input and social interaction is compromised, as children with deaf or hard of hearing parents or who are in frequent contact with deaf or hard or hearing peers are less likely to show EBD (e.g., Marschark et al., 2012; Meadow, 2005; Polat, 2003; Tsou et al., 2021; Wolters et al., 2011).

Association between Maths Ability and EBD in Children with Hearing Difficulties

Evidently, children with HD are at higher risk of both MD and EBD. However, very few studies have examined the relationship between maths ability and EBD in children with HD, and those have focused mainly on older students. Marschark et al. (2015) found that having a diagnosis of attention deficits negatively impacted maths calculation performance (β = -6.6, p < .05) in 13-16-year-old students with HD (n=500). To the best of our knowledge, the relationship between maths and peer problems has not been examined in children with hearing problems. However, longitudinal evidence shows that greater social integration, indexed by peer acceptance, friendships, and social network involvement, is associated with higher academic achievement and progress over time (Antia et al., 2012; Antia & Kreimeyer, 2015).

Current Study

Maths achievement has consistently emerged as a significantly strong predictor of academic success and later employment opportunities for TD children. There is evidence to suggest that maths abilities may be related to some measures of EBD (e.g., anxiety), but the evidence is inconsistent or lacking for other measures, such as internalising and externalising symptoms, peer problems, and victimisation. One group of children often cited as being at higher risk of both MD and EBD is children with HD. It is possible that the relationship between maths ability and EBD is also different (i.e., stronger or weaker association) in this population compared to children with typical hearing. Literature on the relationship between maths ability and EBD in both children with and without HD would benefit from further research. Therefore, the current study aimed to examine the longitudinal bidirectional relationships between maths and EBD in primary school children with and without HD.

To do so, we employed four longitudinal datasets from three countries: the Millennium Cohort Study (MCS; U.K; Hansen et al., 2014); the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (LSAC; Australia; Mohal et al., 2023); the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study: Kindergarten Class of 1998-99 (ECLS:1998; USA; Tourangeau et al., 2018); and the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study: Kindergarten Class of 2010-11 (ECLS:2011; USA; Najarian et al., 2017). First, we examined the datasets for differences in maths ability and EBD between children with and without HD. Based on prior literature, we expect to find a statistically significant difference in maths ability between the two groups, with lower performance in the HD group. Given the mixed findings in the literature, we do not have specific hypotheses for the EBD variables, but we expect to find overall greater EBD in the HD group. Next, we employed structural equation modelling to answer the following questions: 1.

What is the developmental trajectory of the relationship between maths ability and EBD in children with typical development (TD) in the earliest years of formal education? Specifically, we examined whether EBD variables at age 5 (preschool) were related to maths ability at age 7; and if maths ability at age 7/8 was related to later EBD at age 9/11.

2. Is early EBD (age 5) related to maths ability (age7/8), and is maths ability related to later EBD in children with hearing difficulties (HD)? How does this association compare to the TD group?

Method

Data for the MCShttps://nces.ed.gov/ecls/dataproducts.asp ECLS:1998, and ECLS:2011 were downloaded from the UK Data Service (https://www.ukdataservice.ac.uk) and the National Center for Education Statistics (Luotonen et al., 1998; Roberts & Hunter, 2004) , respectively. These were then imported into R (R Core Team, 2022) as text files for analyses within R Studio (Posit team, 2022). Access to the LSAC data was requested through the ADA dataverse (https://dataverse.ada.edu.au/), and Stata files were obtained for subsequent analysis in R. All four datasets reported various measures of maths and mental health and used various methods to identify hearing problems, allowing for the testing of replicability of relationships across different measures. These are described below.

MCS

The MCS consists of a stratified sample of children from the UK (n=18818) who turned 9 months-of-age between September 1, 2000, and August 31, 2001 (England and Wales) or between November 24, 2000, and January 11, 2002 (Scotland and Northern Ireland; Hansen et al., 2014). Parent/guardian and child data were collected at 9 months, then at 1, 3, 5, 7, 11, 14, and 17 years. We analysed data collected at 5, 7, and 11 years to capture pre school entry, shortly after the beginning of formal maths instruction, and later measures.

Sample

In the MCS, data related to the child was collected using computer assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) and computer assisted self-interviewing (CASI). Questions were either open ended or consisted of a restricted set of answers. After the field data collection, interviewers coded parent responses to medical/health questions using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10). The ICD-10 codes generated from parental responses relating to hearing ability in infancy, ages 1, 3, 5, and 7 were used to determine hearing status of children for this study. We include measures from before age 5 since early HD such as repeated infections can have knock on effects even if they do not continue into the school years.

To be included in the HD group, parents must have responded "yes" to the question "Have they ever had a hearing test, were there any problems with their hearing then?" at infancy, or the child must be identified as having ear/hearing related problems based on ICD-10 coding. To be included in the TD group, children must have passed the hearing test at infancy (determined by parental response) and must not have any ICD-10 codes relating to ear/hearing problems (see Supplementary Material 2 in Salins et al., 2025 for ICD-10 codes used). Additionally, to be included in the TD group, children must not have had any reported speech difficulties, determined by the following questions: 1.

Age 3, 5 or 7: "Do you have any concerns about child's speech and language problems?" If a parent reported any concerns other than a lisp, the child was excluded.

2. Age 3: "Are they understood by you (parents) at home?" If the parent responded with 'rarely' or 'no' the child was excluded. 'Sometimes' or 'yes' was permitted.

3. Age 7: "Did the child receive special accommodations for speech and language problems or hearing problems?" Excluded if the parent responded yes.

Variables

Maths. The Progress in Maths test developed by the National Foundation for Educational Research (NFER) was adapted to assess maths skills in the MCS. The test used 20 questions to assess children's abilities in numbers, shapes, measurement, and data handling.

Anxiety, Depression, Peer Problems, Attention and Behaviour.

Children's EBD were measured using the parent report Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; Goodman, 1997). The SDQ provides five subscales, however we followed McArthur et al (2022) and created a set of custom subscales to align with constructs of interest here.

McArthur et al. also reported that when attention was measured separately from other variables, the results appeared to differ, that is, the relationship with reading was driven by inattention rather than hyperactivity. To further test this hypothesis, our subscales are identical to the ones used by Salins et al. (2025). The final subscales are peer problems (original 5 items from SDQ), anxiety (3 items from the SDQ emotional problems subscale), depression (1 item from the SDQ emotional items subscale), externalising behaviour (5 items from the SDQ conduct problems and 2 items from SDQ hyperactivity subscale) and attention (3 items from SDQ hyperactivity subscale).

After excluding samples with missing data from the variables described below, the MCS dataset consisted of 797 children with HD and 4457 with neither hearing nor speechrelated difficulties.

LSAC

The LSAC consists of data from two cohorts of Australian children tested every two years from 2004 to 2014. Testing of the Birth Cohort (B-Cohort; n = 5,107) began in their first year of life (0-1 years old); while testing of the Kindergarten Cohort (K-Cohort; n= 4,983) began at age 4 or 5. For this study, we combined data from both cohorts at ages 4-5, 6-7, 8-9, and 10-11 (Waves 3-6 from the B-cohort and Waves 1-4 from the K-cohort). The data from age 4-5 was used for the identification of hearing difficulty, but not in the analyses.

Sample

Children were included in the HD group if parents responded "yes" to either "Does your child have any of these ongoing conditions? Ear infections" or "Does your child have any of these ongoing problems? Hearing problems" in Wave C (age 4/5), Wave D (6/7), or Wave E (8/9). To be included in the TD group, parents must have responded "no" to both those questions in all three Waves of data collection. Additionally, parents must have responded "no" to the question, "Does your child have speech language problems at age 4/5 and 6/7; and to the question "Were they late learning to speak?" at age 4/5. The LSAC sample consisted of 499 children with HD and 1841 children without hearing or speechlanguage problems. Thus, the measures of anxiety, depression, peer problems, attention, and behaviour are identical to those used in the MCS described above.

ECLS:1998

The ECLS:1998 comprises 21,260 children from the USA who were in Kindergarten in 1998 and were regularly tested from age 5 to 13 years (Grade 8; Tourangeau et al., 2009).

We used data collected during the fall term at age 5 (Kindergarten), 7 (Grade 1), and 9 (Grade 3).

Data were collected from 19,173 children at age 5, 16,593 at age 7, and 14,349 at age 9.

Sample

To classify children into HD and TD groups, we used parental responses to the question, "Does your child have difficulty hearing speech?" at age 5 and age 7. For the HD group, parents must have responded "yes" at (at least) one of the two time points and never responded "no". In many instances, answers to the question above were missing for either ages 5 or 7. To identify the TD group, parents must have said "no" at least once and never said "yes". Additionally, they must have responded "no" to the presence of any disability at age 7 -this very conservative rule was adopted because ECLS:1998 did not provide information about the nature of any possible disabilities, and thus a response of yes, or a missing response, could indicate a hearing disability. After excluding children with missing data for at least one variable, the sample included in the HD and TD groups were 217 and 6126 respectively.

Variables

Maths. The focus of maths assessment was conceptual knowledge, procedural knowledge, and problem solving. Specifically, number sense, number properties, operations, and measurement skills were assessed.

Internalising, Peer Relations, and Behaviour. Teachers completed an adapted version of the Social Skills Rating System (Gresham & Elliot, 1990), called the Social Rating Scale (SRS). The modified scale included several items taken directly from the SRS, while others were modified to create four scales (self-control, interpersonal skills, externalising problem behaviours and internalising problem behaviours; Najarian et al., 2019). For our study, we included items related to internalising problem behaviours (both anxiety and depression; n=4), externalising problem behaviours (behaviour, n=5), and peer relations (n=5; interpersonal subscale of SRS). Teachers responded to each item on a four-point Likert scale from Never (1) to Very Often (4). Reliability statistics ranged between .76-.89 for the timepoints included in our analyses (Rock & Pollack, 2002).

ECLS:2011

The ECLS:2011 comprises 18,174 children from the USA who were in kindergarten in 2010, and who were assessed regularly from kindergarten to Grade 5 (see Tourangeau et al., 2018, for more details). To match the other analyses, we focused on data collected when children were aged 5 (Kindergarten fall term; n=15,756), 7 (Grade 1 spring term; n = 15,132), and 9 years (Grade 3 spring term; n=12,896). Broadly, the analysis used the same variables as the ECLS:1998, however, a few additional measures were included, including measures of attention at all three ages, and of victimisation (bullying) and social anxiety at age 9.

Sample

Parental responses to the question "Have you obtained a diagnosis of a problem from a professional" at age 5 (Kindergarten) and age 7 (Grade 1) were used to classify children into the HD and TD groups. A sub-sample of children underwent hearing evaluation during the fall Grade 2 data collection. However, this data could not be used for two reasons: first, because of privacy concerns, the suppressed data is not easily accessible to researchers outside the USA; and second, because the sample size would have been too small for the modelling approach adopted here. To be included in the HD group, parents must have responded "yes" to (at least) one of the two time points and never responded "no".

To be included in the TD group, parents must have responded "no" at both time points and "no" to the presence of any disability at age 5. The data for the question related to speech language problems was not provided in the publicly available dataset; therefore, the broader "disability' variable was used for inclusion into the TD group. After excluding children with missing data for any of the variables described below, the samples included in the HD and TD groups were 296 and 1241 respectively. Bullying or Peer Victimisation. This variable was measured with 4 items adapted from the 21-item scale of Bullying, Fighting, and Victimization (Espelage & Holt, 2001) and measured the extent to which the child was victimized (as opposed to being the bully). The four questions related to different types of victimisation: being teased/made fun of, being the subject of false stories, being subjected to physical violence, and being left out. Children were asked to use a 5-point rating scale ranging from 'Never' to 'Very Often' to indicate how often they experienced each type of victimisation in a school year. The internal reliability for the 4 items was .74 in grade 3 (La Greca & Stone, 1993). Children used a 5-point scale ranging from 'Never' to 'Very Often' to rate how often they worry about what others think of them, whether others like them, and how often they are afraid others won't like them. The reliability for the three items was .83 in third grade (age 9).

Discussion

The purpose of the current study was twofold; firstly, to determine whether students with HD show differences compared to hearing children in their maths ability and EBD; secondly to examine the longitudinal bidirectional relationships between maths ability and EBD in children with and without HD. We addressed these questions using four existing datasets from three countries -USA, UK, and Australia.

EBD and Maths Ability in Children with and without Hearing Difficulties

Children with HD were found to perform significantly more poorly in maths than children without hearing loss in three of the four datasets. These findings align with previous research showing lower maths achievement in children with severe-to-profound hearing loss who have not had early exposure to fluent sign language (e.g., Santos & Cordes, 2022) and those with non-permanent HD (Su et al., 2020). Although children with HD showed overall lower scores, there was no difference found in the Australian dataset which used NAPLAN as the measure of maths ability. Interestingly, Wang et al. (2019), also using NAPLANnumeracy scores of 11-12-year-olds (Grade 5) as the outcome measure, found that students with slight/mild unilateral or bilateral hearing loss had similar scores to hearing students. One possibility is that, as a nationally administered assessment, appropriate adjustments are emplaced for students with recognised disabilities to support them to participate to their maximum potential; however, we cannot confirm how many students this would apply to in the current sample. Additionally, the criteria for having a hearing difficulty from the LSAC dataset is the only one of the four datasets to include reference to ongoing ear infections. This may have resulted in the capture of some children in the hearing difficulty group who have had ongoing ear infections but do not have associated hearing problems.

The findings for some aspects of EBD were consistent across datasets and age; children with HD were consistently rated as having poorer attention and increased externalising symptoms. Regarding peer relations, there was a consistent pattern of poorer peer relationships for children with HD at ages 5 and 7, with the effect size in all datasets decreasing with increasing age. By age 11, differences were seen in only two datasets (LSAC and ECLS:1998). This suggests that overall, initial peer relationship difficulties for children with hearing loss may show some improvement as children get older; indeed, our findings indicate that students with HD do not report a higher rate of victimisation at age 9. Stevenson et al.'s (2015) meta-analysis indicated that parents, teachers, and students themselves consistently reported higher peer problems; meta-regression for age suggested that some effects did decline with age, but not significantly so. Evidence from children with a history of ear infections at age 4/5 years does indicate that initial peer problems do not persist into later years (Hogan et al. 2014), although those children with more persistent hearing problems (reported at 0-1 and 4-5 years-of-age) did have continuing peer problems until 10 years of age.

Children with HD also presented with significantly higher internalising/depression problems compared to TD at all time points in three out of four datasets. The exception was MCS, where they showed greater internalising problems only at age 7. Previous studies have found differences between older and younger children, e.g., Hancock et al (2017) reported that 12-17-year-olds with hearing problems as being 3-4 times more likely to present with internalising problems; this association was not present in 4-11-year-olds once other developmental factors had been controlled for. However, Niclasen et al. (2013) also reported higher internalising difficulties in 9-11-year-old children with a history of otitis media.

The findings regarding anxiety are only available for two datasets and show inconsistent results. Australian children with HD demonstrated greater anxiety at all time points while British children with HD were reported to have greater anxiety problems only at age 7. A third dataset, the ECLS:2011 included a measure of social anxiety at age 9, with children with HD having greater social anxiety. Previous studies have reported that children with hearing loss are at a higher risk of developing anxiety problems than their hearing peers (Long et al., 2021; Theunissen et al., 2012 Theunissen et al., , 2014)), although this finding does not always hold when other developmental factors such as comorbid communication problems or poor health are controlled for.

Unidirectional or Bidirectional Associations Between EBD and Maths Ability

Our next question addressed the directionality of relationships between maths ability and EBD, firstly whether EBD variables at age 5 are related to maths ability at age 7/8, and secondly if maths ability at age 7/8 was related to later EBD at age 9/11. Several previous cross-sectional studies have reported on the relationship between EBD and maths but have not addressed the issue of directionality (e.g., Vieira et al., 2024), whilst others have reported a directional relationship but have not always been able to pinpoint the precise aspects of EBD that explains the relationship. For example, Allen et al (2024) reported that an overall behaviour and emotion assessment from teachers in Grade 1 predicted Grade 4 maths achievement, above and beyond Grade 1 numeracy skills. Our current analyses allowed us to more specifically isolate the aspects of EBD and address the longitudinal directionality of the relationship.

Attention and Mathematics

Early measures of attention (age 5) significantly and consistently predicted maths scores at age 7; better attention/fewer attention problems predicted higher maths scores. This was observed for TD children in all three datasets that offered a measure of attention. For HD children, this same relationship was observed in two datasets (MCS and LSAC) where attention was parent-rated, but not in ECLS:2011 where attention was teacher rated. There were no statistically significant differences between TD and HD students in the strength of the relationship from early attention to later maths achievement. These findings are aligned with previous research showing early assessment of attention skills and teacher and parent ratings of attention problems at ages 4-6 years significantly predict maths achievement in third grade and beyond (Duncan et al., 2007). Salla et al. (2015) also found that early childhood inattention, but not hyperactivity, significantly predicted lower teacher rated maths performance at age 12. It is argued that attentional difficulties in the classroom results in less productive instructional time in the early grades when students are building the necessary foundational skills needed for success in later mathematics. Inattention has been found to be related to academic enablers (e.g., interpersonal skills, motivation, and study skills) which subsequently impact negatively on maths outcomes (Jenkins & Ogg, 2021).

For both TD and HD children, there was also a consistent pattern of findings showing that better maths skills predicted better attentional skills a few years later. The strength of this relationship did not differ between the two cohorts. Previous longitudinal research supports this finding, e.g., Wakeman et al (2023) found that mathematical difficulties predicted later inattention. However, recent evidence from Korean students reports that while self-reported attention predicts maths achievement one year later, difficulties in maths achievement did not predict later attention difficulties (Park et al., 2025).

Peer Relationships and Mathematics

Of the other EBD measures, early peer relationships were most frequently associated with later maths ability, although the findings were somewhat inconsistent. For TD students, in two of the four datasets (LSAC and ECLS:1998), early peer relationship difficulties predicted later lower maths ability. In ECLS:2011 early peer relationship difficulties predicted better maths ability. In the MCS dataset, there was no statistically significant relationship. For students with HD, an association of poor peer relationships with later maths ability was only found in one dataset (ECLS:1998), and the strength of this relationship was significantly higher for students with HD compared to TD students. Unlike the other three datasets where peer / interpersonal relationships are rated by the teacher, in the MCS, peer relationships were rated by the parent. Previous studies have indicated some differences in the ratings of parents and teachers to various aspects of EBD in both hearing (De Los Reyes & Kazdin, 2005) and hearing loss populations (e.g., Wong et al., 2020), and this may account for the disparity in findings across the four datasets reported here.

Many previous studies examining the impact of peer relationships on academic achievement have been cross-sectional and often focus on students older than those in the current datasets. In one of the few studies focused on young children, Kiuru et al. (2014) found that peer acceptance (along with positive teacher affect) positively predicted broader academic skills (latent measure of maths and reading) in Grade 4, even after controlling for kindergarten academic skills. Yu et al. (2023) reported that student-peer relationships were more closely associated with maths ability in Grade 4 and 8 than parent-student and teacherstudent relationships, and Oberle & Schonert-Reichl (2013) found that peer acceptance accounted for 10% of variance in maths achievement in 4 th and 5 th Grade primary students. In a longitudinal study, DeVries et al. (2018) found that peer problems at Grade 5 predicted later lower self-rated maths ability at Grade 7 but did not predict later mathematical achievement. This suggests that some inconsistencies across studies may be due to differences in how mathematical outcomes are operationalised. Finally, in a cross-sectional study of 13-17-yearolds, Li et al. (2020) found that positive peer relationships predicted higher maths achievement, and that academic motivation played a mediating role in this relationship.

Negative interactions with other students are more likely to lead to lower academic motivation as well as other school related difficulties (Askell-Williams & Lawson, 2015; Wentzel, 2015), which ultimately impact on academic outcomes. Wentzel et al. (2021) also propose that motivation (mainly academic self-beliefs and negative affect) and active engagement partially mediated the effect of peer social acceptance on academic achievement. This finding aligns with others suggesting that when other EBDs are accounted for (e.g., hyperactivity, conduct problems, and emotional problems) peer problems are not related to maths achievement.

The relationship of maths ability to later peer relationships was more consistent in both TD and HD samples (seen in all datasets except LSAC) and of similar strength in both cohorts. For both cohorts, maths difficulties were also predictive of later victimisation. The findings of Kiuru et al. (2014) support this bidirectional finding; in addition to the earlier reported finding of early peer acceptance predicting later academic achievement, it was also found that initial academic skills in kindergarten predicted peer acceptance a year later.

Internalising and Mathematics

Greater internalising difficulties predicted poor maths achievement for TD but not HD students in both ECLS datasets. Note that the ECLS datasets include teacher ratings of internalising that include multiple items and address both anxiety and depression. In contrast, the MCS and LSAC datasets measure anxiety and depression separately; both are parentreported and depression is assessed with one parent rated item. Greater anxiety did predict poorer maths achievement for children with HD (MCS) but no such relationship was found in the LSAC dataset. Burchinal et al. (2020) did find that lower levels of internalising behaviour (e.g., anxiety, withdrawal) predicted later higher maths achievement in third grade, and Agnafors et al. (2021) found that parent ratings of internalising difficulties at age 3 predicted below average grade performance in maths at age 12. However, the previous findings associated with internalising difficulties are not consistent. Duncan et al. (2007) found that internalising and externalising symptoms at the start of school (measured by formal assessment and parent and teacher report) did not significantly predict 3 rd grade maths performance (see also DiPerna et al., 2007). The inconsistent findings across the datasets used in the current study, and across previous studies, raises concerns about whether a lack of consistency in findings could be due to measurement and respondent effects. Previous studies using both teacher (DiPerna et al, 2007) and parent (Agnafors et al., 2021) ratings of internalising problems also report contrasting findings, with teacher ratings not significantly predicting later maths achievement but parent ratings of greater internalising problems predicting later below grade maths performance.

In contrast to the inconsistent prediction of internalising problems to later maths achievement, the relationship of maths to later internalising problems was very robust, particularly for TD children.There was only one non-statistically significant relationship between maths and later depression (LSAC). For hearing difficulty children, maths difficulties were also predictive of later anxiety in two of the three datasets that included a measure (the exception being LSAC). In one dataset (MCS) this relationship was found to be significantly stronger for children with HD than for TD children. Whilst some studies have reported that low maths achievement predicts later overall behavioural difficulties, there is limited evidence linking maths to specific later internalising problems; one exception is Park et al. (2025) who reported that difficulties in maths achievement was a statistically significant predictor of later depression.

Externalising Symptoms and Mathematics

Externalising symptoms show a consistent pattern of no statistically significant relationship with later maths outcomes in TD and HD students. McArthur et al. (2022) previously observed that when attention was included alongside measures of externalising symptoms, poor attention (but not greater externalising symptoms) was a statistically significant predictor of later reading achievement. This is confirmed by the findings from the current study. Similar findings come from Jenkins and Ogg (2021) who found that inattention, but not hyperactivity/impulsivity, had a direct negative association with maths achievement. Other longitudinal studies have failed to show that early externalising symptoms directly predicts later maths achievement (Agnafors et al., 2021; DiPerna et al., 2007; Duncan et al., 2007; Fisk & Lombardi, 2021) but may indirectly predict later achievement via task-focused behaviour (e.g., Becherer et al., 2021).

As with the other measures of EBD, there was a consistent pattern across the datasets of lower maths ability predicting later externalising symptoms. This was found in all four datasets for TD students and in 3 of 4 datasets (except ECLS:2011) for HD students; the relationship was stronger in the MCS dataset for HD compared to TD children. Overall, this suggests a very consistent pattern of findings given that there was lower power due to the smaller sample sizes for the hearing difficulty cohorts. In their longitudinal analysis, Wakeman et al. (2023) also found that maths difficulties predicted externalising symptoms five years later.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged, particularly those arising from the use of secondary longitudinal datasets. First, the classification of HD across datasets is likely to vary in accuracy and sensitivity. In many large-scale cohort studies, hearing difficulty and loss is identified through parent report or broad screening indicators rather than clinical assessment, which may result in under-identification or misclassification.

Consequently, the current findings may be most representative of children with mild, fluctuating, or undiagnosed HD, who are often present in mainstream classrooms, but whose challenges may go unrecognised. This is important, as these children may experience subtle but persistent difficulties in accessing linguistic input and participating in social interactions, both of which are critical for learning but are less likely to trigger formal support. As such, the present results may underestimate the extent of difficulties faced by children with more severe or clinically identified hearing loss, while simultaneously highlighting the oftenoverlooked needs of those with less visible impairments.

Second, as with any secondary data analysis, the study is constrained by the nature and quality of the available measures, which differ across datasets. Variability in measurement, particularly in how constructs such as EBD and academic outcomes are operationalised, may contribute to inconsistencies in findings. However, this variability also provides a stringent test of the robustness of observed longitudinal relationships across different measurement contexts. Notably, even after accounting for the autocorrelated nature of constructs over time, associations between EBD and maths outcomes were not always consistent, which may partly reflect differences in how maths achievement was assessed across studies (e.g., standardised tests versus curriculum-based measures). In addition, concerns about the reliability of EBD measures, especially in early childhood, warrant consideration. Parent-reported measures at younger ages may be less stable and more susceptible to bias, which could attenuate or obscure associations with academic outcomes.

A further limitation is the lack of control for potentially important confounding variables, such as children's reading ability and socioeconomic status (SES), both of which are known to be associated with academic outcomes and socio-emotional functioning. The absence of these controls limits the extent to which the observed associations can be attributed specifically to HD or EBD, as opposed to broader underlying risk factors.

Furthermore, the absence of a maths outcome measure at the earliest time point limits our ability to make definitive conclusions about the unique impact of EBD on later maths ability over and above the variance accounted for earlier mathematical skills.

In addition, the present study did not examine potential gender differences which has been identified as a potential moderator in the link between emotional and behavioural difficulties and academic achievement, including maths (e.g., Dias et al., 2022; Park et al., 2025). As such, it remains unclear whether the observed patterns operate similarly for boys and girls, and future research should explicitly test for gender-specific pathways. Together, these limitations underscore the need for future research using more precise and developmentally sensitive measures, as well as designs that can better capture the interplay between hearing status, socio-emotional functioning, and academic development.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations, our analysis of four large datasets has addressed several important gaps in the literature. The findings of both maths difficulties and EBD suggest that, despite a lack of definitive information regarding formal diagnosis of hearing loss, the children classified as having HD in these four datasets do show many similarities to the performance of children with diagnosed hearing loss or those with persistent ear infections.

Importantly, these are children who may slip through the gap of educational and clinical support provisions, resulting in social and linguistic isolation and subsequent difficulties in psychopathology and learning.

Our findings also address the often-unexplored question of direction of relations between EBD and maths achievement and our focus on individual aspects of EBD allowed us to isolate specific patterns of relationships. As found in previous research, there was a consistent pattern of maths ability at ages 7-9 years predicting all aspects of later EBD. This was apparent for both TD children and children with HD, with a handful of findings showing stronger effects for students with HD. A bidirectional relationship was most clearly seen in the relationship of attentional problems and mathematics; early attentional problems predicted later lower maths outcomes, which in turn predicted later attentional difficulties. Of the other EBDs, peer relationships were also notable for similar bidirectional effects, although the findings were not as consistent as for attentional difficulties. This raises several important implications; firstly, for children showing low maths ability in early primary school, and particularly those at greater risk of showing lower performance such as children with HD, it is important to monitor and support ongoing emotional and behavioural wellbeing alongside providing support for mathematical learning. Furthermore, greater focus should be given to the impact of classroom inattention and poor peer relationships on later mathematical learning; again, this may be particularly important for students with HD who have greater attentional problems and poorer relationships at this young age. It is possible that these factors interact such that attentional difficulties and less peer support result in fewer learning opportunities in the classroom. Interventions to support early classroom attention and enhance peer relationships may be a key mechanism through which to engage young children in the important early learning opportunities that provide the foundation numerical skills for later mathematical learning.
