Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Materials in Escalation Crossover

Public, political, and legal attention has increasingly concentrated in recent years on the continually evolving commercial, cultural, and technological landscape of child sexual exploitation. A series of high-profile cases, gaining public outcry, has placed the spotlight on practices and technologies that seem to blur the boundaries between fantasy and harm. In late 2025 there was global and political outrage relating to online retailers Shein and Temu, after they advertised and sold child-like sex dolls on their digital platforms (Pizzirani, 2025). In recent years, Australia, among many other countries, has intensified efforts to combat the importation and possession of child-like sex dolls (CLSDs 1 ), with 47 dolls being intercepted by the Australian Border Force between July 2024 and June 2025 (Poposki, 2025). In late 2025 and early 2026, Grok, a free artificial-intelligence (AI) assistant tool, enabled users to generate sexually explicit images of people, including child sexual exploitation material (CSEM; Croft, 2026). This sparked a second global public outcry, regulatory scrutiny, and condemnation from politicians, calling for stronger legal frameworks around generative AI and its possible harmful functions. The Internet Watch Foundation also reported a 26,362% rise in realistic AI videos of child sexual abuse in 2025, with 65% categorized as the most extreme (Internet Watch Foundation, 2026). Earlier in 2020, the Australian Senate was called to review and examine "hentai", a pornographic form of Japanese anime and manga, for child exploitation or pornographic material as a matter of urgency, noting the abundance of obscene content, including child rape, in some titles (Stirling, 2020). Australian authorities are exploring stricter regulatory controls on the importation of hentai (Butler, 2020), including several arrests in 2024 and 2025 (Australian Federal Police, 2024; Brischetto, 2025). Similarly, in late 2025, a Sydney-based author was charged with CSEM offences 1 Refer to Table 3 for all abbreviations related to fantasy sexual materials within this manuscript. relating to her erotica novel, Daddy's Little Toy, which allegedly contains references to sexual content involving a minor (Bolza, 2025). This news quickly became a widely reported controversy, indicating how written fictional content can spark public and legal scrutiny under CSEM laws. These recent developments have collectively led to a heightened sense of moral urgency about the distinctions between fantasy and harm in a technologically mediated world.

According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual-Fifth Edition Text Revision

(DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association, 2022), pedophilia is defined as a paraphilia entailing sexual attraction to prepubescent children aged 13 or younger, characterized by recurrent and intense sexually arousing fantasies, urges or behaviors involving sexual activity with children. Pedophilic disorder is where an individual has acted on these urges, for example, through engagement with CSEM or contact offending. Such behaviors have lifelong harmful impacts on victims, including psychosocial problems, self-harm, psychiatric disorders, and physical health diagnoses (Hailes et al., 2019).

Due to the stigma and moral opprobrium attached with pedophilia and pedophilic disorder (Jahnke et al., 2015), as well as the harmful impacts to victims, some individuals experiencing pedophilic attractions may be troubled by their interests and feelings that are considered morally "forbidden" in modern society (Houtepen et al., 2016). While not explicitly included in the DSM-5-TR as a paraphilic disorder, hebephilia is the sexual attraction to pubescent children, roughly aged 12-14 (Blanchard et al., 2009). This term has not come into widespread use, even among professionals working with sex offenders, however, research has demonstrated the utility of specifying a hebephilic group for research purposes to compare across pedophilic, hebephilic, and teleiophilic individuals (sexual attraction towards adults; Blanchard et al., 2009). Seto (2017) estimates the prevalence of pedophilia in community samples of men to be 1%, with a slightly higher number for hebephilia, making these to be relatively rare. Seto (2017) further proposed that pedophilia, and chronophilia (sexual attractions directed toward specific age groups) more broadly can be understood as sexual orientations for age which share commonalities with sexual orientations for gender.

Sexual attraction to children is known to be a motivating factor for offending behavior; however, it is a misconception to consider the two as synonymous as many individuals who have committed sexual offences against children do not have sexual attractions to children (Savoie et al., 2021). In fact, it is suggested that only roughly 25 to 50 per cent of incarcerated child sex offenders are motivated primarily due to sexual attractions to children, complicating the presumed causal link between such attractions and child sex offences (Beier et al., 2015). Many individuals with a sexual interest in children do not act on their attractions, referred to as "nonoffending" or "virtuous" pedophiles or minor attracted persons in recent literature (Cantor & McPhail, 2016; Jones et al., 2021). To refrain from offending, these individuals are suggested to need sufficient self-control and effective coping mechanisms and strategies to relieve their sexual arousal to children (Houtepen et al., 2016; Jones et al., 2021).

Fantasy or Fictional Sexual Materials

Fantasy or fictional sexual materials (FSMs) can be understood as sexually explicit or suggestive content that depicts children in fictional or non-real scenarios (Lievesley et al., 2023). FSMs offer a purely fictional, non-contact medium for sexual gratification that is fundamentally detached from the abuse of real persons (Al-Alosi, 2018; Lievesley et al., 2023). Lievesley et al. (2023) suggested that FSMs can serve as a sexual outlet for release, often functioning as a means of instigating or enhancing sexual experiences through fantasizing or masturbating. In this sense, it differs from CSEM as FSMs donot involve the abuse of a real child, nor do they portray real children. Instead, they present images, depictions, or renders of fictitious characters appearing to be children (Al-Alosi, 2018). Lievesley et al. (2023) suggest that FSMs are distinct from other forms of sexual expression due to i) their basis in fantasy or fiction, and ii) where the situation being fantasized would entail behaviors that could be illegal or harmful in real life. As such, whether FSM has clinical utility for individuals with sexual attractions to children is a question for further research. While a standardized accepted definition of FSM has not been yet operationalized, FSMs can take several different forms including CLSDs, AI generated images, virtual or illustrated avatars (such as Japanese anime or manga), self-generated artistic drawings, and self-generated stories (Al-Alosi, 2018; Lievesley et al., 2023). The risk implications of FSMs are highly contested given the dearth of research, particularly in the context of escalation crossover.

Escalation crossover refers to an under-researched group of individuals who display a particular trajectory of progressing from online offences to contact child sexual offences (Ahmad et al., 2025); however, little is known about the mechanisms and drivers that influence this trajectory (Fortin et al., 2018). Some empirical findings suggest that there may be a behavioral basis for concern with FSM use noting that deliberate sexual fantasizing, particularly when vivid, plausible, and paraphilic, is linked to sexual behavior (Willis & Bartels, 2024). There appears to be limited research exploring FSMs and their utility for individuals with pedophilic interests. This presents barriers for clinicians and rehabilitation programs to understand and develop tailored treatments. There is a need for further research about FSMs to understand whether these materials can function as an inhibiting factor against offending behaviors, or if they have the potential to increase risk towards contact offending.

Child-Like Sex Dolls

Recent technological advancements have enabled the development of artificial substitutes or augmentation devices for human sexual interactions, such as sex dolls or humanoid robots (Danaher & McArthur, 2017). The production of CLSDs, or anatomically correct, life-size dolls made to look like pubescent or prepubescent children, has been commercialized by companies based in China, Hong Kong, and Japan (Brown & Shelling, 2019). CLSDs are often manufactured with anatomically correct body openings that can be used for sexually penetrative purposes, such as genitals and mouths (Desbuleux & Fuss, 2024a) and that can fit an adult penis (Brown & Shelling, 2019). Customers are also able to customize the dolls' features to their liking, including tailoring of the skin, hair color, eye color, facial features, body shape, and calibration of the weight to resemble a real-life child as much as possible (Brown & Shelling, 2019; Maras & Shapiro, 2017; Torjesen, 2017). Other than their sexual function, sex dolls generally have been described as having purposes other than sexual gratification and are discussed as being "posthuman partners" in contrast to advanced sex toys (Döring & Poeschl, 2020). Their use has been considered as an aid for people, such as those with sexual paraphilias or disorders (Döring & Poeschl, 2020; Levy, 2009; McArthur & Twist, 2017). Brown and Shelling (2019) hypothesized that engagement with CLSDs may result from shifting from viewing CSEM towards physical rather than virtual representation of their pedophilic interests. CLSDs have witnessed steadily rising popularity over recent years (Langcaster-James & Bentley, 2018); however, the evidence of the risk and functionality of such objects remains scarce (Döring et al., 2020).

The emergence of CLSDs has led to a highly contentious and polarized debate of "good versus bad" with both arguments underpinned with an almost entirely absent pool of evidence (Desbuleux & Fuss, 2024a). Harper et al. (2023) and Harper and Lievesley (2022) explored three potential models of sex doll ownership, including dolls as protective, dolls as risk-enhancing, and dolls as functional life tools. Model 1 suggested that owning a child doll resembling a preferred sexual target could help those with sexual attractions to children to achieve sexual gratification and reduce sexual fantasies about children (Moen & Sterri, 2018; Rutkin, 2016). Model 2 suggested that the rehearsal of sexual fantasies and the reinforcement obtained via sexual gratification can contribute to strengthening the sexual attractions to children and the development of implicit theories supporting sexual abuse against children (Brown & Shelling, 2019; Chatterjee, 2020; Danaher, 2017). Model 3 suggested that, particularly considering that such dolls have been described as "posthuman partners", they may not be positively or negatively related to the sexual risk, but rather, be seen as a functional part of the lives of individuals who are unable to fulfil their sexual attractions in real life (Ferguson & Hartley, 2009; Harper et al., 2023). A recent study conducted by Desbuleux and Fuss (2024a) undertook a review of empirical literature on CLSDs, noting that there are only a handful of empirical studies conducted in this area. The review concluded that current research does not suggest that sexual expression with CLSDs increases risk for contact offending; many users indicate that they use the CLSDs as a safe and "victimless" sexual outlet in contrast to other outlets such as CSEM. The authors further argue that currently, legislators criminalize the use of CLSDs in many jurisdictions around the world to protect the normalization of the sexual abuse of children, however, they must consider that criminalization may be counterproductive as the CLSDs may have a potential to reduce risk of contact offending.

Virtual CSEM

Virtual CSEM is considered a subset of CSEM and can depict highly severe or deviant forms of abuse without real-world limitations. The creation of Virtual CSEM is not new; for example, in 2010, a school principal from the United States physically cut and pasted the heads of their child students onto nude adult bodies (Stelmack vs State, 2010).

However, in the age of AI, creating high quality images and videos that are indistinguishable from real ones has never been easier (Eelmaa, 2022; Steel, 2024). Virtual CSEM, also known as pseudo child pornography, fictional child pornography, deepfakes, or fantasy images, is digital sexual content that involves fictitious minors in computer-generated, AI-generated, graphic, and textual formats (Christensen et al., 2021; Eelmaa, 2022; McLelland & Yoo, 2007). Virtual CSEM can include entirely computer or AI generated images; alterations of real images to look like a child, for example altering an image of an adult to morph a child's face onto the adult body (Simpson, 2009); or altering real images of children so the child appears to be engaged in sexual acts (Christensen et al., 2021). This can also extend to the sexually explicit depictions of fictitious minors, including through drawings, cartoons, animations (including Japanese anime and manga formats), video game spaces, literature (also known as text-based or narrative CSEM), paintings, dolls, and sculptures (Christensen et al., 2021; Eelmaa, 2022; Lehmann et al., 2025).

Virtual CSEM has been the subject of ethical and moral debates, particularly as technological advances have blurred the line between what is considered "real" and "virtual" (Mateo, 2008). Despite the limited research in this area, polarizing arguments exist in whether Virtual CSEM harms or does not harm children, and that it can potentially serve as a gateway to contact offending due to desensitization (Maras & Shapiro, 2017). Furthermore, it has been proposed as the initial stage in a broader escalation pathway, a "fantasy escalation effect", progressing from Virtual CSEM to CSEM and finally to contact offending as the individual seeks to recreate the original levels of satisfaction (Christensen et al., 2021; Sullivan & Sheehan, 2002). Nonetheless, the relationship between Virtual CSEM and contact offending is not deterministic and may depend on other motivational or facilitating factors (Lehmann et al., 2025). This relationship is even less clear in relation to the more niche or arguably benign types of Virtual CSEM, such as text-based CSEM.

Computer or AI-generated Virtual CSEM.

Hereafter referred to as Virtual CSEM-AI; AI content generators, or generative AI, are tools trained on a large dataset to enable the creation of text, images, or videos from text-based prompts (Steel, 2024). These tools, including "Undress AI" or "Nudify" allow for generating entirely synthetic images, or deageing and undressing of individuals based on real images, with their use rising exponentially (Steel, 2024). Other visual-generation tools, such as Grok and Stable Diffusion, have been found to use CSEM in its training data to facilitate individuals in the creation of new, customized Virtual CSEM-AI based on simple prompt engineering (Levine, 2023). Recently, a narrative review and conceptual synthesis of literature conducted by Ó Cardha et al. (2026) examined the harms associated with Virtual CSEM-AI. The authors suggest that Virtual CSEM-AI reflects a fundamental shift in the facilitating mechanisms of child sexual exploitation, potentially functioning as an accelerant for its normalization and reinforcement of related behaviors. Findings of this review note that Virtual CSEM-AI has the potential to harm through means such as the normalization and desensitization of extreme content and being a behavioral bridge to offending. The authors suggested that such dynamics challenge the view of Virtual CSEM-AI being a controlled or protective outlet, however, there may be certain scenarios where a carefully controlled form of synthetic material (noted as being distinct from Virtual CSEM-AI) enables users to stay away from offending behaviors.

Another review investigating the legal challenges of Virtual CSEM-AI concluded that despite no physical abuse being involved, Virtual CSEM-AI sustains the same cycles as CSEM of victimization (i.e., deepfakes; if the material is based on real children), desensitization, and fueling of paraphilic fantasies that can increase risk of contact offending (Parti & Szabó, 2024).

Illustrated Virtual CSEM.

Hereafter referred to as Virtual CSEM-I; is a form of Virtual CSEM in illustrated formats such as cartoons, drawings, and animations (Christensen & Vickery, 2023). This includes anime, a Japanese form of animation, and manga, the Japanese comic or graphic novel version that serves as the basis for anime (Stirling, 2020). A notable proportion of anime and manga, including hentai, features CSEM, including depictions of children engaged in explicit sexual activities and poses as well as depictions of child sexual abuse (Stirling, 2020). "Lolicon" refers to anime with sexually explicit depictions of underage girls, often associated with abnormal and extreme sexual desires or practices, with the male equivalent called "shotacon" (Cooray et al., 2025; Jones et al., 2021).

Text-Based Virtual CSEM.

Hereafter referred to as Virtual CSEM-T; a form that uses written or spoken text that can be classified as CSEM if it involves clear child characters and contains explicit sexual depictions in a way to imply real sexual acts (Lehmann et al., 2025). A recent review conducted by Lehmann et al. (2025) found that literature on Virtual CSEM-T identified the theoretical potential of such material in contributing to the development, reinforcement and possible escalation of atypical sexual interests. The review found that out of nine studies, only two discussed the possible cathartic or substitutive functions of Virtual CSEM-T, while six studies emphasized its potential to enhance risk. The authors suggested that while some preliminary studies had positioned Virtual CSEM-T as a risk factor for contact sexual (re)offending, it may also have a potential to function as a protective outlet for those with pedohebephilic interests. Ultimately, the review concluded that given the parallels to other controversial and legislative ambiguous materials, such as CLSDs, future research should move to rigorously examine the psychological, criminological, therapeutic, and societal dimensions of Virtual CSEM-T.

Motivation-Facilitation Model

The motivation-facilitation model (Seto, 2019) operationalizes sexual offending as a product of the combination of motivating and facilitating factors that are present in an individual and their environment. Motivating factors include those that increase the likelihood of an offender seeking out deviant sexual stimuli, such as paraphilia, high sex drive, and intense mating effort. Facilitating factors are those that can increase the likelihood of the sexual offence to occur especially when paired together with motivating factors, and can be trait (e.g., antisociality), state (e.g., mood) and situational (e.g., vulnerable victims, absence of guardians). This presents a comprehensive explanatory model for the link between pedophilia and offending; suggesting that although sexual attractions to children can motivate sexual offending, the link is contingent on the presence of facilitating factors (Lievesley et al., 2023).

Application of the motivation-facilitation model to FSMs use suggests that FSMs' function may be bifurcated as either a risk or inhibitory factor to contact offending (Lievesley et al., 2023). As noted by Ó Cardha et al. (2026) in relation to Virtual CSEM-AI, FSMs have the potential to act as both facilitation and situational factors of sexual offending through reinforcement of sexual scripts, lowering inhibitions, and their accessibility, anonymity, and customizability. Further, as discussed by Lievesley et al. (2023), pedophilia is noted to be a motivating factor to drive offending and so engagement with FSM may heighten sexual arousal as a facilitating factor to increase the likelihood of offending. Repeated engagement with FSMs, particularly in forms offering realistic sexual experiences such as CLSDs, may play a role in reinforcing offence-supportive beliefs and the internalization of implicit theories regarding the acceptability of sexual relations with children leading to the subsequent increase of risk in contact offending (Lievesley et al., 2023). Extending Ó Cardha et al.'s (2026) analysis through Lawless Space Theory (Steel et al., 2023a) -a framework for cyber criminality -it is possible that the scalability and adaptability of FSMs in their virtual forms can allow offenders to engage in the production of highly customized sexual content that aligns to their sexual interests. This may, in turn, entrench distorted beliefs, normalize depictions of abuse, and reduce inhibitions to lead to contact offending.

On the other side of the debate, it is possible that FSM use can act as an inhibitor by means of a sexual outlet to reduce the likelihood of contact offending, allowing for sexual release and catharsis particularly in scenarios where there are no co-existing facilitating factors (Grady et al., 2019; Lehmann et al., 2025; Lievesley et al., 2023). Harper and Lievesley (2022) noted that in the context of CLSDs, it may be that the ownership of dolls may function as a motivating factor, such as through strengthening atypical sexual interests or feelings of dissatisfaction with living partners, or as a facilitating factor, such as through contributing to the development of offence-supportive cognitions. Noting the limited nature of research about FSMs, particularly with the rise of new technologies opening avenues for broadening the range of FSMs, such as through AI, there is a call for further empirical research to understand the use of FSMs to help inform clinicians and policymakers professional decisions (Lehmann et al., 2025; Lievesley et al., 2023).

Aims of the Current Study

Despite their varied forms, CLSDs, Virtual CSEM-AI, Virtual CSEM-I, and Virtual CSEM-T share conceptually congruent features in being fantasy or fictional based noncontact materials that do not entail the physical contact abuse of a real child in their production or engagement. These materials also serve the same purposes with regard to their use, being sexual arousal, fantasizing, gratification or release; and, as noted by Lehmann et al. (2025), have parallels in being controversial or legislatively ambiguous. These similarities provide a rationale for examining CLSDs and Virtual CSEM as a unified category. To our knowledge, while research on individual forms of FSMs have been conducted, no study to date has consolidated this spectrum of FSMs to assess empirical evidence about their roles with regard to escalation crossover. FSMs are conceptually relevant to escalation crossover as they occupy a unique and contested position at the boundary of fantasy and harm; a matter that is particularly important when considering the risk that FSMs may present in the pathway to contact offending.

Noting the evidentiary gap in FSM research, this study follows on from the suggested areas of immediate relevance by Lievesley et al. (2023) as well as being a natural progression from previous findings of Ahmad et al. (2025; 2026) relating to understanding the mechanisms and drivers that influence the escalation crossover trajectory. In our research, we present the first empirical synthesis about a comprehensive FSM landscape and investigate the empirical evidence base informing the debate regarding risk versus inhibiting functions. This research is particularly important in the current, complex backdrop of public anxiety, political pressure, and rapid evolution of technological reality, where the prevailing discourse is marked with polarized viewpoints in an evidential vacuum.

Method

We carried out a systematic review, conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Page et al., 2021).

Search Strategy

The search strategy was developed iteratively after a review of literature to assist in the identification of relevant terminology. A search of EBSCOhost including American Psychological Association (APA) PsycArticles, APA PsycINFO, and the Psychology and behavioral sciences collection; PubMed, including Criminal justice database, Psychology collection and the social science database; and Scopus was conducted in December 2025 to identify published peer-reviewed articles that presented an examination of FSMs relating to pedophilia or child sex offenders. The full search commands are available in Supplemental Material Appendix A. To supplement the database search, searches of databases and grey literature were also performed on Google Scholar and Research Gate with the following generalized search terms: "AI generated CSEM/CSAM", "child sex dolls", "Japanese anime/manga CSEM/CSAM", "deepfake CSEM/CSAM", "written/narrative CSEM/CSAM".

Searches were refined to exclude articles published in languages other than English.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies identified in the searches were assessed for eligibility in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria in Table 1. Eligibility with respect to escalation crossover was defined broadly to capture any related data on FSM use in populations or contexts relevant to pedophilia or child sexual offending risk. In one of these cases, this involved "tickling" and touching two of the perpetrator's primary school-aged students on the genital and buttock areas over their clothes. The second case involved 50 counts of aggravated "indecent dealing with a child" and 11 counts of digital rape against two child complainants. The study did not provide any further detail to clarify the temporal sequencing of these offences in relation to CLSD acquisition, making difficult to ascertain whether the perpetrators escalated to contact offending after CLSD use.

Nonetheless, the evidence of co-occurring CLSD possession and contact offending histories within the same individuals is an important finding, although the extent to which such co-occurrence reflects an escalation pattern is unclear. Notably, most of Richards et al.'s sample were also convicted with CSEM offences in addition to possession of a CLSD, ranging from less serious to the most serious types as per the Oliver scale (R v Oliver, 2003). In two cases without CSEM convictions, the CLSD perpetrator was found in possession of material "ignorable" according to the Oliver scale and entailed photographic images of children in gymnastic poses or swimwear. No other studies in the review contained data relevant to the question of CLSDs' potential role in risk enhancement. Desbuleux and Fuss (2024b) found that pedo-hebephilic individuals, amongst their sample of sex doll owners, reported a strong and significantly greater decrease in compulsive sexual behavior when compared to participants with a teleiophilic sexual interest. Notably, all pedo-hebephilic participants reported consuming pornography but with a lower frequency since using a doll. Participants in the artificial companion group reported a significantly greater decrease in pornography consumption than participants in the toy group. Pedohebephilic participants also reported that they used their doll as an alternative sexual outlet as they could not act on their atypical sexual interests in real life and were more likely to act out illegal fantasies on their dolls when compared to teleiophilic samples (Desbuleux & Fuss, 2024b). These included sex with minors (84.21%), sexual violence (10.53%), and incest (5.26%). Phillipps and Willis (2025) found that two participants reported possessing CLSDs capable of "sex" which they utilized as a substitute for interactions with real children. One participant in Harper and Lievesley's (2022) study also stated that their purpose of owning a CLSD was to prevent contact offending behavior. Further, pedo-hebephilic participants were more likely to report a change in their sexual fantasies or interests, including a loss of interest in sexual intimacy with children as the doll enabled them to fulfil all their sexual needs relating to the attraction of children (Desbuleux & Fuss, 2024b). Harper and Lievesley (2022) found no evidence that proclivity or arousal for hypothetical child sexual abuse were higher among CLSD owners when compared to non-owners.

Protective or Functional

The multifunctional purpose of doll ownership was also explored in three articles. Harper and Lievesley (2022) demonstrated that CLSDs were equally used for both sexual and emotional gratification. Jones et al. (2021) reported that the participant who crafted a homemade doll said it gave them a sense of "companionship and belonging". Phillipps and Willis (2025)also emphasized the role of CLSD in relational wellbeing beyond masturbation, including cuddling. Christensen and Vickery (2023) undertook a search of newspaper articles using a systematic search on the ProQuest Database to identify characteristics of Virtual CSEM offenders, including Virtual CSEM-AI, Virtual CSEM-I, and Virtual CSEM-T. The authors found that across 26 offenders from articles published between 2008 and 2021, that most cases occurred in 2019. Newspaper articles reported crimes in Australia (48%) followed by the United Kingdom (24%), the United States (16%), and Canada (12%). All offenders were male, except in one case involving a female and male co-offenders. The average age at offence onset was 32.81 years (average age at sentencing being 39.71 years), with the youngest offender 19 years old and oldest 73 years old. Twenty percent of articles indicated some form of mental health issues or developmental disorders on the part of the offender, including autism spectrum disorder, depressed mood disorder, pedophilic disorder, compulsive personality disorder, and voyeuristic disorder. Most cases involved both Virtual CSEM and CSEM, except for seven cases that involved only Virtual CSEM. Charges varied from possession (84%), followed by production or recording (32%), access (20%), and distributing (16%).

Characteristics of Virtual CSEM

Christensen and Vickery (2023) noted that it was difficult to distinguish between Virtual CSEM and CSEM possessed, likely due to the sheer amount of the material.

Nevertheless, amongst the cases they analyzed, there was an average of 10,354 images/videos across both types of material. The majority of the Virtual CSEM comprised cartoons, anime, or computer-generated files. The offenders reported on appeared to have an interest in certain types of Virtual CSEM, including graphics interchange format (GIFs) made from photographs from local children; transposing faces of children onto sexually explicit photos (including photographs of children the offenders had abused in the past); alterations of television shows; and a range of cartoons, comics, or AI-generated images. Eighty-six percent of cases involved pre-pubescent children depicted in the material, including toddlers, and 14% of cases solely involved pubescent children. Risk-Enhancing

Christensen and Vickery (2023) found that almost half of the cases included additional charges, most of which related to contact sexual offences (including sexual assault, sexual penetration of a child, and rape). Many articles highlighted the "very real" link between Virtual CSEM and child sexual abuse, with one article arguing that the sexualization process is comparable regardless of whether the materials depict cartoons or real victims. In one case, a prosecutor indicated that sexual offending may have been spurred due to the normalization of the cartoon material through its use to groom children. In a different case involving possession of a large quantity of Virtual CSEM, the offender himself said that he was concerned about the effect the material had on him, and that he could see himself being at risk of engaging in worse offending as a result. Eelmaa (2022) explored discourse on a Reddit forum concerning Virtual CSEM (Virtual CSEM-AI and Virtual CSEM-I) and found that the sexualization of minors in Virtual CSEM was seen by some posters as promoting pedophilia, which was presented concurrently with the statement that Virtual CSEM use can consequentially increase sexual offending against children. Others believed that this premise was irrational as attraction was a natural orientation that cannot be chosen. Studies were cited to establish the link between violent pornography and sexual offending to support the argument that the sexualization of minors in Virtual CSEM can increase sexual offending by the same pathway. It was also expressed on the Reddit forum that deepfakes and hentai are "gateway" materials to condition and normalize sexual stimulation with children, behavior that can "progress towards escalation". Similarly, Jones et al. (2021) found that non-offending pedo-hebephilic individuals utilized various legal outlets to manage their sexual desires, including Virtual CSEM-I (anime); however, many with exclusive pedophilia indicated that they were unable to satisfy their urges using such techniques. It is also notable that the legality of these materials varies across countries and jurisdictions, and so activities described as legal in the data may not be legal across all regions.

Protective or Functional

Both Jones et al. (2021) and Phillipps and Willis (2025) identified the use of Virtual CSEM-I as a legal outlet (noting that these materials may not be legal in all regions of the world) for the management of sexual desires among non-offending populations. Both studies emphasized the pivotal role of such material in regulating sexual behavior. Eelmaa (2022) found arguments on the Reddit forum identifying Virtual CSEM as a justified method of therapy or self-control, offering a "healthy release" for people who otherwise may offend in real life. Noting that in some jurisdictions around the world Virtual CSEM is criminalized, some Reddit users suggested that making such material illegal would cause an increase in sexual offending as people would have nothing left but to look for "the real thing". Others also argued that deepfakes can offer a genuine experience to make society safer. Participants repeatedly drew a line between real and virtual. The existence of an immediate victim was a central factor of this debate, where it was argued by posters that "no victim, no crime."

(2017) conducted an exploratory study on Virtual CSEM-T from interviews with users and professionals, finding that both groups pointed to the potentially more stimulating nature of Virtual CSEM-T as a fantasy enhancer. Some professionals believed in the risk potential of Virtual CSEM-T as a precursor to CSEM that could lead to contact offending. Users explicitly acknowledged Virtual CSEM-T as a risk factor for contact offending to continue their sexual fantasies given that "the narrative is stronger than the image to take people to the next level." Professionals explained that deviant sexual fantasies can be easily enhanced with a narrative, as opposed to a visual, as the individual has control in the scenario, including age, gender, and appearance of the child. Both Chaplin et al. (1995) and Quinsey and Chaplin (1988) investigated how individuals with and without a history of child sexual abuse offending responded to Virtual CSEM-T by measuring penile responses. Both studies not only found a significant level of sexual arousal in response to Virtual CSEM-T in individuals with a history of child sexual abuse offending; they also found a markedly higher response to child-focused narratives (with a preference for victim-suffering narratives) as opposed to consensual adult narratives. Discussion

In this systematic review, we explored the current state of knowledge regarding the role of FSMs in escalation crossover, specifically including CLSDs, Virtual CSEM-AI, Virtual CSEM-I, and Virtual CSEM-T. Across the body of evidence, the reviewed studies document two contrasting patterns between FSM engagement and offending risk. However, this observation must be qualified with regard to the nature of evidence available: most of the research was cross-sectional and correlational, with no longitudinal studies identified.

Consequentially, our findings discussed below remain provisional, and may reflect associations rather than causal relationships.

In contemporary CLSD discourse, there is a tendency to assume their use as a riskenhancing means (Brown & Shelling, 2019; Chatterjee, 2020; Danaher, 2017); however, while the current evidence base does not permit a definitive conclusion about risk, such assumptions may represent a common assumption as opposed to an evidence-based conclusion. At the societal level, the tendency to assume the use of CLSDs, as well as other types of FSM, to be risk-enhancing may be shaped by predominant moral and ethical concerns; where narratives of a sexualized child evoke strong normative opposition and panic (Hawkes & Dune, 2013). Considering the widespread nature of this opposition and the profound discomfort that topics on such materials can generate, it can be difficult to disentangle the matter from its moral dimensions. Particularly in the absence of a robust and sufficient evidence base, the normative weight of the topic may inevitably shape how arguments are received. This is the case with legislative action in many parts of the world which has been driven principally by societal unease and precautionary reasoning despite research connecting the use of CLSDs with contact offending being sparse (Lehmann et al., 2025).

Although findings at this stage are too tentative to conclude what role CLSDs play, suggestive findings support the protective or functional hypothesis where the use of CLSDs may be an alternative sexual outlet for those with paraphilic fantasies to regulate their sexual needs, in addition to potentially providing a series of other wellbeing-related roles (including attachment formation and control over one's environment) for their owners. Although Richards et al. (2025) found two cases in which CLSD owners also had contact child sexual offences, they interpreted that while some CLSD users may already have perpetrated contact abuse, those who have not are unlikely to "crossover" to contact offending.

A recent systematic review identified that only 0.8 to 12% of CSEM offenders escalate to contact offending (Ahmad et al., 2025), which is potentially suggestive of individuals achieving sexual gratification via such material instead of abusing children in real life. Similarly, in the context of CLSDs, it is possible that the utility of a doll may help individuals form proxy intimate attachments and achieve sexual satisfaction to increase overall life satisfaction and wellbeing, which may also include reducing likelihood of contact sexual offending (Moen & Sterri, 2018; Rutkin, 2016). Nevertheless, the risk-enhancing hypothesis cannot be ignored as there is an alternative interpretation where some men use CLSDs to avoid sexually harming children but fail to control their urges and thus escalate to contact offences. Despite convergent findings suggesting that the use of CLSDs may provide a structured and controlled outlet that, unlike CSEM, may be considered "victimless", and is associated with reductions in behavioral risk markers as well as a diminished interest in sexual contact with children; there remains the potential that for some, CLSD use may be risk-enhancing. Notably, almost 80% of cases from Richard et al.'s study (2025) included possession of CSEM in addition to the CLSD. This co-occurrence can be interpreted in multiple ways. One possibility is that individuals with strong attractions to children may seek diverse and immersive ways of non-contact sexual stimulation and expression while still holding strong inhibitions against contact offending. Another possibility is that CSEM engagement desensitizes viewers and leads to engagement with CLSDs due to a desire to be "engagers" of sex acts with CLSDs, as a more tangible means of fantasy enactment. In such cases, sexual fantasies about child sexual abuse may develop into behavior through purchase and importation of the CLSD, and this pairing of the fantasy with the behavior, when repeated, can increase the individual's desires to achieve gratification through contact offending (Arrigo & Purcell, 2001; Maras & Shapiro, 2017; Pfaus et al., 2020). As such, the CLSD may become a step between using CSEM and contact offending (Brown & Shelling, 2019). However, we note that the available data do not distinguish between these interpretations and co-occurrence alone cannot support a directional claim about an escalation crossover pathway. Additionally and notably, Richard et al.'s study (2025) found that in two cases without CSEM convictions, the CLSD perpetrator was found in possession of material "ignorable" according to the Oliver scale which entailed photographic images of children in gymnastic poses or swimwear. Similarly, Ahmad et al. (2026) found that some individuals with pedophilic attractions engaged in selective consumption of CSEM, preferring "acceptable" content, such as "non-nude" over typical CSEM content. Non-nude is defined as imagery of children in sexually suggestive poses or non-provocative clothing but does not depict full nudity. As such, it is possible that this pattern of consumption of utilizing non nude content and CLSDs and in the absence of CSEM, may reflect morally selective engagement with material that is perceived to be less harmful. Ahmad et al. (2026) explained this through the lens of Aronson (1994), where individuals with sexual attractions to children achieve selfaffirmation of viewing themselves as reasonable through consumption of morally permissible or "victimless" content and asserting that they do not i) physically engage in the sexual abuse of children, and ii) engage with the exploitative or hurtcore forms of content.

Overall, while the risk versus protective mechanism of CLSDs in escalation crossover remains unclear, the evidence tentatively suggests that doll use at least may be functional or protective, particularly noting their dual role as a sexual proxy and relational companion for its users. Even if CLSDs have a functional effect as an outlet on most users, it may have an escalating effect on a subset of users that cannot be dismissed when evaluating its broader implications. Psychologist Svein Øverland offered the following perspective when interviewed for a news media report, "We cannot reject that for a very small group, [CLSDs] can have an effect. But I think that if you open that door, you will enter a very ugly room, where we justify the use of such dolls, which can be very harmful" (Zondag & Bulai, 2017).

The extant literature on Virtual CSEM provides a nuanced picture of its role in relation to escalation crossover. This includes support for the risk-enhancing hypothesis. For example, many arguments in Christensen and Vickery's (2023) study centered on the link between engaging in Virtual CSEM and contact offending. The potential for escalation crossover as a consequence of viewing Virtual CSEM was highlighted, with evidence indicating a substantial number of cases including contact offences. Eelmaa (2022) likewise presented many arguments framing Virtual CSEM as a direct facilitator of contact offending.

Christensen and Vickery (2023) provided a hypothetical example, similar to the use of CLSDs, where an individual begins engaging in Virtual CSEM to masturbate, becomes desensitized, escalates to CSEM and finally escalates to contact offending to reach the initial level of gratification. This risk-enhancing hypothesis in which Virtual CSEM is viewed as a gateway or a step along a progressive pathway to contact offending is consistent with some studies arguing that the risk lies in the pairing of fantasy (sexual abuse of a child) with masturbation and subsequent orgasm that conditions and reinforces the strength of the fantasy, thereby potentially increasing the likelihood of behavioral escalation towards contact offending (Christensen et al., 2021; Maras & Shapiro, 2017; Ost, 2002; Sullivan & Beech, 2004). Notably, Jones et al. (2021) found that many individuals with exclusive pedophilia indicated that they were unable to satisfy their urges using Virtual CSEM-T, with some pursuing more "inventing" ways of coping, such as through a CLSD. Conversely, evidence also points to a potential protective or functional role for Virtual CSEM. Both Jones et al. (2021) and Phillipps and Willis (2025) suggested that Virtual CSEM appears to support safe sexual self-regulation for some individuals, where a number of individuals who remain nonoffending utilize such material as alternatives. Many arguments identified by Eelmaa (2022) supported the protective role of Virtual CSEM for public safety as a means of a safe sexual release. Here, a differentiator of realism was identified where Virtual CSEM-AI was seen as more problematic due to the potential existence of a real victim (e.g., in production), as opposed to Virtual CSEM-I which may be considered to be "art."

Virtual CSEM-T paint a generally consistent picture on how Virtual CSEM-T is experienced and its indicative risk-enhancing role in escalation crossover. Several sources emphasized its role as a fantasy generating or enabling medium and its capability in eliciting sexual arousal. This is consistent with research suggesting that while Virtual CSEM-T cannot be viewed as a literal script for contact offending, it can function as a cognitive facilitator of escalation crossover (Gee et al., 2006). Early laboratory studies (Chaplin et al., 1995; Quinsey & Chaplin, 1988) indicated that Virtual CSEM-T can elicit measurable sexual arousal in individuals with a known history of child sex offences and can function as indicators of sexual interest in children; Seto and Eke (2017) found Virtual CSEM-T to be a correlate of pedophilic interest. Further, Crookes et al.'s (2017) findings regarding the fantasyenhancement potential of Virtual CSEM-T due to the individual's control over the scenario is consistent with research suggesting that Virtual CSEM is a feasible pathway to escalation crossover due to its ability to be manipulated to depict children as actively engaging in or enjoying abuse (Christensen et al., 2021). Christensen et al.'s, (2023) identification of pedophile manuals further indicated increased risk of escalation crossover by enhancing individuals' perceived competence. Blalock and Bourke (2022) found that such manuals can reinforce offence supportive cognitions by portraying child sexual offences as accepted or beneficial and offering step-by-step instructions for victim selection, grooming, and exploitation. These findings are consistent with a recent systematic review on Virtual CSEM-T conducted by Lehmann et al. (2025), who found that most literature consistently identifies the potential of Virtual CSEM-T in contributing to the development, reinforcement, and possible escalation of atypical sexual interests. Similar to Lehmann et al. (2025), in the current systematic review, we also found only two studies indicating Virtual CSEM-T's possible cathartic or therapeutic functions (Jones et al., 2021; Phillipps & Willis, 2025).

Similar to other forms of FSM, this is consistent with research suggesting that such material may play a protective or regulative function in the absence of other facilitating factors (Lievesley et al., 2023). It has been suggested that these materials may form part of a harm reduction approach likened to the harm-reduction function of methadone for opioid addiction, particularly for those who are exclusively attracted to children as it can enable sexually gratification without the harm of a real child (Seto, 2025).

Taken together, research suggests that FSMs can potentially reinforce deviant scripts and increase risk of contact offending; however, it can also serve as an alternative outlet for sexual arousal and have a potential protective or functional utility. This finding is consistent with Lievesley et al.'s (2023) application of the motivation-facilitation model to FSM use, arguing that its function may be bifurcated as both a risk and inhibitory factor in relation to escalation crossover. Noting that pedo-hebephilia is a motivating factor towards sexual offending, engaging with FSMs could act as a facilitator to increase contact offending risk by contributing to the development of offence-supportive beliefs and implicit theories. On the other hand, FSM can also reduce the likelihood of contact offending by allowing for sexual release, which may reduce motivations of seeking out real children. It may also be possible for the role of FSMs to vary over time and across individuals, depending on the level of engagement, types of FSM, and patterns of consumption. These results indicate the need for future research to consider the contextual and individual-level factors when evaluating the utility of FSMs regarding their risk-enhancing or protective role. Particularly, future research should explore the individual factors that differentiate whether FSMs may have riskenhancing outcomes for some individuals, and protective or functional outcomes for others.

In the absence of robust evidence differentiating individuals for whom FSMs may be riskenhancing from those for whom it may be protective, it may be wise to observe a precautionary or "better safe than sorry" (Hendriks-Lundh, 2023) approach regarding the use of FSMs.

Limitations and Future Directions

Prior to this review, discussions about FSMs and escalation crossover have largely been grounded in theoretical and conceptual formats, or focused on FSM types in isolation. In this systematic review, we extend research in the field of FSMs and escalation crossover in three ways. Firstly, we provide the first synthesis to empirically capture the breadth of FSM research across a wide array of its formats, including CLSDs and Virtual CSEM, and present an inaugural effort in mapping the landscape of FSMs within a single review that enables cross-format comparison. Secondly, we evaluate all available evidence against a specific research inquiry to understand the role of FSMs within the context of escalation crossover for a more informed, and less moralistic, debate on the utility of such materials. Thirdly, and as discussed below, we propose consolidated, standardized terminologies and definitions for FSMs to advance cumulative knowledge across the field.

Variability in Nomenclature

There is a significant variability across existing research regarding the terminology used for different types of FSMs, including for CLSDs (inflatable or synthetic dolls, inanimate dolls, child sexual abuse dolls, anatomically correct dolls), Virtual CSEM-AI (AI-generated CSEM, computer-generated images, computer generated files, deepfake, digitally altered children), Virtual CSEM-I (child-oriented anime or manga, lolicon, shotacon, cartoons, Japanese style anime involving children), and Virtual CSEM-T (narrative CSEM, text-based material, written stories, pornographic stories, text-based fantasies, child sex stories, fictional stories). The lack of consistent operationalization within the literature leads to challenges in its discussion (Lievesley et al., 2023). To advance cumulative knowledge across the field, we recommend that future research employs consolidated, standardized terminologies and definitions for such FSMs. The terminology and definitions outlined in in Table 3 and Figure 2 captures a recommended conceptual classification and dictionary for future research, including definitions for FSMs; FSM -child related; CLSDs; and Virtual CSEM; including AI, I and T modalities. Regarding the proposed definition of Virtual CSEM-T, we note that Lehmann et al. (2025) proposed to omit the "for sexual purposes" clause to avoid evaluative language and keep the term strictly descriptive. Such an approach aids neutrality; we suggest retaining a rephrased clause as "intended for or used for sexual gratification or arousal" to reflect the inherent nature of such materials and preserve consistency with the overall FSM conceptualization.

In our proposed definitions, we also recommend excluding any material that portrays or is based on real children in forms of Virtual CSEM (including deepfakes) as we believe such materials are beyond the scope of what can be considered "virtual" and should be treated as CSEM due to their inherent exploitation of real children. Even if the final product appears to be fictional, the underlying source material captures a real victim who has been sexualized and therefore constitutes a direct form of exploitation. However, we acknowledge that operationalizing this definition can present substantial practical challenges, particularly for AI-generated material. Firstly, it is known that existing CSEM can be used to train AI generated material, meaning offenders can use AI to produce new synthetic depictions of previously abused children (Brown & Shelling, 2019). Even in non-CSEM contexts, imagegenerative AI models have been found to be have been trained using real images of children without their knowledge or consent (Elliott, 2024). While AI models are trained on statistical patterns learned from real images and the synthetic output is not a direct collage of the training images, there is still a real risk that AI models may "leak" elements of their training data, for example, a child's face (Lavoipierre, 2024). The second challenge lies in determining the extent to which a given image, illustration, or story, may represent a real child. Despite these challenges, we maintain that any materials clearly depicting a real child should be included from the FSM category and instead be treated as CSEM. We acknowledge that determining whether a real child is clearly depicted can be challenging in practice, especially in cases where it is unclear whether the depiction is based on a real child or is entirely synthetic. We invite future research to consider and apply these definitions for greater conceptual clarity and consistency within the field of FSM.

Reconceptualizing Escalation Crossover

The findings of this review also have implications for how escalation crossover is conceptualized and operationalized. In this review, we considered studies across the diverse formats of FSMs, including CLSDs, AI generated, illustrated, and text-based, several of which exist outside purely digital contexts, such as CLSDs or hand drawn illustrations. This suggests that the definition of escalation crossover, currently defined as a particular trajectory of progressing from online offences to contact child sexual offences (Ahmad et al., 2025; Ahmad et al., 2026), may be unnecessarily narrow when applied to FSMs. The current definition has emerged from the recognition that internet-facilitated offending may be a potential precursor to contact offending for some individuals. Drawing on the evidence from this review and considering the current backdrop of the expanding landscape of FSMs, we propose a conceptual refinement of the definition of escalation crossover. Escalation crossover should be redefined to the transition from non-contact behaviors (as opposed to from online behaviors more narrowly), to contact offending. This expanded conceptualization acknowledges that non-contact sexual behaviors can sit outside the digital realm and include types of FSMs, such as CLSDs or illustrations that are created or stored offline. While these materials are frequently circulated in digital environments, their existence is not limited to these spaces.

We acknowledge that broadening the definition as such introduces a degree of conceptual ambiguity. For example, non-contact child sexual abuse can include both online and offline behaviors, such as voyeurism (spying on or watching another person), exhibitionism (exposing yourself in public), or exposing children to pornography (Hortle et al., 2024), and other behaviors that do not involve direct physical contact with a child. These behaviors vary significantly based on offender agency, nature of contact with the victim, and degree of harm, blurring the boundaries between so-called "contact" and "non-contact". The ambiguity is particularly profound in behaviors such as live streaming child sexual abuse, where the viewer, who although has been said to be "hands off" (Christensen & Woods, 2024) due to not being physically present, actively dictates and facilitates abuse against a real child victim (ECPAT International, 2017). Such examples challenge the modality-based distinction between contact and non-contact offending. Conceptually, the examination of escalation crossover is to understand the characteristics, risk factors and pathways that enable an individual to crossover from behaviors that do not involve physically and personally abusing a child, towards behaviors that do. As such, the question focuses less on whether a behavior is online or offline, and more on: i) offender engagement, including levels of active participation or direction of sexually abusive behaviors; ii) the severity of victim harm and the functional role in generating and facilitating harm to a real child; and iii) the contribution of sexualization of children and reinforcement of offence-supportive beliefs. Nonetheless, these are unchartered waters and categorizing behaviors among a non-contact to contact continuum risks obscuring important differences between offender involvement and victim harm, as evident in live streaming child sexual abuse. These are questions for further research to consider and investigate.

Methodological Limitations

The methodological and sample constraints of the studies considered in this systematic review are noteworthy. Most studies relied on self-reported retrospective survey data which, particularly for stigma-eliciting topics such as this one and, in the context of ongoing discussions about criminalization, are susceptible to self-presentation biases. Most designs were also cross-sectional, limiting the extrapolation of any causal inferences about whether such materials can have a risk-enhancing or inhibiting role in escalation crossover.

Sample sizes in most studies were small or jurisdictionally specific, limiting the generalizability of their findings. Some studies used international internet-recruited samples.

Many studies also considered the presence of contact offending in their samples; however, this was done without also considering the temporal nature of these offences. For example, both Richards et al. (2025) and Christensen and Vickery (2023) identified contact child sexual offences in their samples but could not report on whether this occurred before or after FSM engagement. This is a limitation for exploring the role of FSMs in escalation crossover, as it hampers inferences about whether engagement with FSMs can escalate users to contact offending.

There is a need for future research to further empirically explore the function of FSMs as risk-enhancing facilitators or protective/functional pathways in escalation crossover among samples of individuals with sexual attractions to children. Some individuals may sincerely perceive their engagement with FSMs as risk mitigating strategies to protect themselves from offending against real children, and it is important for further research to utilize designs that would evaluate such assumptions empirically; that is, to explore whether FSM-based strategies indeed represent genuine forms of risk reduction with respect to contact offending, or whether such beliefs may rather reflect cognitive distortions or similar. The current state of research provides insufficient evidence in this regard, and as such, clinicians need to rely on professional judgement to determine whether FSM use may be a protective, functional mechanism, or reflect a dynamic risk factor in an individual case.

Importantly, given the many different forms and uses of FSMs, in this review we highlight that a functional or protective pathway may exist for some individuals, suggesting that a blanket prohibition or a uniformly risk-based stance may not be grounded in evidence.

Further research is required to support decision making frameworks regarding FSM use. Such research should prioritize understanding the contextual and individual level factors that may differentiate outcomes among FSM users and would also benefit from adopting longitudinal designs to establish directionality of effects of FSM use. Further research is also needed to keep pace with the rapid and ongoing technological developments in the area, including to understand the diversity in FSMs that are readily available, and how digital tools and platforms are evolving to inform risk assessment for clinicians and authorities.

Empirical Focus

Our review focused exclusively on published, peer-reviewed, empirical studies on FSMs and did not include grey literature, theoretical, or conceptual papers. While this offers methodological rigor to our review and ensures our findings are based on observable research outcomes, only 13 studies met the eligibility criteria, and the small volume of empirical evidence limits the overall generalizability of our findings. Non-empirical papers may have illuminated additional insights into the engagement and effects of FSMs.

Implications

While public, academic, and legislative discourse about the effects of FSMs continue, this systematic review suggests that FSMs plays a complex role that may be risk-enhancing for some, and protective for others. The role of FSMs may also vary over time and contexts within individuals, depending on their level of engagement, the types of FSM, and patterns of consumption. These findings raise important implications for clinical practice, and this dual function must be measured in clinical, policy, and legal contexts to avoid uniform assumptions about FSMs and weigh their use against contextual and individual factors.

Clinicians may be guided to consider individual-level factors such as level of engagement, motivations for use (i.e., coping strategy or substitute outlet), and other co-occurring risk factors when evaluating the risk of FSM use. Policymakers and legal frameworks should differentiate Virtual CSEM from CSEM that involves real children to acknowledge differences in victimization. Researchers should shift towards adopting a standardized dictionary for consistency in research. A nuanced and evidence-based approach that balances FSMs' harm-reduction potential with their risk-enhancing potential is essential for future policy, practice, and research applications.

Conclusion

The aim of this systematic review was to synthesize the current understandings of FSMs with particular regard to their role in escalation crossover. Our findings suggest a bifurcated pattern of FSM engagement, where it can play both a protective/functional or a risk-enhancing role. Regarding characteristics associated with use, we found that CLSD owners were more likely to be male, aged between 37 and 43, single, and sexually attracted to children. Virtual CSEM users were likely to be male and 33 years old. CLSD ownership was suggested as potentially protective or functional; correlated with reductions in compulsive sexual behavior, pornography consumption, and noted as an outlet for individuals in fulfilling their sexual needs. Virtual CSEM was frequently described as a potential gateway of escalation crossover due to the sexualization process being comparable with CSEM, but also as a potentially legal (depending on region of use), outlet for non-offending individuals.

Multiple studies noted the use of CLSDs and Virtual CSEM concurrently with CSEM engagement. In the absence of robust evidence differentiating individuals for whom FSM may be risk-enhancing from those for whom it may be protective, it is important to recognize that FSMs do not appear to operate through a uniform risk pathway, and their role is likely to be complex and context dependent. As such, a blanket prohibition or a uniformly risk-based stance may not be empirically robust, and a balanced, nuanced approach is needed across policy, clinical and legal context.
