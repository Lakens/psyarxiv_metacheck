Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Introduction

Imagine two physicians who make the same clinical decision. They both decide not to give an aggressive medical intervention to a terminally ill patient. One physician acts out of compassion, believing further treatment would only prolong suffering. The other acts from indifference to the patient, motivated primarily by convenience and financial considerations. To an outside observer, their behavior is identical. Although the observable behavior is the same, nearly everyone regards these decisions as morally different. We praise one physician and condemn the other not because of what they did, but because of why they did it.

Intuitions like the ones described above reflect a fundamental property of moral cognition.

People evaluate actions by inferring the underlying motives that produced them across everyday social interaction (Hofmann et al., 2014), organizational decision-making (Silver & Silverman, 2022), and legal systems (Nadler & McDonnell, 2012), such that two people performing the same action may be judged very differently based on the reason they act. In fact, legal systems formalize this intuition by categorizing identical conduct into different offenses, defenses, and sentences depending on the mental state and intention behind it (Hessick, 2006). Consider a murderer and a would-be murderer whose gun misfires. The outcomes differ markedly, yet people judge the wrongness of their conduct and the quality of their character as nearly the same, even while judging that the two deserve different punishment (Cushman, 2008). Conversely, people who perform very different actions may receive quite similar moral evaluations when observers infer similar underlying motives. A cash bribe, a consulting fee for work never performed, and a noshow job arranged for a spouse share almost nothing in common as behaviors, yet all three are condemned in nearly identical terms once each is framed as the sale of official judgment for private gain. Moral evaluation, therefore, is fundamentally inferential. It is shaped not just by what the agents do, but by what observers think they wanted or sought to accomplish through their actions (Carlson et al., 2022; Cushman, 2008; Reeder, 2009). Indeed, observers spontaneously infer motives from relatively sparse behavioral information (Heider, 1958; Aarts, Gollwitzer, & Hassin, 2004; Malle & Holbrook, 2012)-often without conscious awareness (Hassin, Aarts & Ferguson, 2005)-and this capacity emerges early in development (Meltzoff, 1995; Gergely et al., 1995; Hamlin, Wynn & Bloom, 2007; Baillargeon, Scott, & Bian, 2015). These inferred motives and efforts shape judgments of blame, praise, trustworthiness, and moral character, often independently of behavioral outcomes (Bigman & Tamir, 2016; Goodwin, Piazza, & Rozin, 2014; Reeder, 2009). People judge the desire to harm someone else as morally wrong, even when no actual harm occurs (Cushman, 2008), and they distinguish between physically identical harmful acts on the basis of the motive behind them, judging an aggressor who acts from self-defense far less harshly than one who acts to benefit himself (Reeder et al., 2002). Thus, humans appear to treat motivation, and not behavior alone, as a primary object of moral evaluation across development and social contexts.

Given the centrality, one might expect moral psychology to possess a mature theory describing the structure of moral motivation itself. Surprisingly, despite decades of research on moral judgment, moral reasoning, moral identity, and moral behavior, the field has not adopted a general architecture for representing moral motivational states. Existing theories have identified many important moral motives and have greatly advanced our understanding of how morality develops, how people reason about moral problems, and why particular motives influence behavior (Blasi, 1983; Graham et al., 2013; Haidt, 2001; Janoff-Bulman & Carnes, 2013; Rai & Fiske, 2011). However, comparatively little attention has been devoted to a more fundamental question of how moral motivational states themselves should be represented. This is not to say that no one has confronted this representational problem at all. Research on moral dilemmas has always assumed competing motivations, and over the past decades it has produced formal models that separate those motivations rather than treating them as a single continuum. Process dissociation estimates deontological and utilitarian inclinations as independent parameters, so that a person high on both can be distinguished from a person low on both even when the two produce the same choice (Conway & Gawronski, 2013). The CNI model goes further, separating sensitivity to consequences, sensitivity to moral norms, and a general preference for inaction versus action irrespective of consequences and norms, which allows inaction that is driven by sensitivity to moral norms to be distinguished from inaction that holds regardless of what is at stake (Gawronski, Armstrong, Conway, Friesdorf, & Hütter, 2017). Related work has modeled trade-offs between specific moral concerns, such as the opposition between fairness and loyalty confronting a potential whistleblower (Waytz, Dungan, & Young, 2013), and has asked whether such trade-offs operate the same way when choices carry real consequences (Bostyn, Sevenhant, & Roets, 2018). We regard this literature as establishing a core representational insight, and much of what follows attempts to generalize it. Our question is what happens outside the dilemma paradigm, where no comparable architecture has been adopted, and whether separability achieved for one pair of inclinations within one experimental design is sufficient for moral motivation in general.

This distinction is crucial because every scientific theory in psychology attempts to answer at least one of two conceptually separate questions. First, what psychological processes exist?

Second, how should those processes be represented? The first question concerns explanatory content; the second concerns theoretical architecture. These questions are closely related but logically distinct. Researchers may correctly identify the psychological processes underlying a phenomenon yet adopt a framework that obscures important distinctions among those processes. Consequently, explanatory power depends not only on identifying the relevant psychological variables but also on representing them in a manner that preserves psychologically meaningful differences.

We argue that moral psychology has paid comparatively little attention to this second question. Existing theories differ substantially in the motives they emphasize, the mechanisms they propose, and the moral phenomena they seek to explain. Yet they often share an implicit assumption that moral motivation fits within relatively simple motivational structures. Whether emphasizing moral valence, approach and avoidance, prescriptive and proscriptive concerns, or specific moral domains, these theories generally compress complex motivational states into relatively few representational dimensions. Such simplification is often useful, but it comes at a theoretical cost. Whenever psychologically distinct motivational states occupy the same representational location, information is necessarily lost. This loss of information has consequences. A person who experiences no meaningful moral motivation differs psychologically from one who experiences equally strong but opposing motivations, even if both ultimately produce identical behavior (e.g., no action at all). Likewise, an individual reluctantly performing what they believe is the morally right action differs fundamentally from one who enthusiastically performs the same action. Similarly, a person who simultaneously wishes to retaliate against another individual and to forgive them occupies a different motivational state than someone who experiences neither desire. Yet these distinctions become difficult or impossible to preserve when motivational states are represented within insufficiently expressive theoretical architectures. As a result, existing representations may successfully explain many aspects of moral cognition while nevertheless struggling to adequately capture motivational conflict, mixed motives, emotional experience, moral character inference, and context-dependent variation.

To address this problem, we propose the Motivation of Moral Action (MoMA), a cognitive architecture that represents moral motivation as a multidimensional motivational state defined by independent approach and avoidance orientations toward perceived moral good and perceived moral bad. Rather than proposing new moral motives, MoMA represents how multiple motives combine into dynamic motivational states. By treating moral valence and motivational direction as independent dimensions, the framework preserves motivational distinctions that simpler representations necessarily compress while remaining compatible with existing theories concerning empathy, identity, fairness, loyalty, and other moral motives.

The crossing of direction with valence has a direct predecessor in research on prescriptive and proscriptive morality, which distinguishes activating motivations to do good from inhibitory motivations to refrain from harm and shows that the two rest on distinct motivational systems and carry different cognitive, evaluative, and political signatures, proscriptive morality being concrete, mandatory, and condemnatory while prescriptive morality is more abstract, discretionary, and commendatory (Janoff-Bulman, Sheikh, & Hepp, 2009; Janoff-Bulman & Carnes, 2013). That is a natural starting point for the framework developed here. MoMA preserves it and extends it in two respects. First, it treats the resulting orientations as independent, continuous dimensions rather than two contrasting regulatory systems, so an individual may be high on both at once. Second, it fills the two cells that the prescriptive-proscriptive contrasts leave unoccupied: motivation directed away from what the agent perceives as morally good, and the motivation directed toward what the agent perceives as morally bad. Prescriptive regulation pairs activation with good and proscriptive regulation pairs inhibition with bad, so the two remaining pairings have no place in the model. Janoff-Bulman and Carnes (2013) do recognize qualities that obstruct the good and qualities that facilitate the bad, which they label prescriptive and proscriptive vices. The difference is representational rather than conceptual. Because the Model of Moral Motives has two rows, corresponding to the two forms of regulation, no location within it allows a person to be simultaneously high on activating the good and high on obstructing it. What is described there as a vice is treated here as its own dimension, which may be high at the same time as its counterpart.

Whether those two cells are psychologically important is a fair question, and we take it up below.

We begin by distinguishing moral motives from moral motivation and examining why theories require explicit architectures. We then review what existing theories, including the formal models developed in dilemma research, already represent and what they leave unrepresented. We then derive the requirements that any comprehensive theory of moral motivation should satisfy and evaluate existing theories against those requirements. Finally, we introduce the Motivation of Moral Action (MoMA), demonstrate how its multidimensional architecture addresses the representational problem, and discuss the implications of this framework for explaining moral behavior, moral judgment, moral character, and future empirical research.

Theoretical Requirements for Representing Moral Motivation

Before proposing a new cognitive architecture of moral motivation, we should clarify what it is intended to represent. Moral psychology has produced numerous theories of moral judgment, reasoning, identity, emotion, and behavior, yet these theories often differ not only in their explanatory mechanisms but also in the psychological entities they seek to describe. Consequently, discussions of moral motives, moral motivation, and moral behavior are sometimes treated interchangeably despite referring to distinct levels of analysis. Clarifying this is essential because a theory can only be evaluated relative to the phenomenon it is intended to represent.

More fundamentally, every scientific theory must determine not only which psychological processes are important, but also how those processes should be represented. Representation is not a secondary issue or a matter of terminology. A theory's representational structure determines which psychological distinctions it preserves, which it collapses, and therefore what kinds of explanations it can ultimately provide. Before evaluating existing theories, or proposing a new one, let's establish what we believe an adequate representation of moral motivation should accomplish.

Moral Motives, Moral Motivation, and Moral Behavior

We distinguish three related but conceptually distinct constructs: moral motives, moral motivation, and moral behavior. Moral motivation refers to something different. Rather than describing any single motive, moral motivation refers to the overall psychological state produced, often by the simultaneous activation of multiple motives, as individuals rarely act from a single motive in isolation. A physician may simultaneously experience compassion toward a patient, professional duty, concern about causing harm, and fear of legal consequences. A whistleblower may experience commitment to justice alongside loyalty to colleagues and concern for their own family. These motives coexist within a single psychological state that ultimately influences decision-making.

Finally, moral behavior refers to the observable actions that result from this motivational state interacting with situational opportunities and constraints. Behavior is therefore neither identical to motives nor to motivation. Individuals with similar motivational states may behave differently because they possess different resources, face different constraints, or encounter different opportunities. Conversely, identical behaviors may arise from fundamentally different motivational states. Distinguishing these levels of analysis is therefore essential for understanding what a theory of moral motivation is intended to explain. Situational context operates at every level: it activates particular motives, directly shifts the motivational state, and constrains which behaviors are available. Behavior feeds back over time, reshaping subsequent motives. MoMA is a cognitive architecture for representing the moral motivational state (dashed region)-formally, a location in a four-dimensional space defined by approach and avoidance orientations toward perceived moral good and bad-rather than a theory of which motives matter or how motivation translates into action. Because the mapping from state to behavior is many-to-one, different motivational states may produce identical behaviors.

Why Representation Matters

Scientific theories do more than identify psychological variables; they specify how those variables are organized. In personality psychology, for example, individuals vary along multiple continuous dimensions rather than belonging to discrete personality categories (McCrae & Costa, 1987; Widiger & Trull, 2007). Similarly, affective science often represents emotions within multidimensional spaces that preserve relationships among emotional experiences (Russell, 1980; Posner, Russell, & Peterson, 2005; but see Ekman, 1992). These representational choices are not descriptive conveniences; they determine which kinds of psychological variation we can observe, compare, and explain.

The same challenge arises for moral motivation. A theory may correctly identify many important moral motives while nevertheless adopting a representational structure that obscures meaningful differences among motivational states. This distinction between explanatory content and representational structure is fundamental. Explanatory content concerns which motives influence moral behavior. Representational structure concerns how those motives combine into coherent psychological states.

A cognitive architecture refers to this representational structure. Specifically, it specifies (a) the psychological entities being represented, (b) the dimensions along which those entities vary, and (c) the relationships among those dimensions. Once specified, this architecture provides the framework for describing, comparing, and explaining individual motivational states.

Representation therefore constrains explanation. When psychologically distinct states occupy the same representational location, no subsequent theoretical refinement can recover the lost distinction. Conversely, representations that preserve psychologically meaningful differences allow richer explanations, more precise predictions, and stronger empirical tests.

Representational Compression

Every representation necessarily simplifies reality. Simplification is often desirable because it facilitates explanation and empirical measurement. However, simplification becomes problematic when psychologically distinct states become indistinguishable within the representational framework itself. We call this loss of theoretically meaningful information representational compression.

Consider two individuals who both decline to donate to charity. One feels little concern for charitable causes and experiences virtually no motivation to donate. The other strongly values charitable giving but is simultaneously committed to supporting an ill family member whose immediate needs exhaust available financial resources. Although both individuals display identical behavior, their motivational states differ substantially. The first reflects motivational indifference; the second reflects motivational conflict. A representational framework that mistakenly places these individuals in the same motivational location necessarily compresses psychologically meaningful differences into a single category.

Representational compression has implications beyond behavioral prediction. Motivational conflict may produce guilt, psychological distress, or future behavioral compensation, whereas motivational indifference may not. Observers may also draw different inferences about moral character depending on which motivational state they believe underlies the behavior. If these distinctions are eliminated at the representational level, they cannot later be recovered through additional explanatory mechanisms.

Requirements for a Cognitive Architecture of Moral Motivation

1. Multiplicity. If moral motivation consists of multiple simultaneously active motives, an adequate cognitive architecture must represent multiple motives simultaneously.

2. Independence. If these motives can coexist independently, then the architecture must preserve their independence.

3. Continuity.

If motivational strength varies continuously, then the representation should accommodate continuous variation rather than discrete categories. 4. Conflict. If individuals experience motivational conflict, then opposing motivational tendencies must be representable simultaneously.

5. Situationality.

If motivational states change across situations, then the architecture must distinguish changing motivational configurations. 6. Generality. Because moral motives arise across diverse domains (e.g., care, fairness, loyalty, authority, honesty, and countless others), the architecture should be sufficiently general to represent motivational states regardless of the particular motives that generate them.

Collectively, these requirements establish evaluation criteria outside any particular theory. So, while existing theories are clearly valuable, we must see if their representational structures satisfy the requirements necessary to represent the multidimensional nature of moral motivational states. We now evaluate existing theories according to these criteria before introducing the Motivation of Moral Action (MoMA).

Existing Theories of Moral Motivation

Moral psychology has produced numerous influential theories, each identifying important psychological processes that contribute to moral judgment and behavior. These theories have substantially advanced our understanding of morality by explaining why people care about particular moral concerns, how moral reasoning develops, how social relationships shape moral decision-making, and how specific motives influence behavior. Therefore, we do not address whether these theories are empirically successful or theoretically valuable. Rather, it is whether they provide a sufficiently general cognitive architecture for representing moral motivational states as defined in the previous section. Using the representational requirements derived above, we evaluate existing theories by the motivational distinctions they preserve and the ones they necessarily compress.

Theories Organized Around Individual Moral Motives

Many influential theories explain moral behavior by emphasizing one or a few particularly important psychological motives. Examples include empathy-based accounts (e.g., Hoffman, 2000; Batson, 2011), moral identity theory (Blasi, 1983; Aquino & Reed, 2002), and other approaches that identify specific motivational processes as primary drivers of moral action.

These theories have generated important insights regarding the development and consequences of particular moral motives. For example, empathy has been shown to promote helping behavior, reduce aggression, and influence judgments concerning fairness and harm (Batson et al., 1981; Eisenberg & Miller, 1987; but see Vachon, Lynam, & Johnson, 2014).

Likewise, moral identity predicts numerous forms of prosocial behavior by emphasizing the importance of maintaining consistency between one's actions and moral self-concept (Aquino & Reed, 2002; Hertz & Krettenauer, 2016).

From a representational perspective, however, these theories primarily highlight which motives influence moral behavior. They do not specify how multiple motives combine into a unified motivational state. As a result, these approaches do not accommodate motivational configurations involving several simultaneously active motives.

Consider an individual who simultaneously experiences compassion toward another person, resentment regarding past mistreatment, professional obligation, and concern about violating personal values. Each of these motives may be recognized independently in the literature, yet the theories generally do not specify how to represent these motives together as a single psychological state. Their explanatory focus lies in identifying influential motives rather than providing a general architecture for representing interactions among multiple motives.

Importantly, this observation should not be interpreted as a limitation of these theories themselves. They were developed to explain specific motivational processes rather than to provide a comprehensive representational framework. Evaluated according to their intended purposes, they remain among the most influential and empirically supported theories in moral psychology.

Single-Dimensional Representations of Moral Motivation

A second class of theories represents moral motivation along one or a few broad motivational dimensions. Examples include approaches that conceptualize morality along continua such as approach versus avoidance, promotion versus prevention (Higgins, 1997), or general moral valence.

Representing motivation along continuous dimensions offers several important advantages.

Continuous representations accommodate differences in motivational intensity, facilitate quantitative measurement, and permit parsimonious theoretical models. Such approaches have proven highly productive across many areas of psychology.

Nevertheless, any representation based on a single motivational dimension constrains the range of distinguishable psychological states. When opposite motivational tendencies occupy opposite ends of the same continuum, increases in one dimension necessarily imply decreases in the other. Psychologically, however, opposing motivations frequently coexist. This limitation is not peculiar to moral psychology, and it has been confronted before.

Attitude researchers long represented evaluation on a single bipolar continuum, which cannot distinguish a person with strong, competing evaluations from one with no strong evaluation at all. Kaplan (1972) called this the ambivalence-indifference problem and proposed measuring positive and negative reactions separately rather than forcing a single bipolar judgment; from those two components, three quantities emerge that a bipolar score cannot yield: total affect, ambivalence, and polarization. The resulting evaluative space model treats positivity and negativity as separable substrates rather than opposite poles of one dimension (Cacioppo & Berntson, 1994; Cacioppo, Gardner, & Berntson, 1997 , 1999). The conclusion that tradition reached is stated in terms that transfer directly to the present case: low activation of both positive and negative evaluative processes reflects neutrality or indifference, whereas high activation of both reflects ambivalence, so that attitudes are represented more completely within a bivariate space than along a bipolar continuum (Cacioppo, Gardner, & Bernstron, 1997). The authors describe the positive and negative processes as stochastically and functionally independent, with distinct activation functions rather than a shared one. Subsequent work showed that positive and negative evaluations can coactivate, that coactivation is measurable, and that it predicts outcomes a net score cannot (Larsen, McGraw, & Cacioppo, 2001; Priester & Petty, 1996). MoMA applies this separability logic to moral motivation and crosses it with motivational direction, yielding four independent dimensions rather than two. The argument developed here is therefore not that moral psychology has made an unusual error, but that a representational problem already solved for evaluation remains open for moral motivation.

One qualification in that literature deserves to be carried over. Cacioppo and colleagues also propose a principle of motivational certainty, according to which evaluative structure tends to gravitate from bivariate toward bipolar when the underlying beliefs become the object of deliberation or a guide to action (Cacioppo, Gardner, & Berntson, 1997). An analogous point might be brought up here: if moral motivation is above all a guide to action, perhaps a single continuum is adequate precisely in the cases that matter. We think the qualification strengthens the framework rather than undermining it, for two reasons. The principle describes a tendency toward bipolarity under deliberation, not a collapse into it, and therefore requires a representation that can express both structures to describe the movement between them. More usefully, the principle becomes a prediction MoMA can test against: the four dimensions should be clearly separable when a person reflects on a moral situation and least separable in the moment of acting, and the degree of separability should vary with deliberation. A single-dimensional architecture cannot state that prediction, let alone test it.

Janoff-Bulman and colleagues describe prescriptive and proscriptive morality as two faces of moral regulation rather than two poles of one continuum, grounded in the distinct approach and avoidance systems studied throughout the self-regulation literature, and they report that both are well represented in the moral repertoire and equivalent in moral weight (Janoff-Bulman, Sheikh, & Hepp, 2009). The Model of Moral Motives that develops this work is a six-cell taxonomy crossing the two forms of regulation with the self, another person, and the group as targets of moral concern (Janoff-Bulman & Carnes, 2013). On whether opposing moral orientations must be represented separately rather than traded off along one continuum, this research supports the present argument rather than targets it.

Formal Models of Moral Conflict and Dilemma Responding

A fourth class of work must be considered separately, because it has already confronted the representational problem we have been describing and has made substantial progress on it.

Research on moral dilemmas is premised on competing motivations. The sacrificial dilemmas that have dominated the field for two decades are constructed precisely so that two moral considerations pull against one another, and early neuroimaging work reported that such dilemmas differ in how far they engage emotional processing, and that judgments running against the emotional response are slower, a pattern the authors read as emotional interference rather than as the output of a single undivided evaluation (Greene, Sommerville, Nystrom, Darley, & Cohen, 2001).

The more important development, for present purposes, is methodological. For many years, researchers scored dilemma responses along a single continuum, counting a judgment as more utilitarian to the extent it was less deontological. Process dissociation broke that dependency by adding dilemmas in which the two considerations converge rather than conflict, which permits deontological and utilitarian inclinations to be estimated as independent parameters (Conway & Gawronski, 2013). The consequence is exactly what this article states throughout: a person high on both inclinations becomes distinguishable from a person low on both, even though the traditional relative score assigns them the same value. This is a genuine instance of representational decompression, achieved more than a decade ago. Conway and Gawronski state the point in terms close to our own, observing that a respondent may reach a judgment easily when one inclination is strong and the other weak, or reach the same judgment only after extensive conflict when the two inclinations are nearly equal, and that the traditional approach cannot tell these cases apart. The conflict-versus-indifference distinction we treat as central to this article's argument has therefore been established prior.

The CNI model extends the approach further. By crossing the type of norm at stake with whether the benefits of action outweigh its costs, it estimates three independent parameters: sensitivity to consequences, sensitivity to moral norms, and a general preference for inaction irrespective of consequences or norms (Gawronski, Armstrong, Conway, Friesdorf, & Hütter, 2017). That third parameter distinguishes inaction driven by sensitivity to moral norms from inaction that holds irrespective of the consequences and norms at stake, so two respondents who both decline to act are no longer scored as having done the same thing. The authors carefully specify that this parameter describes a property of judgments across a dilemma battery rather than a personality trait or a preference that necessarily extends to non-moral decisions. Individual difference work using the model has since shown that these parameters relate differently to personality and clinical variables, which would be impossible if they were facets of a single underlying quantity.

The relationship between these two models is itself helpful for the argument we are making. Gawronski and colleagues introduced the CNI model partly in order to correct a confound in process dissociation. Because the dilemmas used in the earlier work all involved proscriptive norms, which prohibit action, sensitivity to moral norms was confounded with a general preference for inaction in the parameter intended to capture deontological inclinations, and sensitivity to consequences was confounded with a general preference for action in the parameter intended to capture utilitarian inclinations (Gawronski et al., 2017). They corrected this by crossing the content of the norm at stake with the direction of behavior that norm favors, adding dilemmas in which a prescriptive norm prescribes action rather than prohibiting it. The architecture proposed here generalizes that lesson, crossing perceived moral valence with motivational direction for moral motivation at large rather than for one designed class of stimuli.

Alongside the dilemma models, a substantial body of research examines trade-offs between specific moral concerns without invoking sacrificial scenarios. Work on whistleblowing treats the decision as a direct opposition between fairness and loyalty, and shows that individual differences in the relative weighting of those concerns predict willingness to report wrongdoing, that experimentally shifting the weighting shifts that willingness, and that the same manipulation changes actual reporting behavior in an online marketplace (Waytz, Dungan, & Young, 2013).

Other work has tested whether hypothetical dilemma responses correspond to behavior when the consequences are real, and found that they do not. A preference for consequentialist responding, measured with a standard hypothetical battery, predicted choices on a hypothetical version of a task but not on a real version of the same task, although it did predict the affective and cognitive character of the real decision (Bostyn, Sevenhant, & Roets, 2018). A parallel line of work has argued that verbal dual-process accounts are computationally insufficient, and that explaining moral judgment requires specifying the architecture that produces it, in that case the model-based, model-free, and Pavlovian systems familiar from reinforcement learning (Crockett, 2013). What we share with that work is its premise, not its specific architecture. How moral evaluation is represented and computed is a substantive empirical question, not a matter of exposition.

We therefore take this literature to have established, within its domain, the central claim that opposing moral motivations must be represented separately rather than as poles of one continuum. This article argues that this point has not been generalized, and that three specific limitations keep these models from serving as a general architecture of moral motivation.

First, these models represent responses within a designed paradigm rather than motivational states themselves. The parameters are estimated from a battery of dilemmas constructed so the relevant contrasts are orthogonal, and they characterize a person's response pattern across that battery. They do not assign a representation to an individual facing an ordinary moral situation for which no such battery exists, which is most moral situations. A framework intended to represent the motivational state of a person deciding whether to report a colleague, visit a difficult relative, or keep a promise cannot presuppose a factorial stimulus set.

Second, and more consequentially, both of the inclinations separated by process dissociation are orientations toward what the agent takes to be good. Deontological and utilitarian inclinations reflect two conceptions of right action, and a person high on both is pulled between two things, each of which they regard as morally permissible. Nothing in these models represents motivation toward an option the agent themselves judges to be wrong, or motivation away from an option the agent themselves judges to be right. The conflict they capture is conflict within perceived moral good. This is the specific respect in which crossing direction with valence yields something the existing models do not contain.

Third, the generalized inaction parameter is valence-neutral by construction. Separating norm content from behavioral direction, as the CNI model does, is not the same as representing motivation toward what the agent regards as bad. It records a preference for not acting, regardless of whether the forgone action was perceived as good or bad. It therefore cannot distinguish a person who declines to help because helping is costly, while fully recognizing that helping is the right thing to do, from a person who declines because they are drawn toward an outcome they regard as wrong. Those are different psychological states with different downstream consequences, and collapsing them is itself an instance of the compression this article highlights.

None of this diminishes what these models accomplish. They are, in our view, the strongest existing demonstration that representational choices in moral psychology are empirical rather than stylistic, and the comparative strategy we advocate later in this article is modeled on the one they established. The claim is only that their scope is narrower than the problem, and that generalizing their insight requires an architecture that applies outside the dilemma paradigm and that accommodates motivational orientations toward perceived bad as well as toward perceived good.

Domain-Based Theories

Other influential theories represent morality through multiple moral domains rather than through individual motives or single motivational dimensions. Moral Foundations Theory (Graham et al., 2013), Relationship Regulation Theory (Rai & Fiske, 2011), the theory of morality-as-cooperation, which derives its seven domains from recurrent problems of cooperation rather than from descriptive survey structure and reports them to be positively valued cross-culturally (Curry, Mullins, & Whitehouse, 2019), and the Model of Moral Motives (Janoff-Bulman & Carnes, 2013) illustrate increasingly sophisticated attempts to characterize the diversity of moral concerns that guide human behavior.

These approaches substantially expand the representational richness of moral psychology by recognizing that individuals evaluate moral situations across multiple domains rather than according to a single universal principle. They therefore capture important sources of variation that simpler theories cannot represent. Again, this distinction reflects theoretical scope rather than theoretical deficiency. Domain theories were designed to explain diversity in moral content, and they have been remarkably successful. The representational focus here lies at a different level of analysis.

Toward a More General Framework

Collectively, these theories demonstrate the richness and diversity of moral motivation. They identify important motives, describe fundamental moral domains, explain developmental processes, and illuminate numerous mechanisms underlying moral judgment and behavior.

Together they provide much of the empirical foundation upon which contemporary moral psychology is built.

At the same time, the preceding review suggests that most of these theories generally pursue questions different from the one posed in the present article. The formal dilemma models do, however, pose a recognizably similar question, and they answer it convincingly for the materials they were built to analyze. They seek to explain which motives matter, which moral domains organize judgment, or which mechanisms produce moral behavior. Comparatively less attention has been devoted to developing a general cognitive architecture that specifies how multiple moral motives combine into multidimensional moral motivational states while preserving their independence, continuity, and capacity for conflict, and that does so for moral situations at large rather than for a designed class of them. This observation motivates the framework developed in the following section. Rather than replacing existing theories, the Motivation of Moral Action (MoMA) seeks to provide an architecture for locating, combining, and comparing the diverse motives identified by prior theories. The Motivation of Moral Action (MoMA)

The preceding sections argued that an adequate theory of moral motivation requires more than identifying important motives or moral domains. It requires a cognitive architecture that represents how multiple motives combine into coherent moral motivational states while preserving psychologically meaningful distinctions. We now introduce such an architecture, which claims that moral motivational states are best represented as configurations across four independent dimensions defined by the intersection of two fundamental distinctions: motivational direction and moral valence.

Two Fundamental Dimensions

The first distinction concerns motivational direction. Across virtually every area of psychology, motivation involves movement either toward desired states or away from undesired states (Lewin, 1935; Schneirla, 1959; Carver & White, 1994; Higgins, 1997; Elliot, 2006). This underlies numerous theories of motivation and reflects one of the most fundamental organizational principles of behavior.

The second distinction concerns moral valence. Individuals routinely distinguish between outcomes they perceive as morally desirable and outcomes they perceive as morally undesirable (Janoff-Bulman, Sheikh, & Hepp, 2009; Cornwell & Higgins, 2015). Importantly, moral valence refers to an individual's perceived moral evaluation rather than an objective moral truth. People often disagree about what constitutes moral good or moral bad (Graham, Haidt, & Nosek, 2009; Skitka, Bauman, & Sargis, 2005; Atari et al., 2023), yet they organize their motivations around these perceived evaluations. Crossing these two independent distinctions yields four possible motivational orientations.

Why These Are Dimensions

The four components of MoMA are conceptualized as continuous dimensions rather than discrete motivational types or categories. A dimension represents an axis along which individuals may vary in strength, independent of variation along other dimensions.

Treating these components as dimensions has several important consequences. First, individuals may occupy any location along each dimension rather than belonging to mutually exclusive motivational categories. Second, movement along one dimension does not require corresponding movement along another. Finally, complex motivational states emerge from combinations of dimensions rather than assignment to predefined motivational types.

Orthogonality is central to this architecture. The dimensions are assumed to be conceptually independent, meaning that high activation on one dimension neither logically requires nor precludes high activation on another. Representational independence does not imply that dimensions are always empirically uncorrelated; rather, it permits motivational states that would be impossible to represent if dimensions were treated as opposite poles of a single continuum.

The Four Dimensions of Moral Motivation

Each dimension within MoMA represents a distinct motivational orientation toward a particular combination of moral valence and motivational direction.

Approach Good (ApG)

Approach Good (ApG) refers to motivation directed toward performing an action that the individual perceives as morally good. Examples include helping another person because one believes helping is morally right, returning a lost wallet because one values honesty, or volunteering because one sees contributing to others' well-being as morally desirable. Approach Good therefore captures the motivational pull toward actions perceived as morally commendable.

Avoid Good (AvG)

Avoid Good (AvG) refers to motivation directed away from performing an action that the individual nevertheless perceives as morally good. This dimension captures situations in which individuals recognize an action's moral value but remain motivated not to perform it. A person may avoid helping because of personal cost, social anxiety, competing obligations, anticipated embarrassment, or simple exhaustion, while fully acknowledging that helping would be the morally commendable action. Avoiding a morally good action therefore does not imply doing what someone thinks is morally bad per se (i.e., Approach Bad; ApB), just the desire not to do what they think is morally good.

Approach Bad (ApB)

Approach Bad (ApB) refers to motivation directed toward performing an action that the individual believes is morally wrong. Such motivation may arise from anticipated personal gain, revenge, curiosity, thrill-seeking, anger, hatred, or countless other motives. Importantly, Approach Bad does not imply that the individual evaluates the action as morally acceptable. On the contrary, individuals may knowingly feel motivated to act on things they regard as morally impermissible.

Temptation represents one common manifestation of this dimension.

Avoid Bad (AvB)

Avoid Bad (AvB) refers to motivation directed away from performing an action that the individual perceives as morally wrong. This includes motivations to refrain from lying, cheating, harming others, violating promises, or engaging in any action the agent judges to be morally impermissible.

Avoid Bad reflects the motivational pull away from actions perceived as morally undesirable, whether because of internal moral commitments, anticipated guilt, concern for others, social norms, or external consequences. Just as Avoid Good was not the same thing as wanting to do bad, Avoid Bad is not the same as wanting to do good per se.

These four dimensions should not be understood as exhaustive lists of motives. Rather, they represent the four possible orientations that emerge when motivational direction and moral valence are crossed. Individual motives (e.g., compassion, loyalty, empathy, fairness, revenge, justice, fear, guilt, pride, or self-interest) may contribute to one or more dimensions simultaneously.

Are Avoiding Good and Approaching Bad Psychologically Real?

Two of the four dimensions are largely uncontroversial. Every theory reviewed above assumes that people are motivated toward what they regard as morally good and away from what they regard as morally bad. If motivation away from perceived good and motivation toward perceived bad are rare curiosities, then crossing direction with valence is an elaborate way of restating the prescriptive-proscriptive distinction, and little follows from it-although even rare curiosities need to be represented.

Consider first motivation away from perceived moral good. The clearest evidence comes from situations in which people pay a price to avoid an opportunity to do something they agree is worth doing. In a large field experiment on door-to-door charitable solicitation, households given a flyer announcing the hour of a solicitor's visit opened the door much less often than households given no warning, and when the flyer carried a box to check if they did not wish to be disturbed, giving fell drastically (DellaVigna, List, & Malmendier, 2012). The simple flyer reduced dooropening without reducing giving; only the version that made avoidance easy did both. The authors model this as social pressure, the cost of saying no to a solicitor face to face, and estimate that cost at several hundred dollars. These households rearranged their afternoons so they would not be asked. Doing so requires anticipating, in advance, a pull they expect to feel and would rather not face, whether described as moral obligation or the social cost of refusing. An architecture that records them simply as low in prosocial motivation loses the structure of what they did, which was not to decline but to arrange not to be asked.

The same pattern appears under controlled conditions. In the moral wiggle room paradigm, dictators choosing between a self-serving and a fair allocation could reveal the other person's payoffs by clicking a button, at no cost and with the decision to reveal kept private, so that declining carried neither a material nor a reputational penalty. Fewer than six in ten chose to look.

When the payoffs were in fact the same as in a transparent baseline, the self-serving option was chosen by 26 percent of dictators who could see the consequences and by 63 percent of those who could have, and every one of the dictators who declined to look then took the self-serving option (Dana, Weber, & Kuang, 2007). A meta-analysis finds that 40 percent of participants decline freely available information about how their choice affects someone else, that doing so lowers altruistic behavior relative to conditions in which the information is supplied, and that the pattern is consistent with excuse-seeking in the service of maintaining a positive self-image (Vu et al., 2023). Choosing not to know is intelligible only as avoidance of information that would activate a moral demand the person expects to feel. Relatedly, when given a free choice between empathizing with a person in a photograph and describing that same person's outward features, people show a robust preference for the describing option, a preference associated with experiencing empathy as effortful, aversive, and ineffective. Experimentally raising participants' sense that their empathy is efficacious eliminates the avoidance altogether, which indicates that the cost of empathizing rather than indifference to its value is what drives the choice (Cameron et al., 2019). People also appear to regulate compassion downward in anticipation of being asked to give, rather than simply failing to feel it (Cameron & Payne, 2011). In each case, the agent does not dispute the moral value of the forgone action; it is precisely because the agent recognizes that value that avoidance is necessary.

Motivation toward perceived moral bad is the cell most likely to attract skepticism, and the evidence for it is of a different character. People who cheat on problem-solving tasks report more positive affect afterward than those who do not, even though participants asked to forecast their reactions predict guilt and increased negative affect. The effect does not depend on the accrual of undeserved financial incentives, and it survives in a condition designed to strip away selfdeception, persisting even among cheaters who acknowledge that their reported performance is unreliable (Ruedy et al., 2013). The positive affect does not require the agent to have talked themselves into believing the act was acceptable. A transgression the agent regards as wrong can thus carry positive incentive value, which motivation toward the perceived bad perhaps requires. In laboratory studies of everyday sadism, participants could deliver a blast of white noise to an opponent who never aggressed against them, so that any aggression was unprovoked. When aggression was free, sadism and the dark triad traits alike predicted it. When it first required completing a tedious letter-counting task, only sadism did. Participants in the earlier bug-killing study who killed more also reported more pleasure afterward (Buckels, Jones, & Paulhus, 2013).

The cost condition is what makes this evidence rather than illustration, because paying a price to harm cannot be explained as the mere absence of restraint. And some of it concerns stable individual differences. The disposition labeled need for chaos predicts motivations to circulate hostile political rumors and continues to do so after accounting for partisan motivations. The authors trace its origins to social marginalization combined with status-oriented personality, and describe those high on it as wanting to burn down the existing order in the hope of gaining standing in the process (Petersen, Osmundsen, & Arceneaux, 2023). Now an agent who seeks status through destruction need not regard the destruction as morally wrong (although many will), but we include it as an indication that destructive orientations (as one candidate for moral badness) are common and observable.

Another source of evidence is more ordinary and, for that reason, more telling. Experience sampling of everyday desire finds that desires are frequent and mostly unproblematic, but those that conflict with a person's other goals tend to elicit active resistance, and that the resistance succeeds unevenly (Hofmann et al., 2012). We cite this for the structure it documents rather than for its moral content, since most of the desires sampled are not transgressions. It establishes that simultaneous attraction toward an object and effortful resistance to it is ordinary rather than exceptional, and that any architecture in which the two cannot be represented at once will misdescribe it. The philosophical literature reached the same conclusion before the empirical one existed. Stocker opens his essay with the assertion that desiring the bad and not desiring the good are ordinary features of everyday life, and immediately notes that many philosophers resist this, finding it problematic or even incoherent (Stocker, 1979). Those two phrases name the two dimensions at issue here, and the resistance he anticipates is the resistance we are addressing. His argument is that if desiring the bad is problematic, so is its mirror image, desiring the good; a framework that admits one should not refuse the other. Now this evidence does not establish that the four dimensions are equally common. We expect the opposite, and would predict substantially higher typical values on approaching good and avoiding bad than on the other two in most populations and most situations. Asymmetry of this kind is expected rather than embarrassing for bivariate architecture. The evaluative space literature describes its two dimensions as governed by distinct activation functions, a positivity offset and a negativity bias, precisely because separable dimensions need not be equally weighted or equally often engaged (Cacioppo, Gardner, & Berntson, 1997). But frequency and theoretical importance are different, and three considerations suggest that asymmetry is not a reason to drop the two contested cells. First, several of the phenomena above are not rare at all; declining to look at the consequences of one's choice and declining to empathize are modal responses in the paradigms that elicit them. Second, low-frequency states are frequently the diagnostic ones, in the sense that they discriminate sharply between individuals and predict outcomes that common states do not.

Third, and most importantly, the claim that these states are rare can be stated only within an architecture that gives them value. A representation in which avoiding good and approaching bad have no location cannot be used to show they are uncommon, or to identify the people and circumstances in which they are not.

The payoff of keeping them is a set of predictions that the alternatives cannot express.

Moral Motivation States

The central representational unit within MoMA is the moral motivational state. Rather than describing individuals by a single dominant motive, MoMA represents each individual as occupying a location in a four-dimensional motivational space. At any particular moment, with respect to a particular action, an individual's moral motivational state can therefore be represented as M = (ApG, AvG, ApB, AvB) where each component represents the current strength of the corresponding motivational dimension.

This representation preserves information about the simultaneous presence, relative strength, and independence of multiple motivational orientations. Two individuals may therefore occupy different locations within the motivational space despite displaying identical behaviors, while individuals occupying similar locations may behave differently when opportunities or constraints differ. The architecture is designed to represent motivational states themselves, leaving the translation from motivation to behavior to subsequent psychological and situational processes.

Each dimension is drawn along its own ray from a common origin, so that all four components are visible at once and opposing dimensions are never placed on a shared continuum. The display is a convention for viewing four unipolar quantities in the plane rather than a projection that combines them, and it loses no information about the state. A state may therefore be simultaneously high on Approach Good and Avoid Good, or on Avoid Bad and Approach Bad, and such states appear as markers far from the origin in both directions rather than canceling to a common midpoint. A moral motivational state is the full configuration of all four markers, not a single location, so the origin represents the absence of motivation only when all four dimensions are near zero. The state shown combines strong motivation to approach moral good (ApG = 7) with moderate motivation to avoid moral good (AvG = 5), moderate motivation to avoid moral bad (AvB = 6), and weak motivation to approach moral bad (ApB = 2).

Formal Properties

The definitions above can be stated precisely, turning several claims in this article into consequences rather than assertions. Let a moral motivational state be M = (m(ApG), m(AvG), m(ApB), m(AvB)), where each component lies in the interval [0, c] for some scale maximum c.

Each component is unipolar, so a value of zero denotes the absence of that motivation rather than the presence of its opposite. And the components are logically independent, so the value of one places no constraint on the values of the others. The state space is therefore the four-dimensional region [0, c]⁴, not a plane and not a line.

Motives enter through the components rather than alongside them. For a set of active motives, let a(k) denote the activation of motive k and w(k, d) its non-negative contribution to dimension d, so that m(d) is a bounded, non-decreasing function of the sum of a(k)w(k, d) across motives. MoMA does not specify the weights w, which vary across individuals, cultures, and situations and are the province of motive-level theories. It specifies only that the mapping is manyto-many: a motive may carry non-zero weight on several dimensions, and a dimension may draw on several motives (Figure 4).

Representational compression can now be defined. An architecture of rank r is a map from the state space into r-dimensional space. Single-dimension architectures take r = 1, typically through a net score of the form (m(ApG) + m(AvB)) -(m(AvG) + m(ApB)). Whenever r is less than four, and the map is linear, it has a non-trivial kernel and is therefore not injective: distinct states necessarily share a representation. For the net score, the set of states mapping to zero contains the indifferent state (0, 0, 0, 0) together with every conflicted state of the form (t, t, 0, 0).

Figure 6 depicts two members of that set. Representational compression is thus not a defect of any particular theory per se but a property of any architecture whose rank is lower than the number of independent dimensions the phenomenon requires, which is why the question of how many dimensions are needed is empirical.

Two quantities follow directly. Total intensity is the sum of the four components, and conflict is the extent to which opposing dimensions are simultaneously active, given by the smaller of m(ApG) and m(AvG) plus the smaller of m(AvB) and m(ApB). These two quantities are independent. The states (2t, 0, 0, 0) and (t, t, 0, 0) have identical total intensity while differing maximally in conflict, and no function of intensity alone can separate them. Distance between states is given by any metric on the space, with Euclidean distance the natural default, and change over time is a trajectory through the space rather than a change in the space itself (Figure 8).

Behavior is not a function of the state alone. Writing behavior as a function of the motivational state together with the situation makes the two claims above precise. Identical behavior arises from distinct states whenever the function returns the same value for different states in a common situation, which is the ordinary case because the function is many-to-one. Different behavior arises from the same state only when the situation differs, since holding both arguments fixed fixes the output.

The predictions developed below follow from this structure. The prediction concerning motivational conflict follows from the independence of conflict and intensity. The predictions concerning predictive accuracy and incremental validity follow from non-injectivity, since variance in behavior that depends on the state but not on its compressed image is by construction unavailable to a lower-rank architecture. The prediction concerning character inference follows if observers infer character from the state rather than from behavior. The prediction concerning situational variability follows from the state's dependence on the situation. None of these require additional assumptions beyond the definitions given here.

Explanatory Consequences of the MoMA Architecture

The previous section introduced MoMA as a cognitive architecture for representing moral motivational states. A cognitive architecture, however, is valuable only insofar as it permits explanations that would otherwise remain difficult or impossible. Because MoMA represents moral motivation as a multidimensional configuration of independent motivational dimensions, it preserves distinctions among motivational states that simpler representational frameworks necessarily compress. In this section, we illustrate how this architecture naturally accommodates several fundamental phenomena that have long occupied moral psychology.

Importantly, the following examples are intended to show the architecture's explanatory consequences, allowing existing psychological processes to be described with greater precision, rather than to propose new psychological mechanisms.

Representing Motivational Conflict

Motivational conflict is a defining feature of human moral experience. Individuals frequently experience competing moral motivations simultaneously, such as wanting to tell the truth while wishing to protect another person, pursuing justice while maintaining loyalty, or helping another individual despite substantial personal cost. Such conflicts are central to moral deliberation because multiple morally relevant motivations often coexist within the same decision.

Within MoMA, motivational conflict does not require a separate explanatory mechanism.

Instead, conflict is represented directly as the simultaneous activation of competing motivational dimensions within the overall motivational state. The architecture therefore distinguishes conflict from simple motivational weakness or indecision. Two individuals may show identical hesitation before acting, yet one may experience genuine competition among multiple moral motivations, whereas another may simply have little motivation in any direction. This ambiguity carries a practical cost, because observers routinely read hesitation as evidence of conflict and adjust their character judgments accordingly, treating quick help and slow harm as creditable and slow help and quick harm as discreditable (Critcher, Inbar, & Pizarrro, 2013). On the present account that inference is underdetermined. Slowness is produced both by strong opposed motivations and by weak ones, and the two states carry opposite implications for what the person will do next.

Although these situations may appear behaviorally similar, they occupy different locations within the motivational space.

This is the moral-motivational form of the ambivalence-indifference problem, and MoMA resolves it as the evaluative space model does by refusing to place opposing tendencies on a shared continuum in the first place. The right panel depicts a state in which moderately strong motivation to approach moral good is exactly counterbalanced by equally strong motivation to avoid moral good (ApG = 4, AvG = 4), while the remaining dimensions remain inactive. Although these two individuals occupy the same aggregate or net motivational position under simpler representations, they differ fundamentally in their underlying motivational configurations. MoMA preserves this distinction by representing moral motivation as a multidimensional state rather than collapsing competing motivations into a single overall value.

Representing Mixed Motivation

Motivational conflict should be distinguished from mixed motivation. Conflict involves competing motivational tendencies pulling behavior in different directions. Mixed motivation refers to situations in which multiple compatible motives jointly contribute to the same moral action. For example, an individual may volunteer at a community shelter because of compassion for those in need, commitment to religious values, professional responsibility, and a desire to contribute positively to society. These motives are distinct and can be mixed, yet they need not compete.

Rather, they combine to produce a stronger overall motivational state directed toward the same outcome. Because MoMA represents motivational states as multidimensional configurations rather than single dominant motives, compatible motives may coexist naturally without requiring one motive to replace another.

Representing Motivational Intensity

Moral motivation is rarely, if ever, all-or-none. Individuals vary not only in the kinds of motivations they experience but also in the strength of those motivations. A person may weakly prefer honesty in one situation yet experience an overwhelming commitment to honesty in another.

Likewise, concern about harming others may range from minimal to extremely strong depending on context.

Because each MoMA dimension is continuous, motivational intensity is represented directly as movement along the corresponding dimension rather than by assigning individuals to different motivational categories. Continuous representation therefore permits subtle variation in motivational strength while preserving the overall configuration of the motivational state across situations and over time.

Identical Behavior, Different Motivation

One of the central reasons for developing MoMA is the observation that identical behaviors frequently arise from different moral motivational states.

Consider three individuals who each donate a substantial sum to charity. One is motivated primarily by compassion for those in need. A second donates out of a sense of moral duty, despite little emotional concern. A third donates primarily to enhance their public reputation while privately believing the action serves their own interests. Although the observable behavior is identical, the motivational states differ substantially.

These differences matter because motivational states generate different predictions regarding future behavior, emotional experience, moral character judgments, and responses to changing circumstances. Observers who learn that an altruistic act was driven by emotion rather than by dispassionate deliberation judge the actor as more moral, so the first and second donors are evaluated differently despite giving the same account (Barasch et al., 2014). A prosocial act that also benefits the actor, meanwhile, can be judged more harshly than no prosocial act at all, which can place the third donor below a non-donor in perceived character even though more good was done (Newman & Cain, 2014). By representing these individuals as occupying different locations within the motivational space, MoMA preserves distinctions that behavioral observation alone cannot capture.

The converse also holds, though under a narrower condition. Different behaviors may arise from the same moral motivational state whenever situations differ, because behavior is produced by the motivational state acting together with situational opportunities and constraints (see Figure 1). Two individuals with identical configurations may donate, volunteer, or do nothing at all depending on what their circumstances permit: one has disposable income and the other does not; one is asked directly and the other never encounters the request; one can act anonymously while the other would face retaliation.

The same holds within a single individual across occasions.

Holding the motivational state fixed, then, behavioral variation is attributable to the situation rather than to motivation, which is why behavior alone identifies neither the motivational state nor the motives that produced it.

Representing Dynamic Moral Motivation

Moral motivation is dynamic. Individuals encounter new information, changing relationships, shifting social roles, and evolving circumstances that alter the relative strength of different motivations. A healthcare professional may initially prioritize patient autonomy but later become increasingly motivated by concerns about preventing harm as clinical conditions change. Similarly, an employee may become progressively more motivated to report organizational misconduct as additional evidence accumulates. Individuals move within the multidimensional space as motivational configurations change.

Moral Character

Observers often infer stable moral character from patterns of motivation rather than isolated behaviors (Goodwin, Piazza, & Rozin, 2014; Reeder, 2009). A single generous act performed reluctantly communicates something different from one performed enthusiastically and consistently across situations. Which way it cuts depends on what the competing motive is.

Observers sometimes grant moral credit for overcoming a contrary desire through self-control (Starmans & Bloom, 2016), yet a reputation for willpower is worth less than one for untempted purity when the desire being overcome is itself discreditable (Berman & Small, 2018). MoMA makes that contingency explicit because the two cases differ in which dimensions supply the opposition, not in how much net motivation the agent has. Likewise, repeated actions arising from similar motivational configurations gradually shape perceptions of traits such as compassion, integrity, selfishness, or trustworthiness. This is why motive, and not behavior alone, carries the weight in character attribution. A person who tells the truth only because they could not have gotten away with lying does not thereby possess honesty as a trait, even though their behavior is indistinguishable from that of an honest person. What differs is the motivational state that produced the act: in the first case a high Avoid Bad driven by anticipated exposure; in the second a high Approach Good attached to truthfulness itself. Observers appear sensitive to exactly this difference. They infer the presence of conflict from cues as thin as how long a decision took, and revise character judgments on that basis alone (Critcher, Inbar, & Pizarro, 2013).

A Worked Comparison: Four Employees and One Decision

The preceding sections state the architecture's consequences in general terms. Because the value of a representation is easiest to assess against a concrete case, we close this section with one.

Consider four employees who each discover that a colleague has falsified safety data, and who each do nothing during the first week. Their observable behavior over that week is identical.

The first employee is torn. She believes reporting is required by fairness and by the risk to others, and she believes equally that reporting would betray a colleague who has supported her for years. The second feels little either way; the falsified data is someone else's problem, and the loyalty at stake is thin. The third wants to report and regards reporting as clearly right, but dreads the confrontation, has begun taking a different route through the building to avoid the colleague, and has twice closed the reporting form without submitting it. The fourth also regards reporting as right, and additionally relishes the prospect of the colleague's humiliation, having disliked him for years; she is aware that this second motive is not one she can take credit for. That awareness is itself morally consequential. Observers judge an agent who wishes she did not hold a discreditable desire less harshly than one who endorses it, so second-order attitudes toward one's own motives enter the evaluation independently of what the agent finally does (Pizarro, Uhlmann, & Salovey, 2003).

Theories organized around moral domains characterize what each employee cares about, and do that well. They do not distinguish the first employee from the second, because the distinction is not in the content of their concerns but in whether those concerns were simultaneously active and opposed. The fairness-loyalty trade-off account captures the first employee exactly, which is the case it was built for (Waytz, Dungan, & Young, 2013). It has no natural place for the third, whose reluctance is not loyalty but anticipated personal cost, or for the fourth, whose additional pull is not a moral concern at all by her own assessment. Formal dilemma models would separate competing inclinations if the situation were posed in a certain way, but both of the inclinations they separate are orientations toward perceived right action, so the third employee's avoidance and the fourth employee's relish fall outside the parameters they estimate. A single moral motivation score, finally, assigns all four the same low value, since none of them acted.

Within MoMA the four occupy different locations. The first is high on approaching good and high on avoiding good, the latter driven by loyalty, which places her in the region the architecture characterizes as reluctance under conflict. The second is near the origin on all four dimensions. The third resembles the first in her position on approaching good and avoiding good, but the content feeding her avoidance differs, which matters for what will move her.

The fourth is high on approaching good and simultaneously elevated on approaching bad, a configuration unavailable in any of the alternatives considered here, because it requires motivation toward an outcome the agent herself does not endorse. The practical consequence is a set of differential predictions. Introducing an anonymous reporting channel removes personal cost without changing anything about moral concern, so it should move the third employee and should not move the second, whose inaction does not reflect cost at all. Strengthening the salience of the harm to future victims should move the second and should have comparatively little effect on the third, who already regards the matter as settled. The first employee should be the most likely to ruminate, the most likely to experience the decision as burdensome regardless of which way it goes, and the most likely to act later if the loyalty pull weakens. The fourth is the one whose report, if filed, observers will evaluate differently once her motives are known, since a right action known to serve the actor's private satisfaction can be judged more harshly than no action at all (Newman & Cain, 2014), and the one most at risk of overstating what she found. A representation that scores all four as unmotivated predicts none of this, and a representation that separates only competing conceptions of right action predicts the first two cases but not the last two.

A Research Agenda for Moral Motivation

The value of MoMA ultimately depends not only on its capacity to explain existing findings but also on its ability to generate new questions and guide empirical discovery. A successful theory identifies phenomena that should exist, predicts relationships not yet examined, and provides a common conceptual language through which seemingly unrelated findings can be integrated. We believe that MoMA offers precisely this type of framework. Rather than representing the endpoint of theoretical development, it establishes a foundation for a broader program of research on moral motivation.

Measuring Moral Motivational States

If moral motivation is best represented as a multidimensional state, then measurement should assess independent motivational dimensions rather than relying exclusively on aggregate indices or single dominant motives.

Traditional self-report measures often focus on particular motives, such as empathy, moral identity, or fairness. These measures have been highly informative regarding specific psychological constructs, but they are not designed to characterize the overall configuration of simultaneously active motivations. MoMA instead predicts that measuring independent motivational dimensions should better represent moral motivation and improve prediction when multiple motives coexist.

Future work may therefore develop instruments that estimate an individual's location within the multidimensional motivational space using self-report (see Stults-Kolehmainen et al., 2020 , 2021 for similar physical movement motivation research), behavioral tasks, experience sampling, or implicit measures (e.g., eye-tracking).

Measurement of separable dimensions need not be invented from scratch, because adjacent literatures have already developed it. Split-semantic-differential procedures assess positive and negative reactions independently rather than through a single bipolar judgment (Kaplan, 1972); objective indices of conflict are computed from those separate components (Priester & Petty, 1996; Thompson, Zanna, & Griffin, 1995); and established trait instruments already treat approach and avoidance systems as separable rather than opposed (Carver & White, 1994). Adapting these procedures to the four MoMA dimensions, and validating the resulting instruments against standard criteria for construct development (Clark & Watson, 1995), is a tractable next step rather than a novel measurement problem.

Development and Cultural Variation

The present framework concerns the representation of moral motivation rather than its developmental origins or cultural content. Consequently, MoMA is compatible with substantial variation in both the motives that become psychologically salient and the circumstances under which those motives emerge.

Developmental research may therefore examine how individuals acquire increasingly complex moral motivational configurations across childhood and adulthood. Similarly, crosscultural research may investigate whether the proposed representational architecture remains stable methodological template already exists. Process dissociation and the CNI model were introduced precisely by showing that a prevailing scoring convention conflated states that a better-specified model could separate, and by demonstrating that the separated parameters behaved differently from one another (Conway & Gawronski, 2013; Gawronski et al., 2017). We are proposing that the same argument be run one level up, with architectures rather than scoring conventions as the objects of comparison. Such comparisons may evaluate explanatory accuracy, predictive performance, measurement precision, and the ability of different architectures to represent motivational conflict, mixed motives, and dynamic change.

By framing assumptions themselves as empirically testable propositions, MoMA encourages a more cumulative science of moral motivation in which competing theories can be evaluated using common criteria.

Formal Predictions

The representational assumptions underlying MoMA generate several predictions that distinguish it from simpler architectures.

1. First, individuals exhibiting simultaneous activation of multiple independent motivational dimensions should experience greater subjective motivational conflict than individuals with comparable overall motivation concentrated on a single dimension.

2. Second, multidimensional representations should predict moral judgment and behavior more accurately whenever multiple motivations are simultaneously relevant.

3. Third, observers should infer different moral character from identical behaviors when they infer different underlying motivational configurations.

4. Fourth, measures assessing independent motivational dimensions should explain variance beyond aggregate indices that collapse motivational states into a single continuum.

5. Finally, motivational states should vary substantially across situations.

Discussion

The present article addressed a question that has received comparatively little explicit attention within moral psychology: How should moral motivation itself be represented? We argued that answering this question requires distinguishing the identification of moral motives from the representation of moral motivational states. Although existing theories have identified numerous motives that influence moral judgment and behavior, comparatively less attention has been devoted to developing a general cognitive architecture capable of representing how those motives combine into coherent psychological states.

MoMA was proposed to address this representational challenge. By representing moral motivation as four independent dimensions defined by motivational direction and perceived moral valence, the framework preserves distinctions among motivational states that simpler architectures necessarily compress. The resulting representational space accommodates motivational conflict, mixed motivation, continuous variation, and dynamic change while remaining compatible with diverse theories concerning the motives that generate moral behavior.

Importantly, MoMA should not be interpreted as replacing existing theories of moral motives. Rather, it addresses a different theoretical level. Existing theories explain what motivates moral action and why particular motives influence behavior. MoMA instead specifies how these diverse motives may be represented within a common cognitive architecture. In this sense, the framework complements rather than competes with theories emphasizing empathy, moral identity, fairness, loyalty, care, authority, or other moral concerns.

It is equally important to be clear about what is not new. The claim that opposing moral motivations can be simultaneously active, and that representing them on a single continuum discards psychologically real distinctions, was established in dilemma research well before this article and has been supported there with formal models and substantial evidence (Conway & Gawronski, 2013; Gawronski et al., 2017). Research on moral trade-offs has shown the same for specific pairs of competing concerns (Waytz, Dungan, & Young, 2013), and the ambivalence literature in attitude research made the general version of the argument decades earlier (Kaplan, 1972; Cacioppo, Gardner, & Berntson, 1997). Our contribution is not the discovery that compression occurs. It is the proposal that separability be treated as a general architectural requirement rather than a paradigm-specific fix, together with the specific claim that the relevant separation runs along two crossed distinctions rather than one, which yields motivational orientations toward perceived moral bad and away from perceived moral good that existing separations do not contain.

Like any theoretical framework, MoMA has important boundary conditions. It is not a theory of moral development, moral judgment, or moral behavior per se. Nor does it specify the origins of particular moral motives or determine which motives are normatively correct. Instead, it provides a representational architecture for describing how multiple motives combine into overall moral motivational states. Whether this architecture ultimately proves superior to alternative representations remains an empirical question.

The framework is also falsifiable in a specific and near-term way. If measures of the four dimensions consistently reduce to two factors corresponding to approaching good and avoiding bad, if the two contested dimensions fail to predict variance beyond those two, or if interventions targeting avoidance of good and attraction to wrongdoing produce indistinguishable effects, then the four-dimensional architecture is more structure than the phenomenon warrants and should be abandoned in favor of the simpler one. We regard this as the most important empirical question the framework raises, and the evidence reviewed earlier as sufficient to motivate the test rather than to settle it.

Nevertheless, architecture is foundational to scientific explanation. The ways in which psychological states are represented determine the distinctions available for measurement, comparison, prediction, and theory development. We therefore suggest that progress in moral psychology depends not only on identifying new motives or refining existing mechanisms, but also on developing increasingly accurate representations of moral motivational states themselves.

Computational Models of Moral Motivation

Because MoMA represents motivational states formally, it naturally lends itself to computational implementation.

Computational simulations, reinforcement-learning agents, cognitive architectures, and large language models (LLMs) would likely all benefit from explicit representations of motivational states when making moral decisions. Representing motivation as a vector that occupies a multidimensional state rather than as a single scalar value provides opportunities to examine how different representational assumptions influence simulated moral behavior, conflict resolution, and learning across repeated decisions.

More broadly, computational approaches provide a means of comparing competing representational architectures directly. Rather than asking whether one theory is conceptually appealing, researchers can evaluate whether different architectures produce more accurate predictions of human judgments and behavior under identical conditions.
