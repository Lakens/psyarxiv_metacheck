Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Schizophrenia remains a major challenge in psychiatric practice, characterized by a heterogenous constellation of symptoms, including positive symptoms (e.g., hallucinations and delusions), negative symptoms (e.g., social withdrawal and diminished motivation), cognitive impairments, and frequently, lack of insight. Standard psychoeducation has served as a vital partner to psychological medicine for decades, trying to reduce the relapse rates by emphasizing medication adherence. However, many conventional models often rely on descriptive clinical phenomenology that fails to resonate with the patient's subjective experience and rarely explain the neurological causes behind the hallucinations or illogical thinking. This disconnection can inadvertently reinforce stigma and fail to support the patient's remaining cognitive capacities to reinforce recovery. Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation (NIPE) emerges as a transformative paradigm, integrating cutting-edge insights from brain science into the educational dialogue. By establishing the explanation of symptoms in neurobiology and shifting the narrative from "mental illness" to "circuit dysfunction", NIPE aims to bypass psychological defenses, objectify the disorder, and foster a collaborative therapeutic alliance. This chapter presents the neurocognitive aspects of the psychoeducational content, the neurocognitive considerations that must be addressed when providing psychoeducation, and examples of NIPE for Schizophrenia, aiming to advance brain health literacy for practitioners who will support patients as active partners in recovery alongside daily functional restoration.

1.4 Rationale and Patient Rights

Underlying psychoeducation is the principle of patient empowerment and respect for patients' rights. Patients should receive accurate, understandable information about their condition and treatment and should be supported to participate in decisions according to their preferences and capacity. (33) Psychoeducational programs commonly address stressors, individual early warning signs, and coping strategies so that patients and families can recognize possible deterioration and seek help promptly. (34) They also provide clear explanations of treatment principles while incorporating patients' other neurobiological mechanisms may also leave important experiential questions insufficiently addressed-for example, why voices occur, why motivation diminishes, why thinking becomes difficult, and why treatment may need to continue over time. (43) Neurobiological explanations should therefore be presented in an accessible, biopsychosocial, and recovery-oriented manner rather than as deterministic accounts of a fixed brain defect.( 44) Therapeutic approaches generally recommend exploring psychotic experiences collaboratively instead of relying on direct contradiction or simply labeling them as "delusions," because therapeutic alliance is associated with engagement and treatment outcomes, while poorer insight may be associated with weaker alliance. (45) These considerations suggest an educational gap: conventional psychoeducation can provide accurate scientific information, but it may not always translate that information into explanations that are cognitively accessible, personally meaningful, and relevant to lived experience. Addressing this gap may require approaches that accommodate neurocognitive difficulties, scaffold metacognitive reflection, reduce self-blame, and support active participation in treatment.( 41) validation and empathy, and family reinforcement. These strategies are intended to translate neurobiological information into cognitively accessible and personally meaningful explanations, shifting psychoeducation from one-way information delivery toward collaborative meaning-making. By linking lived experience with brain-based mechanisms while reducing cognitive load and self-blame, NIPE aims to support understanding, therapeutic engagement and alliance, shared decisionmaking, medication adherence, self-efficacy, and recovery.

2. Defining Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation (NIPE)

Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation (NIPE) is a theory-driven reconceptualization of clinical teaching that integrates mechanistic neuroscience with instructional design, while reconceptualizing both the content (what is communicated), and structure (how it is thought) of psychoeducation. (21) Within this framework, the content emphasizes brainbased mechanisms of symptoms. For example, concepts such as aberrant salience and synaptic pruning may be used to explain positive symptoms of schizophrenia, while treatment effects can be framed in terms of neuroplasticity and receptor modulation. (21) The pedagogy applies cognitive neuroscience informed strategies like dual coding, errorless learning, salience optimization to optimize encoding, and retrieval despite schizophrenia-related cognitive impairments. (46)The goal is to incorporate schizophrenia psychoeducation within a brain-health paradigm while translating biological mechanisms into non-stigmatizing, meaningful narratives, which will produce explanations that are biologically coherent and pedagogically accessible, thereby improving comprehension, reducing stigma, and supporting durable self-management.( 47) NIPE seeks to enhance patient engagement, trust, and the effectiveness of psychoeducational interventions.( 47) Key differences between NIPE and traditional psychoeducation are summarized in Table 2.

2.1. Objectives and Scope of the Chapter

The objective of this chapter is to outline the neurobiological foundations of schizophrenia that are most relevant to Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation (NIPE). Rather than offering an exhaustive review of all pathophysiological findings, the chapter focuses on the neural circuits, neurotransmitter systems, and cognitive mechanisms that help explain the core symptom domains of schizophrenia in a clinically meaningful way. By identifying the concepts most suitable for translation into patient-centered explanations, this section provides the scientific basis for NIPE and establishes the bridge between contemporary neuroscience and practical psychoeducational communication. The material presented here is intended to support understanding, reduce stigma and self-blame, and prepare the reader for the translational and educational strategies described in the subsequent sections.

The usual topics of psychoeducation are adapted for persons with early psychosis (Table 1). For example, the stress-vulnerability-model explains for ultra-high-risk persons the link between stress and the beginning of psychotic symptoms, which is used to motivate them to reduce stress in their everyday life. In addition, specific topics are included such as the types of mental health services, concerns about confidentiality or usual myths about psychosis.

The psychoeducation program of the Australian Early Psychosis Prevention and Intervention Centre (EPPIC) is probably the most detailed for young people with early psychosis.( 48) This program is not focused on group sessions, but on continuous communication with young people (and their families) providing essential information. Detailed check lists, work sheets and figures are provided.

The EPPIC program for First Psychosis

Psychoeducation for family members of young people aims to improve their understanding of the mental status and the problems of their relative. Again, it is useful to start with the family's explanatory model, but it must be considered that the family's illness model may be different from that of the patient. Some families say that they want to learn how to better communicate with the patient (e.g., how to manage odd behavior or how to talk about psychotic symptoms).

Other important topics are the efficacy of psychotropics and psychosocial interventions, the prognosis, methods to support the patient's recovery and available services. (49)

Originally, the concept of early psychosis was developed in order to recognize and treat schizophrenia at an early stage. However, persons with early psychosis often develop bipolar disorders or depression, but not schizophrenia. This must be considered when providing psychoeducation.( 50,51)

While large proportions of persons with schizophrenia have cognitive impairment this is less frequent among persons with early psychosis. Nevertheless, a proportion of these young people will have some kind of cognitive difficulties. Sometimes these symptoms are present even before the onset of psychotic symptoms. 2.3 Pathogenesis of Schizophrenia: A Neurodevelopmental Perspective

Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: ▪ In a healthy brain's development, particularly during late adolescence and early adulthood, the cerebral cortex undergoes a highly regulated, essential process known as "synaptic pruning." During this critical window, microglia (the central nervous system's resident immune cells) carefully eliminate weak, unused, or redundant synaptic connections. (56) This process optimizes the frontoparietal and temporal networks for mature, highly efficient adult cognition.( 57)Concurrently, the healthy brain maintains a precise, reciprocal balance between excitatory (glutamate) and modulatory (dopamine) neurotransmitter systems across cortical and subcortical pathways. (58) In the development of schizophrenia, the prevailing "two-hit" neurodevelopmental model suggests that early genetic vulnerabilities-frequently involving immune-system genes like the C4 complement gene-combine with environmental stressors to severely dysregulate this normal maturation process. (59) In these individuals, microglia become pathological and hyperactive, initiating an excessive synaptic pruning cascade. This aggressively strips away critical dendritic spines, particularly within the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. (60) This profound structural loss subsequently destabilizes the brain's global neurochemistry. The resulting structural dysconnectivity leads to a core hypofunction of N-methyl-Daspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptors, which effectively remove the inhibitory "brakes" on the brain's dopamine pathways. (61) Consequently, the schizophrenic brain develops a paradoxical dopamine imbalance: profound dopamine hyperactivity in the mesolimbic pathway (driving positive symptoms like hallucinations and delusions) occurring simultaneously with dopamine hypoactivity in the mesocortical pathway (driving the negative and cognitive symptoms) (62) • Translational Narrative: "Think of your brain's development during your teenage years as a master landscaper tending to a vast, slightly overgrown botanical garden. In a healthy brain, the landscaper carefully prunes away dead or extra branches so that the main trees can grow stronger and resources flow efficiently. In the development of schizophrenia, a combination of genetics and stress causes the landscaper to become overly aggressive, accidentally cutting away too many healthy, essential branches, especially in the front of the garden, which controls logic and planning. Because of this 'over-pruning,' the chemical messaging system in the garden breaks down: the water sprinklers in the emotional centers get stuck on full blast (which causes hallucinations and delusions), while the thinking centers are left completely dry (which causes mental fatigue, lack of motivation, and memory issues)."

• The core symptom domains of schizophrenia The clinical presentation of schizophrenia is fundamentally organized into three symptomatic domains: positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and cognitive deficits.

Positive Symptoms: The Overactive Brain

Positive symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, and disorganized behaviors) reflect the present of exaggerated idea, perceptions or action which is an excess or distortion of normal functioning. Current neuroscience conceptualizes these not as random errors, but as the brain's predictable response to specific neurochemical and structural network dysregulations. (63) •Delusions: The Aberrant Salience Hypothesis Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: ▪Healthy brain: In a healthy brain, presynaptic dopamine release in the striatum is tightly regulated; it spikes specifically in response to genuinely salient, rewarding, or threatening environmental stimuli, prompting appropriate behavioral responses.( 64) ▪Schizophrenia: In schizophrenia, the traditional dopamine hypothesis has been refined into the Aberrant Salience Hypothesis.( 64) Positron emission tomography (PET) reveals dysregulated, spontaneous presynaptic dopamine synthesis and release in the striatum that occur independently of environmental context causes the brain to assign unwarranted significance (salience) to neutral internal and external stimuli. The brain is forced into a top-down cognitive effort to make sense of these aberrant, dopamine-driven signals of intense significance, forming a delusion.( 65) Translational Narrative: "In a healthy brain, dopamine acts like an 'importance highlighter' that only highlights things you actually need to pay attention to, like a fire alarm. In schizophrenia, this highlighter leaks, highlighting completely random, everyday things, like a red car parked on the street. Your brain is trying to solve a puzzle with the wrong pieces, creating a delusion to explain why normal things suddenly feel so intensely important." • Auditory Hallucinations: Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: ▪Healthy brain: In a healthy brain, during normal internal thought ("inner speech"), an intact arcuate fasciculus transmits a robust corollary discharge (an efferent copy) from Broca's area to Wernicke's area. This signal temporarily suppresses the primary auditory cortex, tagging the thought as self-generated.( 66) ▪Schizophrenia: In schizophrenia, reduced white matter integrity (fractional anisotropy) in the arcuate fasciculus, the tract connecting Broca's area (speech production) and Wernicke's area (speech comprehension), impairs the corollary discharge mechanism. As a result, the patient's "inner speech" or internal thought is not properly tagged as self-generated, leading the primary auditory cortex to process these internal thoughts as external voices.( 67) Translational Narrative: "When you think a thought, your brain sends a tiny, silent signal to your hearing center that says, 'Hey, this is just me talking to myself, don't play it out loud.' We call this a corollary discharge. In schizophrenia, the brain's 'wiring' between your thought center and your hearing center gets a little frayed. That silent warning signal doesn't get through, so your hearing center accidentally plays your own thoughts out loud like a radio. You aren't imagining things; your brain's microphone is just shortcircuiting."

• Disorganized Speech (Formal Thought Disorder):

Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: ▪Healthy brain: In a healthy brain, coherent speech requires strong top-down inhibitory control. The inferior frontal gyrus precisely selects relevant words from the temporal lobe's semantic memory stores while actively inhibiting loosely related concepts.( 68) ▪Schizophrenia: In schizophrenia, disorganized speech reflects a breakdown in the semantic network. Current evidence points to functional dysconnectivity between the temporal lobe (where semantic memories are stored) and the prefrontal cortex (which organizes these memories into coherent linguistic goals). This results in tangentiality and loose associations, as the brain struggles to inhibit irrelevant semantic nodes during conversation.( 69) Translational Narrative: "Imagine your memory center is an organized filing cabinet, and your brain is trying to manage several related ideas at the same time. The systems that help you hold onto the main point, choose the next words, and keep your sentence organized may become overloaded. That can make your speech sound scattered, even though the ideas may make sense to you. Pausing, using one idea at a time, and checking the main point can help.""

•Disorganized Behavior: Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: ▪Healthy brain: In healthy individuals, the supplementary motor area (SMA) and the basal ganglia seamlessly translate cognitive intentions into coordinated, context-appropriate motor sequences.( 70) ▪Schizophrenia:In schizophrenia, bizarre, purposeless, or contextually inappropriate behavior (and broader psychomotor symptoms) is linked to dysregulation within the basal ganglia and the supplementary motor area (SMA). Because the striatum modulates both motor execution and cognitive gating, dopaminergic overload here can lead to poorly sequenced or contextually inappropriate actions The premotor cortex disrupts the translation of cognitive intentions into sequenced motor actions, resulting in fragmented or seemingly illogical behaviors or it means that the brain fails to integrate the intended action with the current environmental context.( 71

In a healthy nervous system, turning the wheel turns the car. In schizophrenia, the wires connecting the steering wheel to the tires get scrambled; the driver knows where to go, but the car might swerve randomly. Disorganized behavior is a glitch in the brain's transmission system."

2.4 Negative Symptoms: The Hypoactive Networks

Negative symptoms (avolition, alogia, anhedonia, blunted affect, and asociality) are often the most debilitating and treatment-resistant aspects of schizophrenia. They represent a loss of normal mental functioning.( 72) •Avolition (Lack of Motivation and Drive): Healthy Brain vs.

Schizophrenia: ▪Healthy brain: In a healthy brain, goal-directed behavior is driven by an elegant "effort-cost computation." The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), ventral striatum and the ventral medial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC) work together to calculate if a future reward is worth the physical or mental effort required to get it. (73, 74) ▪Schizophrenia: In schizophrenia, severe hypoactivation in this specific corticolimbic-striatal circuits disrupts reward prediction. The patient's brain overestimates the effort required and underestimates the value of the reward, leading to motivational impairment.( 73) Translational Narrative: "In a healthy brain, your internal 'CEO' calculates whether doing a chore is worth the reward of a clean house, and gives you a burst of energy to do it. In schizophrenia, this calculation system is broken. Your brain is essentially telling your body, 'The battery is too low, and the reward isn't big enough, so don't move.' You aren't being lazy; your brain is experiencing a biological power outage, physically blocking your drive to start tasks."

• Alogia (Poverty of Speech):

Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: Alogia is a reduction in the quantity of spontaneous speech (distinct from disorganized speech).

•Healthy brain: In healthy individual's brain exhibits efficient activation of the left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca's area) and the middle/superior temporal gyrus. The brain quickly navigates a well-organized "semantic network" to find words associated with a specific concept. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) acts as an executive controller. It plans the structure of a sentence, selects relevant information, and inhibits irrelevant thoughts to keep the conversation on track. In addition, robust white matter tracts also provide high-speed connections between the regions that understand language (Wernicke's area) and those that produce it (Broca's area). (75) •Schizophrenia: In patients with schizophrenia with alogia, reduced activation in temporal-frontal circuits hinders the "fetching" of words (semantic retrieval failure) causing sparse or absent spontaneous speech. Hypofrontality in the dlPFC impairs sentence planning, leading to "poverty of content" characterized by vague or repetitive speech. In addition, white matter disruption or diminished structural integrity fragments inter-regional communication, making fluid, rapid conversation physically difficult for the brain to maintain.( 76) Translational Narrative: "Think of your vocabulary as a massive, well-stocked library. People with schizophrenia and alogia still have all the books (words) inside. However, the brain's "search engine" struggles to fetch words from the mental library. Moreover, the "director" in the prefrontal cortex lacks the energy to plan complex sentences, and the "internal wiring" is too frayed to maintain a fluid, high-speed conversation."

•Anhedonia (Inability to Experience Pleasure):

Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: Recent neuroscience has completely redefined anhedonia in schizophrenia.

▪Healthy brain: The healthy brain experiences two types of pleasure: consummatory (inthe-moment joy, mediated by the nucleus accumbens shell) and anticipatory (looking forward to a reward, mediated by prefrontal-striatal dopamine projections).( 74) ▪Schizophrenia: In schizophrenia, consummatory pleasure is largely intact; however, anticipatory pleasure is severely impaired due to disrupted value representation in the prefrontal cortex.( 77) Translational Narrative: "It is a myth that people with schizophrenia cannot feel joy. If you give them a bowl of ice cream, their brain registers the sweet taste and enjoys it in the exact same way a healthy brain does. The problem is with the 'anticipation' circuitry. Your brain struggles to 'look forward' to the ice cream tomorrow. Because you can't anticipate the joy, you lack the motivation to get up and go to the ice cream shop. The joy is there, but the radar to find it is broken."

•Blunted Affect (Diminished Emotional Expression):

Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: Blunted affect refers to a lack of facial expression, vocal inflection, and expressive gestures. ▪Healthy brain: In a healthy brain, experiencing an emotion internally (via the amygdala and limbic system) automatically triggers the facial motor network to smile or frown.( 78) ▪Schizophrenia: In schizophrenia, patients experience internal emotions just as strongly as healthy controls, but there is a structural disconnect between the emotional centers and the motor execution centers (like the cingulate motor area), leaving the face structurally immobile.( 79) Translational Narrative: "Having a blunted effect does not mean you are an emotional zombie. Inside, you might be feeling intense sadness, fear, or even joy. The problem is that the 'puppet strings' connecting your emotional center to your facial muscles have gone slack. You are feeling the emotion deeply, but the mechanical signal telling your face to smile or frown simply gets lost in transit."

•Asociality (Social Withdrawal):

Healthy Brain vs. Schizophrenia: Social interaction relies on the "social brain network," which includes the medial prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction (responsible for Theory of Mind, or understanding others' intentions (80) ▪Healthy brain:In a healthy brain, social interaction is intrinsically coded as a high-value reward. The withdrawal isn't because you hate people; it's because your brain is exhausted from the effort."

Cognitive deficits, including impairments in attention, working memory, executive function, and processing speed, are recognized as core features of the disorder, often predating the onset of psychosis and acting as the strongest predictors of functional outcome. Schizophrenia: ▪Healthy brain: Healthy thalamocortical gating efficiently filters out irrelevant background sensory noise, allowing the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) to focus metabolic resources entirely on target stimuli.( 84) ▪Schizophrenia: In schizophrenia, the inability to sustain attention relates to dysregulation in thalamocortical gating and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). Delivering Neuroscience-Informed Psychoeducation (NIPE) in Schizophrenia: What to Consider? Psychoeducational programs for schizophrenia typically involve only a limited number of sessions (often fewer than 15). For example, Sibitz et al. (2007) delivered nine group sessions and then studied the effect of adding nine monthly booster meetings. They found that the booster group improved slightly on one negative-symptom scale and on an attitude measure ("external control -powerful others"), but otherwise no significant differences emerged on symptoms, attitudes, insight, or hospitalizations. Sibitz et al. hypothesized that high dropout from boosters and patient heterogeneity (e.g. some with severe cognitive impairment) might explain these mixed results. They concluded that more research is needed to identify which patients (if any) benefit from booster sessions.( 92) Importantly, several other authors have noted that psychoeducational gains often fade over time. In Byerly et al. work, positive effects seen at the end of a psychoeducation course were not maintained at follow-up. This raises the question of whether booster sessions(93)-or some other strategy-are needed to maintain the benefits of psychoeducation after the initial program.( 94,95)

In sum, existing studies suggest that booster sessions alone may have only limited impact for broad outcomes This prompts a broader question: beyond session number and length, how we deliver psychoeducation might influence its longterm efficacy, especially given the cognitive impairments in schizophrenia.( 96)

3.1 Cognitive Considerations in Psychoeducation

Cognitive impairment is a core feature of schizophrenia, not just a secondary issue. Deficits in processing speed, attention, memory, working memory, executive function and social cognition are found in the majority of patients. (97) These impairments strongly predict real-world outcomes (such as social and occupational functioning) and are often more persistent than psychotic symptoms. In effect, patients with schizophrenia may have a reduced "cognitive bandwidth" -they can learn new information, but only if it is presented and reinforced in a way their impaired brains can handle. (98) From this perspective, psychoeducation should not be viewed simply as conveying content, but as a learning process that must be matched to the learner's cognitive profile. Neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE) therefore asks not "What facts should we teach?" but "What are this patient's cognitive capacities, and how must we adapt our teaching accordingly?" For example, a person who processes information slowly or has poor working memory may benefit from very different teaching techniques than a cognitively intact peer. Adjustments might include slowing the pace, chunking information, using visual and written aids, building in repetition and retrieval practice, and involving family or caregivers to reinforce key points between sessions. Below, we review key cognitive domains and practical strategies to optimize psychoeducation.( 99,100) >Processing Speed and Attention

 Impaired Processing Speed:

Slowed information processing is one of the most robust cognitive findings in schizophrenia. On symbol-coding and reaction-time tasks, patients are markedly slower than controls. Clinically, this can look like delayed answers or appearing disengagedbut often it means the patient simply needs more time to absorb the material.( 101)

Practical Adaptations: In sessions, long lectures or rapid-fire explanations can overwhelm a patient's processing system. Break information into shorter segments, use strategic pauses, and frequently summarize key points. Encourage the patient to speak up if they need a moment or a repeat. In effect, match the pace to the patient's cognitive tempo. (This is not "dumbing down" the content; it's ensuring the content is encoded effectively.) Remember: if the patient feels safe and understood, they're more likely to engage with the informational content later.

4. Clinical Applications of NIPE

The following brief clinical applications illustrate how NIPE may be used in routine schizophrenia care. They are intended to demonstrate how neuroscience-based explanations can be translated into patient-centered communication that is paced, validating, and cognitively accessible. In each example, the clinician begins with the patient's lived experience, introduces a brain-based explanation in plain language, and uses teach-back or visual support to confirm understanding. (These examples are brought to narration from actual clinical authors of this chapter's experience.)

Clinical Illustration 1: Auditory Hallucinations

A patient asks why voices are heard when no one else is speaking. A conventional response that the voices are "not real" may feel dismissive and can weaken engagement. In NIPE, the explanation begins with validation of the experience and then introduces a simple neurosciencebased model: the brain normally tags inner speech as self-generated, but in schizophrenia this monitoring process may be disrupted, making internally generated thoughts feel like external voices. A brief diagram, a simple analogy, and teach-back can be used to reinforce the explanation. The educational goal is to reduce fear, reduce self-blame, and support a shared understanding of the symptom.

Clinical Illustration 2: Delusional Meaning and Aberrant Salience

A patient believes that neutral events carry a special personal meaning. Rather than directly challenging the belief, NIPE frames the problem as altered salience processing: the brain may assign excessive importance to neutral stimuli, which makes ordinary events feel highly significant. The clinician can use a simple metaphor as an "importance filter" that is too sensitive. The emphasis remains on mechanism rather than confrontation. This approach is designed to reduce defensiveness, preserve the therapeutic alliance, and promote reflective thinking.

Clinical Illustration 3: Reduced Motivation and Cognitive Slowing

A patient reports low motivation, difficulty starting tasks, and feeling mentally slowed. NIPE explains these symptoms as part of the disorder's neurocognitive profile rather than as laziness or lack of effort. The clinician may describe motivational and cognitive difficulties using a brief, non-stigmatizing explanation of reward processing, processing speed, and working memory limitations. Information is delivered in short segments with pauses, and key points are repeated or written down. The goal is to support comprehension and reduce self-blame while helping the patient link symptoms to brain-based processes.

Clinical Reflection

These examples show that NIPE depends not only on what information is provided, but on how it is delivered. In schizophrenia, where processing speed, attention, working memory, and social cognition may be impaired, explanations must be simplified, paced, and reinforced for them to be retained and used. NIPE therefore shifts psychoeducation from a one-way transfer of facts to a collaborative process of meaning-making that respects both neurobiology and lived experience.

Integrating NIPE into Practice

• Match pace to processing speed: Schizophrenia is characterized by markedly slowed processing speed and attention deficits, Clinicians should speak and respond slowly, allow extra time for questions, and break information into small chunks so the patient can follow. This "goldfish bowl" approach ensures the patient processes each idea before moving on. (114) • Protect attention through environmental management: Patients frequently report difficulty sustaining attention, often being distracted by background noise or clutter. NIPE sessions should therefore be held in calm, quiet settings free of interruptions (e.g. no TV, phones off) to minimize sensory overload. Clear, consistent routines and simple, uncluttered visual aids help keep focus on the topic.( 115) • Scaffold working memory with visuals and repetition: Working memory deficits are a core cognitive impairment in schizophrenia that hinders learning. To compensate, NIPE uses repeated demonstrations and visual supports (charts, pictures, models) so that verbal explanations are anchored to concrete images. For example, teaching about medication can use pill charts or diagrams. Repetition of key points (asking patients to repeat information back, summarizing at each step) reinforces encoding. Indeed, cognitive science shows that strategies like "visual repetition" and contextual encoding significantly aid memory, and that patients benefit most when such strategies are explicitly provided. (116) • Use distributed rehearsal with family involvement: Instead of cramming all education into one session, information is reviewed repeatedly over time (spacing effect) with family present. Research shows that memory for material is far better when subjects engage in repeated retrieval practice rather than one-time study. In practice, this means revisiting core facts (e.g. neurobiology of symptoms) across multiple visits and encouraging family members to quiz or discuss these topics at home. Family participation creates natural "rehearsal" moments (e.g. practicing coping strategies together) that strengthen learning and link education to daily life. (117) • Reduce executive load with implementation intentions: Executive function (planning, organization) is often impaired in schizophrenia, making it hard to form and act on plans. NIPE therefore explicitly teaches "if-then" action plans (implementation intentions) to automate routine tasks. For example: "If I feel confused about my medication time, then I will ask my caregiver to remind me." This strategy pre-binds a cue with an action, offloading the need for on-the-spot decision-making. Experimental psychology shows that forming such if-then plans dramatically improves goal attainment under cognitive load. By reducing the number of steps the patient must remember, implementation intentions help patients follow through on healthy behaviors without executive bottlenecks. (118) • Protect social cognition with explicit validation: Schizophrenia often impairs recognition and interpretation of social cues. NIPE practitioners therefore emphasize validating the patient's experiences and emotions during education. For instance, when discussing paranoid thoughts or hallucinations, the provider listens non-judgmentally and affirms the patient's feelings ("I hear this is very frightening for you") before explaining the neural basis. It is critical not to dismiss or argue against the patient's perspective. Importantly, simple "brain disease" messages can backfire (the public often interprets them as indicating permanent incapacity). Over-reliance on biomedical framing can inadvertently promote "benevolent stigma" (treating patients as childlike or permanently ill). NIPE therefore couples scientific explanations with empathy: it labels symptoms as brain-related (reducing blame) but simultaneously normalizes the experience and stresses the patient's autonomy.( 119) • Address metacognitive limits through reflective dialogue: Many patients have difficulty reflecting on their own thought processes (metacognition). NIPE tackles this by guiding the patient through self-reflection exercises. For example, the therapist might say, "I notice when you explain your delusion, you use very confident language. How do you feel about that?" Such questions gently draw out the patient's insight and encourage them to think about how they think. This mirrors approaches like Metacognitive Reflection and Insight Therapy (MERIT) (120, 121), which use guided dialogue to improve selfawareness in schizophrenia. Over time, these reflective conversations help patients recognize links between their brain state and behavior, reinforcing all the other NIPE strategies. Several studies have shown that psychoeducation for family caregivers of people with psychotic disorders had positive effects on the caregivers themselves. Studies found a reduction of the caregivers' burden, their global morbidities and expressed emotion. These programs consist of a total of 9 to 25 sessions. (32, 43) These groups are conducted without the patients. This means that it is not family therapy, but rather a specialized form of therapy for family members that complements the patients' treatment. The combination of psychoeducation for family members and of psychoeducation for patients is sometimes referred to as "bifocal therapy". Results are now available showing that bifocal therapy is superior to both patient psychoeducation alone and caregiver psychoeducation alone. Bäuml et al reported a 21% relapse rate after 12 months in the bifocal therapy group and a 38% relapse rate in the routine treatment group.( 43) They also found improved compliance of 80% in the bifocal group compared to 58% in the control group. (43) In addition, they found a significantly higher overall level of functioning among patients treated in the bifocal group.( 43)

5. NIPE for Families and Caregivers

Family involvement is essential in schizophrenia care. NIPE provides a parallel track of education for caregivers that achieves multiple goals: • Improving caregiver knowledge and engagement: Teaching families about illness markedly boosts their understanding.

In one study, relatives who attended an 8-week psychoeducation course made large gains in knowledge (especially about medication management) and their patients had significantly fewer hospital days afterward. Patients whose families were educated were almost four times less likely to be readmitted over two years. Metaanalyses similarly show that virtually all family psychoeducational programs reduce relapse risk substantially. This demonstrates that informed, involved families greatly improve long-term outcomes.( 122)

•

Reducing blame and anger: Placing symptoms in brain-based terms may reduce moral blame by emphasizing that behaviors are symptoms of an illness rather than willful acts. However, research suggests that biogenetic explanations have mixed effects on stigma: although they can reduce blame, they may also increase perceptions of dangerousness, social distance, and pessimism about recovery. Therefore, NIPE should present neurobiological explanations cautiously and alongside messages emphasizing recovery, individuality, treatment responsiveness, and the person's agency. For example, explaining that hallucinations or disorganized thinking can be symptoms of schizophrenia may help caregivers respond with greater understanding. However, statements such as "the brain is damaged" or "overactive dopamine pathways cause the behavior" may unintentionally encourage deterministic beliefs. A more balanced explanation would be: "These experiences can be symptoms of an illness involving changes in brain functioning. They are not deliberate, and treatment and support can help-but the symptoms do not define the person.( 123)

•

Explaining symptoms in brain-based terms: NIPE explicitly uses neuroscience analogies that families can grasp. For example, one may explain paranoia by comparing it to a "noise in the signal" in the brain's filter or describe negative symptoms as "missed connections" between frontal cortex and emotional centers. These metaphors demystify odd behaviors and help relatives reframe challenging encounters as part of an illness. Providing a concrete brain story for symptoms gives families tools to answer "why" questions. It also underscores that effective treatments (medications, cognitive strategies) are based on real neurobiology, enhancing credibility.( 124)

•

Reducing expressed emotion: High expressed emotion (hostility/criticism) in families is a strong predictor of relapses. NIPE aims to lower EE by fostering empathy and clear communication. Simply educating relatives about symptom causes and teaching positive communication skills can curb critical comments. Indeed, family psychoeducation interventions have been shown to reduce EE levels and, in turn, significantly lower relapse rates. Caregivers learn to watch for signs of relapse in a supportive way (e.g. calmly asking "What are you thinking?") rather than with suspicion or anger.( 125) • Supporting adherence and relapse prevention: Educated families are better equipped to help patients adhere to treatment plans.

NIPE sessions train caregivers on how to offer reminders and check-ins without nagging, and on strategies like collaborative medication scheduling. In Cassidy et al.'s study, family members' improved knowledge about antipsychotic drugs was a key factor in preventing rehospitalizations. Meta-analyses show that family involvement alone (even without extra behavioral therapy) cuts relapse risk dramatically -for example, one review found family psychoeducation reduced 12-month relapse odds to about one-fifth of usual care.

Having a well-informed support person makes missing doses or early warning signs much less likely.( 126)

•

Encouraging non-controlling support: It is important that caregivers empower rather than overpower the patient. Recovery-oriented philosophy emphasizes that people with schizophrenia should have "equal status" in decision-making and be encouraged towards independence. NIPE leads families to set appropriate boundaries -for example, helping organize daily routines together, but letting the patient make choices about personal goals. Families learn to celebrate small successes (returning a smile, maintaining hygiene) without taking over the patient's responsibilities. This balance of guidance plus autonomy prevents resentment and builds trust. (127)

6. Challenges and Limitations

Practical constraints: NIPE can be demanding to deliver, particularly when patients experience substantial cognitive or clinical difficulties. Patients with severe symptoms or marked cognitive impairment may have difficulty processing complex information even when teaching is carefully adapted, and early sessions may create a risk of information overloading. NIPE therefore requires appropriate pacing, repetition, and cognitive scaffolding. Overemphasizing biological details may also be counterproductive if patients become overwhelmed or disengaged. Clinicians need to assess each patient's cognitive and emotional tolerance and present information in manageable segments to minimize frustration and support engagement.( 128) Avoiding biological reductionism: There is a fine line between providing a helpful neuroscience-based framework and reducing a person to their brain dysfunction. If patients or families interpret schizophrenia exclusively through a "brokenbrain" framework, biological explanations may inadvertently contribute to hopelessness, pessimism, or stigma.( 129) NIPE should therefore embed neuroscience within a recovery-oriented framework, emphasizing that neurobiological vulnerability does not determine an individual's identity, prognosis, or potential for improvement. Neuroscience should be used to support understanding and agency rather than to replace the person's lived experience with a purely biological explanation.( 130) Cultural and individual differences: Biological explanations of mental illness may not resonate equally across cultural and individual contexts.( 131,132) Spiritual, social, familial, or other explanatory frameworks may be important to patients and families and should be approached respectfully rather than dismissed.( 133) NIPE may therefore require cultural adaptation, including the use of locally meaningful examples, metaphors, and explanatory models. Individual differences in education, health literacy, cognitive functioning, symptom severity, and personal preferences should likewise inform the level, pace, and format of psychoeducation. An approach that is effective in one clinical or cultural setting may require adaptation before implementation elsewhere.( 134) Research gaps: NIPE remains an emerging approach, and its specific contribution beyond standard psychoeducation has not yet been sufficiently established. Evidence directly comparing neuroscience-informed psychoeducation with standard psychoeducation remains limited, while the neuroscience components of existing interventions are not consistently defined or measured. This heterogeneity makes it difficult to determine which components are responsible for potential benefits and to compare findings across studies. In addition, evidence from diverse cultural and low-resource settings remains limited, restricting conclusions about the transferability of NIPE across healthcare contexts. Finally, relatively little is known about the durability of potential benefits, particularly with respect to longer-term outcomes such as relapse, functioning, quality of life, and sustained engagement with treatment.( 135)7. Future Directions: Controlled trials of NIPE versus standard psychoeducation: Future research should directly examine whether adding neuroscience-informed and neurocognitive content provides benefits beyond standard psychoeducation. controlled trials should assess outcomes such as illness knowledge, insight, treatment engagement, adherence, relapse, and functional recovery.

Breakdown of studies' components:

To determine which components of NIPE contribute most strongly to its effects, dismantling studies could isolate specific elements of the intervention, such as explanations of neurobiological mechanisms, circuit-based symptom models, visual representations, or cognitive scaffolding strategies. This would help clarify which components are most relevant to changes in insight, engagement, and behavior.

Development of standardized NIPE manuals:

There is currently no widely established, standardized NIPE curriculum for schizophrenia.( 136) Future work should develop and evaluate structured manuals that translate relevant neuroscience into accessible and clinically meaningful language. Potential topics may include neurobiological mechanisms of symptoms, neural circuitry, neuroplasticity, and cognitive and social-cognitive processes. Standardization could facilitate replication, clinician training, and implementation across settings. Cultural diversity: NIPE should be adapted and evaluated across diverse cultural and healthcare contexts. This process should extend beyond translation to include consultation with patients, families, clinicians, and other local stakeholders to ensure that explanatory models, examples, and analogies are culturally meaningful and acceptable. Such work will be important for establishing the transferability and contextual applicability of NIPE across populations. Longitudinal outcome studies: Future studies should examine outcomes over longer follow-up periods to determine whether potential benefits of NIPE are sustained over time. (137) In addition to symptom relapses, outcomes should include insight, everyday functioning, education or employment, quality of life, treatment engagement, and cognitive and social functioning. Longitudinal research will help determine whether the benefits of NIPE are sustained over time and whether they translate into meaningful functional and recovery-related outcomes. Integration of digital and interactive tools: Digital technologies may provide new opportunities to deliver and reinforce NIPE. Smartphone and tablet-based interventions could present neuroscience content through animations, interactive exercises, and quizzes, while virtual or simulated environments could provide opportunities to practice cognitive and social strategies. Interactive and gamified approaches may be particularly useful for increasing engagement among younger patients.( 138) Future research should examine whether digital delivery can improve accessibility, engagement, scalability, and the effectiveness of NIPE while maintaining appropriate clinical and therapeutic support.

8. Conclusion

Schizophrenia can disrupt the continuity of self, thought processes, and the interpretation of lived experience. (139) In this context, NIPE serves as a translational bridge, providing a shared, biologically grounded framework while respecting and validating patients' personal meanings.( 140) By connecting lived experiences with underlying neural mechanisms, NIPE may reduce moralistic interpretations of symptoms and support collaborative meaning-making.( 141) NIPE encompasses two complementary domains. First, it explains symptom development through the lens of circuit-level dysfunction, providing patients and families with a framework that links symptoms to brain processes rather than personal shortcomings. Second, it structures learning to facilitate retention and practical application through visual aids, scaffolded instruction, and other neuroscience-informed teaching strategies that support encoding and retrieval in the presence of cognitive difficulties. Together, these approaches can strengthen clinician-patient communication and facilitate the translation of knowledge into actionable strategies. Importantly, NIPE reframes psychoeducation from the passive transmission of information into an active, collaborative process through which insight and agency may develop. Rather than conceptualizing patients as having "broken minds" to be fixed, NIPE positions them as informed participants in recovery, with targeted strategies directed toward symptom management, engagement, and functional improvement. (140) (141) (142) This approach might reduce stigma, preserve personal autonomy, and align therapeutic goals with understandable, brain-based mechanisms. The analogy of a check-engine light illustrates this practical orientation: symptoms are not necessarily signals of unavoidable decline, but indicators that may prompt evaluation, intervention, and adaptive change. By grounding hope in the brain's capacity for change, NIPE offers a framework for supporting individual recovery while contributing to more constructive understandings of schizophrenia. Collectively, these elements position NIPE as a potential theoretical and practical advancement in psychoeducation-one that combines explanatory depth with educational clarity to support engagement, understanding, and recovery.
