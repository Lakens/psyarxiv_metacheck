Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 14 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Environmental justice activists increasingly pursue public awareness campaigns to garner support as they face injustice. However, little psychological research has investigated the implications of different framings of recognition justice for collective action. Two experiments with qualitative components test the primary hypothesis that recognition of systemic racism in environmental exposures may represent a double-edged sword. We test implications of racismconscious framings of inequitable lead exposures on collective action intentions among Black, Latine, and White US residents. Study 1 (n=590) finds that a racism-conscious frame increases anger and systemic knowledge of racism but does not affect collective action intentions, except among highly racially identified White participants, whose collective action intentions decreased after the manipulation. Results of Study 2 (n=703) suggest that a combined racism-and classconscious frame may prevent (highly-identified) White disengagement from collective action.

We discuss addressing environmental injustice through coalition building while acknowledging the double-edged sword of racism-conscious recognition.

"What's our color? POOR": Exploring the Consequences of Racism-Conscious Framings of Environmental Exposures

In April 2014, the governor-appointed Emergency Manager of Flint, MI switched the city's water supply from Lake Huron to the Flint River to cut costs. A variety of overlapping public health crises ensued: inadequate treatment of industrial toxicants present in the Flint River, a legionnaire's disease outbreak, and (most infamously) widespread lead-in-water contamination. Flint, a largely Black and working-class town with deep Civil Rights and labor movement roots, fought back in inspiring ways: they mobilized, connected with institutions, conducted popular epidemiology studies, and made strong appeals through social and traditional media from both longstanding networks of civil rights groups and newly formed environmental justice coalitions (Pauli, 2020).

While many have demonstrated the clear systemic racism at the root of the poisoning of Flint's water (e.g., Benz, 2019), the community often chose -sometimes explicitly -racismevasive "environmental justice" (rather than "environmental racism") frames. In his seminal work Flint Fights Back, Pauli (2019) demonstrates that activists chose racism-evasive approaches in their heavily mediatized fight to garner broader public support. 1 This tension between racism-conscious and racism-evasive framings observed in Flint may have contributed to the consistent and systematic non-recognition of the pivotal work of Black activists in responding to the Flint Water Crisis (Johnson et al., 2018).

We explore a theoretical "double-edged sword" of this racism-evasion in environmental justice frames in the United States (US). Building on research suggesting symbolic and material benefits of racism-conscious framings for those exposed to environmental injustices on the one hand (e.g., Fuller, 2015; Muhammad et al., 2018; Schmitt et al., 2022), we explore the potential for racism-conscious framings to simultaneously impede broad support for an environmental justice movement among (some) White individuals on the other. We approach our studies in relation to psychological research on colorblind racial ideology (Knowles et al., 2009; Neville et al., 2013) and discuss the nuances of this conundrum through a combination of critical race, environmental justice, and decolonial theoretical lenses.

A Trivalent Model of Environmental Justice

Environmental injustice occurs when communities of color, low-income communities, and otherwise marginalized communities are disproportionately exposed to environmental hazards and excluded from environmental benefits. Environmental justice (EJ) scholars have argued for a trivalent EJ model to address past harms (Murdock, 2020). A distributive justice pillar wherein these are more equitably distributed across the population is important for combatting distributional inequities in environmental harms/benefits. A procedural justice is necessary to involve impacted EJ communities in remediation because one contributing factor to the inequitable distribution of environmental harms/benefits is a lack of involvement of marginalized communities in decision-making (e.g., due to political disenfranchisement, economic exploitation) in the first place. A relatively underappreciated element of this model includes recognition justice in which the lived experiences, identities, and values of those victimized by environmental injustice are acknowledged and respected. Recognition has been called an "inherent precondition" to the other pillars (Schlosberg, 2004; 2007). Fraser (1996) describes three common forms of misrecognition: "[C]ultural domination (being subjected to patterns of interpretation and communication that are associated with another culture and are alien and/or hostile to one's own); nonrecognition (being rendered invisible via the authoritative representational, communicative, and interpretative practices of one's culture); and disrespect (being routinely maligned or disparaged in stereotypic public cultural representations and/or in everyday life interactions)" (p. 7) While all three are indispensable to the project of EJ, we focus here on the nuanced implications of (non)recognition. Environmental injustices occur in the US as a function of intertwined histories of racism, colonization, exploitation of the working class, and wholesale domination of the environment to serve capital, histories that we succinctly term racial capitalism (Foster, 2002; Pulido, 2016). A key part of recognition justice should thus be acknowledging and critiquing systemic racism both as a root cause of disproportionate exposures, as well as a threat multiplier for the social and material processes that occur in the wake of exposures (e.g., Kanner, 1994; Rosner & Markowitz, 2008).

Symbolic and Material Implications of Racism-Conscious Framings

Recognition may take on structural forms: "official" recognition of past harms (Lord & Shutkin, 1994), institutional apologies for exposures (Perez et al., 2021), or justice-centered policy change (Mohai et al., 2009). It may also take on social-psychological forms, such as norms for widespread acknowledgment of environmental injustice (which may have yet to materialize: Schuldt & Pearson, 2023; Song et al., 2020). We see these forms of recognition as mutually constitutive, as structural forms may both stem from and catalyze further social recognition (Martin et al., 2016). More importantly, structural and social recognition may have key material and symbolic benefits.

Structural recognition may contribute to policy regimes that address environmental health disparities (Wang et al., 2022), and may also produce ameliorative effects for EJ communities in the form of "being seen" in one's environmental suffering, a key symbolic benefit (see Perez et al., 2021; Schmitt et al., 2022). Moreover, structural f recognition may give way to social forms of recognition, encouraging broad norm change around the acknowledgement of the reality of environmental racism (see Eisner et al., 2021). Structural forms of recognition may also contribute to the normalization of racism-and class-conscious EJ movements, even resulting in more "successful" activism. For instance, Fuller (2015) compared two Indianapolis communities facing environmental hazards and found that the community that had mounted a more robust activist movement often explicitly employed racism-conscious understandings of victimization which contributed to social networks that could be leveraged for collective action. Likewise, research with a Latine community exposed for decades to water contamination in Tucson, AZ demonstrated that activists often saw their exposures as being the result of racist discrimination and that residents who adopted racism-conscious perceptions of local exposures tended to have higher collective efficacy and civic engagement (Schmitt, 2023; Schmitt et al., 2022).

Given the potential for material-symbolic benefits of recognition, it is worth considering why some activists have strayed away from racism-conscious framings in particular. Might activists be trying to avoid other sub-ideal implications of such critical framings of environmental exposures? -Silva (2001) argues that while the fact of racial inequality has remained constant in US history (i.e., the racialized social system), distinct racial ideologies emerge in different eras which provide individuals with a framework for understanding and justifying the racial status quo. The dominant racial ideology of the contemporary US is one of colorblindness: racial disparities in social standing are explained away via ostensibly nonracial characteristics (e.g., perceived cultural and personal deficiencies, free-market mechanisms). In the context of environmental issues, Bugden (2024) found that colorblind environmental racism is a substantial barrier to broader engagement with the EJ movement, and others have noted that racism-evasive ideologies more generally may drive the minimization of environmental inequities (Swim & Bloodhart, 2018).

Navigating Recognition (In)Justice in an Era of Colorblindness

Bonilla

Neville and colleagues (2013) argue that there are two dimensions of colorblindness: color-and power-evasion. Color-evasion manifests as a rhetorical refusal to "see race," instead upholding the shared humanity of all people. While color-evasion appears progressive, it does nothing to address the impacts of racism at best, and can actually increase racial prejudice at worst (Apfelbaum et al., 2008; Correll et al., 2008). Color-evasion appears to be more a function of White people's discomfort with discussing racism than any real desire to revel in the shared humanity of racialized individuals (Bonilla-Silva, 2021). Color-evasion also gives way to more pernicious power-evasion, which manifests as a denial of the existence of systemic racism, subjectively placing overt racism firmly in the past, and denying any White privilege. Powerevasion often includes the employment of a variety of other hierarchy-enhancing legitimizing myths to explain (and therefore justify) the racist status quo: belief in meritocracy, a just world, and the social dominance of racially dominant groups over subordinated ones (Sidanius & Pratto, 1999). Power-evasion can even take on a more progressive guise whereby proponents reduce the outcomes of racism to simple issues of poverty (i.e., "class reductionism; " Pitcher, 2012). By evading racism, colorblind ideology allows adoptees to justify unequal outcomes between racial groups through individualistic, ostensibly non-racial mechanisms (Knowles et al., 2009). Powerevasion also likely contributes to recognition injustice as described above: a refusal to acknowledge the forms of racialized traumatization experienced by people of color is, in itself, an injustice.

In the cultural context of a colorblind society, it is important to explore whether and how people engage with the reality of environmental racism. A past study found that people in the US were reticent to acknowledge race-based inequalities in climate change impacts (Schuldt & Pearson, 2023), though no studies to our knowledge have assessed such processes in the context of invisible exposures like lead-in-water. Two theoretical perspectives suggest potential ways that (particularly White) people may respond to racism-conscious framings of EJ exposures.

According to the first perspective building on self-affirmation (e.g., Adams et al., 2006), White people may be motivated to deny racism (and privilege) because acknowledging the persistence of racism-based inequality could threaten one's sense of personal merit or one's sense that the world and social system are just, resulting in undesirable collective guilt feelings. These defensive processes may also be particularly prevalent among those highly identified with their White identity (i.e., racial identity relevance hypothesis, Bonam et al., 2019; Lowery et al., 2007). According to the second perspective ("Marley Hypothesis," Nelson et al., 2013), White people may deny present-day racism in part due to ignorance of historical racism, and even brief educational interventions can increase White people's perceptions of racism (Bonam et al., 2019). Two potentially competing hypotheses thus motivate the present research: (1) if White people are motivated to defensively deny racism, then racism-conscious framings of environmental exposures may result in denial of environmental racism and disengagement from ameliorative collective action; but (2) if ignorance drives misrecognition of environmental racism, then exposing White people to racism-conscious framings of environmental exposures may increase perceptions of environmental racism and collective action intentions.

Hypotheses

Hypothesis 1:

In spite of the potential benefits of racism-conscious EJ frames (Fuller, 2015; Schmitt, 2023), there may be potential drawbacks of such frames. If colorblindness is a "dominant" racial ideology, this creates a conundrum for EJ activists in which acknowledging the role of racism in exposures may be at odds with the hegemonic racial ideology that encourages colorblind framings. If the instincts of Flint activists described by Pauli (2019) and the research suggesting that White people defensively deny racism (Adams et al., 2006) are correct, White people may be pushed away from a potential EJ movement as a function of discomfort with and motivated denial of racism-conscious framings (a "disengaging effect;" Hypothesis 1A). Based on the Marley Hypothesis (Nelson et al., 2013), racism-conscious framings of environmental exposures may result in heightened awareness of and support for a potential EJ movement (an "engaging" effect; Hypothesis 1B). Hypothesis 2: Although Hypothesis 1A and 1B may appear contradictory, we also test the conditions in which both hypotheses may be supported. In particular, we hypothesize that the racism-conscious frames may be particularly disengaging for White people highly identified with their race, while the opposite may be true for those who are less highly identified (Hypothesis 2A). Further, we hypothesize -in line with recent extensions of the Marley Hypothesis (Bonam et al., 2019; Rojas Melo Silva et al., 2025) -that racial identity will have positive effects on collective action intentions for Black and Latine participants regardless of framing (Hypothesis

2B).

We explore these hypotheses using two mixed-method studies (using the embedded QUANT-qual design from Syed & Westberg, 2025). Critical mixed methods approaches offer psychological research opportunities to address limitations that have long plagued the field: they acknowledge the social-structural-cultural contexts that shape many psychological phenomena, they engage with the messiness of qualia without losing sight of the generalizable quanta, and they offer an opportunity to triangulate phenomena through multiple ways of knowing (Power et al., 2018; Power & Velez, 2022). Though hypotheses are presented here in the introduction, our hypotheses were in reality derived from the cyclical and iterative process of this research in line with best practices for the "integration" of quantitative and qualitative methods (Fetters et al., 2013). Our hypotheses were generated from both top-down theory-driven and bottom-up datadriven approaches, particularly as the qualitative elements of Study 1 informed the design, preregistration, and implementation of Study 2.

STUDY 1

In Study 1, we recruited Black, Latine, and White participants and randomly assigned them to read one of two lead exposure fact sheets: a racism-evasive or a racism-conscious fact sheet to assess potential downstream consequences for collective action intentions as a test of Hypotheses 1A-C. We chose lead exposures as these are ubiquitous in the US and represent common EJ issues (Muller et al., 2018). All studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board of [anonymized for review].

Method

Power analyses are included in Supplemental Materials. All data, code, and preregistrations can be found here: https://osf.io/wrzus/?view_only=20c81ca8807f45b89428dd9571a7b513 Participants A convenience sample was recruited from Prolific Academic in March-April 2024. This included 595 English-speaking adults currently living in the US. Participants were excluded if they said they could not commit to giving their full attention to the study, or if they reported a racial-ethnic identity other than Black, Latine, or White (nexcluded=5), leaving a final sample of 590 (48.5% men, 48.8% women, 2.0% non-binary, .2% transgender, .2% agender, .3% no gender reported; 35.5% White, 33.0% Black, 30.6% Hispanic/Latine, .8% other racial or ethnic group).

Ages ranged from 18-84 (M=39.48, SD=12.87).

Procedure

After acknowledging an informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions in which they read a fact sheet about lead exposures in the US. Afterward, they completed measures of dependent variables, demographics, and moderators.

Racism-Conscious Framing Manipulation.

We manipulated exposure to racism-conscious vs. racism-evasive framings of lead exposure by randomly assigning one of two fact sheets about lead exposures (see Appendix). In the racism-evasive (control) condition, participants read about sources of lead exposures and possible health impacts with no mention of racial (or other) disparities in exposure. In the racism-conscious condition, the fact sheet included all of the same information, but used the term "Environmental Racism" in the title and related each bullet point back to racial disparities in exposure and impact. The racism-conscious sheet also mentioned "longstanding systemic racism in the United States" (following Skinner-Dorkenoo et al., 2021).

Dependent Variables

All items were measured on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), unless specified otherwise. Environmental justice perceptions, perceptions of sacred value violations, and neoliberal ideology were measured but not analyzed here.

Systemic Knowledge.

As a manipulation check, we adapted a measure of "systemic knowledge" from Skinner-Dorkenoo et al. (2021) to focus on racial disparities in lead exposure. This included four averaged items such as "As a result of structural inequalities, people of color in the U.S. are more likely to deal with lead exposures" (ɑ=.97).

Emotions.

Given that a variety of emotions drive collective action responses to threats (Thomas et al., 2009 , van Zomeren et al., 2008; Witte & Allen, 2000), we assessed three emotions that may be of interest: fear, anger, and empathy. Fear was assessed with seven averaged items adapted from Skinner-Dorkenoo et al. (2021) with items such as "I am scared of getting sick from lead exposures" (ɑ=.92). Anger was assessed with three averaged items adapted from van Zomeren et al. (2012) such as "As a US resident, I feel angry because of lead exposures" (ɑ=.92). Empathy was assessed with three averaged items adapted from Pfattheicher et al. (2020) such as "I feel compassion for those who are most vulnerable to lead exposures" (ɑ=.90).

Belief-Aligned Collective Action Intentions.

We adapted the nine item Belief-aligned Collective Action scale (BCA) from Cervone et al. (2024). Participants responded to six items that assessed intentions to engage in lower-stakes collective actions (e.g., hand out flyers) and three items that assessed more high-stakes, non-normative collective actions changed slightly from the original version of the scale (e.g., block access to a building, block traffic, disrupt a meeting) to "advocate for lead-related issues in my community." Items were assessed on a scale of 1 (Definitely not) to 7 (Definitely) and were averaged to form a reliable composite (ɑ=.93).

Moderators

Race/Ethnicity. We compared the effects of the manipulation for each racial/ethnic group: White, Black, Latine. Participants were asked to report which racial or ethnic category best described them. Though we limited access to the survey to Black, White, and Latine individuals on Prolific, some people who identified as other racial or ethnic groups appeared in the sample (n=5; one as Asian, one as Native American, and three as other). We removed these participants from focal analyses.

Racial Group Centrality and Satisfaction.

In line with Hypothesis 2A, we assessed racial group identity centrality and satisfaction (Leach et al., 2008). At the end of the survey, after having reported the racial/ethnic identity that best described them, participants responded to these scales such that the selected group identity was piped into the text of each item in the scale.

The centrality scale involved three items such as "I often think about the fact that I am [racial/ethnic group]' (ɑ=.90). The satisfaction scale included four items such as "I am glad to be [racial/ethnic group]' (ɑ=.91).

Exploratory Mixed-Methods Analyses

At the end of the survey, participants were asked an open-ended question: "Do you have any thoughts about this study that you'd like to share? (optional)." A sizeable minority of participants responded (n=119, 20.0% of the sample). While we initially intended for this question to be used for pragmatic feedback on the survey (e.g., spelling errors), a cursory reading of the responses appeared to be quite meaningful. As such, we decided to first quantify the presence/non-presence of race(ism) evasion in responses and then use reflexive thematic analysis to explore these responses (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The second and third author independently coded all responses for the presence of race(ism) evasion. Cohen's Kappa (.86) revealed nearperfect intercoder agreement, and any remaining disagreements were rectified by the lead author.

All authors then discussed the coded segments and themed the data. This blend of reflexive qualitative analysis with reductive quantitative analysis reflects a common epistemological approach for mixed-methods research that has been called "qualitative pragmatism" (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Doucerain et al., 2016; Schmitt et al., 2022).

Results

Zero-order correlations are presented in Table 1, main effects are presented in Table 2, and interactions are depicted in Figure 1. Only systemic knowledge and anger differed across conditions. Participants in the racism-conscious condition reported higher systemic knowledge of environmental injustice and higher anger compared to those in the racism-evasive condition. No interactions were observed between condition and racial/ethnic identity on any of these outcomes (ps>.554). However, across conditions, White participants scored significantly lower on all outcomes compared to Black participants (ps<.001), and lower on fear, anger, and collective action intentions than Latine participants (ps<.026). Black participants scored higher than Latine participants on fear, empathy, and collective action intentions (ps<.023).

Focal Three-Way Interactions

We tested for three-way interactions by regressing collective action intentions onto condition, racial/ethnic identity, racial identity centrality, and their interactions (see Table 3). We observed a significant three-way interaction on collective action intentions, highest order interaction: F(2, 577)=4.21, p=.015, R² =.01. A visual depiction of this effect can be found in the top half of Figure 1. Significant two-way interactions between condition and racial identity centrality only emerge among White participants (F(1, 577)=8.19, p=.004) and not among Latine (F(1, 577)=.72, p=.398) or Black (F(1, 577)=.08, p=.552) participants. Probing the interaction among White participants using a Johnson-Neyman analysis, we observed that at levels of racial identity centrality lower than 1.55, the racism-conscious framing significantly increased participants' collective action intentions, but at levels of racial identity centrality higher than 4.32, the racism-conscious framing significantly decreased participants' collective action intentions. Black and Latine participants evinced a different pattern than White participants, as higher racial identity centrality was associated with higher collective action intentions regardless of condition for these groups.

We then tested for a similar three-way interaction using racial identity satisfaction (rather than centrality) by regressing collective action intentions onto condition, racial/ethnic identity, racial identity satisfaction, and their interactions (including all lower-order interactions). We did observe a marginally significant three-way interaction on collective action intentions, highest order interaction: F(2, 577)=2.89, p=.057, R² =.01 (though the three-way interaction between condition, racial identity satisfaction, and the Black dummy code was significant: p=.022, see Table 3). A visual depiction of this effect can be found in the bottom half of Figure 1. Again, significant two-way interactions between condition and racial identity satisfaction only emerge among White participants (F(1, 577)=8.33, p=.004) and not among Latine (F(1, 577)=-.04, p=.838) or Black (F(1, 577)=.11, p=.558) participants. We probed the significant interaction among White participants using a Johnson-Neyman analysis: at levels of racial identity satisfaction lower than 2.98, the racism-conscious framing significantly increased participants' collective action intentions, but at levels of racial identity satisfaction higher than 5.24, the racism-conscious framing significantly decreased participants' collective action intentions.

Again, Black and Latine participants evinced a different pattern than White participants. Specifically, for Black participants, high racial identity satisfaction-similar to centrality-was associated with higher collective action intentions regardless of condition. For Latine participants, this pattern was similar, if less pronounced. Racism-conscious framings may be particularly demobilizing only for highly racially-identified and -satisfied White individuals.

Exploratory Mediation Analyses

We did not observe similar significant three-way interactions on any of the potential mediators (fear, anger, empathy). However, given that interaction effects described above were only observed among White participants, we tested for interactions only among White participants to explore whether fear, anger, or empathy might mediate the effects on collective action intentions differentially for those high and low in racial identity satisfaction/centrality.

These moderated mediation models are summarized in Table 4. In sum, we observed significant interactions between condition and racial identity satisfaction on both fear and anger. No other interactions were significant. The racism-conscious framing increased fear for White participants with low (-1 SD) racial identity satisfaction and decreased fear for those high (+1 SD) in racial identity satisfaction. The racism-conscious framing also increased anger for White participants low (-1 SD) in racial identity satisfaction, but had no effect at high (+1 SD) racial identity satisfaction. Anger significantly mediated the effect of condition on collective action intentions at low (-1 SD) but not high (+1 SD) racial identity satisfaction (with significant moderated mediation).

Quantifying Presence of Racism-Evasion.

We coded for responses that evinced discomfort with or denial of the importance of race and racism. Thirteen total responses were coded as such. See Table 8 for illustrative quotes. We tested for the prevalence of racism evasion across racial/ethnic groups using a one-way ANOVA and found a significant difference, F(2, 587)=4.96, p=.007, η²= .02. White participants were the most likely to engage in racism-evasion (4.7%) followed by Black participants (1.0%), then by Latine participants (.6%). Tukey's posthoc tests revealed that White participants were significantly more likely than Black (p=.029) and Latine (p=.014) participants to engage in racism-evasion, while there were no differences between Black and Latine participants (p=.947).

We then tested for the prevalence of racism-evasion across conditions, racial/ethnic identity, and racial identity centrality. No differences in racism-evasion were observed between conditions, t(592)=1.38, p=.169, d=.11. However, we did observe a significant interaction between participant race/ethnicity and racial identity satisfaction on race evasion, b=.03, SE=.01, p=.018, R² =.01. As seen in Figure 2, racial identity satisfaction significantly predicted racism-evasion in open-ended response among White participants (b=.02, SE=.01, p=.035), but not among Black (b=-.01, SE=.01, p=.195) or Latine participants (b=.002, SE=.01, p=.863). No other tested interactions were found to be significant.

Thematic Analysis.

We generated three themes: (1) racial anxiety, (2) colorblind racial ideology (including color and power evasion), and (3) class reductionism. See Table 5 for illustrative quotes. Racial anxiety involved discomfort with being asked to contend with topics of race/ism. Discomfort likely resulted from answering the racial identity measures immediately before the free-response question. Some (White) participants felt that the survey was intended to make them feel ashamed of their Whiteness. Both color-and power-evasive colorblindness were evident (Neville et al., 2013). Some participants expressed the classic color-evasive trope of affirming the existence of a homogenous, raceless humanity. In power-evasive moves, others explicitly denied that racism had anything to do with lead exposure, often citing examples of exposures in rural White communities. Finally, several participants engaged in what may be called class reductionism: a unique form of racism-evasion whereby disparities were attributed to socioeconomic injustice rather than racism. In an impressively transparent example of both color-evasion and class reductionism, one White participant named this paper for us: "It's not just a race issue it's a class issue. What's our color? POOR."

STUDY 2

Study 2 replicated and extended Study 1 in two ways. First, because we observed the interaction of interest only among White participants, we only recruited White participants in Study 2. We preregistered the observed interaction between condition (racism-conscious vs.

racism-evasive only) and racial identity satisfaction and centrality on collective action intentions (preregistration materials here: https://aspredicted.org/dtbg-gn36.pdf). Second, we added a classconscious and a racism-and class-conscious condition based on the qualitative analyses from Study 1.

Method Participants

A convenience sample was recruited from Prolific Academic in April 2024. This included 793 English-speaking adults currently living in the US. Participants were excluded if they said they could not commit to giving their full attention to the study, if they failed an attention check, or if they reported a racial identity other than White (nexcluded=90), leaving a final sample of 703 (39.7% men, 59.0% women, 0.9% non-binary, 0.1% agender, 0.1% no gender reported). Ages ranged from 18-80 (M=40.50, SD=12.76).

Procedure

After acknowledging an informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions in which they read a fact sheet about lead exposures that were identical to the ones from Study 1, but with the addition of two new conditions (class-conscious, racism-and class-conscious; see Appendix). Afterward, they completed measures of dependent variables, demographics, and moderators.

Dependent Variables

All items were measured on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), unless specified otherwise. We measured systemic knowledge with one item (as a manipulation check), and anger (ɑ=.95), empathy (ɑ=.87), fear (ɑ=.90), and belief-aligned collective action (ɑ=.92) were measured in the same way as in Study 1. Neoliberal ideology was again measured but not analyzed here. We also measured other additional variables that could potentially mediate any effects of framing on collective action intentions.

Additional Exploratory Mediators.

In line with much research on the predictors of collective action (van Zomeren et al., 2008), we measured collective efficacy with two items such as "I believe that we can work together to address lead-related issues in the United States" (ɑ=.84), and politicized identity with two items such as "I identify with those most vulnerable to lead exposures" (ɑ=.88). We also measured three different attributions for lead exposures. We measured individual attributions with three items such as "Lead exposures happen because people lack motivation to make the necessary renovations to their homes" (ɑ=.72). We measured structural attributions with five items such as "Lead exposures happen because of discrimination against minorities and the poor" (ɑ=.89). Finally, we measured random attributions with three items such as "Lead exposures happen because of bad luck" (ɑ=.62).

Exploratory Mixed-Methods Analyses

We asked the same free-response question as in Study 1. The second and third authors coded the data for the presence of race(ism) evasion. Cohen's Kappa (.88) again revealed nearperfect intercoder agreement, and any remaining disagreements were rectified by the lead author.

All authors then discussed the coded segments and themed the data.

Results

Main Effects

See Table 6 for zero-order correlations and Table 7 for main effects. Systemic knowledge, politicized identification, and all exposure attribution measures differed significantly across conditions. We conducted Tukey's post-hoc tests on significant ANOVAs. Participants reported significantly higher systemic knowledge in the class-conscious and the racism-and classconscious conditions compared to the control condition, which was not observed in the racismconscious condition. Those in the racism-conscious condition also reported significantly lower systemic knowledge than those in the racism-and class-conscious condition. Participants reported significantly higher anger only in the class-conscious condition (compared to the control). Participants also reported significantly lower politicized identification with leadexposed communities in the racism-conscious condition compared to the control and the classconscious conditions.

Participants reported significantly higher structural attributions for lead exposures in the class-conscious and the racism-and class-conscious conditions (vs. control), which was not observed in the racism-conscious condition. Those in the racism-conscious condition reported marginally lower structural attributions than those in the racism-and class-conscious condition (p=.056). A different pattern was observed for individual attributions. Participants reported significantly lower individual attributions in the class-conscious condition (vs. control). There were no differences in individual attributions between the racism-conscious condition and any other condition. Only participants in the racism-and class-conscious condition reported significantly reduced random attributions (vs. control).

Interactions between Condition and Racial Identification Variables

Preregistered Interactions.

We first tested our pre-registered hypotheses replicating Study 1. For these preregistered analyses, we only analyzed data from the control and racism-conscious conditions (to match Study 1). Summaries of these models can be found in Table 8.

We did not observe evidence for our hypothesized interaction between condition and racial identity centrality on collective action intentions (p=.571). However, we did replicate the interaction between condition and racial identity satisfaction on collective action intentions (p=.026; see Figure 3). Probing the significant interaction using a Johnson-Neyman analysis, we observed that at levels of racial identity satisfaction lower than 3.14, the racism-conscious framing significantly increased participants' collective action intentions. At a score of 7 on racial identity satisfaction (the highest score possible), the negative effect of the racism-conscious framing was approaching significance (p=.058). Thus, while in Study 1 we observed a crossover effect with White participants at particularly high levels of racial identity satisfaction showing significantly decreased collective action intentions, we did not quite see such a crossover effect in Study 2.

Non-Preregistered Interaction Effects.

We also tested similar interactions across all four conditions. We did not find any such interactions (see Table 9). In other words, racial identity satisfaction only exhibited a significant interaction with the racism-conscious condition, but not with the class-conscious nor with the racism-and class-conscious conditions. This may mean that the extent to which highly race-satisfied White people disengage from collective action following racism-conscious frames is attenuated when class-based disparities are mentioned as well.

Exploratory Mediation Analyses

We first explored mediation of the association between condition and collective action intentions without moderation (see Table 10). Anger was a significant mediator only in the classconscious and racism-and class-conscious conditions. Politicized identity was only a significant mediator in the racism-conscious condition, with this condition resulting in lower identification with downstream consequences for lower collective action intentions. Structural, individual, and random attributions were all significant mediators, but only in class-conscious and racism-and class-conscious conditions.

We then tested moderation mediation models with the a and c paths moderated by racial identity satisfaction (see Table 11). We only observed significant moderated mediation with fear, such that racial identity satisfaction was associated with reduced fear only in the racismconscious condition with downstream effects on collective action intentions that were significantly different from the racism-and class-conscious condition (though neither indirect effect was itself significant). We observed an interesting pattern with empathy where racial identity satisfaction was associated with reduced empathy only in the racism-conscious and the racism-and class-conscious condition, which had significant indirect effects on collective action intentions (though no significant moderated mediation was observed). A similar pattern was observed with anger.

Exploratory Mixed-Methods Analyses

Quantifying Presence of Racism-Evasion.

Twenty-one responses (3%) were coded as engaging in racism-evasion. As seen in Table 6, racism-evasion was correlated with lower systemic knowledge, marginally lower anger, lower structural attributions, and marginally lower racial identity centrality (mostly mirroring Study 1). In Study 1 we observed a significant association between racial identity satisfaction and racism-evasion among White participants, but we did not observe that same pattern in Study 2. Rates of racism-evasion were descriptively somewhat higher in the racism-conscious condition (5.7%) than in the control (1.7%), class-conscious (2.8%), and racism-and class-conscious (1.7%) conditions, but these were only descriptively different given low base rates, F(3, 699)=1.15, p=.326, η²= .00.

Thematic Analysis.

Theming of the data in Study 2 resulted in much the same analysis.

Illustrative quotes are again listed in Table 5. One interesting difference stemmed from the fact that scientific publications (Shearston et al., 2024) and news stories about lead found in menstrual products broke between conducting Study 1 and 2. Some participants expressed concerns specifically about that issue, and some extended ideas about EJ to also include women as a group disproportionately impacted by such hazards. There also appeared to be a theme that became more prominent in Study 2: mentions of the importance of combatting systemic racism.

For example, one participant wrote: "I am particularly angered by ongoing systemic racism in this nation and believe it manifests in many different ways that most never think about. I had never really considered the racial/economic aspects of lead exposure before but can easily see how this is just one more example of how our society needs to be restructured to give people a fair chance at happiness and a good life."

Discussion

Among White participants, preregistered analyses suggested that racial identity satisfaction negatively predicted collective action intentions following racism-conscious framings (supporting Hypothesis 2A). We did not replicate findings from Study 2 with racial identity centrality, against pre-registered hypotheses. Exploratory analyses revealed that racial identity satisfaction was not predictive of collective action intentions when lead exposures were framed in class-or racism-and class-conscious ways. Acknowledging both the economic and racial injustices may prevent highly race-satisfied White people from disengaging from EJ collective action. Tests of mechanisms remain inconclusive, though reduced fear, empathy, anger, and politicized identification with those facing lead exposures among highly race-satisfied White participants may partially explain these patterns.

In terms of main effects, participants tended to respond more positively to the class-only than the racism-only framing. For instance, the class-only condition (vs. control) resulted in higher systemic knowledge, anger, and structural attributions, and lower individual attributions, while the racism-only condition was no different from the control on these variables. Moreover, the racism-only condition reduced politicized identity compared to the control (supporting the "disengaging" Hypothesis 1A). One silver lining may be the fact that on some variables, the racism-and class-conscious condition did not have the same degree of disengagement as the racism-only condition, exhibiting higher systemic knowledge, structural attributions, and lower random attributions compared to the control (extending the "engaging" Hypothesis 1B).

Exploratory mediation analyses also attested to this silver lining: even in the racism-and classconscious condition, heightened anger and structural attributions, and lowered individual and random attributions may contribute to downstream heightened collective action intentions. GENERAL DISCUSSION

Across two studies, we find evidence of a "double-edged sword" of the recognition of environmental racism: though recognition of environmental racism may have material-symbolic benefits (e.g., Fuller, 2015), social recognition in the form of racism-conscious framings of exposures promotes disengagement from collective action for some (White) participants while simultaneously promoting increased engagement for others. Racism-conscious framings of lead exposures drove highly race-satisfied White participants to disengage from collective action intentions. Emotional responses such as decreased fear and anger, as well as decreased politicized identification may drive these demobilizing effects among highly race-satisfied White individuals. Study 1 also revealed that Black and Latine participants scored higher on collective action intentions than White participants and that racial identity satisfaction was positively associated with collective action intentions regardless of framing condition. This fits with recently published work showing that the extent of racial identity has opposite implications for White vs. Black individuals in terms of perceptions of racism (Rojas Melo Silva et al., 2025).

Study 2 expanded on this observed pattern by adding a class-conscious framing and a racism-and class-conscious framing based on qualitative responses in Study 1. A key silver lining of the present studies is that when racism-conscious and class-conscious framings were combined, highly race-satisfied White participants did not disengage from collective action intentions in the same way as observed in the racism-conscious condition. Moreover, participants in the racism-and class-conscious condition recognized the role of systemic inequalities in environmental exposures, exhibited higher structural attributions, and lower random attributions for exposures compared to the evasive condition -patterns not observed in the racism-conscious condition. These results demonstrate the value of including class in the discussion around EJ, at least in terms of encouraging broader support for EJ movements.

One strength of this work is its mixed-method approach. Recent prominent calls (e.g., Power & Velez, 2022; Sullivan, 2020) encourage researchers to triangulate psychological phenomena through combined (critical) quantitative and qualitative methods to increase the ecological validity of findings and highlight their embeddedness within cultural-historical context. We add to the burgeoning literature that critiques status quo social arrangements in ways that cross traditional disciplinary boundaries (see Goudarzi et al., 2022; Jimenez et al., 2022; Schmitt et al., 2023).

Some theoretical and practical implications are worth noting. We discussed two potentially competing hypotheses: (1) an "engaging" effect based on the Marley Hypothesis in which denial of present-day racism is driven by White ignorance and may in turn be remedied by educational interventions, and (2) a "disengaging" effect based on motivated cognition perspectives in which White people deny present-day racism for self-protective reasons (which has also been demonstrated in recent research on the Marley Hypothesis, e.g., Bonam et al., 2019; Rojas Melo Silva et al., 2025). We demonstrate limited evidence for both. Racismconscious framings in Study 1 increased systemic knowledge and anger, and racism-and classconscious framings in Study 2 increased systemic knowledge, increased structural attributions, and lowered random attributions, suggesting a potential engaging effect of educating (White) people about the role of systemic racism in environmental exposures.

However, we also find that racism-conscious (but not racism-and class-conscious) frames result in decreased collective action intentions among highly race-identified (Study 1) and race-satisfied (Studies 1-2) White participants. Ignorance may be more important in the denial of environmental racism (compared to motivated disengagement) for White people low in racial identity satisfaction for whom racism-conscious framings did increase engagement. White participants low in these racial identity constructs may be more likely to form coalitions with communities of color who use racism-conscious framings. But for White people high in racial identity satisfaction -those already motivated to deny White privilege and systemic racism -there may be a disengaging effect of racism-conscious framings.

One relevant concept from Critical Race Theory here is interest convergence, which occurs when "the interest of blacks in achieving racial equality will be accommodated only when it converges with the interests of whites" (Bell, 1980, p. 523). Interest convergence may occur to the extent that (highly race-satisfied) White people feel afraid of lead exposure for themselves.

And so, if class-conscious or racism-and class-conscious framings work toward this end, there may be a practical benefit to such frames for EJ communities insofar as these frames encourage broader support from racism-evasive White people. We are wary of encouraging EJ communities (of color) to capitulate to the White supremacist status quo. Racism is a key structuring factor in environmental exposures and by extension in the health and wellbeing of many communities of color. Acknowledging this fact may be a key part of recognition justice for such communities, irrespective of the demobilizing effect that such acknowledgments might have for some White people. Still, EJ scholars have long lamented the "race or class" debates that have bogged down the field. Pulido's (1996) contention is helpful here: "It is problematic in that a narrow focus on racism obscures a nuanced understanding of how racism interacts with various economic forces, including, relations of production and regimes of accumulation, to create highly oppressive circumstances…" lest we arrive at an "antiracist politics devoid of an economic critique" (p.

148).

Our studies suggest that Flint activists' instincts to downplay race and focus on class in their activism may have garnered broader support. These findings suggest that activists need not entirely eschew acknowledging racism, but that combining class-and racism-conscious framings may promote action aimed at structural change without demobilizing highly race-satisfied White people.

No EJ community is a monolith (e.g., Flint was 38.7% White at the time of contamination, 42.9% voted for Donald Trump in 2016; 2016 Michigan Presidential Election Results, 2016; Murembya & Guthrie, 2016). Our results may apply to the plethora of EJ communities in more conservative contexts in which exposed White people may be turned off from racism-conscious movements (e.g., as has been found in Tucson, AZ: Schmitt et al., 2022; 2023).

Thus, EJ activists may benefit from thinking through the complexities of strategically using interest convergence "in order to determine when to harness this dynamic to reach strategic ends, and when to avoid the diluted messaging that can occur when interests only temporarily align" (Pierson-Brown, 2023, p. 700).

We can also interpret (and critique) the present findings through a decolonial lens (Fanon, 1952 (Fanon, /1967)). The present studies suggest that recognition achieves progress toward symbolic decoloniality, and past literature suggests that recognition can move us toward material decoloniality. In other words, the social recognition achieved through racism-conscious framings exhibits some success in increasing solidarity, and literature suggests that racism-conscious EJ movements produce positive material change for exposed communities. But any progress achieved through recognition is fundamentally limited in ways described at length by Fanon (1952 Fanon ( /1967) ) and more recent extensions by Coulthard (2014). These perspectives see recognition (e.g., EJ policy, White people acknowledging systemic racism) as representative of a liberal colonial politics of recognition. Coulthard (2014) proposes: "Although this approach may alter the intensity of some of the effects of colonialcapitalist exploitation and domination, it does little to address their generative structures, in this case a capitalist economy constituted by racial and gender hierarchies and the colonial state… Hence Fanon's claim that the colonized simply go from 'one way of life to another, but not from one life to another'; the structure of domination is modified, but the subject position of the colonized remains unchanged-they become 'emancipated slaves.'" (p. 35-39)

Mere recognition of systemic racism in environmental exposures coupled with limited policy efforts to address disparities may thus accommodate the victims of environmental injustice to the status quo, serving the very interests of culpable parties. Decolonial scholars have extended this to critique Western EJ conceptions more generally, pointing out that the goal of merely redistributing environmental harms reflects a "coloniality of justice" that evades root causes (Álvarez & Coolsaet, 2020). Nonetheless, we see recognition as an essential -if limitedprecondition to radically transformative collective movements that build just and sustainable futures beyond the dictates of racial capitalism.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be noted. The present work could certainly be accused of using White participants as the norm against which people of color are compared. Though Study 1 recruited a diverse sample of Black, Latine, and White participants, we chose to follow up on the observed interaction with only White participants in Study 2. Future work should investigate the implications of class-conscious framings for people of color. We also chose to focus on Black, Latine, and White people in part because these are the three largest racial-ethnic demographic groups according to the US census, and in part because of funding constraints. Though this follows in the footsteps of similar past research (e.g., Ballew et al., 2021; Lies et al., 2025), future work should focus in particular on the perspectives of Native Americans who have faced the brunt of environmental racism in the US.

We also acknowledge that by merely cross sectionally assessing (rather than manipulating or assessing longitudinally) mediators, we cannot necessarily speak to the causal role that these potential mediators play (Pirlott & MacKinnon, 2016). Future work should more rigorously test for mechanisms underlying the observed effects using more adequate designs. Further, the racism-conscious and racism-and-class-conscious conditions in Study 2 differed in meaningful ways beyond mere class or race focus. In particular, the racism-and-class-conscious condition used the term "environmental justice," which may itself be the reason for the reduced effect of racial identity satisfaction on collective action intentions among White participants (especially when compared to the term "environmental racism" used in the racism-conscious condition).

Recognition justice should also ideally be explored from the perspective of EJ communities themselves. Might racism-evasive or class-conscious framings of environmental exposures be experienced as existential misrecognition for EJ communities of color? We know that many EJ communities make explicitly racism-conscious exposure attributions (Muhammad et al., 2018; Schmitt et al., 2022), so such frames may serve important psychological functions for those exposed. Future work should engage with EJ communities to explore the implications of exposure attributions and recognition, ideally using community-based participatory approaches (Davis & Ramírez-Andreotta, 2021; Schmitt & Sullivan, 2022).

Conclusion

Adequately recognizing environmental injustice as rooted in systemic racism comes with costs and benefits. What are EJ communities to do when caught between (1) the desire to adequately rebuke racial capitalism for its structuring role in environmental health disparities, and (2) the desire to build broad coalitions of solidarity across race and class lines, in part by downplaying adequate recognition of injustice to appease colorblind White people? Though it is not for us to suggest the best course of action for any community faced with the brutality of environmental devastation, we may turn to thinkers and activists of contemporary and historical struggles for wisdom on this front. The question of achieving recognition is moot if it is not comingled with radical action (Coulthard, 2014; Fanon, 1952 Fanon, /1967)). Those in power -fossil capitalists, resource extractors, and those tasked with remediating environmental injusticesbenefit from the inaction of the masses in the face of futures made ambiguous and uncertain by pollution (Auyero & Swistun, 2009). Recognition alone may address the violence of environmental injustice primarily on a symbolic level, but for the material conditions to sustainably change, radical collective action is necessary: "[We] must wage [our] war on both levels: Since historically they influence each other, any unilateral liberation is incomplete, and the gravest mistake would be to believe in their automatic interdependence. Besides, such a systematic tendency is contrary to the facts. This will be proved. Reality, for once, requires a total understanding. On the objective level as on the subjective level, a solution has to be supplied" (Fanon, 1952 (Fanon, /1967, p. 13) , p. 13) The important thing is that communities act to achieve EJ and that the rest of us -academics, students, people -act in solidarity and in tandem.
