What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Purpose: Dynamic assessment shows high discriminant validity in classifying children with and without language disorders across languages and task domains, but studies in non-Indo-European languages are rare. We adapted the language subtest of the Predictive Early Assessment of Reading and Language (PEARL) (Petersen & Spencer, 2014) into the Dynamic Assessment of Narrative in Cantonese (DANIC) and examined the classification accuracy of the prototype DANIC tool in a group of Cantonese-speaking children, as an initial validation before implementing the tool in multilingual Cantonese populations.

Method: Sixty-one children participated. Thirty-nine met inclusionary criteria and were included in the final analyses, including 16 with language disorders (LD) and 23 typically developing (TD) peers. Children were administered a hearing screen, the DANIC, which consists of a pre-test, a teaching phase, and a post-test, as well as standardised tests of language and nonverbal IQ.

Results: Pre-test, post-test, and modifiability scores from the DANIC showed significant group differences, but gain scores did not. Logistic regression and Receiver Operating Characteristic analyses identified the overall modifiability rating as the most parsimonious classifier: a rating of 2 or lower on the 0-4 scale identified children with LD with a sensitivity of 93.8% and a specificity of 91.3%. Adding post-test scores raised specificity to 95.7% but did not significantly improve model fit.

The DANIC is efficient and can be administered with high fidelity and scored reliably. Future studies should examine the utility of this tool in assessing multilingual children from Hong Kong and other Cantonese-speaking regions.

Introduction

Cantonese is a major Chinese language with 85-100 million native speakers worldwide and ranked within the top 15-25 most spoken languages in the world (Eberhard et al., 2024). The current study reports on the adaptation of an existing tool of dynamic assessment (DA) into Cantonese and the classification accuracy of this newly adapted tool.

According to Kidd and Garcia (2022), a strong Indo-European bias exists in published articles in the field of child language acquisition research, with 54% of the surveyed articles about the English language, and another 30% about 38 other languages in the Indo-European family. Though DA is frequently featured in recent speech-language pathology literature, a quick search of articles published in the last 10 years identified DA studies featuring Dutch, English, French, German, and Spanish (e.g., Daelman et al., 2025; Hadjadj et al., 2024; Maragkaki & Hessels, 2016; Petersen et al., 2024; Romero et al., 2021), and a lack of representation of languages outside of the Indo-European family. Given this status quo, the current study stands to contribute to the typological diversity of the child language disorder literature. In the following sections, we describe the demographics and assessment practice of Hong Kong, where the current data were collected, summarise the literature on child Cantonese narrative language and DA of narrative language, and introduce our research questions.

Demographics and assessment practice in Hong Kong

Hong Kong is linguistically diverse. The top three most commonly used languages among people aged five years and above are respectively Cantonese (spoken by 93.7% of the population), English (58.7%) and Mandarin (54.2%) (Census and Statistics Department, 2022a). Cantonese is the dominant home language: in a 2021 territory-wide survey of residents aged 6 to 65 years, 92.3% reported Cantonese as the language most often used at home, followed by English (2.1%), Mandarin (2.1%), other Chinese dialects (1.9%), and other languages (1.5%; Census and Statistics Department, 2024). Multilingual competence is nonetheless growing. Between 2018 and 2021, the proportion of residents who rated their spoken English as sufficient for daily use rose from 29.0% to 43.6%, and the corresponding proportion for Mandarin rose from 28.0% to 41.4% (Census and Statistics Department, 2022b). The population of ethnic minority residents who speak a heritage language at home is also increasing; for example, the Pakistani population, many of whom acquire Urdu as their home language, grew by 37% between 2006 and 2016 (Hamdani et al., 2025).

Compared with other age groups, children aged 6-14 years have the lowest proportion using Cantonese as their main home language (89.7%); the remaining 10.3%, or approximately 53,400 children, most often speak English (4.2%), Putonghua (3.1%), other Chinese dialects (0.8%), or other languages (2.3%) at home (Census and Statistics Department, 2024). Comparable figures are not available for children younger than 6 years, the age range covered by the preschool language test described below, because the survey sampled only residents aged 6 to 65 years. Although there are no official prevalence statistics for Hong Kong, a population-based study in England estimated that approximately 10% of children at school entry have a language disorder (LD), either as a primary disorder in the absence of other conditions (i.e., developmental language disorder, DLD; 7.6%) or concomitant with other conditions such as intellectual disability or autism spectrum disorder (2.3%; Norbury et al., 2016). Applying the 10% rate to the 517,500 children aged 6-14 years in Hong Kong (Census and Statistics Department, 2024) suggests that more than 50,000 children in this age group may have LD, and applying the same rate to the approximately 53,400 children whose main home language is not Cantonese suggests that more than 5,000 of these children may have LD. Early and accurate identification of LD in a culturally and linguistically diverse population like that of Hong Kong is therefore an important but challenging endeavour.

There are two locally normed standardised language assessments. The Hong Kong Cantonese Oral Language Assessment Scale (HKCOLAS; T'sou et al., 2006) is the first and only standardised assessment tool of Cantonese oral language abilities with norm reference for children ages five to 12 years. This comprehensive assessment consists of six subtests and takes between 60 to 90 minutes to administer. The Hong Kong Test of Preschool Oral Language (TOPOL; Child Assessment Service, 2020) is a spoken language assessment tool for children ages 2;6 [years;months] to 5;11. With a total of four subtests, the administration time is about 45 to 60 minutes. Although results from the HKCOLAS and the TOPOL are often considered the "gold standard" for diagnosis of LD, in reality, speech-language pathologists (SLPs), especially those working in school settings, do not always have the luxury to administer the complete assessment due to its time-consuming nature. Instead, they rely on informal assessments to evaluate children's strengths and difficulties to design interventions. As such, a brief and diagnostically informative DA measure holds potential for adoption in clinical settings (Wong et al., 2026). Moreover, the HKCOLAS and the TOPOL were normed on primarily Cantonese-speaking children. Using these tools to assess culturally and linguistically diverse children may risk over-diagnosing them as LD.

Narrative language in Cantonese-speaking children

A narrative is an oral account or recount of imaginary or real events (Bruner, 1986).

Narratives offer comprehensive insights into an individual's language development in a contextually relevant manner, encompassing vocabulary, grammar, and social competencies (Botting, 2002; Sheng et al., 2020). Because of the strong predictive relationships between early narrative proficiency and later language and academic outcomes (Justice et al., 2010), evaluating narrative proficiency is a crucial aspect of language assessment. Narrative proficiency is typically assessed on two fronts: macrostructure and microstructure.

Macrostructure refers to the overall organisation of a story, including episodic structure and story grammar components (Stein & Glenn, 1979). Microstructure delves into the finer details of language use, examining elements like lexical items, morphosyntax, and connectives that contribute to forming a cohesive narrative (Gagarina et al., 2016). A prominent framework for analysing story macrostructure by Stein and Glenn (1979) deconstructs stories into elements such as setting, initiating event, internal response, plan, attempt, consequence, and reaction. Across different languages, children with LD are found to narrate stories with shorter length, fewer macrostructure elements, less complex sentences, more syntactic errors, more limited vocabulary, and reduced overall coherence (Boerma et al., 2016; Norbury & Bishop, 2003; Sheng et al., 2023).

Limited research has been conducted on the development of Cantonese narrative skills. Wong et al. (2004) examined narrative retells in 100 typically-developing (TD) Hong Kong Cantonese-speaking children aged 5 to 9 years, focusing on word scores (use of 26 target words from the story), structure scores (use of target grammatical structures such as relative and subordinate clauses), and mean length of communication units. All three measures increased significantly with age; the structure score was the most sensitive, distinguishing all age groups except the 8-and 9-year-olds. To et al. (2010) investigated narrative skills in 1120 Cantonese-speaking school-age children aged 4;10 to 12;1, pinpointing microstructure components that best predicted age through a story retell task.

Key microstructure elements such as syntactic complexity, semantic score, referencing, and connective use were assessed, revealing significant age-related differences in narrative proficiency. Regression analyses indicated that vocabulary and syntactic complexity were the primary predictors of grade level. Moreover, syntactic complexity showed the largest differences between TD and LD groups. More recently, Lai et al. (2026) examined narrative production in 25 school-aged children with DLD (M = 9;7) and 25 age-matched TD peers (M = 9;8), all predominantly monolingual Cantonese speakers, using the Cantonese version of the Multilingual Assessment Instrument for Narratives (MAIN) in story retelling and storytelling tasks. Children with DLD scored significantly lower than their TD peers on macrostructure (story structure and story complexity) and microstructure (syntactic complexity and grammaticality) measures, whereas lexical diversity, mean length of utterance, productivity, and internal state terms did not differ between groups. Diagnostic accuracy, however, was limited: story structure and syntactic complexity reached acceptable sensitivity (91.7% and 83.3%, respectively) but low specificity (41.7% and 70.8%), and story complexity and grammaticality showed marginal sensitivity and specificity (both 70.8%).

The authors therefore recommended using narrative measures in combination with other assessments. This small body of literature highlights the importance of including both macrostructure and microstructure measures in a DA of narrative abilities, and suggests that static narrative measures alone may not classify individual children with sufficient accuracy.

Dynamic assessment of narrative language

It is well-known that standardised norm-referenced measures, while necessary and informative, are not appropriate to be the sole basis of diagnostic decision making. In culturally and linguistically diverse populations, static standardised measures are especially problematic because they are built on the premise that individuals of the same age have had the same opportunities for learning. However, multilingual children's learning opportunities are typically distributed across different languages and cultures, diverging from those assumed by the test norms. DA has evolved in response to this particular limitation of static measures by measuring an individual's learning potential rather than the end product of learning (Feuerstein et al., 1980; Peña et al., 2001; Vygotsky, 1986). Two recent review articles (Hunt et al., 2022; Orellana et al., 2019) have reported generally positive outcomes in the effectiveness of various DAs (e.g., word learning, morphological rule learning, word labelling) in identifying LD in bilingual and multilingual children.

Several studies have specifically focused on narratives as the context for DA (e.g., Hadjadj et al., 2024; Henderson et al., 2018; Kramer et al., 2009; Kuchler et al., 2026; Peña et al., 2014; Petersen et al., 2017 Petersen et al., , 2024)). Kramer et al. (2009) examined whether the Dynamic Assessment and Intervention (DAI), a tool focusing on narrative ability (Miller et al., 2001), could accurately distinguish English-speaking third-grade Native Canadian Indian children who had possible language learning disorders (n = 5) from their TD peers (n = 12). Peña et al. (2014)also used the DAI with 18 Spanish-English bilingual children with LD and two samples of TD controls, with one sample well-matched to the LD group and the other sample matched on fewer variables (mean ages = 68-70 months). In both Kramer et al. (2009) and Peña et al. (2014), the pre-test, two teaching sessions, and post-test took place over a 7-14day span, with the total duration of DA lasting >70 minutes. The teaching sessions followed mediated learning principles and involved structured steps and teaching strategies as specified in the DAI manual. Though both studies involved bilingual children, the DA was conducted solely in English. The DAI showed a classification accuracy of 94% in Kramer et al. (2009). In Peña et al. (2014), classification accuracy was 89% and 81%, respectively, for analyses that included the two TD control samples.

While effective, the DAI took a considerable amount of time to administer, transcribe, and score, which may have prevented its wide adoption by clinicians who do not have the luxury of time. Cognizant of this barrier, Petersen and colleagues (Petersen & Spencer, 2014; Petersen et al., 2017; 2024) developed more time-efficient DA tools and supported the validity of these tools with empirical evidence. Petersen et al. (2017) administered a single 25-minute session consisting of a pre-test, a teaching phase in which the examiner cycled up to four times through a brief set of structured teaching steps, modifiability ratings, and a post-test in their sample of 42 kindergarten-to-third-grade Spanish-English bilinguals (10 LD and 32 TD). Scoring of pre-and post-test was completed in real time within the 25-minute session without the need to transcribe the samples. Petersen et al. (2017) reported that clinician ratings of students' modifiability after a single DA session achieved 100% sensitivity and 88% specificity. Petersen et al. (2024) Most recently, Kuchler et al. (2026) developed the Narrative Dynamic Assessment Task (NDAT), a test-teach-retest narrative DA designed with attention to cultural appropriateness for multilingual children. The NDAT uses story stimuli from the MAIN (Gagarina et al., 2016) and the modifiability rating scale of Petersen et al. (2017); in the Teach phase, the examiner uses graduated prompts to teach two story grammar elements that the child omitted at Test. The NDAT was administered in English to thirty 5-to 6-year-old multilingual children in the United States (mean age = 5.58 years), most of whom spoke Spanish at home. Examiners administered the NDAT with 100% fidelity in an average of less than 17 minutes, and children's scores improved significantly, with a medium effect size, on the taught story elements but not on untaught elements or on total scores. Interscorer reliability was good for total story and modifiability scores but poor for some individual elements. Because only two children in the sample had reported language concerns, the classification accuracy of the NDAT has yet to be examined.

The current study

In light of recent developments of highly-concentrated and streamlined protocols of DA using narrative as the context, we selected the PEARL-L (Petersen & Spencer, 2014) for adaptation into Cantonese because of its strong psychometric properties and clinician-friendly features such as time-efficiency and structured intervention approach. Moreover, pilot work by Petersen and colleagues suggested that this tool can be readily adapted into languages such as Mandarin (Cheung, 2022) and Spanish (Romero et al., 2021) and retain its key psychometric properties. In the current study, we took a series of steps to complete the crosslinguistic adaptation of the PEARL-L into the Dynamic Assessment of Narrative in Cantonese (DANIC). To validate the DANIC, we tested five-and six-year-old Cantonesespeaking children with and without LD, for three reasons. First, in Hong Kong, first grade usually commences between the ages of 5;8 and 6;7. The five-to-six age range therefore represents a transition point at which accurate identification of LD is critical for timely support, particularly because early narrative proficiency predicts later language and academic outcomes (Justice et al., 2010). Second, this age range is consistent with the intended age that the PEARL was designed to cover (Petersen & Spencer, 2014). Third, Cantonese narrative studies of children with LD have examined school-age children across a wide age range (To et al., 2010) or older school-age children (Lai et al., 2026), and Lai et al. (2026) For question 1, we evaluated four variables derived from the DANIC and conducted group comparisons using simple t-tests or its nonparametric equivalent. These variables include a measure of static performance (i.e., the pre-test score) and three conventional indicators of learning: post-test scores and gain scores, which focus on learning outcomes and are motivated by the Vygotsky principle of zone of proximal development (Vygotsky, 1978 (Vygotsky, , 1986)); and modifiability ratings, which focus on the child's response to instruction and follow Feuerstein's mediated learning experiences approach (Feuerstein et al., 1991). Petersen et al. (2017) also found that a novel indicator, the duration of the first teaching cycle, contributed to classification accuracy; we could not examine this indicator because the teaching procedure was revised midway through data collection (see Fidelity and reliability).

Based on systematic reviews reporting that pre-test to post-test gain scores generally did not differentiate children with and without LD, whereas clinician judgements of modifiability consistently did (Hunt et al., 2022; Orellana et al., 2019), we predicted that group differences would be seen on all but the gain score variable, and that modifiability ratings would show the largest difference between the two groups.

All of the variables that reached significance under question 1 would be entered into regression models to find the best DANIC indicators for distinguishing TD and LD cohorts.

Classification accuracy would be evaluated as sensitivity (how well the measure correctly identifies children with LD), specificity (how well the measure correctly identifies children who have typical language), positive likelihood ratio (LR+, a confidence index of truly having a disorder) and negative likelihood ratio (LR-, a confidence index of not having a disorder) of each indicator (Battaglia et al., 2002; Straus et al., 2005). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves would be generated to empirically derive the best cut-point for each measure. We predicted that the DANIC would yield measures that could accurately identify children with and without LD with good accuracy (e.g., 80% or higher).

Method Participants

This study was approved by the PolyU Institutional Review Board (reference number: HSEARS20211011008). Participants were recruited through clinician referrals, word of mouth, and flyers posted at the university clinic. Interested families scanned a QR code to fill out some basic information. Research assistants then contacted the families to do an initial screening and invited the families to participate if the children passed the initial screening.

Sixty-one children were enrolled in the study with their primary caregiver signing a consent form and the children giving their verbal assent.

Children were included in the final analysis if they met the following inclusionary and exclusionary criteria. To be included in either the TD or the LD group, children must 1) have normal hearing, normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and no physical impairments; 2) use Cantonese as their primary language (daily use > 60%); and 3) have a nonverbal IQ score of 70 or higher. For the LD group, children must meet two additional criteria: having obtained a standard score of 6 or lower on at least two subtests of the HKCOLAS (T'sou et al., 2006), following the recommendation of the test developer; and having evidence of functional impact of LD either manifested as parental concerns on language skills or current enrolment or history of enrolment in speech therapy services. For the TD group, children must meet two additional criteria: having a standard score of lower than 7 on no more than one subtest of the HKCOLAS; and having no parental concerns on language skills and no current enrolment or history of enrolment in speech therapy services. Co-occurring attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or dyslexia did not exclude a child from the LD group, in line with the CATALISE consensus on DLD (Bishop et al., 2017), whereas children with ADHD or any other developmental diagnosis were not eligible for the TD group.

Four children were excluded because Cantonese was not their primary language. One child was excluded because of a cleft palate and lips. Two children were excluded from the TD group because of a diagnosis of ADHD. Twelve children were excluded because they had an autism diagnosis but scored well on the HKCOLAS, thus did not meet criteria for LD.

Three children were excluded because they did not fulfil both the functional impact and language test score requirements at the same time. In the end, a total of 16 children (5 girls, 11 boys) met criteria for the LD group, including 10 children with DLD and 6 children with high-functioning autism and language disorder, and 23 children (12 girls, 11 boys) met the criteria for TD. One child in the LD group (a boy aged 5;5) was included as an exception to the test score criterion. He scored below the cutoff on only one HKCOLAS subtest (Narrative Retell = 4); his scaled scores on the remaining subtests were within the typical range (Cantonese Grammar = 10, Textual Comprehension = 9, Word Definition = 7, Expressive Nominal Vocabulary = 7; Lexical-Semantic Relations was not administered because of his age). Materials

DANIC Adaptation.

The first and the last authors' teams worked closely in adapting the PEARL-L to Cantonese, following recommendations of culturally and linguistically appropriate practice in adapting test materials (Peña, 2007; Zhang et al., 2021). A joint team of six members, including the first author, the last author, and four graduate students, who are native speakers of Cantonese, met four times over the course of three months to review the PEARL materials, select stories for adaptation, translate the English stories into Cantonese together sentence by sentence, and decide on the final stories to be used. Three stories were selected and underwent multiple rounds of revisions with regard to clarity, language complexity and cultural appropriateness. Feedback was sought from two clinicians who specialise in local paediatric populations, and pilot testing was completed with two children of the targeted age range to provide further input on the materials. The two final selected stories (the Mud story and the Toy story) probe the same narrative macrostructure and microstructure elements. Appendix 1 shows a sample story script for the mud story. Like the PEARL-L, the DANIC consists of three distinct phases: a pre-test, a teaching phase, and a post-test. The implementation of the DANIC took approximately 10 minutes, depending on the participant's responsiveness.

Pre-test. In the pre-test, children listened to a short story without any pictorial support and were then asked to retell the story. Retell was scored online using three scoring schemes.

The story macrostructure scoring scheme assesses plot elements including character, setting, problem (a.k.a. initiating event), feeling, attempt, consequence, ending, and end feeling. A maximum of 2 points could be attained for each element, giving a subtotal score of 16. Using the sample story in Appendix 1 as an example, an element scored 2 when it was retold completely and specifically, such as naming the main character (Ho Ho) or describing the problem in full (fell into a mud puddle). An element scored 1 when it was mentioned but was incomplete or imprecise, such as referring to the character with a pronoun (he), giving only part of the problem (fell), or describing a feeling with a related but less specific word (cried rather than very angry). An element scored 0 when it was omitted. The second scoring scheme was language complexity, which assesses the use of subordinating conjunctions such as causal, temporal, and adversative connectives. Each production of conjunction was worth 1 point, and the maximum score was 7. The third scheme rates the story episode complexity by considering the groupings of story grammar elements. If a child scored 2 points on the problem, attempt, consequence, and ending, the child would gain the highest episode complexity score of 5. Together, the pre-test had a maximum score of 28 points. The examiner politely interrupted and provided prompts if the child missed any of the elements, starting with a first-level prompt, i.e., an open-ended question, e.g., "故事人物係邊個?" [who is the character in the story?], to prompt the child to mention the missed macrostructure element. If the child could answer the question accurately, the examiner would administer over-correction and ask the child to start over at the beginning of the story or the macrostructure element before the one missed. If the child could not answer the question accurately, the examiner would provide a second-level prompt, a direct model, and ask the client to repeat it, e.g., "故事人物係浩浩。 放學之後， 媽媽帶浩浩去公園玩。 你講一次" [Ho Ho is the story character. After school, Mummy took Ho Ho to play in the park. Now you say it], followed by the over-correction procedure.

Modifiability rating.

Immediately after the teaching phase, the examiner completed the modifiability rating scale (see Appendix 2). This scale was adapted from the PEARL-L using translation and back-translation, as well as replacement of anchor point descriptions using more culturally appropriate examples. This 5-point scale prompts the examiner to note down and score the child's behaviours in terms of the amount and level of prompts the child needed to retell the stories (prompt), the participant's comfort level or frustration with the task (confidence), behavioural challenges and attention during teaching (disruption), and how quickly the participant retells the story and complete the teaching phase (rate), before giving an overall rating of the child's modifiability (learning). A score of 4 represented relative ease in learning while a 0 represented difficulty with learning. During pilot testing, it was noted that the examples of high confidence behaviours in the original rating scale may be culturally non-transferable. To be specific, the original English description said "When students appear to be happy with their responses, smile, or say "I like this" or "This is easy," these are examples of high confidence behaviours." As these overt verbal and nonverbal behaviours almost never occurred in the current sample, these were replaced with "When students show no signs of avoidance or hesitation, this item should be rated as high."

Post-test. The administration and scoring for the post-test were identical to the pretest, except that it was with a new story. The examiner again scored the retell performance online using the scoring schemes. The two stories were used in counterbalanced order: if the Toy story was used in the pre-test and the teaching phase, the Mud story would be used in the post-test, and vice versa.

HKCOLAS

The six subtests from the HKCOLAS were administered according to the test manual The Primary Test of Nonverbal Intelligence (PTONI, Ehrler & McGhee, 2008) The PTONI provides a nonverbal measure of cognitive abilities including visual and spatial perception, analogical thinking, and sequential reasoning of the participants. Each participant was presented with a sequence of images on each page within the picture book and had to select the image that did not fit with the rest.

Procedures

Children were brought to the university clinic by their primary caregivers. After signing consent, the primary caregivers filled out a case history questionnaire collecting information about the children's health and development history, language use, and family background. Children first underwent a hearing screening through a pure tone audiometry in a quiet clinic room. The participants' hearing on the left and right was tested with four frequencies (0.5, 1, 2 and 4kHz) at 35dB respectively. Stimulus duration was 2 seconds and the inter-stimulus duration was randomised to prevent participants from guessing. Passing three or more frequencies in either ear indicated the absence of hearing impairments and fulfilment of the eligibility criteria. Next, the DANIC was administered, followed by the HKCOLAS and the PTONI. This task order was to ensure that the examiner did not have any bias or a priori opinion of the child's language skills before administering the DANIC. The examiners were blinded to the diagnostic status of the participants during administration of the test protocols.

Fidelity and reliability

Five Master's students in SLP from two consecutive years (2022 and 2023) acted as examiners. All students were in the final year of their Master's programme. The three students from the 2022 cohort received a total of three hours of training on the DANIC from the last author's research team. The two students from the 2023 cohort received a total of three hours of training conducted by the 2022 cohort. The training involved presentation on the background and components of DANIC, demonstration, hands-on practice of test administration and scoring, and calibration of scoring through discussions. During the transition of the two cohorts, the student clinicians in the first cohort reflected that the repeated retelling of the same story and the over-correction procedure may have caused some participants to become frustrated. Therefore, the 2023 cohort examiners were not required to use over-corrections but still needed to go through the multiple rounds of retelling. We will return to these points in the discussion. Audio-recordings of first two sessions from each examiner were checked for the fidelity of administration of the DANIC using a 30-item (for 2022) or a 27-item (for 2023, taking out the over-correction procedure in the three retells) checklist. Fidelity ranged from 90% to 100%. The missed items primarily consisted of omission of the overcorrection procedures in the 2022 cohort. Examiners received feedback about their fidelity in the initial sessions.

The pre-test, post-test, and modifiability ratings were rescored from audio-recordings by an independent and blinded rater (the third author). Following Kuchler et al. (2026), interscorer reliability was evaluated with point-to-point agreement and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Point-to-point agreement was calculated as the number of matching scores divided by the total number of scores. Data analysis 487

The reliability analyses based on the polychoric correlation reported Cronbach' s α of .83 for the ten pre-test items (i.e., character, setting, problem, feeling, attempt, consequence, ending, end feeling, language complexity, story episode complexity), .78 for the ten post-test items, and .91 for the five modifiability items (i.e., prompt, confidence, disruptions, rate and learning). It suggests adequate to high reliability across sub-items (Adeniran, 2019). For subsequent analysis, we used total scores for pre-and post-tests, as well as overall modifiability ratings. No outliers were excluded, as none fell more than 3 standard deviations (SD) below the mean. Diagnosis status was binary-coded (0 for TD, 1 for LD) for subsequent analyses. Analyses proceeded in three steps, namely group comparisons (Research Question 1), selection of the best-fitting combination of indicators (Research Question 2), and ROC analysis of the selected model, and were performed in R version R 4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026).

To address the first research question, statistical differences between TD and LD groups were analysed for pre-test and post-test sum scores, gain scores, and overall modifiability ratings. Independent t-tests were used for continuous data (pre-test, post-test and gain scores), ensuring homogeneity of variance and normality of residuals. For count data (modifiability ratings), the Mann-Whitney U test was applied, with two-tailed Exact Sig. reported. Different types of effect size metrics obtained in both tests were interpreted using Metsämuuronen' s (2024) refined thresholds.

For the second research question, we used stepwise logistic regression models to find the best DANIC indicators for distinguishing TD and LD cohorts (Ge et al., 2024).

Significant indicators identified in the previous group analysis were added in a forward step, including pre-test, post-test sum scores and overall modifiability ratings. We used three criteria to select a reduced set of indicators to establish the best performing function: 1) likelihood-ratio tests comparing nested models; 2) lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC, Osuji et al., 2016); 3) ensuring statistical significance of all variables in the model. When these criteria disagreed, the more parsimonious model was retained. Models were fitted by maximum likelihood with the glm function (binomial family) in R, and nested models were compared with the anova function.

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the selected model and, for comparison, of the model combining posttest sum scores and overall modifiability ratings, in distinguishing children with and without LD. Predicted logit scores from the best-performing logistic model were used as the test variable. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed using: 1) the area under the curve (AUC); 2) sensitivity and specificity; 3) positive (LR+) and negative (LR-) likelihood ratios, where a larger LR+ (sensitivity / (1 -specificity)) and a smaller LR-((1 -sensitivity) / specificity) indicate better differentiation (Dollaghan, 2007); and (4) the optimal cutoff point, determined by the maximum Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity -1; Youden, 1950).

Classification metrics were calculated using the reportROC version 3.6 R package (Du & Hao, 2025).

Discussion

The current study was motivated by two practical clinical needs in the Hong Kong Hamdani et al., 2025). An alternative efficient approach to identifying young students for future language difficulty is needed, which prompted us to adapt the PEARL-L, an English narrative DA tool, into the DANIC, and validate it in the current study.

In this initial validation study, the DANIC differentiated Cantonese-speaking children with and without LD with high accuracy. A single overall modifiability rating, obtained from a DANIC session of approximately 10 minutes, identified children with LD with a sensitivity of 93.8% and a specificity of 91.3%. Its negative likelihood ratio (LR-= 0.07) met the benchmark for ruling out LD with confidence, and its positive likelihood ratio (LR+ = 10.78) was very informative (Dollaghan, 2007; Plante & Vance, 1994). Below, we discuss the findings for each research question, their clinical implications, and the limitations of the study.

Group differences in DANIC indicators

Consistent with our predictions, children with LD scored significantly lower than their TD peers on the pre-test, the post-test, and the modifiability rating, but not on gain scores.

The pattern of effect sizes is informative. The group difference at pre-test was already large (d = 0.94), indicating that a brief static retell captures some of the narrative weakness that characterises LD across languages (Boerma et al., 2016; Lai et al., 2026; Norbury & Bishop, 2003). After the teaching phase, the difference nearly doubled (d = 1.71), because TD children benefited more from brief instruction than children with LD did. The modifiability rating, which reflects how readily a child responds to instruction rather than what the child already knows, showed the largest effect according to Metsämuuronen's (2024) thresholds (r = .91). This ordering mirrors findings with English-speaking and bilingual children (Henderson et al., 2018; Petersen et al., 2017) and is consistent with the conclusion of systematic reviews that clinician judgements of modifiability are among the most robust DA indicators (Hunt et al., 2022; Orellana et al., 2019).

Gain scores did not differ significantly between the groups, although TD children gained more on average and the effect was medium-sized (d = 0.59). This result replicates a consistent finding in the DA literature (Orellana et al., 2019; Peña et al., 2014; Petersen et al., 2017). Difference scores compound the measurement error of two tests and vary widely across individuals. In addition, both groups improved on average after the teaching phase, which narrows the difference in gains. Although the group difference in gains might reach significance in a larger sample, its modest size suggests that gain scores add little diagnostic information beyond post-test scores and modifiability ratings.

Classification accuracy of the most parsimonious model

For the second research question, the most parsimonious model contained the overall modifiability rating alone. The resulting classification accuracy (AUC = .95; sensitivity = 93.8%; specificity = 91.3%) compares favourably with that of English narrative DA tools used with bilingual and culturally diverse children (Henderson et al., 2018; Petersen et al., 2017 Petersen et al., , 2024) ) and with that of the DAI (Kramer et al., 2009; Peña et al., 2014). It is also higher than the accuracy of static narrative measures in Cantonese-speaking children with DLD: in Lai et al. (2026), the most sensitive static measures had a specificity of 70.8% or lower. In other words, a brief teaching episode and a structured observation of the child's response to it reduced false positives, the type of error that leads to over-identification of children who may lack experience with the task. That a single modifiability rating carried the diagnostic weight is consistent with Petersen et al. (2017), in which the overall modifiability rating was the best classifier, and with Henderson et al. (2018), in which modifiability scores alone classified all children correctly. It is also noteworthy because the ratings were made by student clinicians with only three hours of training, yet showed good interscorer reliability (ICC = .78). Adding post-test scores raised specificity from 91.3% to 95.7%, reducing the number of misclassified TD children from two to one, but this improvement was not statistically significant in our small sample. Product indicators such as post-test scores may therefore still provide complementary evidence (Hunt et al., 2022), a possibility that should be tested in a larger sample.

These results extend the evidence base for narrative DA to a non-Indo-European, isolating language. The PEARL-L targets story grammar and subordinating conjunctions.

Story grammar is relatively independent of the language being assessed, and Cantonese, like English, marks causal, temporal, and adversative relations with overt connectives that can be taught and scored in real time. The successful adaptation suggests that the core architecture of the PEARL-L, namely a model story, a teaching phase with graduated prompts and faded visual support, and a parallel post-test, transfers well across typologically distinct languages, consistent with pilot work in Mandarin and Spanish (Cheung, 2022; Romero et al., 2021). In addition, our LD group included children with DLD and children with autism and language disorder, suggesting that the DANIC may be useful for identifying language disorder whether it occurs in isolation or alongside autism, although the two subgroups were too small to be analysed separately.

Scoring reliability was good to excellent for the two indicators that carried the diagnostic decisions in this study, the total story score (ICC = .90) and the overall modifiability rating (ICC = .78). These values are similar to those reported by Kuchler et al. (2026) for the NDAT (total story score, ICC = .80; examiner's overall judgement of modifiability, ICC = .74) and suggest that the DANIC can be scored reliably in real time after brief training. As in Kuchler et al. (2026), however, reliability varied across individual elements and was poor for language complexity. Language complexity is a count of subordinating conjunctions, which are short and easily missed when a retell is scored in real time. Item-level scores, such as those used to select intervention targets, should therefore be interpreted with caution until scoring guidelines for these elements are refined. interval of the AUC ranged from .90 to 1.00. Second, the cutoff was derived and evaluated in the same sample, which tends to overestimate classification accuracy. Cross-validation in a larger, independent sample is needed before the cutoff is applied clinically (see Romero et al., 2021, for an example). Third, only children who clearly met criteria for either LD or TD were analysed. Children with borderline profiles, who are the most difficult to classify in practice, were therefore excluded, which may have inflated classification accuracy (Hunt et al., 2022).

In addition, because the selected model relies on a single 5-point rating, classification depends on examiners applying the scale consistently: a one-point difference at the cutoff changes the classification. Fourth, the teaching procedure was modified midway through data collection: the over-correction procedure was dropped for the 2023 cohort because some children appeared frustrated by repeated retelling. This change may have altered the children's experience of the teaching phase and their modifiability ratings, and it prevented us from analysing teaching duration, an indicator that contributed to classification accuracy in Petersen et al. (2017). The sample was too small to compare the two procedures, and future studies should adopt the revised procedure consistently. Fifth, modifiability ratings are shaped by culturally specific expectations of how children behave in a learning task. As mentioned in the procedures, we had to modify the confidence rating descriptions given how children behaved in the current study. Future research needs to be cognizant of the generalisability of the rating scale to multilingual Cantonese-speaking children. Sixth, interscorer reliability was moderate for several story elements and poor for language complexity, and exact agreement on modifiability ratings was modest. Scoring guidelines and training examples for these items should be refined. Finally, and most importantly, participants were primarily Cantonesespeaking, so the validity of the DANIC for multilingual children, its intended target population, remains to be established.

Future studies may apply the DANIC in other Cantonese-speaking regions, for example, Macau, where Cantonese is the primary language but there is a lack of standardised normative language measures created for the local population (Sheng et al., 2023). In addition, a considerable proportion of Hong Kong children use a language other than Cantonese at home, including English, Mandarin, other Chinese varieties, and heritage languages such as Urdu (Census and Statistics Department, 2024; Hamdani et al., 2025).

Children who use these non-mainstream languages at home may receive limited Cantonese input and perform poorly on standardised measures of Cantonese. Future investigations should examine the utility of the DANIC in identifying these multilingual children. Future studies should also examine the predictive validity of the DANIC, for example whether DANIC results predict later language and literacy outcomes or response to intervention, and extend the tool to younger children.

Conclusion

This study adapted the PEARL-L into Cantonese and provides initial evidence that the resulting tool, the DANIC, accurately differentiates five-and six-year-old Cantonesespeaking children with and without LD. A single overall modifiability rating, obtained in a session of about 10 minutes, identified children with LD with a sensitivity of 93.8% and a specificity of 91.3%, and the tool was administered with high fidelity and scored reliably by student clinicians. These findings extend the evidence for narrative DA to a non-Indo-European language and lay the groundwork for validating the DANIC with multilingual children in Hong Kong and other Cantonese-speaking regions.

Implications for clinical practice

The DANIC has several features that make it attractive for clinical use in Hong Kong.

First, it takes about 10 minutes to administer and is scored in real time without transcription, compared with 60 to 90 minutes for the HKCOLAS and 45 to 60 minutes for the TOPOL.

SLPs working in schools could therefore use it as a first-tier tool to decide which children require a full standardised assessment, or to supplement standardised results when these are inconclusive (Wong et al., 2026). Second, the decision rule is simple: a child whose overall modifiability rating is 2 or lower is classified as at risk of LD (Appendix 3), so no calculation is required. Third, the DANIC can be learned quickly: student clinicians achieved high fidelity, and good reliability for the total story score and the overall modifiability rating, after three hours of training, and the teaching phase follows a scripted procedure with graduated prompts. Fourth, the teaching phase directly informs intervention planning, because it reveals which story grammar elements and connectives a child can learn with support. The clinical acceptability of the DANIC from the perspective of practising clinicians is examined in a companion study (Wong et al., 2026). Nevertheless, in line with international consensus that a diagnosis of DLD should rest on converging evidence (Bishop et al., 2017), the DANIC should be interpreted together with case history, evidence of functional impact, and other assessment data rather than used as a stand-alone diagnostic test.

Because the DANIC evaluates how a child learns rather than what the child has already learned, it holds particular promise for children whose home language is not Cantonese, for whom the HKCOLAS and the TOPOL may underestimate language ability. This promise, however, remains to be tested, as discussed below.
