What we found

We found 12 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Groupitizing refers to the ability to enumerate items faster when they are organized in small groups.

This phenomenon may involve mental arithmetic in adults and likely reflects conceptual understanding of how numbers can be decomposed. This study investigated the development of groupitizing in children from kindergarten to second grade (N=98, ages 5-8, 54 girls) and what it reveals about their underlying representations of numbers. Grouping structures were manipulated to compare different numerical decompositions of the same numerosity (e.g.,6=2×3 vs.6=3+2+1) and to examine whether children could exploit them. Response times and error patterns showed that kindergartners could already groupitize and recruit multiplicative representations, especially for the most regular decompositions that maximized the number of equal-sized subsets. By first grade, these benefits extended to less regular decompositions. The overall pattern of groupitizing performance and error distributions remained remarkably similar across grades, and individual differences in groupitizing were specifically associated with arithmetic, but not counting, abilities. Taken together, these findings show that groupitizing emerges before formal schooling and is refined as children acquire an increasingly broad repertoire of numerical decompositions. The results support the theory that a language-of-thought combining additive and multiplicative expressions underlies conceptual development in the number domain.

[div-01]

One of the central challenges of numerical cognition is understanding how humans acquire and represent exact number concepts. Numbers are not only mentally represented as magnitudes (e.g., Siegler & Opfter, 2003; Sullivan & Barner, 2014; Gennari et al., 2023; Kutter et al, 2018), but also as discrete entities that can be decomposed and recombined in multiple ways (Pica et al., 2004; Shepard et al., 1975; Dehaene, 2011; Dehaene et al., 2025). Such knowledge of number composition plays a central role in arithmetic, allowing different numerical expressions to refer to the same quantity (e.g.,6 = 2 ×3 = 3 ×2 = 3 +3 = 5 + 1). Yet, although a number's different decompositions are mathematically equivalent, they may not be cognitively equivalent.

While the origins of number concepts are still debated (e.g., Carey & Barner, 2019; Spelke, 2017; Guerrero & Park, 2023; Halberda & Feigenson, 2008), a recent syntax-of-sets hypothesis (Dehaene et al., 2025) proposes that to understand a number is to acquire one or several mental expressions that relate this number to other numbers using addition and multiplication (e.g.,8 = 4 ×2 = 7 +1 = 5 + 3). This proposal builds on language-of-thought theories, according to which humans learn abstract knowledge by combining symbols into structured mental programs (Goodman et al., 2014; Tenenbaum et al., 2011; Yang & Piantadosi, 2022; Fodor, 1975). It extends these ideas by proposing a shared language-of-thought for geometry and arithmetic, which provides intuitive foundations for how numbers can be mentally represented as expressions of other numbers through additive and multiplicative operations. Under this view, each number can be associated with its shortest mental expression, called its Minimum Description Length (MDL). Numbers with simpler MDLs are hypothesized to be easier to learn and manipulate. If numbers are encoded through structured mental representations, then the enumeration of sets that are perceptually organized to prime such structures should reveal whether and how these representations are recruited during numerical processing.

One experimental paradigm examines how observers exploit grouping cues during rapid enumeration. Historically, psychophysical investigation of exact enumeration has largely relied on arrays in which items were randomly distributed, thereby limiting the availability of perceptual grouping cues (e.g., Folk et al., 1988; Li et al., 2010; Gray & Reeve, 2014; see Wender & Rothkegel, 2000, for an early exception) and revealing only a major subdivision between fast subitizing of small quantities, and slow serial counting of larger ones (Jensen et al., 1950; Mandler & Shebo, 1982; Piazza et al., 2002; Revkin et al., 2008). Specifically, subitizing refers to the prompt and accurate identification of small numerosities, typically up to three or four items (Kaufman et al., 1949; Burr et al., 2010). Beyond this range, enumeration of randomly organized sets relies on serial counting, which consists of sequentially enumerating items one by one (Mandler & Shebo, 1982). This strategy leads to response times that increase approximately linearly with array size, resulting in the well-established set-size effect (Trick & Pylyshyn, 1994). McCandliss and colleagues (2010) introduced a task in which participants had to enumerate either grouped or ungrouped collections of dots. Participants enumerated arrays more rapidly when they were grouped into small subsets containing no more than four items. This phenomenon, termed "groupitizing", refers to the ability to capitalize on grouping cues to improve the speed and efficiency of exact enumeration. Subsequent studies demonstrated that groupitizing cannot be solely explained by more efficient serial counting strategies, such as faster counting, counting on, or skip counting (Starkey & McCandliss, 2014; Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020), and also showed that grouping influences numerosity estimation (Anobile et al., 2020; Maldonado Moscoso et al., 2020).

In adults, recent studies have shown that groupitizing involves both subitizing, in order to assess the cardinal values of subsets (e.g., Maldonado Moscoso et al., 2020; Wege et al., 2022; Maldonado Moscoso et al., 2025), and mental arithmetic, in order to compute the total numerosity of the array (Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020; Maldonado Moscoso et al., 2022). More specifically, Ciccione and Dehaene (2020) proposed that groupitizing relies on mental multiplication whenever grouped arrays can be represented as a set of equal subsets. Consistent with this hypothesis, they found that groupitizing benefits were maximized when grouping afforded multiplicative decompositions.

Arrays divided into equal subsets (e.g., 9 as 3 groups of 3 dots) were enumerated faster than arrays divided into unequal subsets (e.g., 9 as groups of 4, 3 and 2 dots), particularly when the repeated subsets shared an identical spatial arrangement. Likewise, for prime numerosities, decompositions maximizing the number of equal subsets (e.g., 11 as 3 groups of 3 and one group of 2 dots) yielded greater enumeration benefits than when subsets were mostly unequal (e.g., 11 as one group of 4, one group of 3 and 2 groups of 2 dots). Futhermore, groupitizing benefits also varied across multiplicative decompositions of the same numerosity (e.g., 6 presented as 2 groups of 3 vs. 3 groups of 2), with decompositions into a smaller number of groups enumerated faster, thus paralleling the operand order effect in symbolic multiplication (Aiken & Williams, 1973; Zimmerman et al., 2016).

Error analysis (Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020) additionally showed that participants produced more multiplicative errors during groupitizing, such as slips in the multiplication table (e.g., answering 6 instead of 9). Overall, these findings suggest that groupitizing depends not only on the presence of grouping cues, but also on the numerical structure of grouped collections.

In children, the first developmental study of groupitizing (Starkey & McCandliss, 2014) found no evidence of groupitizing in kindergarten, whereas a grouping advantage was observed in first grade and continued to increase through third grade. According to the authors, groupitizing does not emerge until first grade because younger children, such as kindergarteners, lack the conceptual understanding of how numbers can be combined and decomposed into smaller sets. Moreover, they found that groupitizing was positively associated with symbolic arithmetic skills, consistent with the view that a shared knowledge of number (de)composition contributes to both groupitizing and arithmetic tasks (Starkey & McCandliss 2014; Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020). Extending these findings, Guillaume et al. (2023) showed that groupitizing continues to expand from third to eighth grade and uniquely predicts mathematical achievement beyond socio-economic, domain-general and domain-specific measures. Together, these studies highlight the central role of number composition knowledge in mathematical development and show that groupitizing can be taken as a simple behavioral measure of non-symbolic arithmetic and a predictor of later mathematical achievement.

Despite these advances, groupitizing has not been directly used to investigate young children's mental representations of numbers. If it reflects children's knowledge of number composition, then the precise pattern of groupitizing performance should also provide insights into how numbers are internally represented and manipulated during development. It was recently found that arrays divided into 2 subgroups, rather than 3, yielded greater groupitizing benefits in children from third to eighth grade. Moreover, arrays containing subgroups of 4 rather than 3 dots also showed greater benefits from fifth grade onward, a result interpreted as reflecting an increasing reliance on arithmetic knowledge during development (Guillaume et al., 2023). Crucially, however, the effects of individual numbers (and their decompositions) on groupitizing were not directly investigated: analyses focused on the overall effects of grouping across numerosities, which makes it hard to evaluate how specific configurations are internally represented across development. The present study tried to fill this and other gaps in the literature by addressing three complementary research questions.

First, can groupitizing already be found in kindergarten, thus revealing a knowledge of number composition before formal schooling? Contrary to previous developmental findings, we hypothesized that groupitizing should already be observable in kindergarteners and reflect children's conceptual understanding of numerical compositionality. This prediction follows from recent theoretical work, which argues that understanding the compositional structure of numerical concepts can precede the full mastery of arithmetic procedures, the counting list or the successor function (Dehaene et al., 2025; Guerrero & Park, 2023; Halberda & Feigenson, 2008). If so, groupitizing should emerge as soon as children acquire sufficiently structured representations, such as representations of small numbers as sets composed of smaller sets, rather than only after formal schooling. Consistent with this prediction, a growing body of developmental evidence suggests that children possess conceptual understanding of sets of sets, recursive patterns, and intuitions of multiplicative relations from the earliest stages of development (e.g., Cheung & Dale, 2016; Wynn et al., 2002; Zosh et al., 2011; Halberda & Feigenson, 2008; Ciccione et al., 2026). For example, 14month-old infants failed to represent a collection of four objects when they were presented as a single set but successfully represented this same collection when it was organized into two subsets of two (Feigenson & Halberda, 2004).

Second, which numerical decompositions are easier for children to represent and exploit during groupitizing? If children as young as kindergarteners groupitize, then their groupitizing performance should already be constrained by how they mentally represent numbers. Specifically, numerical decompositions with a shorter "minimal description length" (MDL) in a language combining + and × operations should yield greater groupitizing benefits than others, as their associated mental representations are hypothesized to be easier to acquire and manipulate (Dehaene et al., 2025). Accordingly, we predicted larger groupitizing benefits when numbers can be grouped into sets of sets (e.g., 6 as 2 groups of 3 dots), rather than unequal subsets (e.g., 6 as groups of 3, 2 and 1 dots). Similarly, for prime numbers that cannot be expressed as a single multiplication, we expected greater groupitizing benefits for decompositions maximizing the number of equal subsets (e.g., 5 as 2 groups of 2 and 1 dots) rather than for decompositions involving mostly unequal subsets (e.g., 5 as groups of 3 and 2 dots). To test these predictions, we systematically compared alternative decompositions of a given numerosity. Note that without such an experimental manipulation, the enumeration advantages observed across different grouping structures, whether multiplicative or not, might be attributed to mere perceptual chunking. To further evaluate the mental multiplication account of groupitizing performance, we also examined whether children, as previously observed in adults by Ciccione and Dehaene (2020), were more likely to make multiplicative errors when enumerating grouped rather than ungrouped arrays.

Third, how does groupitizing reflect the development of number composition knowledge during the first years of arithmetic learning? From kindergarten through the first years of elementary school, children undergo major changes in their numerical knowledge with the start of formal schooling. We expected children's sensitivity to different numerical decompositions to become increasingly refined with arithmetic learning, while some signatures of intuitive arithmetic structures would already be evident in kindergarten.

Method Participants

We tested 103 children, with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Participants' age and declared gender were recorded. Information on participants' race or ethnicity was not collected due to French data protection regulations. Three distinct groups of children were recruited: kindergarten, first grade and second grade pupils. They all came from public schools attended predominantly by children from middle-class families in Paris, France. Invitations to participate in the study were sent by e-mail to the directors of identified schools. After their acceptance, recruitment letters were sent to the legal guardians of the children. All children with guardian consent participated in the study.

The recruitment and experimental procedure were approved by the ethical committe of Université Paris-Saclay, under the code CER-Paris-Saclay-2025-001. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time, without giving any justification. One participant in kindergarten did not complete the experiment and was therefore excluded from data analyses. Out of 102 remaining participants, 98 met the inclusion criteria for measurement of vocal onset (see "Measurement of vocal onset and exclusion of data" section below) and were thus included in analyses. As a result, we analyzed data from 98 participants (54 girls, age: 6.45 ± 0.93 years), including 29 children in kindergarten (16 girls, age: 5.52 ± 0.51 years), 37 children in first grade (22 girls, age: 6.27 ± 0.45 years) and 32 children in second grade (16 girls, age: 7.50 ± 0.51 years).

Experimental procedure

Data were collected in May 2025, during the spring semester of the academic year. Each participant attended a single session (lasting around 15 minutes). Children were tested individually in a quiet room outside the classroom. While seated in front of a laptop, they completed three tasks, with short breaks in between: (i) a main groupitizing task, (ii) a counting task, and (iii) an arithmetic task. The experimenter was seated next to the participant throughout the session and guided them through the experimental procedure. No feedback was provided on task performance, except for general encouragement independent from actual accuracy.

Groupitizing task

Stimuli

Similarly to previous studies (e.g., Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020; Guillaume et al., 2023; Starkey & McCandliss, 2014), stimuli consisted of black dots with a diameter of 3 millimeters presented on a white background. The arrays contained between 3 and 8 dots. Arrays of 3 dots were identical across all conditions and served as filler trials. Arrays of 4 to 8 dots were constructed according to the following four experimental conditions (see Figure 1 for examples of the stimuli), inspired by Ciccione & Dehaene (2020):1) "No groups" condition: dots were randomly located without being divided into subgroups, such that no explicit decomposition was visually emphasized.

2) "Maximally different groups" condition: dots were divided into subgroups that differed in size, such that the underlying decomposition contained as few identical addends as possible (4 = 3 + 1;5 = 3 + 2;6 = 3 + 2 + 1;7 = 3 + 2 + 1 + 1;8 = 3 + 3 + 2). This condition was designed to select decompositions with unequal subsets that minimized regularity, thereby favoring addition over multiplication.

3) "Minimally different groups, different shape" condition: dots were arranged into subgroups of equal size, with each subgroup forming a distinct shape; for prime numbers 5 and 7, two subsets had the same size (4 = 2 + 2;5 = 2 + 2 + 1;6 = 3 + 3;7 = 3 + 3 + 1;8 = 2 + 2 + 2 + 2). This condition was designed to promote decompositions that maximized equal-sized subsets, thereby encouraging addition and/or multiplication. 4) "Minimally different groups, same shape" condition: dots were also divided into subgroups of equal size as in the previous condition, but all equally sized subgroups shared an identical shape. This condition capitalized on both numerical and perceptual regularity to maximize opportunities for exploiting repeated subgroups and multiplication strategies, thus yielding the shortest MDL for a given number.

Note that in all grouped conditions, every subgroup contained at most three dots, thus remaining within the well-established subitizing range in children (Jansen et al., 2014; Paliwal & Baroody, 2020) and reducing the potential for counting within subgroups. Since the number of subgroups necessarily varied from one numerosity to another, we ensured that the total number of subgroups, summed across numerosities 4 to 8, was identical in each grouped condition (14 subgroups in total). Within each numerosity, the total surface occupied by dots was controlled across the three grouped conditions. In the "no groups" condition, two versions were created. Half of the stimuli matched the grouped conditions in terms of total surface, whereas the other half matched them in terms of the minimum distance between dots (see Figure 1).

Each numerosity was presented three times in each condition. For each numerosity, stimulus sets were generated independently using a custom Python program. Each stimulus set contained one stimulus for every experimental condition. Dot locations were generated randomly while satisfying distance constraints within and between subgroups. Because generating valid stimulus sets under these constraints required many iterations, 20 stimulus sets were generated before data collection.

For each participant and numerosity, three distinct stimulus sets were then randomly sampled without replacement to avoid presenting the exact same stimuli multiple times, which could have led to learning effects.

Procedure

Participants were asked to say aloud the number of dots as quickly as possible, in response to the question "How many dots are there?". Experimenters were explicitly instructed not to use the words "count" or "group" when introducing the task. The task consisted of 4 blocks of 18 trials each, for a total of 72 experimental trials. Each block corresponded to one experimental condition. Each trial began with a central fixation cross presented for 800 ms, followed by the presentation of the dot array. The array remained on screen until the response was given. Experimenters pressed the spacebar to advance to the next trial once the participant had responded verbally. To allow precise measurement of vocal response times, every trial was audio-recorded. Recording began when the stimulus appeared on screen and ended 1 s after the experimenter pressed the spacebar. To provide a safety margin for the audio recording, a 2-s blank screen was inserted before the experimenter could enter the participant's response. Once the response had been entered, a further 2-s inter-trial interval preceded the following trial. For each participant, both the order of the blocks and the order of the stimuli within each block were randomized. To ensure that participants understood the instructions (e.g., responding with the number alone, without adding irrelevant words), the task began with 4 practice trials. The same 4 practice stimuli (arrays of 3 to 6 dots presented in a randomized order) were used for all participants.

Measurement of vocal onset and exclusion of data

The recording of each trial was reviewed by a research assistant, who listened to the audio, manually inspected its spectrogram and defined the onset. To avoid experimental bias, the assistant was blind to both the experimental condition and the target numerosity. Of the 7 344 vocal responses recorded, 6 891 (93.83 %) yielded a valid measurement of vocal onset. The remaining 453 vocal responses (6.17 %) were excluded and their response times coded as NA. Specifically, 134 vocal responses (1.83 %) were excluded because participants changed their answer, whereas 319 (4.34 %) were excluded because the experimental procedure was not correctly followed (e.g., the participant talked about something unrelated, sneezed or did not provide the response as instructed, such as saying "Easy peasy! It's 4."). In addition, 4 participants produced invalid vocal-onset measurements on at least 30% of trials and were excluded from analyses. After vocal-onset measurements had been extracted, response times below 0.2 s or above 20 s were excluded. No response times fell below the lower threshold, whereas only one response time exceeded 20 s and was thus removed.

Analysis

Response times for correct responses and error rates were analyzed. Since arrays of 3 dots fell within the subitizing range and were identical across experimental conditions, analyses were restricted to array sizes ranging from 4 to 8 dots. When participants did not provide a scorable response (e.g., because the spacebar was mistakenly pressed before they responded), accuracy was coded as NA rather than incorrect, as these trials reflected missing data rather than task errors (0.26% of all trials). Response times were coded as NA when participants changed their response, because the recorded vocal onset no longer corresponded to a unique response. In these cases, the first response was retained for accuracy analyses because it reflected participants' initial spontaneous enumeration before any self-correction (1.83% of all trials).

Response times were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with condition, grade, array size and their interactions as fixed effects, and participant as a random intercept. The significance of fixed effects was assessed using Type III analyses of variance with Satterthwaite's approximation for degrees of freedom. When significant main effects or interactions were detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were performed using Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons. Additionally, to characterize how response times increased with array size at the individual level, a response-time slope was computed for each participant in each condition (for a similar approach, see Starkey & McCandliss, 2014). Each slope was estimated by fitting a simple linear regression with response time for correct responses as the dependent variable and array size from 3 to 8 dots as the independent variable. Arrays of 3 dots were included both to capture the transition from subitizing to larger numerosities and to increase the number of observations available for each individual regression. The resulting regression coefficient (i.e., the slope) quantified the increase in response time associated with each additional dot, thereby providing an individual measure of the set-size effect (Trick & Pylyshyn, 1994) within each condition. Lower slopes indicate a smaller increase in response time as array size increases and thus a smaller set-size effect. Because groupitizing is characterized by a reduction in such effect (Starkey & McCandliss, 2014; Guillaume et al., 2023; Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020), slopes in grouped conditions provide an individual estimate of groupitizing efficiency.

Error rates were analyzed with a generalized linear mixed-effects model with a binomial error distribution and logit link function, using the same model structure as for response times. Fixed effects were assessed using Type III Wald chi-square tests, followed by Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means when appropriate.

Arithmetic task

To assess participants' arithmetic knowledge in an age-appropriate manner, experimenters presented 9 arithmetic questions orally, in a random order: 2 + 2, 3 + 3, 4 + 4, 3 + 2, 4 + 3, 6 + 2, 3 + 1, 4 + 1 and 6 + 1. These questions were specifically selected to mirror the numerical decompositions elicited by the three grouped conditions in the groupitizing task. For each response, experimenters recorded the accuracy (correct or incorrect), as well as the total time required to complete the arithmetic task. Results

Groupitizing is already evident in kindergarten and expands across grades

Consistent with the well-established set-size effect (Trick & Pylyshyn, 1994), response times increased with additional dots, yielding a significant main effect of array size (Table 1). A main effect of grade was also found, indicating progressively faster enumeration across grades. Crucially, we observed a main effect of condition, together with significant condition × grade and condition × array size interactions (Figure 2A). Post-hoc Tukey tests revealed distinct developmental patterns of grouping effects. In kindergarten, response times did not differ between the "no groups" and "maximally different groups" conditions (p = .42), whereas both "minimally different groups" conditions yielded faster responses than the "no groups" condition (all p < .001). Responses were fastest in the "minimally different groups, same shape" condition, which differed significantly from all other conditions (all p ≤ .001). In contrast, for first-and second-grade participants the advantage of "minimally different groups, same shape" over "minimally different groups, different shape" conditions was not significant (p = .47 and p = .36 respectively). Unlike kindergarteners, they were also faster in the "maximally different groups" than in the "no groups" condition (both p < .001).

Taken together, these findings show that groupitizing can already be observed in kindergarten children when asked to enumerate collection of dots. However, this enumeration advantage was initially restricted to the conditions with the most regular decompositions. Only by first grade did the advantage extend to all grouped conditions, consistent with children progressively benefiting from a growing repertoire of numerical decompositions. Furthermore, the additional benefit of identical subgroup shapes was significant only in kindergarten, suggesting that perceptual similarity may facilitate the processing of multiple subgroups presented simultaneously in younger children.

An alternative explanation for these findings is that the grouping advantage reflects learning over the course of the task, as participants become progressively familiar with grouped displays even in the absence of performance feedback. To test this possibility, we examined response times on the first trial encountered in each condition (Figure 2B). Only first trials involving arrays of 4-8 dots were considered, as arrays of 3 dots were identical across conditions. Of these 332 trials, 289 were answered correctly (87.05%), and 267 (80.42%) had valid response times and were retained for analyses. Response times were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with condition and array size as fixed effects, and participant as a random intercept. Type III analyses of variance revealed significant main effects of condition (F(3, 196.83) = 17.06, p < .001) and array size, (F(4, 250.85) = 33.34, p < .001). Post-hoc Tukey comparisons showed that both "minimally different groups" conditions yielded faster responses than the "no groups" condition (different shape: p = .0039; same shape: p < .0001), whereas the "maximally different groups" condition did not differ from the "no groups" condition (p = .39). The "minimally different groups, same shape" condition was the fastest overall, differing significantly from all other conditions (all p ≤ .004; see Figure 2B). Additional analyses examining condition, grade and their interaction while controlling for array size confirmed that children responded faster in the "minimally different groups, same shape" condition than in the "no groups" condition across all three grades (all p ≤ .002; see Supplementary Figure 1). Notably, for kindergarten children, the "minimally different groups, same shape" condition yielded faster responses than all other conditions on the first trial (all p ≤ .027). Taken together, these findings indicate that groupitizing is already evident from the very first presentation of a regularly organized grouped array, even in kindergartners.

Groupitizing benefits increase with the regularity of numerical decompositions

The significant condition × array size interaction further indicated that the magnitude of groupitizing benefits varied across numerosities. Importantly, this interaction was not moderated by grade, as the condition × array size × grade interaction was not significant (Table 1). As already illustrated in Figure 2A, groupitizing benefits generally increased with numerosity and largely followed a gradient across grouped conditions, with the smallest benefits observed in the "maximally different groups" condition and the largest in the "minimally different groups, same shape" condition. Post-hoc Tukey comparisons indicated that for numerosities 4, 6, 7 and 8, the two "minimally different groups" conditions yielded significantly larger benefits than the "maximally different groups" condition (all p < .001). By contrast, the three grouped conditions did not differ for numerosity 5 (all p > .76). In addition, the "minimally different groups, same shape" condition yielded larger benefits than "minimally different groups, different shape" only for numerosities 6 (p = .021) and 7 (p = .011).

To quantify the magnitude of these effects, we expressed groupitizing benefits as the percentage reduction in mean response time relative to the "no groups" condition (i.e., ,𝑅𝑇 no groups -𝑅𝑇 grouped . /𝑅𝑇 no groups × 100, computed separately for each participant, array size, and grouped condition). This analysis confirmed that the largest benefits were observed for the most regular decompositions. They were particularly pronounced for numerosities 6 and 8, with the largest effect observed for numerosity 6 (see Supplementary Figure 2). For example, the "minimally different groups, same shape" condition reduced response times by approximately 48% for numerosity 6 and 31% for numerosity 8 relative to the "no groups" condition. In comparison, the "maximally different groups" condition reduced response times by only 5% and 6% for numerosities 6 and 8, respectively. Overall, these findings show that groupitizing benefits increased with the regularity of numerical decomposition, with the largest benefits observed when numerosities could be divided into equal-sized subsets (e.g., 6 as 2 groups of 3 dots instead of groups of 3, 2, and 1 dots).

Groupitizing reflects arithmetic knowledge from kindergarten onward

Previous work has linked groupitizing to arithmetic performance in children (Starkey & McCandliss, 2014; Guillaume et al., 2023), suggesting that groupitizing relies on arithmetic representations (Starkey & McCandliss, 2014; Guillaume et al., 2023; Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020; Dehaene et al., 2025). If groupitizing indeed involves mental arithmetic, this relation should already be observable at the earliest stages of arithmetic learning in kindergarten. Because arithmetic knowledge develops rapidly during the first school years, we also examined how this relation evolved across grades. Participants were divided into arithmetic-performance quintiles within each grade based on their CRPM (Correct Responses Per Minute) arithmetic score. Figure 3A illustrates response-time curves across array sizes for each arithmetic-performance quintile. In kindergarten, children with higher arithmetic performance showed non-monotonic and flatter response-time curves in the two "minimally different groups" conditions. In both "minimally different groups" conditions, differences between arithmetic quintiles also became increasingly pronounced as array size increased. By contrast, response times increased more linearly with array size for all arithmetic quintiles in the "no groups" condition, while only modest differences between arithmetic quintiles emerged in the "maximally different groups" condition. The same pattern was observed in first grade, with even flatter response-time curves among children with higher arithmetic performance in both "minimally different groups" conditions. In contrast, second-grade children showed a similar qualitative pattern but little differentiation according to arithmetic performance, with largely overlapping response-time curves across all conditions.

To formally test these descriptive patterns, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model predicting response times from condition, array size, arithmetic performance quintile, grade, and all interactions as fixed effects, and participant as a random intercept. Type III analysis of variance revealed a main effect of arithmetic performance quintile (F(4, 82.9) = 16.44, p < .001), as well as significant interactions for condition × arithmetic performance quintile (F(12, 4702.7) = 3.66, p < .001), array size × arithmetic performance quintile (F(16, 4702.0) = 2.50, p < .001) and condition × array size × arithmetic performance quintile (F(48, 4700.8) = 2.12, p < .001). These interactions were further moderated by grade, as reflected in significant interactions for condition × arithmetic performance quintile × grade (F(24, 4702.4) = 2.44, p < .001), array size × arithmetic performance quintile × grade (F(32, 4701.8) = 2.48, p < .001), and condition × array size × arithmetic performance quintile × grade (F(96, 4700.6) = 1.34, p = .015). Importantly, the three-way condition × array size × grade interaction was not significant (F(24, 4700.7) = 0.83, p = .700), indicating that grade alone did not affect the basic pattern of grouping effects across array sizes. In other words, developmental differences in groupitizing emerged most clearly when children's arithmetic performance was taken into account, independently from their grade.

For each condition and grade, we computed Pearson correlations between individual arithmetic performance, assessed again using the CRPM arithmetic score, and individual responsetime slopes across array sizes (Figure 3B). In kindergarten, arithmetic performance was unrelated to response-time slopes in the "no groups" and "maximally different groups" conditions but was significantly associated with both "minimally different groups" conditions. In both conditions, children with higher arithmetic performance showed better groupitizing efficiency, as indicated by lower response-time slopes. By first grade, significant associations extended to all grouped conditions, suggesting that children exploit a broader repertoire of numerical decompositions. In contrast, no significant associations were observed in second grade. To assess the specificity of this association, we repeated the analyses using counting performance instead of arithmetic performance.

No significant associations were found between counting performance and response-time slopes in any condition or grade (see Supplementary Figure 3). Taken together, these findings suggest that groupitizing specifically capitalizes on arithmetic knowledge rather than on counting abilities from kindergarten onward. Moreover, the association between arithmetic performance and groupitizing extends from the most regular to a broader range of numerical decompositions during the first year of formal schooling.

Error patterns are more strongly biased toward multiplicative responses during groupitizing

We next analyzed error rates to determine whether the grouping advantage observed in response times came at the expense of accuracy (Figure 4A). Response accuracy was influenced by grade (χ²(2) = 6.67, p = .036) and array size (χ²(4) = 17.83, p = .001), but not by condition (χ²(3) = 3.20, p = .362). Neither the grade × condition nor the condition × array size interaction was significant (both ps > .20), whereas a significant grade × condition × array size interaction was observed (χ²(24) = 43.42, p = .009). Taken together, these findings indicate that the response-time benefits associated with grouping were not accompanied by a reduction in overall accuracy.

Accuracy remained high across conditions, ranging from 87% for the "no groups" condition to 92% for the "minimally different groups, same shape" condition.

We therefore examined whether grouping influenced the type of errors (Figure 4B). Incorrect responses were classified into three mutually exclusive categories following a classification similar to that used by Ciccione and Dehaene (2020): multiplicative errors, off-by-one errors, and other errors. Multiplicative errors included responses corresponding either to a factor of the correct numerosity (e.g., responding 3 instead of 6; responding 4 instead of 8), or to slips to adjacent multiplication facts (e.g., responding 9 instead of 6 when presented 2 groups of 3; responding 6 or 10 instead of 8 when presented with 4 groups of 2). Off-by-one errors differed from the correct response by one, and all remaining incorrect responses were classified as other errors. One extreme response (20 for a target numerosity of 4) was excluded from the analyses.

Across all conditions, most errors were off-by-one responses, with children typically reporting numerosities differing from the target by ±1. However, multiplicative errors were more frequent in the two "minimally different groups" conditions than in the "no groups" and "maximally different groups" conditions, particularly for larger numerosities (Figure 4B). Following Ciccione and Dehaene (2020), statistical analyses were restricted to array sizes that could be decomposed into equal-sized subgroups (4, 6, and 8), providing the clearest test of multiplicative errors. We fitted a generalized linear mixed-effects model predicting whether an incorrect response was multiplicative rather than another type of error, with condition, grade, and their interaction as fixed effects, and participant as a random intercept. Array size was included as a covariate to account for potential differences across numerosities. Type III Wald chi-square tests revealed a significant effect of condition (χ²(3) = 14.50, p = .002), after controlling for array size (χ²(2) = 7.83, p = .020). Neither grade (χ²(2) = 0.90, p = .637), nor the condition × grade interaction (χ²(6) = 1.80, p = .937), were significant. Post-hoc Tukey comparisons indicated that multiplicative errors were significantly more frequent in both "minimally different groups" conditions than in the "no groups" (all p ≤ .0017) and "maximally different groups" (all p < .0001) conditions. No significant differences were observed between the two "minimally different groups" conditions (p = .888) or between the "no groups" and "maximally different groups" conditions (p = .601). Together, these findings indicate that equalgrouped displays specifically increased the likelihood of multiplicative errors. Importantly, this was already observed in kindergarten, suggesting once again that regular decompositions evoke multiplicative representations before formal multiplication instruction.

Discussion

Groupitizing reflects structured numerical representations from kindergarten

A central goal of the present study was to use groupitizing as a window onto children's early numerical representations. As hypothesized, our findings indicate that kindergarten children already rely on structured numerical representations during enumeration, well before formal schooling.

Importantly, kindergarteners showed a groupitizing advantage only for arrays organized into mostly equal-sized subgroups, but not for arrays organized into mostly unequal subgroups. This finding argues against an explanation of groupitizing in terms of perceptual chunking alone in kindergarten: indeed, if merely dividing arrays into smaller subsets facilitated enumeration (e.g., by reducing memory demands or helping children keeping track of counted items), all grouped conditions should have produced comparable benefits. Instead, only the most regular numerical decompositions facilitated enumeration. Perhaps the cleareast illustration comes from arrays of 8 dots, which could be organized either as 4 groups of 2 dots, or as 2 groups of 3 and 1 group of 2 dots. Although the first decomposition contains more groups, children were faster in enumerating the corresponding sets, suggesting that groupitizing depends primarily on the efficiency of the numerical decomposition rather than on the number of perceivable groups. This interpretation is further supported by the correlational analyses: in kindergarten, both conditions with multiplicative decompositions were associated with arithmetic performance, whereas the condition with purely additive decompositions was not. Counting performance was also unrelated to any grouping condition, further suggesting that items were not simply counted more efficiently when grouped. Taken together, our findings suggest that kindergarten children already possess structured numerical representations that they exploit to facilitate enumeration.

Analyses stratified by arithmetic performance provide further evidence that, from kindergarten onward, groupitizing already relies on arithmetic representations rather than mere counting. As illustrated in Figure 3A, kindergarten children with the highest arithmetic performance showed a shallow increase in response times across array sizes in the groupitized condition with "minimally different groups, same shape", which facilitated access to multiplicative knowledge.

Despite the addition of five extra dots from 3 to 8, response times increased by only about 1.17 s (compared to 4.05 s for unstructured sets). Moreover, the response-time curve was no longer monotonic, but showed local decreases from 5 to 6 and from 7 to 8. Such response-time patterns are difficult to reconcile with a serial counting strategy. Instead, these findings suggest that, for these children, increasing numerosity no longer entails a proportional increase in enumeration time; rather, what matters is whether familiar numerical decompositions can be used. This marked attenuation, and in some cases disappearance, of the canonical set-size effect is widely considered a behavioral hallmark of groupitizing (Starkey & McCandliss, 2014; Guillaume et al., 2023; Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020), and supports the view that children exploit arithmetic shortcuts, rather than serial counting, to determine the total numerosity. Importantly, this general response-time pattern was not limited to the highest-performing children. There was a clear gradient across arithmetic quintiles in both kindergarten and first grade (Figure 3A). While small reversals of the response-time curve were observed across all arithmetic levels, children with stronger arithmetic performance showed progressively flatter response-time curves, whereas those with weaker arithmetic performance exhibited steeper set-size effects. This arithmetic gradient is consistent with the hypothesis that the magnitude of the groupitizing benefit scales with children's arithmetic knowledge (Dehaene et al., 2025; Starkey & McCandliss, 2014).

Although we replicated Starkey & McCandliss's (2014) finding that groupitizing continues to develop during the first years of formal schooling, our observation of an early groupitizing ability in kindergarten is novel and may at first appear inconsistent with theirs. Rather than reflecting a genuine contradiction, however, these differences may provide important insights into the conditions under which groupitizing develops. One likely explanation concerns the numerical decompositions afforded by the stimuli. Starkey and McCandliss (2014) focused their analysis on arrays of 5, 6 and 7 dots only, whereas we included here arrays from 4 to 8 dots, in order to allow comparisons between the even numerosities 4, 6 and 8 (which can be arranged into equal subgroups) and the prime numerosities 5 and 7 (which cannot). Crucially, the two studies also used different decompositions for numerosity 6. While Starkey and McCandliss (2014) systematically presented arrays of 6 dots as 3 groups of 2, the present study presented them as 2 groups of 3, which is likely easier (Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020; Aiken & Williams, 1973; Zimmerman et al., 2016). As further discussed below, not all decompositions are equally effective in supporting groupitizing, particularly in kindergarten: minimal description length counts.

A second, non-mutually exclusive explanation concerns children's mathematical learning experiences. Although our kindergarten participants were on average younger than those tested by Starkey and McCandliss (2014), likely due to differences in school-entry cutoff dates between France and the US, they were assessed later in the year. The authors collected their data during the fall semester (September to November), whereas the present study was conducted during the spring semester in May. Furthermore, in France, preschool and especially kindergarten are increasingly considered as participating in early schooling and may therefore involve a greater amount of explicit instruction than in the US. Thus, despite being younger, our participants had benefited from several additional months of opportunities to learn about numbers and arithmetic over the course of the kindergarten year. Taken together, these considerations suggest that the developmental trajectory of groupitizing is better understood as the progressive acquisition of new numerical decompositions through mathematical learning, rather than as a qualitatively new enumeration strategy emerging only with formal schooling. Future studies directly comparing children in different educational systems or assessed at different stages of the school year could help clarify which aspects of early mathematical experience contribute to the development of groupitizing.

Groupitizing reveals that regular numerical decompositions are easier for children to represent and exploit

Beyond showing that kindergarteners can groupitize, the present findings also indicate that numerical decompositions are not equally easy for children to exploit during groupitizing. Consistent with findings in adults (Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020), groupitizing benefits generally followed the same gradient across grouped conditions, with greater savings when numerosities were arranged into more equal-sized subsets rather than into unequal ones.

Based on language-of-thought accounts, according to which early numerical representations are based on multiplicative and additive structures (Dehaene et al., 2025), arranging the same numerosity into equal-sized subgroups (e.g., 4 as 2 groups of 2, 6 as 2 groups of 3 and 8 as 4 groups of 2) was predicted to yield the largest groupitizing benefits, because the corresponding numerosity can be represented through a shorter and more efficient minimum description length (e.g., 3 × 3 being shorter than 4 + 2 + 1). Our response times findings confirm these predictions and suggest that children exploited repeated subgroups through multiplicative representations or recursive representations of sets of sets. The pattern of errors also changed and was more biased toward multiplicative responses when numerosites were divided into equal subsets. Both response times and error analyses thus provide converging evidence suggesting that intuitions of multiplication already underlie groupitizing in kindergarten. This interpretation is consistent with developmental studies showing that young children possess an understanding of sets of sets long before formal multiplication knowlege or instruction (e.g., Feigenson & Halberda, 2004; Wynn et al., 2002; Zosh et al., 2011; Halberda & Feigenson, 2008; Cheung & Dale, 2016). Our findings extend this literature by suggesting that a simple enumeration task can already recruit multiplicative representations in young children and provide developmental support for the mental multiplication account of groupitizing (Ciccione and Dehaene, 2020).

Interestingly, numerical regularity appeared to contribute more to groupitizing than perceptual regularity: indeed, only arrays of 6 and 7 significantly benefited from groups having the same spatial configuration, suggesting that identical subgroup shapes provide, at most, an additional benefit beyond an already regular numerical decomposition. Likewise, in adults, the advantage of identical subgroup shapes was mainly observed for larger arrays (9 dots and above; Ciccione & Dehaene, 2020). To better understand the multiplicative representations underlying groupitizing, future studies could assess whether this additional perceptual advantage primarily reflects the numerical range tested, the mastery of increasingly complex numerical decompositions, or both.

Moreover, while our findings provide preliminary evidence that groupitizing can recruit multiplicative representations in children, the present study did not compare multiplicative decompositions of the same numerosity, such as 2 groups of 3 vs. 3 groups of 2 for 6, and 4 groups of 2 vs. 2 groups of 4 for 8. In the present design, 8 was represented as 4 groups of 2 dots in order to remain within the well-established subitizing range of young children (Jansen et al., 2014; Paliwal & Baroody, 2020) and to control for the number of subgroups across grouped conditions. Future studies could directly compare multiplicative decompositions of the same numerosity, which are not necessarily cognitively equivalent. Analogously to the order effect in mental multiplication with Arabic digits (Aiken & Williams, 1973; Zimmerman et al., 2016), we would expect greater groupitizing benefits for multiplicative decompositions involving fewer groups (e.g.,6 = 2 groups of 3 rather than 3 groups of 2;8 = 2 groups of 4 rather than 4 groups of 2).

As for prime numbers such as 5 and 7, whose groupings necessarily combined multiplicative and additive components, the results differed. Numerosity 7 followed the same gradient across grouped conditions as non-prime numerosities. Numerosity 5, however, revealed a developmental change (Figure 2A). In kindergarten, groupitizing benefits followed the same gradient observed for other numerosities. Yet by first grade, all decompositions of 5 yielded comparable enumeration savings, suggesting that children had become equally able to exploit these different expressions of 5. One possible explanation is that numerosity 5 occupies a privileged place in early mathematical learning, by being the number of fingers in one's hand and a major foundation for base-10 representation. This may explain why children progressively master a broader repertoire of its decompositions, including purely additive ones such as 3 + 2, than for other larger numerosities.

Future studies could investigate whether this privileged status of numerosity 5 explains its distinct developmental trajectory.

Groupitizing as a window onto the development of compositional representations of numbers

Finally, we sought to investigate how patterns of groupitizing performance develop across the first years of schooling, a period in which arithmetic knowledge and associated numerical representations expand rapidly with the beginning of formal instruction in mathematics. Surprisingly, however, the qualitative pattern of groupitizing performance remained remarkably similar across grades. Most response-time and error patterns were already evident in kindergarten and became more pronounced by first grade, suggesting that the simplest multiplicative expressions are available early in development and persist across the first years of elementary school. Nonetheless, a more nuanced developmental pattern emerged once children's arithmetic performance was considered. In kindergarten, children with the lowest arithmetic performance did not show a groupitizing advantage. Better arithmetic was only predictive of performance with the more regular grouped displays, which also yielded the largest groupitizing benefits. By first grade, the association between groupitizing and arithmetic performance extended to all grouped displays. By contrast, it was no longer observable in second grade (Figure 3B), and response-time curves largely overlapped across arithmetic-performance quintiles in every grouped condition (Figure 3A). This developmental pattern does not necessarily imply that arithmetic representations no longer contribute to groupitizing in second grade. A likelier explanation is that the task had become too easy for second-grade children, who all possessed equivalent groupitizing abilities for this range of small numbers. A closer inspection of Figure 3B reveals that the weakest second-grade children and the strongest kindergarten children achieved comparable arithmetic performance. Most secondgraders had likely mastered the simplest numerical decompositions presented in this study, whether multiplicative or additive. If so, grouped arrays ranging from 4 to 8 dots may no longer have been sufficiently demanding to reveal meaningful individual differences in groupitizing among secondgrade participants. Nevertheless, Figure 3A indicates that groupitizing performance in the highest and lowest arithmetic quintiles began to diverge as array size increased in the "minimally different groups, same shape" condition. Future studies could therefore extend our paradigm by using larger numerosities and more complex multiplicative decompositions (e.g.,9 = 3 × 3;12 = 3 × 4;15 = 3 × 5,20 = 4 × 5), which may better reveal individual differences across schooling. Longitudinal studies will also be needed to determine how changes in children's groupitizing performance track the development of arithmetic knowledge and numerical representations over time.

Conclusion and pedagogical implications

Overall, our findings show that groupitizing may serve as a simple behavioral tool for studying children's mental representations of numbers and provide insights into the development of exact number concepts. In support of theories proposing a key role for number composition knowledge (Dehaene et al., 2025; Guerrero & Park, 2023; Halberda & Feigenson, 2008), our results indicate that groupitizing, even in kindergarteners, goes beyond perceptual chunking and reflects a conceptual understanding of how numbers combine through multiplicative and additive operations.

Interestingly, we found no evidence of an association between groupitizing and counting performance, consistent with the hypothesis that compositional representations of numbers may develop partly independently of verbal counting knowledge. Furthermore, our results provide developmental evidence supporting two distinctive features of the syntax-of-sets hypothesis (Dehaene et al., 2025).

First, the hypothesis predicts that numbers with a shorter minimum description length (MDL) in a language-of-thought combining + and × operations should be easier to conceptualize and manipulate. Response-time patterns corroborated this prediction and suggested that the ease of processing a number, early on in development, does not depend solely on its magnitude, but also on its MDL. Multiplicative decompositions may allow children to enumerate some larger numbers more readily than some smaller ones (e.g., 8 as 4 groups of 2 before 7 as 2 groups of 3 plus 1). Prime numbers, lacking a simple decomposition, may be more difficult to acquire and exploit fluently, thus leading to larger numerical concepts (such as 8) being mastered earlier than smaller ones (such as 7).

Second, our findings offer yet a different kind of illustration of the early links between the arithmetic and visuo-spatial domains (Siegler & Opfer, 2003; Dehaene et al., 2009), thereby providing further support for the hypothesis of a shared language-of-thought for geometry and arithmetic (Dehaene et al., 2025). Our results indicate that kindergarteners can quickly exploit the organization of subgroups of dots presented simultaneously, especially when subgroups shared the same geometrical shape (e.g., two identical lines of 2 dots, two identical triangles of 3 dots).

These findings could have important implications for early mathematics education. In particular, the use of grouping cues may help children rapidly identify which decompositions are associated to which numbers, with additive configurations possibly needing further explicit instruction. More generally, teaching children to arrange sets of objects into subgroups reflecting different numerical decompositions, with clear spatial organization, could support the development of increasingly rich and flexible representations of numerical concepts and strengthen their links with arithmetic and geometry.
