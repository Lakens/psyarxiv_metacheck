Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

From throwing dice to reading tea leaves, humans across diverse cultures, eras, and geographies rely on decision aids that produce randomness. What purpose do these aids serve, and why do cultures create them with such regularity? In this Perspective, we unify these seemingly disparate tools as randomization devices, and argue that they culturally evolved to solve two recurrent problems, one cognitive and one social. Specifically, randomization devices produce strong, unpredictable randomness, whereas human cognition does not; and randomization devices externalize decisions in ways that create a social buffer between decision-makers and outcomes. The design of such devices reflects these constraints, even in relation to unique mechanistic features. We focus on one such property -causal opacity -as an important feature of randomization devices that subserves their function and arises in diverse literatures spanning cultural evolution, computer science, and political philosophy. Taken together, we argue that the widespread recurrence of randomization devices reflects widespread cognitive constraints and social demands.

1. Introduction

It's Election Day, 400 B.C., and a new cohort of public officials is set to take office in Athens. Instead of checking a box or writing a name, citizens partake in an unusual ritual: They approach a large marble slab -a kleroterion -and insert a bronze token into one of its rows of slots. When the time comes, citizens watch as a simple mechanism of falling dice decides, row by row, who will take office. A white die; they are chosen. A black die; they are not.

Thousands of miles away in Northern Canada, a Naskapi oracle holds the shoulder bone of a caribou over hot coals and carefully observes how the bone cracks in the heat. Hunters believe this pattern of cracks provides a map, guiding them in the direction of live caribou.

On the complete opposite side of the planet, a Chinese court diviner performs a nearly identical ritual, burning the scapula of an ox and interpreting the patterns of cracks it produces -not to help guide a hunt, but to foresee what the future holds for the king.

Although these practices may appear esoteric or rare, this is not the case. Across disparate cultures, geographies, and epochs, humans have engaged in practices like the above, and many more like it. We burn bones, roll dice, read tea leaves, flip coins, interpret the movements of animals, and more (Figure 1). But why? What binds these seemingly disparate practices together, and what is their purpose? Here, we argue that these diverse cultural practices share a key function in common: they produce randomness. More specifically, we unite these artifacts and practices together as randomization devices -tools designed to generate random outputs that support decision-making.

Although any given device seems specialized, diverse cultures often independently created such devices, whether by burning bones or interpreting the motion of spiders. These artifacts serve important purposes even today. For example, to achieve cryptographic security, some companies such as Cloudflare rely on lava lamps, as their dynamic heating results in random motion ('Lavarand').

Why did these tools evolve with such regularity across diverse cultures, and what underlying features unite them? In this Perspective, we establish randomization devices as a coherent class of cultural tools, unifying practices and artifacts with ostensibly disparate functions, often explored in isolation across diverse disciplines. Then, we offer an integrated account of the cultural evolution of randomization devices by outlining the cognitive and social constraints that engender such devices. First, we bring together representative examples from the ethnographic record to illustrate (a) the broad range of randomization devices across cultures and (b) the diverse functions and implementations of these devices. Then, we suggest that randomization devices constitutively provide solutions to both a cognitive problem (the mind's inability to generate truly random outputs) and a social one (no human-originated output can be trusted to be unbiased). The cognitive and social problems contextualize not just the recurrent invention of these tools, but the specific cases (e.g., domains or situations) in which they are deployed. Cultures use randomization devices precisely where human-generated randomness proves insufficient, as we expand upon in the sections that follow. For both the cognitive problem and the social problem, diverse fields -spanning cultural evolution, computer science, and political philosophy -describe one especially important feature of randomization devices: causal opacity. We analyze the nature of causally opaque versus causally transparent randomization devices, integrating the approaches discussed in these literatures across different levels of analysis. Together, these arguments unify an otherwise-disparate set of tools under a common theory, offering a new lens through which to understand one of humanity's most recurrent and ubiquitous cultural inventions.

2. A diversity of randomization devices across cultures

Throughout history, diverse cultures have produced an enormous variety of randomization devices, a small fraction of which appears in Table 1. Consider how these tools work. Scapulimancy, the burning of shoulder bones, exploits microscopic variations in the material structure of bone -variations so small and so numerous that they are functionally unpredictable. Amathomancy captures the complex patterns that arise from perturbations to soil or sand, as in the case of Bamana sand divination, in which a diviner rapidly strikes a sand surface to produce a series of marks. Here, too, the patterns in the sand result from functionally unpredictable variation in the dispersion of small particles. Other traditions co-opt other sources of noise: Capnomancy uses the flow of smoke, hydromancy the motion of water, and theriomancy the movement of wild animals.

These devices frequently form the basis of diverse divination rituals around the world; indeed, the suffix -mancy denotes these practices as such. While divination has multiple purposes, functionalist accounts hold that these practices are "primarily cognitive" (Hong & Henrich, 2021; Hong, 2025a; Hong, 2025b) methods used to "generate information, usually to assist subsequent decision-making" (Hong, 2025b). And as Boyer (2020) argues, people "generally use divination when other ways of finding out the underlying facts are out of reach". Therefore, divination often appears in high-stakes, socially or morally consequential settings, which we discuss below, and when decisions must be made under conditions of high Knightian uncertainty (many 'unknown unknowns'). These high-stakes, uncertain scenarios invite a particular kind of reliance on randomization devices. For instance, in the aforementioned case of Naskapi scapulimancy, divination is much more frequent in times of food shortage, during which "the shoulder-blade oracle may be consulted as often as every three or four days" (Moore, 1957).

Randomization devices, however, are not limited to divination, aiding in many cases of decision-making under uncertainty. For example, we often use randomization devices to help with anti-coordination -i.e., when we want to 'mismatch' adversaries. Consider the case of encryption, wherein true-random sources secure sensitive data such as financial information or passwords from adversaries. A modern example of a randomization device built explicitly for the purpose of anti-coordination is Cloudflare's 'Lavarand' system, where live cameras pointed at a wall of lava lamps integrate the randomness inherent in the fluid dynamics of heated wax to seed cryptographic random number generators. This is a (somewhat more whimsical) instantiation of the electrical randomization devices of decades past -e.g., RAND Corporation's 'random number tables', which were distributed for use in research and computing, or ERNIE's electrical random generators, used in lotteries; see Table 1.

Additionally, many randomization devices throughout history helped allocate resources to equally meritorious recipients, assisting in cases of procedural justice. As with the kleroterion, for instance, Florentines in the Renaissance engaged in the process of 'imborsazione' (lit. 'into the purse'), which involved sealing slips of paper with citizens' names into individual leather purses, depositing them into a chest, and randomly drawing from them when public office needed to be filled. And even today, in modern Japan, schoolchildren engage in small acts of randomization by playing the 'ghost-leg lottery' (Amida-kuji 阿弥陀籤) -a race through a hand-drawn grid of randomly placed horizontal rungs -to determine who gets which sweets. These examples and others suggest that randomization devices likely cluster around a number of functional domains, including decision-making under uncertainty, anti-coordination, and procedural justice.

These devices vary dramatically in their cultural context, their physical forms, and their historical moments. They even vary in their use cases; we rely on some for decision-making under uncertainty, others for anti-coordination, and still others for procedural justice. What these devices do share, however, is their function: they consistently produce unpredictable and uncontrollable outputs. Below, we expand upon the cognitive and social pressures which we argue brought about their recurrent invention across diverse epochs and ecologies.

3. Solving a cognitive problem: Randomization devices produce what the mind cannot

Humans appear to be poor randomizers; when asked to generate sequences of random numbers, people make a variety of systematic errors. We favor certain numbers, avoid repetition, and disprefer 'runs' or 'streaks' (for review, see Wagenaar, 1972; Bar-Hillel & Wagenaar, 1991; Nickerson, 2002). Such results have led many scholars to suggest that humans entirely lack a capacity for randomization -e.g., Slovic et al. (1974) write: "[p]eople have a very poor conception of randomness; they don't recognize it when they see it and they cannot produce it when they try". In other words, the possibility of a "mental random device" -a cognitive instantiation of a randomizing device -has been nearly universally rejected (e.g., Bar-Hillel & Wagenaar, 1991). Perhaps randomization devices culturally evolved, then, to fill in for the absence of a mental random device.

We believe there is merit to this hypothesis, though recent work paints a more nuanced picture of human random behavior. In some cases, recent results challenge the premise that humans cannot randomize at all, instead suggesting that surprisingly strong random behavior arises in certain tasks. For example, a variety of studies demonstrate high degrees of context-sensitivity in random behavior, such that individuals produce near-random behavior in dyadic, strategic games (Rapoport & Budescu, 1992; Budescu & Rapoport, 1994; Boger et al., 2026). Thus, perhaps in some contexts, humans can successfully produce near-random outputs. A separate line of work helps situate random behavior in terms of our other cognitive capacities. For example, consistent individual differences arise when people generate randomness; random behavior is highly idiosyncratic, in ways that remain stable across tasks and even across time (e.g., Schulz et al., 2021; Boger et al., 2025; Boger & Yousif, 2026). And more generally, generating randomness implicates a variety of cognitive capacities, such as working memory or executive control (e.g., Biesaga & Nowak, 2024; Baddeley, 1966). These findings point to the possibility that human randomness is better characterized as a constrained, context-sensitive, and idiosyncratic capacity, rather than an absent one.

Even under this more nuanced picture of random behavior, however, we argue that randomization devices still provide substantial functional benefits (see Figure 2). Human-generated randomness likely suffices for everyday decisions that require some degree of stochasticity, such as how to fairly share, or how to play rock-paper-scissors. But in other contexts -e.g., when other cognitive or social pressures come into play, as when a person must decide where to lead a hunt during a time of food scarcity or when a healer must treat a mysterious and seemingly random illness -our ability to randomize falls short, creating a need for external randomization devices.

In some cases, the output of a randomization device requires further interpretation in order to culminate in a decision, such as the subsequent interpretation of the cracks in a bone or the movement of birds. A natural tension arises then for the cognitive problem: Given that humans struggle to produce and perceive randomness, perhaps they distort or read meaning into a randomization device's output, whether knowingly or not. This tension has been acknowledged in the literature. For instance, critics of Moore's (1957) foundational account of Naskapi scapulimancy have argued that the practice could not function as advertised, since diviners retain considerable interpretative discretion in reading the bone's cracks, thus undermining the randomness of the device (Slaughter, 1981; Vollweiler & Sanchez, 1983). Certain randomization devices explicitly avoid this issue (a topic we return to later in discussing causal opacity), but we also note that simple heuristics allow for users of a randomization device to circumvent their own biases. As mentioned above, many randomization devices capture physically random processes, such as the flow of smoke, the motion of water, or the spread of heat. A user of these devices might pre-specify or explicitly state a decision rule to avoid introducing personal biases into interpretation -e.g.

, "if the smoke flows to my left, the answer to my question is 'yes', and if it flows to my right, the answer to my question is 'no'." Thus, the fact that some randomization devices require interpretation need not invalidate the cognitive problem. Rather, randomization devices directly address our inability to produce randomness, providing a qualitatively different output from what the mind produces.

Like other cultural tools, randomization devices can augment or offload processes in human cognition. These devices thus contribute a new case study to a larger question in cognitive science about the kinds of cognitive processes that can be culturally offloaded and the kinds of genuinely new cognitive capacities external tools can provide (Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Heyes, 2018; see also Amir & Pitt, 2026). However, unlike other tools that produce new capacities (such as symbolic number systems, which enable the mind to distinguish large exact numbers), randomization devices offer an entirely new capacity -the ability to produce truly random outputs -without that capacity then entering into cognition itself. In other words, the creation of cultural products such as symbolic number systems allows the mind to distinguish large exact numbers, which approximate number systems cannot; but the creation of randomization devices does not allow the mind itself to produce truly random outputs. In this way, randomization devices offer a unique class of cultural tools that greatly aid cognition but produce 'no effect' on cognition itself (as in the framework of Amir & Pitt, 2026).

4. Solving a social problem: Randomization devices offer impartiality

The cognitive problem is insufficient on its own to fully explain the cultural evolution of randomization devices. Even if humans possessed a mental true-random device (i.e., some mechanism that produces perfectly random outputs without constraints from central cognition), a second, distinct problem would nonetheless remain: no individual's outputs, however random, can be trusted to be fully impartial. As a thought experiment, imagine a random lottery that narrows a pool of candidates down to two finalists. A single person then chooses between those two finalists, perhaps even 'at random'. Everything preceding the human intervention is impartial, yet the injection of human choice feels as if it corrupts the entire process. In other words, our perception of an outcome's fairness depends not only on the actual randomness produced, but also on the impartiality of the random-generating process. In this way, even the (unlikely) existence of a mental true-random device would not render external randomization devices obsolete. Convergent but distinct cultural evolutionary pressures thus likely contributed to the development of external randomization devices that solve this additional, social problem. In line with this argument, the use of randomization devices in the ethnographic record extends far beyond cases where cognitive limitations are the chief concern, whether in our ability to randomize or in other processes like working memory. Humans regularly use such devices to impartially and fairly assign roles, prizes, and even punishment. Randomization devices like the Athenian kleroterion and the Florentine imborsazione have many analogs across cultures. For example, military drafts -most (in)famously in the Vietnam War -often utilize randomization devices to draw candidates from an eligible pool, and prize lotteries allocate winnings through similarly random processes, such as a ticketed raffle or a bingo cage. In many decisions (random or not), we designate neutral, external sources as key decision-makers, such as a referee in a game, a judge in a trial, or even the mechanism of an auction when selling goods. As such, considerations of fairness and impartiality permeate many key cultural conventions or devices; randomization devices are no exception.

Though some randomization devices allow for human input or tampering, such as using weighted dice in place of fair dice, others attempt to reduce or even forbid it by design. For example, some devices operate publicly, such that any tampering would be visible to observers; others rely on the behavior of non-human animals (e.g., spider divination), such that humans' desires or wishes cannot affect the outcomes; and still others rely on dynamic physical processes (e.g., a live fire), entirely removing the potential influence of living beings. In each case, the 'task' imposes little to no cognitive load. For example, to choose the winner of a simple lottery, one needs to randomly generate only a single number. Yet the involvement of a human necessarily introduces bias, if only because of people's individual differences in random behavior (Boger et al., 2025; Boger & Yousif, 2026). The latter two cases -as in spider divination and scapulimancy -raise an especially interesting distinction between the generation of a device's output and its subsequent interpretation. Randomization devices externalize only the generation step. Even in cases where humans must interpret the device's output, such as a diviner reading cracks on a scapula, the device ensures that the input to that interpretation could not be tampered with or produced with prejudice. Randomization devices thus create impartiality by externalizing the random-generating process.

Scholars of decision theory and philosophy have long recognized that randomization devices resolve these social concerns of partiality. Stone (2011) argues that the distinctive value of lotteries lies precisely in their impartiality, which he terms the "sanitizing" effect. Others describe the same phenomenon in a variety of ways: Icard (2021) calls it the relinquishing of "authorship", Boyer (2020) "ostensive detachment", and Dowlen (2009) the "blind break". Similar arguments can be found in the broader literature on sortition, which emphasizes the legitimizing effect of randomness (Broome, 1984; Elster, 1989); and in the literature on divination (e.g., Ahern, 1981), where scholars suggest that randomization devices ensure "at least in the eyes of the beholders, that no one has inappropriately affected the outcome of the divinatory procedure" (Johnston, 2001). These findings converge with other evolutionary accounts that underscore sensitivity to others' motivated reasoning as a regular and deep feature of social cognition (Mercier & Sperber, 2017).

The psychological literature provides convergent evidence for the prevalence and importance of the social problem; human involvement reduces our perceptions of impartiality (e.g., Keren & Teigen, 2010), an effect that emerges early in development (Shaw & Olson, 2014; Grocke et al., 2015). A variety of findings in cognitive and developmental psychology implicates this judgment as one that may stem from our understanding of 'agents' more broadly. Even in infancy, humans seem to appreciate that agents can produce either order or disorder, but that inanimate forces can produce only disorder (Newman et al., 2010). As such, it's possible that the mind possesses an early-emerging (and perhaps innate) expectation that the involvement of an agent opens the door to a disruption in randomization. Research on statistical reasoning in infants provides strong evidence for a robust, early-emerging sense of 'fairness' in sampling. Infants as young as 8 months old make rational statistical inferences (e.g., about the sampling distribution of marbles in a box; Xu & Garcia, 2008; Téglás et al., 2007; Téglás et al., 2011), such that they expect a randomly drawn sample to reflect the population's proportions. However, such intuitions flexibly incorporate knowledge about preferences and whether, for example, such draws occurred fairly, insofar as infants expect blindfolded draws to be random but sighted or preference-laden draws to be non-random (Xu & Denison, 2009). Thus, although we struggle to produce random sequences, we hold strong intuitions about whether certain sampling processes occurred randomly or fairly, making the social problem especially thorny.

Beyond concerns of partiality, the externalization of decisions to randomization devices garners a related social benefit: the outsourcing of responsibility. The outcomes of many decisions -from mundane ones, like where to eat, to consequential ones, like whom to elect -often carry implications for the decision-maker. If an outcome is unfavorable, it can invite blame or generate conflict (see discussion in Boyer, 2020). For instance, if a single person drafts men for war 'randomly', then when a drafted soldier sustains an injury, the blame can still fall on the decision-maker. In these cases, external randomization devices create a social buffer between the decision-maker and the outcome. As the personal costs to the decision-maker increase, the need for an externalizing solution often increases too. Consequently, many randomization devices emerge in exactly these high-stakes circumstances (e.g., drafting men for war, choosing where to hunt during a time of food scarcity, and so on). These patterns suggest that randomization devices not only generate randomness but also reshape how we socially attribute and evaluate decisions.

5. Causal opacity and causal transparency

Having established that randomization devices solve cognitive and social problems, we now highlight a design feature that reflects both constraints simultaneously and has been a topic of past inquiry (e.g., Hong & Henrich, 2021). Randomization devices vary in their causal opacity -that is, how visible, understandable, and verifiable their random-generating process is to both users and onlookers. Some devices are causally opaque: Scapulimancy produces cracks whose physical origins are complex and hidden, and whose interpretation requires specialist knowledge (Boyer, 2020; Singh, 2018). Other devices are causally transparent: The kleroterion operates by a simple, publicly visible mechanism whose output is verifiable by others.

Causal transparency requires not only verifying the outcome of the randomization device, but also understanding its possible space of outputs. For example, imagine a program that rolls a die with an unknown number of sides. After each roll, the user of the device receives a picture of the face on which the die landed, in a way that obscures its shape. The outcome here is verifiable, but such a device is not fully transparent in the sense that its possible outputs remain unknown, making the user unable to evaluate the device. The die might have 3 sides, or it might have 300 sides; these two different possibilities result in vastly different evaluations of what it means for the (unknown) n-sided die to land on '2' four times in a row. For each device, the degree of causal opacity -realized both by its verifiability and by its space of outputs -serves a functional role.

Interestingly, considerations of this idea arise independently across disparate literatures spanning cultural evolution, computer science, and political philosophy. Below, we synthesize these traditions and argue that these different approaches examine the same underlying phenomenon across different levels of analysis. Then, we connect these issues to the functional domains of randomization devices and the problems they solve.

In the field of cultural evolution, scholars have suggested that certain randomization devices, and many cultural devices more broadly, 'work' because they are causally opaque. The majority of this discussion has centered on divination practices. For example, Henrich (2015) writes that if people "came to understand that bird augury or bone divination didn't actually predict the future, the practice would probably be dropped or people would increasingly ignore ritual findings in favor of their own intuitions". Hong (2025a) develops this idea further, arguing that causally opaque practices are more readily transmitted across generations precisely because it is hard for practitioners to rationally evaluate and reject these practices (at least, relative to causally transparent ones). In other words, causal opacity is a feature, not a bug. This is consistent with a broader principle in cultural evolutionary theory that practices can persist and spread because of their downstream benefits, even when the proximate mechanisms that produce those benefits remain opaque (Boyd & Richerson, 1985; Henrich, 2015; Derex et al., 2019). A complementary account, championed by Sperber (1996), holds that opaque beliefs are harder to evaluate, and precisely because of that difficulty, are both more stable and more readily transmitted across generations. Consistent with this, empirical work on ritual transmission suggests that causally opaque practices are not only rated as more efficacious (Legare & Souza, 2012) but also reproduced more faithfully (e.g., Nielsen et al., 2015).

Certain functional domains of randomization devices -namely, anti-coordination -even seem to require causal opacity in some sense. Specifically, the output of a randomization device must remain hidden from an adversary in order for that device to achieve anti-coordination. For instance, if a poker player uses a decision aid for when to bluff, they must ensure their opponents do not know the nature of that decision aid, lest their behavior be predicted and exploited. In this way, such devices remain causally transparent to the user, who can verify the output, but must do so while being causally opaque to adversaries.

In political philosophy and computer science, domains of inquiry far removed from divination, scholars have underscored the importance of causal transparency in randomization devices. Interestingly, the need for causal transparency was a major part of the need for, and development of, pseudo-random algorithms -processes whose outputs approximate randomness, but arise from deterministic formulas -as an alternative to true-random sources -algorithmically unpredictable sources of randomness. Most instantiations of randomness in a computer for use in games, simulations, and so on, rely on pseudo-random algorithms instead of a true-random device. Knuth (2014) notes two reasons why this preference emerged, both of which invoke causal transparency. First, in many situations (e.g., for simulations or statistical analyses), we often want computers to create reproducible randomness -but "it is impossible to reproduce calculations exactly a second time" with a true-random device (Knuth, 2014). Knowledge of the pseudo-random algorithm and its seed allows any person to recreate a set of random outputs, as when we set a random seed in Python or R; but definitionally, true-random devices do not allow for such reproducibility. Second, and perhaps more importantly, "such machines have tended to suffer from malfunctions that are extremely difficult to detect". In other words, causal opacity comes with a cost: When the randomization device breaks, it is not clear (a) whether it is broken, (b) why it is broken, and (c) how to fix it. Pseudo-random algorithms address these issues by way of their causally transparent design -they contain just a few parameters and a mathematical formula, as opposed to true-random devices which contain specialized hardware that may falter at any number of often interrelated stages. A similar theme arises in the political philosophy literature, where the legitimacy of sortition has been argued to depend on the causal transparency of the randomization device. For example, Dowlen (2009) writes that sortition and lotteries work precisely when "those participating or closely connected with the result can witness the procedure to ensure that no wilful interference is taking place", making the process transparent to its participants. Similarly, Thibaut and Walker (1978) write that "the procedure most likely to produce justice is that procedure which facilitates the fullest possible report of inputs prior to determination", a direct acknowledgment of causal transparency.

Notably, scholars of different traditions have focused on opacity in randomization devices at different levels of analysis. Cultural evolutionists have asked whether the device's opacity supports its transmission across generations, political philosophers have asked whether the generation process itself is transparent and verifiable, and computer scientists have asked whether the underlying mechanism can be inspected and whether its outputs are reproducible. The fact that researchers from diverse disciplines and perspectives regularly converge on the importance of causal opacity across multiple levels of analysis suggests that this constitutes a key feature of randomization devices as a class of tool.

The consequences of causal opacity and causal transparency relate back to the cognitive and social problems described above. Devices that aid in procedural justice -e.g., imborsazione, the kleroterion, or the Vietnam draft lottery -seem to often implement causally transparent designs. The reason for this may be that causal transparency can solve the social problem: It ensures that onlookers know that the process works impartially, and even allows onlookers to verify the device's outputs and process themselves. Perhaps as the social problem grows, the need for causal transparency similarly increases.

For the cognitive problem, causal transparency plays a different role. Causally transparent devices allow users to easily diagnose when (or if) the device falters, as in the case of pseudo-random algorithms above. This ensures that, to verify the functioning of a randomization device, a user need not rely on their perhaps flawed evaluation of its randomness. Note that we do not mean to argue that all of the 'best' randomization devices use causally transparent designs, or even that a device must be causally transparent in order to solve the social problem. Indeed, in many cases, causal opacity helps the device's transmission, as in the case of divination practices. Perhaps, then, the need for causal transparency and its relationship to the social problem grows when one must coordinate across a larger community. For example, in a small community, onlookers may personally know the diviner and thus trust their interpretation of the randomization device; but in a case like the Vietnam draft, the randomization device must coordinate across millions of citizens, making causal opacity no longer feasible. Thus, the degree of causal opacity a randomization device requires may scale inversely with the size of the audience whose trust it must earn.

6. Concluding thoughts and future directions

The cultural evolution of randomization devices has been examined, in fragments, across diverse disciplines. Here, we present a unifying account of these devices. In doing so, we focus on two problems randomization devices solve, one cognitive and one social. Though the cognitive and social problems exist independently (as, e.g., even in the absence of the cognitive problem, the social problem alone likely creates enough of a need for randomization devices), they interact in certain ways. For example, a strong version of the social problem might worsen the cognitive problem, insofar as a decision-maker might be so intent on generating a perceived 'fair' outcome that they choose an explicitly non-random way of doing so. Conversely, a strong version of the cognitive problem might worsen the social problem, insofar as onlookers might evaluate a decision-maker's 'random' outcome as unfair or non-random if they struggle to generate randomness in other settings. Though one problem may appear more dire or fundamental than the other in certain settings, the broad range of functional domains for randomization devices makes it difficult to explicitly denote one problem as more important than the other. In this way, we argue that randomization devices constitutively solve both the cognitive problem and the social problem, insofar as they produce strong randomness, externalized from an individual's inputs or control; as long as the resulting randomness has some function, then a device's output seems to solve both problems. As such, both problems are of great import on their own, and they interact in ways that magnify the need for randomization devices.

Along with bringing together ideas from diverse disciplines, our framework generates unique and testable predictions, both ethnographically and empirically. First, earlier in this work, we highlighted the role that randomization devices play in divination practices such as shamanism, which occur with substantial regularity across cultures (Singh, 2018; for extended discussion, see Singh, 2025). Interestingly, not every culture relies on specialized diviners (or shamans) -and cases of shamanic absence can be interesting and informative on their own. For instance, Singh (2018) notes that these practices did not culturally evolve in some societies, such as the Tiwi of Northern Australia -perhaps because, as Hart and Pilling (1960) note, "their physical environment was on the whole a satisfactory and not a hostile universe". This potentially implicates environmental (in)stability as a causal factor in reliance on divination. As we saw above in the case of the Naskapi, the bone oracle was more frequently consulted in periods of food scarcity. Similarly, Trobriand Islanders (in Papua New Guinea) relied on magic for open-sea fishing but not inner lagoon fishing, because the latter provided "abundant results without danger and uncertainty" (Malinowski, 1948). Thus, randomization devices, both in the context of divination and outside of it, are likely used with higher frequency in more unpredictable environments and when the stakes are higher. Our framework predicts this insofar as higher-stakes settings exacerbate both problems; in such settings, (a) the cognitive problem worsens as various cognitive resource limitations might further impede our ability to produce randomness, and (b) the social problem worsens because higher-stakes settings necessarily carry more dramatic consequences, thus increasing the need for a fair, external outcome. Systematic cross-cultural and within-society examinations could shed light on this prediction, perhaps even partialing out the roles of environmental harshness (e.g., low mean) and environmental unpredictability (e.g., high variance) on the use of these devices. A meta-ethnographic analysis of this type -where, e.g., one quantifies the relationship between a culture's environment and its reliance on randomization devices, or catalogs regularities in the various domains in which randomization devices arise -using large ethnographic corpora (such as eHRAF World Cultures or the Standard Cross-Cultural Sample) strikes us as a particularly feasible and interesting avenue for future work.

Relatedly, our initial foray into the ethnographic literature suggests that randomization devices cluster into at least three recurrent functional domains: (1) anti-coordination, in which randomness prevents others from exploiting predictability in behavior (Von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944; Miller, 1997), as in the case of encryption, (2) procedural justice, in which randomness ensures impartiality and reduces the risk of social sanctions, as in the kleroterion, and (3) decision-making under uncertainty, in which randomness is invoked to navigate choices whose outcomes are unknowable, as in the case of scapulimancy. These functions are analytically distinct, resolving discrete social and cognitive problems, yet all are served with the same kinds of tools. Importantly, these functions are not exhaustive, which further reflects the broad application of randomization devices. Other devices might serve functions beyond the three classes defined here, and many randomization devices can be used to serve multiple functions. For example, many societies culturally evolved dice and dice-like objects, not only for decision-making under uncertainty (Dotson, 2019; Madden, 2026), but also for anti-coordination (Reinhold, 1995), procedural justice (Stone, 2011), and games (e.g., gambling). This convergence suggests that randomization devices are not a grab bag of unrelated artifacts, but rather a coherent class of tools that occur with regularity because they resolve challenges that also appear with regularity. A systematic mapping of the domains in which randomization devices appear or do not appear, along with a closer analysis of the distinct functions these tools serve, would sharpen our understanding of what conditions are necessary or sufficient for their evolution.

Next, to the empirical predictions: Although a substantial literature analyzes human random behavior, relatively less attention has been paid to the ways in which we conceptualize and interact with external randomization devices. For example, here we argued that randomization devices diverge from other tools in that their outputs qualitatively differ from what cognition produces. Whether or not people intuitively appreciate this distinction remains an open empirical question, as is when and whether people choose to use randomization devices at all. Future work might test whether individuals rely on randomization devices in higher-stakes or more demanding settings; whether individuals prefer randomization devices for some problems over others (e.g., feeling the need to use randomization devices for procedural justice but not anti-coordination); and whether either of these two possibilities covaries with an individual's assessment of how a randomization device differs from their own ability to produce randomness. Our framework also offers predictions for features such as causal opacity. Whereas prior work in cultural evolution primarily analyzes causal opacity as it relates to group-or culture-level transmission, it is an open question whether individual people are sensitive to the causal opacity of randomization devices. Might the broad, large-scale transmission of causally opaque devices reflect individual-level, small-sample sensitivities to these same features? This could be tested by offering people access to a causally opaque randomization device when playing a strategic game (e.g., matching pennies) and examining whether they transmit that device to others.

Synthesizing evidence from disparate disciplines, we have suggested that randomization devices evolved because the mind cannot always produce strong, unpredictable randomness, and because no individual's outputs, however random, can be fully trusted to be bias-free. Despite their remarkable diversity, randomization devices thus denote a unified class of cultural tool that offers new abilities to its users without that ability affecting cognition itself.
