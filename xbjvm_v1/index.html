To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Young adults in the United States are having less sex than previous generations, but less sex is not necessarily safer sex. Guided by the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behavior (COM-B) model, we examined whether perceived access to sexual health resources (opportunity) and the importance placed on contraception (reflective motivation) correspond with protective behavior, and what barriers young adults report when access does not lead to use. A national quota-based online sample of 2,373 U.S. adults aged 18-29 completed a survey in May 2026. Among sexually active participants, greater perceived access was associated with higher odds of typical condom use (OR = 1.19), birth control use (OR = 1.44), and past-year STI testing (OR = 1.24).

Yet among those who perceived these resources as easy to access, 54.5% did not typically use condoms, 53.5% did not typically use birth control, and 42.3% had not been tested in the past year. Only 16.3% of participants who had vaginal intercourse used condoms consistently.

Contraception importance was only weakly correlated with access but was strongly associated with use (condoms: OR = 12.45; birth control: OR = 8.29). The association between birth control access and use was stronger among partnered than single participants. Among those with access who did not use protection, common barriers included reduced pleasure with condoms (41.0%), concerns about hormonal side effects (49.2%), and social media content about hormonal birth control (41.8%). Interventions should pair efforts to expand access with approaches that address pleasure, side-effect concerns, and the information environments young adults rely on.

Plain Language Summary

Young adults in the United States are having less sex than previous generations, but having less sex does not necessarily mean having safer sex. We surveyed 2,373 young adults aged 18-29 about how easy it is for them to get condoms, birth control, and STI testing, and whether they actually use them. Young adults who found these resources easier to get were somewhat more likely to use them. But about half of sexually active young adults who said condoms or birth control were easy to get did not usually use them, and many who did use them did not use them every time. Young adults' views on the importance of contraception were more strongly linked to use than how easy it was to get. Common reasons for not using protection included feeling that condoms reduce pleasure, worries about side effects of hormonal birth control, and negative content about birth control on social media. Making sexual health resources available is important, but it is not enough on its own. Programs should also address the concerns that keep young adults from using protection.

Access Matters but Isn't Sufficient: Understanding Protective Sexual Health Behavior

Among Young Adults

Young adults are having less sex than they used to. Across multiple U.S. datasets, rates of partnered sexual activity have declined and sexual inactivity has increased over recent decades, with substantial changes observed across demographic groups, but especially among younger adults (Herbenick et al., 2022; Twenge et al., 2017; Ueda et al., 2020). These trends have contributed to discussion of a contemporary "sex recession." Research has increasingly sought to understand why younger adults are engaging in less sexual activity (e.g., Lei, 2021; South & Lei, 2021). However, one question that has received far less attention is this: if young adults are having less sex, does that mean they are less at risk for sexual health concerns? At first glance, declining sexual frequency might suggest declining sexual risk. Fewer partners and encounters cut down on chances for sexually transmitted infections (STIs; Aral, 1994; Pinkerman et al., 2002) and unintended pregnancy. However, having less sex is not the same as safer sex. Among sexually active individuals, sexual health depends on the use of protective behaviors, including condoms and other barrier methods, hormonal contraception, and preventive health services such as STI testing (CDC, 2024; Steiner et al., 2021). Declining sexual activity at the population level might be obscuring important patterns in risk-taking among those who remain sexually active. Understanding the sexual health of young adults requires looking beyond how often young adults are having sex and considering whether they have access to, and utilize, resources that can help protect their sexual health.

Access to sexual health resources is an important component of sexual health. Condoms and other barrier methods can reduce the risk of STI transmission and unintended pregnancy (e.g., CDC, 2024a; CDC, 2024b; Holmes et al., 2004), and hormonal methods (e.g., implants are among the most effective ways to prevent pregnancy (Sundaram et al., 2017; Winner et al., 2012). Access to STI testing facilitates the identification and treatment of infections and may help prevent further transmission (CDC, 2024c). More broadly, access to sexual and reproductive health services provides the resources necessary for informed decisions about sexual health (CDC, 2024d; WHO, 2024). However, access to these resources is not universal.

Cost, transportation, privacy and confidentiality concerns, limited availability of services, and lack of knowledge about where to obtain services all create barriers to sexual health care resources (Reeves et al., 2023 (Reeves et al., , 2024)).

Even when these barriers are removed, access does not guarantee use. Access gives people the chance to protect themselves, but whether they do so may also depend on factors such as concerns about side effects or reduced pleasure, partner preferences, and relationship context.

Examining both access and use provides a more complete understanding of sexual health behavior among a young generation that may be having less sex overall but may not necessarily be having safer sex.

Theoretical Background: Access Is Opportunity, Not Behavior

Efforts to improve sexual health have often focused on one of two mechanisms: removing structural barriers to access or persuading people that protection matters. Yet neither alone may be sufficient, because behavior depends on more than the availability of resources or the belief that protection is important. The distinction between access to sexual health resources and their use can be understood through the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behavior (COM-B) model (Michie et al., 2011). COM-B proposes that behavior occurs through the interaction of three necessary conditions: capability, or having the knowledge and skills needed to perform a behavior; opportunity, or having the environmental and social conditions that make the behavior possible; and motivation, the processes that energize and direct behavior.

Motivation can be reflective, involving conscious evaluations, beliefs about what is good or important, intentions, and plans, or automatic, involving emotions, impulses, and habits that operate with little deliberate thought (Michie et al., 2011). Importantly, because all three components are necessary, having the opportunity to perform a behavior does not guarantee that it will occur.

Applied to sexual health, access to condoms, contraception, emergency contraception, and STI testing can be conceptualized primarily as an opportunity. Work applying COM-B to sexual health has found that condom use and STI testing depend on capability and motivation as well as opportunity (Bru Garcia et al., 2022; Rodrigues et al., 2023). How much someone values contraception-a conscious judgment about its importance-is one form of reflective motivation. Yet someone can think contraception matters and find it easy to get, and still not use it every time. In the current study, we treat perceived access as an indicator of opportunity and the importance placed on contraception as an indicator of reflective motivation, and we examine reported barriers to understand what may get in the way of use when the opportunity is there.

Research on condom and contraceptive use supports this gap between having the opportunity and acting on it. For example, lower condom use has been associated with perceptions that condoms reduce sexual pleasure, partner preferences, anticipated partner disapproval, and difficulties communicating or negotiating condom use (Brown et al., 2008; Randolph et al., 2007; Rodrigues & Lopes, 2022; Wetzel et al., 2024). These patterns may also differ by gender and relationship status. The burden of pregnancy prevention often falls on women, who are typically the ones using hormonal and long-term methods, whereas men may be more involved in decisions about condom use and less aware of what other methods a partner uses (Breakey et al., 2026; Fennell, 2011). Relationship context may also shape which methods people rely on. As relationships become characterized by greater trust, commitment, and perceived monogamy, individuals may feel less need for condoms and move toward hormonal methods or discontinue use altogether (Fortenberry, 2019; Rodrigues & Lopes, 2022; Santos et al., 2024). Decisions about contraceptive use similarly extend beyond access, with concerns about side effects, hormones, and method acceptability contributing to contraceptive nonuse or discontinuation (Frost et al., 2012; Le Guen et al., 2021; Littlejohn, 2012; Moreau et al., 2007).

Increasingly, these perceptions may also be shaped by the information environments in which young adults make contraceptive decisions. Social media platforms such as TikTok have become prominent sources of reproductive health information among young adults (e.g., Stoddard et al., 2024). Across platforms, side effects are the most frequently discussed contraceptive topic, and content often emphasizes negative experiences with hormonal methods and may contain incomplete or inaccurate information (Beacham et al., 2026; Pleasants et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2022; for reviews, see Caron et al., 2025; Narayan et al., 2026). Moreover, only 19.4% of these videos are made by health care professionals (Stoddard et al., 2024). de Moel-Mandel and colleagues (2025), for example, coded popular contraception videos on TikTok and found that most of the information was low quality, favored "natural" methods over hormonal ones, and was often distrustful of doctors. Young adults may therefore have access to contraception while also being exposed to content that discourages its use. From a COM-B perspective, these findings illustrate how opportunity may be present while motivational and social factors discourage protective sexual health behavior.

Despite substantial research identifying barriers to protective sexual health behaviors, less is known about the extent to which perceived access corresponds with actual use among young adults, and what reasons are given for not using accessible resources. Examining access alongside use, and interfering barriers, can help clarify whether having the opportunity to engage in protective sexual health behavior translates into doing so.

Current Study

The current study examines whether perceived access to sexual health resources translates into their use among young adults. Guided by COM-B, we distinguish among the importance individuals place on contraception (i.e., a reflective motivation), their perceived access to sexual health resources (i.e., an opportunity), and their reported use of those resources (i.e., the behavioral outcome). We focus particularly on two areas of protective behavior: pregnancy prevention and safer sex. For pregnancy prevention, we compare the importance participants place on contraception, their perceived access to contraceptive resources, and the methods they report using. For safer sex, we compare perceived access to condoms and STI testing with use and recent testing. Because our survey assessed the importance of contraception but not of STI testing, analyses involving importance focus only on contraceptive behaviors. We describe importance and perceived access in the full sample and examine correspondence between access and use among participants for whom each behavior was relevant based on their recent sexual activity. We also test whether these patterns differ by relationship status and gender.

We also look at why access may not lead to use or may lead to inconsistent use. We describe the barriers participants endorse, and reasons given for nonuse, including concerns about hormonal side effects, social media messaging, reduced pleasure with condoms, and cost. This lets us identify motivational and interpersonal barriers that remain even when opportunity is present. Although the data are observational, they offer a test of a basic idea from COM-B: having the opportunity to protect one's sexual health does not mean people will do so.

Research Questions

RQ1: How important is contraception to young adults, and how accessible do they perceive contraception and other sexual health resources to be?

RQ2:

To what extent does perceived access to sexual health resources correspond with their reported use, and where do gaps emerge between access and use?

RQ3:

To what extent does the importance placed on contraception correspond with contraceptive access and use? RQ4: Do associations and gaps among importance, access, and use differ by relationship status and gender?

RQ5: What barriers to protective sexual health behavior do young adults endorse, and among those who perceive relevant resources as accessible but do not use them or use them inconsistently, what reasons do they report for nonuse?

Method Participants and Procedure

The sample consisted of 2,373 young adults aged 18 to 29 (M = 23.86, SD = 3.35) living in the United States. Data were drawn from a national online survey fielded by Prodege, a market research firm that recruits respondents from its online research panel and network. Data collection took place over three weeks from May 3 to May 23, 2026. To be eligible, respondents had to be 18 to 29 years old and currently living in the United States. Prodege screened panelists against these criteria before they were invited to begin the survey.

Because the goal was a large and diverse sample of young adults rather than a probability sample, Prodege used quota-based recruitment. Quotas were set for age, gender, race/ethnicity, region of residence, and relationship status, guided by U.S. Census benchmarks. Bisexual, gay, and lesbian respondents were deliberately oversampled so that the experiences of sexual minority young adults could be examined with adequate precision. The targetable incidence rate was 53%, and 19% of respondents who began the survey did not complete it. No weights were applied, so all analyses are based on unweighted data.

Respondents gave informed consent and then completed a self-administered online questionnaire that took about 20 minutes on average. The survey covered sexual behavior and desire, romantic and sexual relationships, sexual health, mental health, and demographic characteristics. Before beginning, respondents were told that some questions concerned sensitive sexual topics and that their answers would be kept confidential. Prodege compensated participants in accordance with its standard payment structure. All procedures were approved by the Indiana University Institutional Review Board.

Throughout fielding, Prodege applied both automated and manual checks to protect data quality. Cases showing low engagement or poor response quality were excluded before the data were released to the research team. These included respondents who missed attention-check items, gave straight-line responses across item batteries, or finished in an implausibly short time.

In the final sample, 54% of participants were 18 to 24 years old, and 46% were 25 to 29.

Roughly equal shares identified as men (47.8%) and women (48.0%), and 4.2% reported another gender identity. With respect to sexual orientation, 63% identified as heterosexual, 13% as bisexual, 9% as lesbian, 8% as gay, and 7% as another identity. Regarding relationship status, 40.5% of participants were single or not dating (n = 960), 12.2% were casually dating (n = 289), and 42.2% were partnered (n = 1,001). The remaining 5.2% (n = 123) reported that their relationship status was "complicated," preferred not to disclose their status, or reported another relationship status. Most participants identified as White (56.5%), followed by Hispanic/Latino (18.2%), Black (12.9%), Asian (6.0%), and multiracial or another racial/ethnic identity (5.0%).

See Table 1 for additional demographic information. Descriptive analyses of importance, perceived access, and barriers used the full sample. Analyses of access and use were restricted to participants for whom each behavior was relevant: those reporting vaginal or anal intercourse in the past three months for condom use (n = 1,455), vaginal intercourse in the past three months for birth control use (n = 1,344), and sexual activity or at least one in-person partner in the past three months for STI testing (n = 1,589).

Measures

Gender. Participants reported their current gender identity by selecting from a list of options (e.g., man, woman, non-binary, agender, another identity). Because few participants reported gender identities other than man/woman, we were unable to conduct meaningful comparisons of those with diverse gender identities.

Relationship Status.

Participants chose the option that best described their current relationship status: (a) not dating and not looking for a relationship, (b) single but open to dating, (c) casually dating, (d) in a committed monogamous relationship, (e) in a consensually nonmonogamous relationship, (f) "it's complicated," or (g) prefer not to say. For analyses, participants who were not dating or were single but open to dating were classified as single, those who were casually dating were classified as casually dating, and those in monogamous or consensually non-monogamous relationships were classified as partnered. Participants who selected "it's complicated," "prefer not to say," or another relationship status (n = 123) were excluded from relationship-status comparisons.

Sexual Activity.

Several indicators were used to describe sexual activity and to define the subsamples for whom each protective behavior was relevant. First, participants answered yes or no to the question "Are you sexually active?" Second, they reported how many people they had engaged in in-person sexual activity with over the past three months, using a drop-down menu ranging from 0 to 100 or more. Sexual activity was defined for participants as any partnered sexual act, including vaginal or anal intercourse, oral sex, and/or mutual masturbation.

Third, as part of a broader sexual behavior inventory, participants reported how often they had engaged in vaginal and anal intercourse during the past three months, on a scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 8 (more than once a day). Participants reporting any vaginal intercourse in the past three months were considered at potential risk of pregnancy and were shown the contraceptive use items described below. Participants reporting any anal intercourse were shown the item on condom use during anal sex.

Importance of Contraception.

The importance participants placed on contraception was measured with a single item: "How important is using contraception in your sexual decisionmaking?" Responses were made on a 5-point scale from 1 (not at all important) to 5 (extremely important), with higher scores indicating greater importance. Consistent with COM-B, this measure was treated as an indicator of reflective motivation.

Perceived Access to Sexual Health Resources.

Perceived access served as the indicator of opportunity. Participants rated how easy or difficult it would be to access each of seven resources if they needed them: condoms; birth control (e.g., pill, IUD, implant, patch, or ring); emergency contraception (the morning-after pill); STI testing; information about ovulation or cycle tracking; abortion services; and reliable information about sexual health or contraception.

Ratings were made on a 5-point scale from 1 (very difficult) to 5 (very easy). Participants could also select "not sure," and were treated as missing data. Each resource was analyzed separately.

For analyses of correspondence between access and use, each resource was also coded as accessible (somewhat easy or very easy) or not accessible (all other valid responses).

Use of Protective Sexual Health Behaviors. Typical methods.

Participants indicated which forms of protection or contraception they or their partner typically used during sex, selecting all that applied from the following options: condoms, birth control (e.g., pill, IUD, implant), withdrawal, emergency contraception, regular STI testing, ovulation or fertility tracking, another method, or none. They could also indicate that they were not sexually active or that the question did not apply. Each method was coded as selected (1) or not selected (0).

Consistency of use.

Participants who reported vaginal intercourse in the past three months estimated the percentage of times they had used each of the following when they had sex during that period: condoms, contraceptives or birth control, withdrawal, ovulation or fertility tracking, and emergency contraception. Estimates were made on a sliding scale ranging from 0% to 100% in 10-point increments. Participants who reported anal intercourse estimated, on the same slider, the percentage of times they had used a condom during anal sex. Participants who had vaginal sex also answered a more general item, "In the past 3 months, how often did you use protection during vaginal sex?" Responses ranged from 1 (never) to 5 (all the time), and the response "I did not have vaginal sex" was treated as missing.

Emergency contraception use.

Participants reported how many times they or a sexual partner had used emergency contraception in the past three months, using a drop-down menu ranging from 0 to 15 or more. STI testing. Participants indicated when they were last tested for STIs: within the past three months, three to six months ago, six to 12 months ago, more than 12 months ago, or never.

"

Prefer not to answer" responses were treated as missing. Responses were also dichotomized to indicate whether participants had (1) or had not (0) been tested in the past year.

Barriers to Protective Behavior.

We assessed barriers to protective sexual health behavior in three ways. First, participants rated their agreement with four statements describing possible motivational and structural barriers to using protective resources: "Concerns about the risks or side effects of hormonal contraception discourage me from using it," "Content I've seen on social media about hormonal birth control makes me less likely to want to use it," "Concerns that condoms reduce sexual pleasure make me less likely to use them," and "Cost makes it difficult for me to access sexual health resources." A fifth item, "People my age regularly use contraception," was used as an indicator of perceived social norms. Participants rated the five items on a 5-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), and each was analyzed separately.

Second, participants who reported vaginal intercourse in the past three months and used condoms less than 100% of the time were asked about the main reasons they did not always use condoms. They selected all reasons that applied from the following: condoms reduce pleasure, trust in their partner or a lack of worry about STIs, use of another form of contraception, not having a condom available, spontaneity, their partner's preference, their own preference, alcohol or substance use, a latex allergy (their own or their partner's), or another reason.

Third, participants who did not list birth control among the methods they typically used were asked, "Besides side effects, what are some reasons you might choose not to use hormonal birth control?" They selected all reasons that applied from the following: it does not feel natural or healthy, not liking the idea of hormones, feeling that it places too much responsibility on women, not feeling informed about their options, friends not using it, previous negative experiences with hormonal birth control, not needing it (e.g., not having sex with men), religious or personal beliefs, cost, or another reason. For both sets of reasons, each reason was coded as selected (1) or not selected (0).

Analytic Plan

Analyses proceeded in five stages corresponding to the study research questions. Before primary analyses, variables of interest were examined for missing data, distributions, and appropriate coding. Descriptive statistics were calculated for participant characteristics and key study variables. Analyses involving contraceptive use were restricted to participants for whom the corresponding behavior was relevant based on reported sexual activity.

RQ1: Importance and perceived access. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the importance participants placed on contraception and their perceived access to each sexual health resource.

RQ2: Perceived access and utilization. Binary logistic regressions examined associations between perceived access (continuous) and reported use of condoms, birth control, and STI testing among participants for whom each behavior was relevant. Typical condom and birth control use were coded as selected (1) or not selected (0), and STI testing was coded as tested (1) or not tested (0) in the past 12 months. To describe access-use gaps, we calculated the percentage of participants who perceived each resource as accessible (responses of 4-5) but did not report corresponding use.

RQ3: Importance, access, and contraceptive use. Spearman correlations examined associations between contraception importance and perceived access to condoms and birth control. Binary logistic regression analyses examined whether contraception importance was associated with typical contraceptive use after accounting for perceived access among behaviorally relevant participants. Separate models were conducted for condom use and birth control use, each coded as selected (1) or not selected (0). Categorical specifications of importance and perceived access were examined, and their interactions were evaluated to determine whether the association between importance and use varied by perceived accessibility.

Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals are reported.

RQ4: Sexual and relational context.

Descriptive comparisons examined whether contraception importance and perceived access differed by relationship status and gender. Chisquare tests examined whether the proportion of participants reporting perceived access without corresponding use differed across these groups among behaviorally relevant participants. Binary logistic regression analyses examined whether associations between perceived access and typical contraceptive use differed by relationship status and gender. Typical condom use and typical birth control use served as separate dependent variables. Models included perceived access, contraception importance, the relevant demographic variable, and a corresponding interaction term (access × gender or access × relationship status). Significant interactions were examined using predicted probabilities.

RQ5: Reasons for nonuse despite access. Descriptive analyses characterized barriers to protective sexual health behavior, as well as reasons for nonuse or inconsistent use among those who perceived relevant resources as accessible (responses of 4-5). Condom-related analyses examined reasons for inconsistent condom use among participants who reported relevant sexual activity during the previous three months. Birth control analyses examined reasons for nonuse among eligible participants who did not report typically using birth control. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for each reported reason. Where sample sizes permitted, chi-square tests examined differences in reported reasons by relationship status and gender.

Across analyses, we evaluated statistical significance at p < .05 and reported effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals alongside significance tests. Analyses addressing RQs 1-5 were considered planned analyses, whereas subgroup comparisons of reasons for nonuse or inconsistent use by relationship status and gender under RQ5 were considered exploratory. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; therefore, findings from exploratory analyses should be interpreted with caution.

Results

RQ1: Importance and perceived access.

Participants reported moderate levels of importance regarding contraception in their sexual decision-making (M = 3.15, SD = 1.44), with 45.2% indicating that contraception was "very" or "extremely" important. Perceived accessibility varied across sexual health resources. Condoms were rated as the most accessible resource (M = 4.35, SD = 1.05), with 80.7% reporting that condoms were somewhat/very easy to access. Cycletracking information (M = 4.04, SD = 1.11) and reliable sexual health information (M = 3.95, SD = 1.10) were also perceived as relatively accessible, with 71.1% and 68.9% reporting easy access, respectively. Perceived accessibility was lower for birth control (M = 3.82, SD = 1.21), emergency contraception (M = 3.81, SD = 1.17), and STI testing (M = 3.68, SD = 1.20), with 64.1%, 64.2%, and 59.1% reporting easy access, respectively. Abortion services were perceived as the least accessible resource (M = 2.94, SD = 1.41), with 36.6% reporting somewhat/very easy access. Uncertainty was also highest for abortion services, with 19.0% selecting "Not sure." Perceived importance was positively but weakly correlated with accessibility of condoms (r = .093, p < .001), birth control (r = .044, p = .044), and reliable sexual health information (r = .062, p = .004). No significant correlations were observed for the remaining resources (see Table 2).

RQ2: Perceived access and utilization. Among participants for whom each behavior was relevant, greater perceived accessibility was associated with higher odds of typical condom use (OR = 1.19, 95% CI [1.06, 1.33], p = .002), typical birth control use (OR = 1.44, 95% CI [1.30, 1.60], p < .001), and STI testing in the past 12 months (OR = 1.24, 95% CI [1.14, 1.35], p < .001; see Table 3). However, among participants who perceived these resources as accessible (i.e., somewhat or very easy to access), 54.5% did not report typical condom use, 53.5% did not report typical birth control use, and 42.3% had not been tested for STIs in the past 12 months.

These access-use gaps were somewhat larger in the full sample (59.1% for condoms, 62.0% for birth control, and 47.7% for STI testing), which included participants who were not sexually active, but remained substantial when analyses were restricted to participants for whom each behavior was relevant (see Supplementary Table S1).

RQ3: Importance, access, and contraceptive use (restricted sample).

Among participants who reported vaginal or anal intercourse in the past three months, contraception importance was weakly correlated with perceived condom accessibility (ρ = .072, p = .006).

Among participants who reported vaginal intercourse in the past three months, contraception importance was also weakly correlated with perceived birth control accessibility (ρ = .061, p = .025). Logistic regression analyses showed that contraception importance and perceived accessibility were significantly associated with typical condom use (N = 1,455) and birth control use (N = 1,344). Participants who believed contraception was extremely important (vs. not at all important) had higher odds of typical condom use (OR = 12.45, 95% CI [7.97, 19.44], p < .001) and birth control use (OR = 8.29, 95% CI [5.42, 12.69], p < .001), after accounting for perceived accessibility. Participants reporting very easy (vs. very difficult) condom access had higher odds of typical condom use (OR = 3.39, 95% CI [1.32, 8.70], p = .011), while those reporting very easy (vs. very difficult) birth control access had higher odds of typical birth control use (OR = 2.99, 95% CI [1.48, 6.03], p = .002). The interaction between contraception importance and perceived accessibility was significant for condom use (OR = 1.13, 95% CI [1.02, 1.26], p = .019), such that perceived accessibility was more strongly associated with typical condom use at higher levels of contraception importance. This interaction was not significant for birth control use (p = .984; see Table 4). Results were largely consistent in the full sample, although the association between condom access and use was no longer significant (see Supplementary Table S2).

RQ4: Sexual and relational context.

Interactions between perceived accessibility and gender were not statistically significant in predicting typical condom use, χ²(1, N = 1,410) = 0.94, p = .333, or birth control use, χ²(1, N = 1,306) = 0.29, p = .590. Similarly, the interaction between condom accessibility and relationship status was not significant, χ²(2, N = 1,379) = 1.43, p = .488. However, a significant interaction was found between perceived birth control accessibility and relationship status in predicting typical birth control use, χ²(2, N = 1,272) = 14.55, p < .001. The association between accessibility and birth control use was stronger among participants in relationships compared with single participants (OR = 1.60, 95% CI [1.24, 2.06], p < .001). No significant difference was found between casually dating and single participants (p = .562). Predicted probabilities showed that as perceived accessibility increased, the likelihood of reporting typical birth control use increased from approximately 12.3% to 55.1% among participants in relationships, compared with 25.8% to 31.9% among single participants. These findings suggest that the association between perceived accessibility and birth control use varies by relationship status (see Figure 1).

RQ5: Reasons for nonuse despite access.

Overall, the most commonly endorsed barriers were concerns about hormonal contraception side effects (35.1%), limited abortion access affecting willingness to engage in sexual activity (32.5%), cost (29.8%), reduced pleasure from condoms (29.7%), and social media influence (29.2%). Among participants who perceived condoms as accessible but reported nonuse or inconsistent use (n = 937), 41.0% endorsed concerns about reduced sexual pleasure. Endorsement differed significantly by relationship status, χ²(2, N = 888) = 13.73, p = .001, and gender, χ²(1, N = 915) = 8.11, p = .004. Concerns about reduced pleasure were more common among casually dating participants (53.6%) than single participants (41.7%) or those in relationships (37.0%), and among men (46.5%) than women (37.1%).

Among participants reporting vaginal intercourse in the past three months who perceived birth control as accessible but did not report typically using it (n = 502), 49.2% agreed that concerns about hormonal side effects discouraged use, 41.8% agreed that social media content about hormonal birth control made them less likely to want to use it, and 30.9% endorsed cost concerns. Social media concerns differed significantly by relationship status (p = .008) and gender (p = .015), with higher endorsement among casually dating participants (55.1%) compared with those who were single (33.9%) or in a relationship (41.5%), and among women (47.0%) compared with men (36.2%).

When asked about potential reasons other than side effects for avoiding hormonal birth control, participants most frequently selected disliking the idea of hormones (29.1%), feeling that hormonal birth control was unnatural or unhealthy (28.7%), and perceiving no need for it (22.7%). Other reasons included previous negative experiences with hormonal birth control (21.9%), feeling that responsibility for contraception falls disproportionately on women (18.5%), not feeling sufficiently informed about available options (13.5%), religious or personal beliefs (11.6%), friends not using hormonal birth control (8.0%), and other reasons (3.2%) (see Table 5).

Contraceptive Use Consistency.

Among participants reporting vaginal intercourse in the past three months (n = 1,422), 43.8% reported no condom use, 39.9% reported inconsistent use (i.e., during less than 100% of encounters), and 16.3% reported consistent use. For anal intercourse (n = 647), these percentages were 33.4%, 53.8%, and 12.8%, respectively. For birth control, 40.0% reported no use, 33.3% reported inconsistent use, and 26.7% reported consistent use. Typical method selection was significantly associated with reported consistency across all three measures (all p < .001). However, among participants selecting condoms or birth control as typical methods, only 36.6% reported consistent condom use during vaginal intercourse and 58.1% reported consistent birth control use, suggesting that typical method selection did not necessarily indicate consistent use.

Discussion

The present study examined whether perceived access to sexual health resources translates into their use among young adults. Our findings suggest that access matters but is not sufficient. Participants who perceived condoms, birth control, and STI testing as more accessible were more likely to report using them, yet roughly half of sexually active participants with easy access did not typically use condoms or birth control, and over 40% had not been tested for STIs in the past year. The importance participants placed on contraception was strongly associated with use, even after accounting for access, and was only weakly correlated with access itself. The association between birth control access and use was stronger among partnered than single participants, whereas gender did not moderate associations between access and use. Finally, among those with access who did not use these resources, concerns about reduced pleasure, hormonal side effects, and social media content were common.

These findings are consistent with the COM-B model's proposition that behavior emerges from the interplay of capability, opportunity, and motivation rather than from any single component (Michie et al., 2011). Perceived access, our indicator of opportunity, and contraceptive importance, our indicator of reflective motivation, were each independently associated with protective behavior. They were also largely distinct from one another: importance was only weakly correlated with perceived access to condoms (ρ = .07) and birth control (ρ = .06). Young adults who value contraception are not necessarily those who find it easiest to obtain, and vice versa. Yet neither component guaranteed use, consistent with prior applications of COM-B to sexual health (Bru Garcia et al., 2022). This pattern points to a broader theoretical contribution. Much of the sexual health literature implicitly treats either access or attitudes as the primary lever for change. Our findings suggest that neither alone appears sufficient. Understanding protective behavior requires attending to the conditions under which opportunity and motivation do, and do not, translate into action.

Consistent with research showing that structural barriers such as cost, transportation, and limited availability constrain young adults' use of sexual health services (Reeves et al., 2023 (Reeves et al., , 2024)), greater perceived access was associated with greater use, and nearly a third of participants reported that cost made it difficult to access sexual health resources. Access was also uneven across resources: STI testing and especially abortion services were perceived as less accessible than condoms, and nearly one in five participants were unsure how accessible abortion services were. At the same time, the access-use gaps we observed suggest that availability is only one piece of the puzzle. Prior research has largely focused on identifying who lacks access and why.

Our findings extend this work by showing that roughly half of sexually active young adults who perceived resources as readily available did not typically use condoms or birth control. Our estimates of this gap are likely conservative, because many participants who reported condoms or birth control as their typical method did not use them consistently: only 36.6% of those who typically used condoms reported using them during every instance of vaginal intercourse, and only 58.1% of those who typically used birth control reported consistent use. For a substantial proportion of young adults, then, the barrier to protective behavior does not appear to lie in obtaining resources but in the decision to use them, and to use them consistently. The perceived importance of contraception was strongly associated with use, even after accounting for perceived access. Compared with participants who rated contraception as not at all important, those who rated it extremely important had roughly 12 times higher odds of typical condom use and 8 times higher odds of typical birth control use. Although the two predictors were measured on different scales and comparisons should be made cautiously, these associations were considerably larger than those for ease of access. This suggests that young adults' evaluations of protection carry information about their behavior that is not captured by whether resources are available to them.

Importance and access also interacted with condom use, such that the association between access and condom use was stronger among participants who placed greater importance on contraception. This interaction is consistent with COM-B's view of opportunity and motivation as distinct but interacting components of behavior (Michie et al., 2011) and suggests that efforts to improve access may be most effective among those who already value the behavior. The interaction did not emerge for birth control, which may reflect differences in how these behaviors unfold. Condom use is an event-level decision that must be made in each sexual encounter, so having condoms accessible may matter most for those motivated to use them in the moment. Birth control, once initiated, requires less ongoing decision-making. Because our data is cross-sectional, however, we cannot conclude that importance causes use (see Limitations).

The barriers participants reported offer insight into why accessible resources go unused.

Among participants who perceived condoms as accessible but used them inconsistently or not at all, 41.0% reported that reduced pleasure made them less likely to use condoms, echoing a long line of research identifying diminished sensation and disrupted intimacy as reasons for inconsistent condom use (Randolph et al., 2007; Rodrigues & Lopes, 2022). Among participants who perceived birth control as accessible but did not use it, nearly half reported that concerns about hormonal side effects discouraged use. This is consistent with research identifying side effects and method acceptability as leading reasons for avoiding or discontinuing hormonal methods (Frost et al., 2012; Le Guen et al., 2021; Littlejohn, 2012; Moreau et al., 2007).

Notably, 30.9% also reported that cost affected their use. Perceiving birth control as "easy to access" may therefore not capture whether it is affordable: individuals may know where to obtain it, but without insurance or the financial resources to pay for it, it remains effectively inaccessible.

Social media also emerged as a meaningful influence. Among participants with access who did not use birth control, 41.8% reported that social media content about hormonal birth control made them less likely to want to use it. Given the largely negative and often low-quality contraceptive content young adults encounter online (see Introduction), our findings suggest that these information environments may shape not only what young adults believe about hormonal contraception but also whether they use it when it is available to them. Any resulting shifts away from hormonal methods may also have longer-term population-level implications. Declines in adolescent and young adult pregnancy over recent decades have been attributed largely to improvements in contraceptive use (Lindberg et al., 2016). If young people increasingly move away from hormonal methods, these gains could slow or reverse, although continued declines in sexual activity may offset some of this effect. Future research tracking contraceptive method choice alongside unintended pregnancy rates would help clarify whether these consequences emerge.

Notably, these concerns remained salient even among young adults who had access to protective methods and perceived contraception as important. Theories of attitudinal ambivalence offer one explanation. People can hold positive and negative evaluations of the same object at once (Kaplan, 1972; Thompson et al., 1995); a young adult may see birth control as important for preventing pregnancy while also worrying about its effects on their mood, body, or sexual experiences. Ambivalent attitudes are less stable and less predictive of behavior than consistently positive or negative attitudes (Armitage & Conner, 2000; Conner et al., 2002), because whichever evaluation is most salient in the moment tends to guide action (van Harreveld et al., 2015), and mixed feelings about pregnancy have been linked to less consistent contraceptive use (Higgins et al., 2012). This may help explain why protective behavior was often inconsistent rather than absent. Inconsistent use may also reflect cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957): when behavior falls short of what people believe matters, they may downplay their need for protection or their personal risk. Several reasons participants gave for nonuse, such as not needing it or trusting their partner, are consistent with this kind of justification, although our data cannot determine whether these reasons preceded or followed nonuse. Within COM-B, then, opportunity and reflective motivation may be offset by competing motivations, so that a young adult can value protection and have it within reach, yet still decide that, in a given moment or with a given method, the drawbacks outweigh the benefits.

The association between birth control access and use depended on relationship status. Among partnered participants, the predicted probability of typical birth control use rose from approximately 12% at the lowest level of perceived access to 55% at the highest. Among single participants, it changed little across levels of access (26% to 32%), and casually dating participants did not differ from single participants. These patterns are consistent with research showing that contraceptive decisions are embedded in relationships, and that as trust, commitment, and perceived monogamy grow, couples often shift from condoms toward hormonal methods (Fortenberry, 2019; Rodrigues, 2022; Santos et al., 2024). For partnered young adults, sex may be more regular and predictable, making ongoing methods such as birth control a more natural fit once they are accessible. Single young adults may have sex less frequently or less predictably, which may weaken the link between having access to birth control and using it.

The association between birth control access and use was stronger among partnered than single or casually dating participants. This is consistent with research showing that contraceptive decisions are embedded in relationships, and that as trust, commitment, and perceived monogamy grow, couples often shift from condoms toward hormonal methods (Fortenberry, 2019; Rodrigues & Lopes, 2022; Santos et al., 2024). For partnered young adults, sex may be more regular and predictable, making ongoing methods such as birth control a more natural fit once accessible. Relationship context also shaped barriers: casually dating participants were most likely to cite reduced pleasure as a reason for inconsistent condom use and to report that social media discouraged hormonal birth control use. This is notable because casual partnerships may carry greater STI risk, yet it is precisely in this context that pleasure concerns were most likely to discourage condom use. By contrast, gender did not moderate associations between access and use, suggesting that the access-use gap is not specific to men or women, although the burden of pregnancy prevention often falls on women (Breakey et al., 2026; Fennell, 2011). Gender did shape specific barriers, however: men more often cited reduced pleasure, whereas women more often reported that social media discouraged hormonal birth control use.

Implications for Sexual Health Interventions

Taken together, our findings suggest that interventions focused exclusively on increasing the availability or affordability of sexual health resources may not fully address the barriers young adults face. Expanding access remains essential, particularly for STI testing and abortion services, which were perceived as the least accessible. But its benefits may be limited if young adults do not see protection as important, or if concerns about pleasure, side effects, or hormones outweigh that importance. Interventions may therefore benefit from targeting multiple COM-B components at once: addressing hormonal concerns directly with accurate information about side effects and the full range of methods, including non-hormonal options; framing condom use in ways that acknowledge, rather than dismiss, pleasure, particularly for men and young adults in casual relationships; supporting partner communication about contraception; and countering lowquality social media content by expanding credible, engaging sources of sexual health information created or, at the very least, vetted by health professionals.

Interventions may also benefit from attending to channel factors: small, seemingly minor features of a situation that make the path from intention to action easier or harder (Lewin, 1951).

In a classic demonstration, students who received persuasive information about tetanus shots were far more likely to get vaccinated when they were also given a map with the health center circled and asked to plan when they would go (Leventhal et al., 1965). Similarly, even young adults who value protection and know where to get it may not use it when doing so takes effort.

Having to plan ahead, book an appointment, pay for services, or ask a pharmacist for a product can be enough to stop them. Condoms could be made freely available in private, low-effort settings (e.g., university health centers), STI testing could be offered on a walk-in or at-home basis (e.g., at-home testing kits from services such as myLAB Box, Nurx, and CVS), and young adults could be prompted to make concrete plans for obtaining contraception and STI testing.

Interventions could also address ambivalence directly. Motivational interviewing (MI), a counseling approach designed to help people explore and resolve mixed feelings about a behavior, has shown promise for increasing condom use (Kuyper et al., 2009) and contraceptive uptake (Wilson et al., 2015), although results have been inconsistent, particularly for brief or phone-based formats (e.g., Project Reach; Kirby et al., 2010). Mobile phone programs offer a way to reach young people privately and at a larger scale than in-person efforts, but evidence that they change contraceptive behavior, rather than knowledge, remains limited (Ippolito & L'Engle, 2017). Simply reinforcing that protection is important does not acknowledge young adults' concerns about pleasure, side effects, or hormones. Approaches that acknowledge these concerns and help young adults weigh them against their goals may be more effective at closing the gap between valuing protection and using it.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal conclusions. We cannot determine whether access and importance lead to use, whether use shapes perceptions of access and importance, or whether these associations reflect unmeasured third variables, such as perceived pregnancy or STI risk.

Longitudinal and experimental designs would help clarify the direction of these effects.

Second, all measures were self-reported and may be subject to recall and social desirability biases, particularly for sensitive behaviors such as condom use and STI testing. To the extent that participants overreported protective behaviors, the access-use gaps we observed may be conservative estimates. Future research could link survey data to more objective indicators of use, such as prescription records or documented STI testing.

Third, our measures of opportunity and motivation were limited. Perceived access was assessed with a single item and may not reflect objective availability or affordability, as the cost findings suggest; future research should assess insurance coverage and income directly.

Similarly, contraceptive importance was assessed with a single item that could not distinguish young adults who feel consistently positive about contraception from those who also hold reservations, and importance was not assessed for STI testing. Measuring positive and negative evaluations of each method separately would allow a direct test of ambivalence.

Fourth, barriers were assessed only among participants who had access but did not use, or inconsistently used, a given resource. These data describe the concerns reported by this group but cannot show whether such concerns distinguish non-users from consistent users.

Fifth, our data come from a large, diverse national sample recruited through an opt-in online panel. Although quotas helped ensure diversity, the sample was not probability-based and the data were unweighted, so our estimates should not be read as precise nationally representative figures. Findings may also not generalize to young adults outside the U.S., where health care systems and reproductive health policies differ.

Finally, although COM-B guided our interpretation, the present study did not directly test the full model. Capability was not measured, and our indicators captured only some facets of opportunity and motivation. Future research that measures all COM-B components, ideally with longitudinal and dyadic designs, would allow a more complete test of how they jointly shape young adults' sexual health behavior.

Conclusion

As young adults engage in less partnered sexual activity, it is tempting to assume that less sex means less risk. Our findings suggest that the sexual health of young adults depends not only on how often they have sex, but on whether they protect themselves when they do. Among sexually active young adults, perceived access to condoms, birth control, and STI testing was associated with greater use, yet roughly half of those with access did not typically use condoms or birth control. The importance young adults placed on contraception, their relationship context, and concerns about pleasure, hormones, and what they encounter on social media all helped explain when access did and did not translate into protection. Access matters, but it is not sufficient.
