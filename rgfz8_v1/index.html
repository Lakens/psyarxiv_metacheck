Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Adolescent mental health in India has become a major concern. Reports from UNICEF (2024) and NCERT (2022) note that almost 1 in 10 individuals in India is currently facing a mental health concern, affecting almost 7.3% of the young population in the nation. Clinical reports in the country highlight that this prevalence further increases for adolescents, with 23.3% mental health morbidity (Balamurugan et al., 2024; Malhotra & Patra, 2014). Potential reasons for this state, especially in India, are often understood to prevail due to multiple factors such as issues with peers, family, academic stress, body image issues, internet addiction, conduct problems, and heightened psychological distress (Rajkumar et al., 2022; Balamurugan et al., 2024; Malhotra & Patra, 2014; Mahanta et al., 2026). Wherein, distress due to depression, stress, and anxiety further contributes to the depreciated mental health among adolescents.

Looking at the national statistics, school surveys in the country noted that more than 11% of students report concerns of anxiety, with 14% experiencing extreme emotions of sadness, anger, guilt, and pressure (NCERT, 2022). Herein, prominent reasons included mood swings, feelings of self-harm, fear and panic attacks, along with extreme emotions following personal/self responsibility to do well in school, where 84% of students share concerns of feeling distressed. Policy-wise, India has been working towards students' stress management and mental well-being by suggesting nationwide interventions for emotional regulation and management (NEP, 2020); however, the impact is still lacking in substantial evidence. Studies report that despite the policy's framework on mental health, students in school still experience a high prevalence of deteriorated mental well-being. Systematic studies and narrative reviews culminate that this ranges between 3.80% and 50% of reported depression and anxiety currently (Mahanta et al., 2026; Ray et al., 2025; Girase et al., 2022). Although school-based mental health initiatives have been found to help reduce the stigma around mental health, trainable emotion regulation skills are still lacking.

Research on self-compassion interventions among adolescents across the world has been found effective in this context. Globally, studies have found consistent evidence of self-compassion in helping teens manage their mental health, with its significant association with reduced psychological distress, reduction of body image concerns, guilt, shame, and self-criticism (Chio et al., 2021; Crisan et al., 2023; March et al., 2018; Young & Kotera, 2022). Cumulative benefits on well-being outcomes report improved well-being, adaptive coping, and resilience (Bluth et al., 2017 (Bluth et al., , 2023;; Miyagawa et al., 2023; Ueno & Amemiya, 2024). Conceptually, Neff (2003) proposes self-compassion as an approach of turning compassion inwards, where an individual engages in increased compassionate self-responding with reduced uncompassionate selfresponding in moments of suffering, failures, and feelings of inadequacy. Her work provides that self-compassion as a construct includes six sub-factors which together work in tandem to create an overall self-compassionate mind (Neff, 2003; Neff et al., 2019). In order to have higher selfcompassion, individuals are to have higher scores on the positive indicators of compassionate self-responding, such as self-kindness, common humanity, and mindful acceptance, and lower scores on uncompassionate self-responding, including self-judgement, isolation, and overidentification (Neff, 2003; Neff et al., 2019). Logically, this multi-dimensional model suggests treating oneself with kindness (self-kindness) in moments of struggle and failure instead of overly judging oneself (self-judgement); while also viewing the hardships as a part of humanity (common-humanity) instead of isolating the tough experiences which could be mindfully accepted as they are (mindful acceptance) rather than over-reacted with (over-identification).

Following this theoretical model, Neff and colleagues developed and validated the scales, including the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) for adults (Neff, 2003), the Self-Compassion Scale for Youth (SCS-Y; Neff et al., 2019), the State Self-Compassion Scale (SSCS; Neff et al., 2021) and their subsequent short forms such as the Self-Compassion Scale Short-Form (SCS-SF; Raes et al., 2011) and the State Self-Compassion Scale Short-Form (SSCS-SF; Neff et al., 2021). All her scales and models note that in order to be self-compassionate, an individual must retain a higher score on self-kindness, common humanity, and mindful acceptance, along with lower scores on self-judgement, isolation, and over-identification (Neff, 2003 (Neff, , 2016;; Neff & Tóth-Király, 2025). Psychometric findings on the scale construction over these years have established it as a reliable and valid scale (Neff & Tóth-Király, 2025). At one side where its internal consistency had increasingly evidenced item reliability, psychometric validity of the scale has reported its factorial extraction in multiple-factor structures (Neff et al., 2019). Research has found that the scale largely establishes as a general factor solution, where 6-factor solutions provide an excellent fit in most populations.

A large sample study (Neff et al., 2019) from twenty diverse countries, including China, Japan, Korea, the U.S., Brazil, Switzerland, Spain, the UK, Hungary, Norway, Belgium, Australia, and more, also found similar evidence for the bi-factor ESEM model of the SCS, which encompasses the global self-compassion factor along with the six subscales. Although this study captured the factor confirmation for bi-factor ESEM models among the SCS for adults (Neff et al., 2019), studies have obtained similar component structures across adolescent samples from diverse nations (Neff et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2022; Nazari et al., 2022). Methodologically, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) confirmed these findings for a long time for a six-factor structure and total score; however, after 2019, Neff (Neff et al., 2019) recommended the use of bifactorial exploratory structural equation modelling (ESEM) as the most appropriate analysis for validating the factor structure of the SCS in diverse samples (Neff et al., 2019). The same has been proposed for the Self-Compassion Scale for Youth as well (SCS-Y; Neff et al., 2021), where the authors particularly elaborate on their two major reasons for conducting bifactor ESEM models over CFA models. Neff and colleagues (2021) particularly suggested the use of the bifactor ESEM framework because, firstly, it allowed estimated cross-loadings of the factors on other factors which CFA restricts because of its property of forced zero loadings on other factors. Secondly, this framework also allowed the cross-loading of all six factors on the overall general factor of the self-compassion construct, which provided a theoretically consistent and holistic understanding of multi-dimensional interconnected self-compassion.

Nonetheless, there is an ongoing debate regarding how self-compassion is operationalized and whether the factor structure of the SCS and related scales is robust. For example, Muris et al. (2019) suggest that compassionate self-responding and uncompassionate self-responding are separate and independent constructs and that self-compassion should be defined only by the former construct, as the latter merely reflects psychopathology. Furthermore, Chio et al. (2021) claim that these two types of self-responding operate independently, particularly in dialectic cultures which value the balance of two opposites, such as positive and negative emotions. Therefore, further research in underrepresented samples is needed to delineate our understanding of the operationalization and assessment of self-compassion.

Given the unparalleled benefits of self-compassion in multiple cultures, exploration of its efficacy in collectivistic and unitedly diverse countries like India thus becomes equally important (Chopra et al., 2025). Since constructs of the self may unfold differently across cultures (Kitayama et al., 2022), exploration of its psychometric properties helps for two major reasons. First, exploring and establishing the factorial validity will provide evidence for its use in different cultural contexts like India. Second, an advanced investigation of its internal factor structure will inform the ongoing work on self-compassion in the nation. Previous studies on the validation of the SCS-Y among Indian youth for its English version had found evidence for a total score and six-factor structure (Chopra et al., 2025) among adolescents. The study examined the factor structure of the SCS-Y using three CFA models, namely, one-factor CFA, two-factor CFA, and six-factor CFA. Goodness-of-fit indices, factor covariance estimation, and reliability indices together supported the one-factor and six-factor CFA models of self-compassion. Although the previous work, as a first inquiry of self-compassion validation in the nation, confirmed its valid and reliable use among adolescents, it only used the CFA approaches. Since the original authors of the scale (Neff et al., 2019) suggest an advanced estimation for the bifactorial estimation of the scale, the present study examined the factor structure using more comprehensive approaches, including bifactorial ESEM and alternative models proposed by previous studies. Thus, the present study will become an informed step for globally consistent research and provide insight into how self-compassion is operationalized among Indian adolescents. Secondly, the present study also builds upon the previous work by extending the linguistic validation of the self-compassion scale for youth (SCS-Y) in Hindi (SCS-Y, Hindi), making it the first validation of the tool for Hindi-speaking populations in the nation.

Present Study

With this in mind, the present study aimed to explore the factorial validity of the SCS-Y in the Indian context. To fill the important gaps in linguistic reception of items, the study used both an English version (Study 1) (SCS-Y, English) as well as a Hindi translated version of the scale (SCS-Y, Hindi) (Study 2) for validation. Analytically, it proposed to measure the construct through nine models as used in the previous research (Miyagaya et al., 2022; Neff et al., 2019), where all factor structures such as single factor, two factor, as well as six-factors solutions were parallelly tested using both CFA and ESEM (Figure 1). On the one hand, the study extends the previous research (Chopra et al., 2025; Neff et al., 2021), it also becomes the first investigation of the translation and validation of the Hindi version of SCS-Y in India. Additionally, the objective of exploring demographic variability in self-compassion among adolescents in the country advances further knowledge of its cohort-specific unfolding in contemporary society. Since research assembles cumulative evidence of the efficacy of self-compassion in relation to adolescent distress, the present study becomes a grounded reference for its need by gender, age, and grade.

Study 1: English Scale Validation (SCS-Y, English)

Study 1 tested the nine models of self-compassion validation for the English language selfcompassion scale for youth (SCS-Y, English) among adolescents in India. It followed the previous validation studies of SCS-Y (Self-Compassion Scale for Youth; Neff et al., 2021a) along with the SCS (Self-Compassion Scale for Adults; Neff et al., 2019) and the SSCS (State Self-Compassion Scale; Neff et al., 2021b). The study is the first exploration of nine model structures of self-compassion among youth, particularly in India. It also expands on the previous work (Chopra et al., 2025), which tested the self-compassion scale for youth (SCS-Y) validation in India through three models such as one-factor CFA, two-factor CFA, and six-factor CFA. Since global validation exploration of the scale has often reported the bifactor ESEM solution, the present study examines the same for Indian adolescents. The study also assesses the reliability estimation using Cronbach's alpha coefficient (α) and McDonald's omega coefficient (ω), to completely understand its psychometric reliability and validity for Indian contexts. Additionally, the study also explored the demographic variability of self-compassion and its six components among Indian adolescents across age, gender, and grade.

Method

Participants: The participants included a total of 900 adolescents from 7 schools across Delhi. The data were collected as per Simonsohn's (2015) criterion, which suggests two and a half times the data of the original study for its effective replication. Given that Neff et al. (2021; Study 1) had 279 participants, the present data was targeted to have 698 participants and above. It was collected using convenience sampling from all students in all the mentioned classes. Selection of schools was finalized on the first come permissions. All students in the study studied in classes 7th-12th in both public and private schools in Delhi National Capital Region. Here, 165 (18.8%) students studied in class 7th, 175 (19.8%) students in class 8th, 213 (24.1%) from class 9th, 200 (22.6%) in class 10th, 46 (5.2%) in class 11th, and 85 (9.6%) in class 12th. The age of the participants ranged from 12 to 17 years with an average age of 13.7 years (SD=1.45). In terms of gender, 543 (61.1%) students were male participants and 346 (38.9%) students were female participants. The distribution represents the general gender distribution in co-ed schools. All these participants were proficient in English and studied in schools following a central board of secondary education (CBSE curriculum).

Measures: The Self-Compassion Scale for Youth (SCS-Y; Neff et al., 2021) was used to assess self-compassion in adolescents. This scale is in English and comprises 17 items in total. The scale measures a total score of self-compassion along with six sub-scores on six sub-scales of self-compassion, namely, self-kindness (eg; 'I try to be kind and supportive to myself when I'm having a hard time') vs self-judgement (eg; 'When I notice things about myself that I don't like, I get really frustrated'), common-humanity (eg; 'When I feel I'm not "good enough" in some way, I try to remind myself that other people sometimes feel this way too'), vs isolation (eg; 'When I feel sad or down, it seems like I'm the only one who feels that way'), mindful acceptance (eg; 'When something upsetting happens, I try to see things as they are without blowing it out of proportion') vs over-identification (eg; 'When I feel frustrated or disappointed, I think about it over and over again'). On the scale, the participants reported their behavior to themselves in difficult times, as mentioned in the statements. Their responses ranged on a Likert scale from 1 to 5, with 1 representing 'Almost Never' and 5 indicating 'Almost Always'.

Procedure of Data Collection:

The data were collected from 7 schools in total. Entry to all these schools was made through the school management and school principals. Once the school principals (heads of the schools) understood the study, permissions were granted for data collection. The school principals in all these schools organized a meeting with the school management committees for the same, which was attended by the parents, teachers, and school heads. All authorities, including parents, guardians, and teachers, provided their informed consent for the study through a signed document. After this, the students completed a pen-andpaper survey seeking demographic information along with information on their self-compassion. The survey commenced as a class activity, which was concluded in one class that spanned for 45 minutes. After the survey, the participants were informed about self-compassion and also offered an opportunity for self-compassion training. Participants' confidentiality was ensured at all steps, with no collection or documentation of the names of adolescents, their parents, or the school. The participants were also thanked for their participation after the completion of their survey, and provided with a complimentary session on emotional well-being by the first author.

Analysis:

Following the validation studies on the SCS-Y (Neff et al., 2021) along with the SCS (Neff et al., 2019) and the SSCS (Neff et al., 2021b), we compared and contrasted nine models using CFA and ESEM. These models were one-factor CFA (representing a general selfcompassion factor), two-factor correlated CFA and ESEM (representing two correlated factors of compassionate and uncompassionate self-responding), six-factor correlated CFA and ESEM (representing the six facets of self-compassion), bifactor CFA and ESEM (representing a general self-compassion factor and its six facets), and correlated two-bifactor CFA and ESEM models (representing two correlated general factors of compassionate and uncompassionate selfresponding and the six specific components). The CFA and ESEM models differ in how crossloadings are defined. While the CFA models constrain cross-loadings to be zero, the ESEM model allows cross-loadings that should be targeted to be as close to zero as possible (Morin et al., 2016 (Morin et al., , 2020)). Following previous studies (Morin et al., 2016 , 2020 , Neff et al., 2019 , 2021), we selected the ESEM models if they showed (1) improved model fit, (2) well-defined factors with reasonable main loadings, (3) cross-loadings with acceptable magnitude, and (4) decreased factor correlations relative to CFA alternatives.

Following previous validation studies on the self-compassion-related measures (Neff et al., 2019), we compared the bifactor models with the first-order correlated counterpart. We retained the bifactor model if (1) it showed a similar level of fit, (2) a well-defined general factor, and (3) a subset of specific factors that were well-defined (Morin et al., 2016 (Morin et al., , 2020)).

Based on previous studies (Neff et al., 2019, we estimated these models with the weighted least squares mean-and variance-adjusted estimator (WLSMV) using Mplus version 8.7. In the bifactor models, we used an oblique target rotation (Morin et al., 2016 (Morin et al., , 2020;; Neff et al., 2019 Neff et al., , 2021)). We used sample-size-independent fit indices to test the model fit of these nine models: the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). Based on commonly used guidelines (Hu and Bentler, 1999; Yu, 2002; Marsh et al., 2005), we considered values greater than 0.90 and 0.95 for the CFI and TLI as indicating adequate and excellent fit, whereas values smaller than 0.08 or 0.06 for the RMSEA as showing acceptable and excellent fit, respectively.

We assessed the reliability of the obtained models using Cronbach's alpha coefficient (α) and McDonald's omega coefficient (ω). If the bifactor model was selected, we calculated omega hierarchical (ωH), which represents the percentage of variance in the total scores explained by the general factors (Rodriguez et al., 2016). Following the previous validation studies on the selfcompassion-related measures (Neff et al., 2019, we divided ωH by ω to examine the reliable variance in the total score that was not explained by error. The value above 0.75 supports the use of the total scores, as it indicates that about 75 percent of the variance is explained by general factors (Reis et al., 2013).

Following Neff et al. (2021a), we compared the age, gender, and grade-related differences in the SCS-Y scores using bivariate correlations, independent-samples t-tests, and an analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post-hoc tests, respectively. We also explored the age and gender interactions and gender and grade-related interactions (see Neff et al., 2021, for a similar approach).

Results and Brief Discussion

Model evaluation: Table 1 displays the goodness-of-fit indices for the nine models of the SCS-Y. The one-factor and two-factor CFA models did not achieve the criterion for model fit. The two-factor ESEM and six-factor CFA models showed acceptable, but not optimal, fit. The sixfactor ESEM model displayed excellent fit, and the fit indices of this model improved relative to the six-factor CFA alternative (ΔCFI = 0.076, ΔTLI = 0.090, ΔRMSEA = -0.038). Regarding the bifactor solution, the bifactor CFA model showed poor fit, and the bifactor ESEM and the twobifactor CFA did not converge. The two-bifactor ESEM model had a model specification error. a Equality constraints were placed on the factor loadings of the two Over-Identification items for model convergence. b The residual covariance matrix was not positive defined.

Overall, the examination of the model fit indices supported the six-factor ESEM model of the SCS-Y. Therefore, we further inspected the details of this model in terms of factor loadings, correlations, and reliability. In this model, all factor loadings on target factors were significant (Table 2). The factor loadings were acceptable for self-judgment (λ = .453 to .629, M = .527), isolation (λ = .287 to .859, M = .563), mindfulness (λ = .388 to .585, M = .464), and over-identification (λ = .379 to .726, M = .553). The factor loadings for self-kindness were relatively lower (λ = .308 to .405, M = .365). We found that one item (CH1; λ = 1.027) showed a factor loading greater than one (λ = .242 to 1.027, M = .596). Although we found multiple statistically significant cross-loadings in this model, only one item (CH3) had a higher crossloading than its target loading. We found decreased factor correlations of the ESEM model (r = .046 to .632, M = .299) relative to the CFA alternative (r = .164 to .939, M = .522). Finally, we calculated Cronbach's alpha coefficients and McDonald's model-based reliability based on the six-factor ESEM solution of the SCS-Y (Table 3). While the common humanity subscale showed acceptable coefficients, the other subscales were relatively close to or lower than the conventional criteria for these indicators (α and ω > .70). Demographic differences: Next, we examined demographic differences in the six subscales of the SCS-Y. Age was significantly associated with self-judgment (r = .113, p < .001), isolation (r = .087, p = .010), and over-identification (r = .082, p = .014), but the magnitude of these correlations was small. We found significant gender differences in self-judgment, t (887) = 2.78, p = .006, d = 0.19, isolation, t (887) = 2.82, p = .005, d = 0.19, and over-identification, t (887) = 4.98, p < .001, d = 0.34. Girls were significantly higher in self-judgment, isolation, and overidentification than boys (Table 4). We found a grade-related difference in over-identification, F (5, 872) < 3.49, p = .004, partial η² = .02. Subsequent pos-hoc Bonferroni tests indicated that students in 8 th grade (M = 3.05, SD = 0.09) were significantly lower in over-identification than those in the 10 th (M = 3.43, SD = 0.08) and the 12 th grades (M = 3.57, SD = 0.14).

Discussion Summary:

The results provided evidence for the six-factor ESEM of the SCS-Y in that it showed a better fit to the data relative to the alternative models, decreased correlations among the factors relative to the six-factor CFA model, and acceptable factor loadings of most scales. However, factor loadings of several scales were not optimal alongside the relatively low reliability, suggesting that some improvement or modification of the SCS-Y may be needed in contexts other than the original study (Neff et al., 2021). Additionally, we did not find support for the bifactor and two-bifactor models; thus, the six facets of self-compassion may operate independently without formulating an overarching self-compassionate mind in our sample.

Given that our sample size exceeded the criterion of the replication study (Simonsohn, 2015), we consider our results relatively stable. Nevertheless, we proceeded to Study 2 to replicate our findings in a different sample in India using the Hindi version of the SCS-Y (SCS-Y, Hindi).

Study 2: Hindi Scale Validation (SCS-Y, Hindi)

Study 2 attempted to replicate the validation procedure for the adapted Hindi language version of the SCS-Y (SCS-Y, Hindi) among Indian adolescents. As in Study 1, this study explored the nine models using CFA and ESEM (Figure 1) estimations. Although previous factorial validity of SCS-Y among Indian adolescents (Chopra et al., 2025) provided evidence for one-and six factor CFA structures; it did not explore the same for the Hindi language scale. Bridging the previous gaps, the present study becomes the first research exploration that examines the scale validation for the Hindi-adapted SCS-Y (SCS-Y, Hindi). This study explores both the CFA as well as bifactor ESEM estimations for the scale in its Hindi language adaptation; as suggested by the original authors (Neff et al., 2021a), across multiple scale validations (explained in Study 1).

Like Study 1, this study also explores demographic variability of self-compassion and its significant factor structures for its Hindi-language participants across age, grade, and gender.

Method

Participants: The participants in the present study included a total of 714 students from 6 schools in the capital, Delhi. The data were collected from public schools in Delhi, using convenience sampling. Most of the public schools have a majority of students who are proficient in the Hindi language, with only a few of them being proficient in both Hindi and English. Classwise, a total of 259 (36.3%) students studied in class 9th, 256 (35.9%) studied in class 10th, 116 (16.2%) studied in class 11th, and 83 (11.3%) studied in class 12th. The age of the participants ranged between 13 and 18, with an average age of 15.0 years (SD=1.12). With respect to gender, 471 (66%) students were male participants and 242 (33.9%) were female participants. The gender distribution represents the actual gender-based distribution in schools in the capital, which has a 2:3 ratio of genders. All the students participating in the study were proficient in Hindi and studying in schools that followed the educational curriculum of the central board of secondary education (CBSE).

Measures:

The study used a Hindi version of the SCS-Y. Similar to the original scale (Neff et al., 2021), the scale was composed of 17 items. All the 17 items on the scale together provided a total score of self-compassion, along with six scores from the sub-factors of the self-compassion, such as self-kindness (Items 1, 9 and 15) vs self-judgement (Items 3, 7 and 12), commonhumanity (Items 4, 8 and 13) vs isolation (Items 2, 10 and 16) and mindful acceptance (Items 6, 11 and 17) vs over-identification (Items 5 and 14). The participants were instructed to provide honest responses during difficult times on several items, using a 5-point Likert-type scale, which collected their responses from 1, 'Almost Never' to 5, 'Almost Always'

Hindi Version of the Self-Compassion Scale for Youth (SCS-Y Hindi):

The Hindi version of the SCS-Y was made from the direct translation of the scale in Hindi. The procedure followed a two-step process in which a Hindi language expert first translated the entire scale into Hindi, along with the first author's discussion to keep the language in everyday dialect. Once the scale was translated into Hindi, its back translation was initiated with another bilingual expert who translated the Hindi version back to English. Once the process was complete, the authors crosschecked the translated versions of both Hindi and English for their similarity. Additionally, the school counsellors, proficient in Hindi, were also asked to check the item readability for students. Feedback from all levels, including those from school counsellors, authors, and bilingual experts, was included and resolved in subsequent discussions. Finally, as the researchers agreed with the translated version, a pilot version was conducted with the students from a Hindi medium school. The administration of the scale and collecting responses from the students ensured the translated version's accuracy in their understanding of the scale in Hindi, as similar to its English version. The Hindi Version of the SCS-Y (attached in the supplementary material) collects the responses of adolescents towards themselves on their difficult days, using a 5-point Likert-type scale from 1, 'Almost Never' to 5, 'Almost Always'.

Procedure: The overall procedure of permission, informed consent, and data collection for the participants on the Hindi version of the scale followed the same approach as Study 1. All the participants in the study belonged to public schools, which had both Hindi and Bilingual mediums of education. All students were proficient in the Hindi language and filled out the Hindi survey versions. The participants took a total of one class (45 minutes) for the entire procedure, which included a small rapport formation (10 minutes), brief information about the survey collected for mental health (5 minutes), survey conduction (15-20 minutes), and debrief (10 minutes). After the conduction of the survey, all the participants were thanked for their participation and proceeded to a complimentary session on emotional well-being, conducted by the first author.

Analysis: We followed the data analysis procedure of Study 1 to test the factor structure of the Hindi version of the SCS-Y. In particular, we compared and contrasted nine models of the SCS-Y using CFA and ESEM. The model selection was based on model fit indices, standardized factor loadings, and correlations among factors. We examined the reliability of the retained models using α and ω coefficients. For the bifactor model, we examined whether the use of the total scores was justified by calculating ωH/ω. We also explored age-, gender-, and grade-related differences in the SCS-Y scores.

Results and Brief Discussion

Model evaluation: Table 1 displays the goodness-of-fit indices for the nine models of the Hindi version of the SCS-Y. The one-factor and the bifactor CFA models showed poor fit. The two-bifactor ESEM did not converge, and the bifactor ESEM had a specification error. While the twofactor CFA and ESEM models displayed acceptable fit, these models were not optimal among the estimated models. Similar to Study 1, we found that the six-factor CFA and ESEM models showed excellent fit to the data. Furthermore, the fit indices of the ESEM model improved relative to the CFA alternative (ΔCFI = 0.021, ΔTLI = 0.023, ΔRMSEA = -0.015). Therefore, we retained the six-factor ESEM model. Additionally, model fit indices indicated the two-bifactor CFA model of the Hindi version of the SCS. Therefore, we decided to inspect the six-factor ESEM and two-bifactor CFA models in detail.

Regarding the six-factor ESEM model, the factor loadings were acceptable for self-kindness (λ = .159 to .809, M = .479), self-judgment (λ = .394 to .555, M = .500), common humanity (λ = .372 to .799, M = .608), isolation (λ = .492 to .707, M = .620), mindfulness (λ = .319 to .566, M = .474), and over-identification (λ = .429 to .461, M = .445). All loadings were significant (Table 5). Although we found multiple statistically significant cross-loadings in this model, only one item (SK3) had a higher cross-loading than its target loading. The factor correlations of the ESEM model (r = .079 to .578, M = .285) were smaller relative to those of the CFA alternative (r = .162 to .828, M = .461). Reliability indicators suggest that the reliability of the self-kindness, self-judgment, mindfulness, and over-identification scales was relatively low, while that of common humanity and isolation was acceptable (Table 3).

Regarding the two-bifactor CFA model, we found that two general factors were well-defined (λ = .400 to .657, M = .509, for compassionate self-responding; λ = .350 to .6614, M =. 512, for uncompassionate self-responding; Table 6), with all factor loadings significant. Factor correlation between these two general factors was significant and moderate (r = -.376, p < .001), suggesting that they were related but distinct aspects of self-compassion. In this model, each item showed significant factor loadings on the target factor except SK3. In particular, we found significant factor loadings for self-judgment (λ = .223 to .424, M = .335), common humanity (λ = .220 to .814, M = .350), isolation (λ = .285 to .624, M = .322), mindfulness (λ = .184 to .414, M = .329), and over-identification (λs = .233). To test the reliability of the two general factors, we calculated ω and ωH, separately for each factor. We found that the compassionate selfresponding and uncompassionate self-responding factors were reliable (ω = .826 and .802, ωH = .708 and .690, respectively). Furthermore, 85.7 % and 86.0% of the variance in the total scores of these factors were explained by them (ωH/ω). These results justified the use of the total scores of the two types of self-responding.

Overall, the inspection of model fit, factor loading, and reliability indicators supported the two-factor CFA model over the six-factor ESEM counterpart. We then proceeded to the examination of demographic differences in the two general and six specific factors.

Demographic differences:

Age was significantly associated with self-judgment (r = .103, p = .006), over-identification (r = .080, p = .032), and uncompassionate self-responding (r = .089, p = .017), but the magnitude of these correlations was small. We found significant gender differences in over-identification, t (711) = 4.45, p < .001, d = 0.35, isolation, t (887) = 2.82, p = .005, d = 0.19, and uncompassionate self-responding, t (711) = 2.62, p = .009, d = 0.21. Girls were significantly higher in over-identification and uncompassionate self-responding than boys (Table 4). We found a significant grade-related difference in over-identification, F (3, 705) = 5.24, p = .001, partial η² = .02. However, subsequent post hoc Bonferroni tests did not indicate any significant difference. Summary: Overall, the results provide evidence for the two-bifactor CFA solution of the Hindi version of the SCS-Y, where compassionate self-responding and uncompassionate selfresponding were two related, but distinct components of self-compassion that encompass six subcomponents. A notable demographic difference was that girls tend to be higher in uncompassionate self-responding, particularly due to the relatively higher levels of overidentification.

General Discussion

The present study examined the psychometric validity of the factor structure of the English and Hindi versions of the SCS-Y among Indian adolescents. Results from the two independent large samples indicated that the single-and two-factor solutions through both CFA and ESEM provided an adequate fit for both English and Hindi versions. Subsequently, six-factor investigations as well as single-and bifactor CFA and ESEM model investigations provided favourable goodness-of-fit indices for both language versions (Table 1).

However, converging results from all investigations, including model fit indices, factor loadings, and reliability analysis, together championed the evidence for six-factor ESEM for the English version (SCS-Y, English) while supporting two-bifactor CFA for the Hindi version of the scale (SCS-Y, Hindi). Simply put, the present study supported the use of six scores from six related yet distinct sub-factors of self-compassion, along with additional two general factors of compassionate self-responding and uncompassionate self-responding for the Hindi version. These results are in line with previous studies (Chopra et al., 2025; Costa et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2022; Neff et al., 2021; Neff & Miyagawa, 2025; Rakhimov et al., 2023) that support sixfactor solutions representing the multidimensionality of the self-compassion-related scale. The present findings particularly bridges upon the previous validation ofSCS-Y among the Indian adolescents (Chopra et al., 2025); while also confirming its factorial validation of six factors.

Additionally, the study also covered the previous gaps in linguistic validation for the tool in Hindi, which the present study explored and validated in Study 2 (SCS-Y, Hindi). The exploration and validation of the tool in both formal languages in the country further make it consistent with the adaptational studies on the SCS-Y in diverse samples, which have reportedly found an adequate fit for six-factor ESEM and two-bifactor CFA for Polish, Swedish, Chinese, Italian, and Portuguese versions (Barłóg et al., 2026; Costa et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2022; Henje et al., 2024; Levantini et al., 2025). Importantly, our results are based on two large samples, which exceed the criterion for the required sample size of a replication study (Simonsohn, 2015). Therefore, we consider our results robust and the SCS-Y as a valid and reliable measure of the six facets of self-compassion among adolescents in India.

Factorially, across the two studies, the findings report the use of six factors of self-compassion. Additionally, if diversified, it also provides a substantial score for compassionate vs uncompassionate self-responding for the Hindi SCS-Y. On one side, the findings of Study 1 support the original study (Neff et al., 2021), as these findings did not observe any evidence for the two-bifactor solutions (compassionate self-responding and uncompassionate selfresponding). On the other hand, results of Study 2 had findings more similar to the adaptational versions that support the two-factor solutions. One of the major reasons for the discrepancy between Studies 1 and 2 can be attributed to linguistic receptibility. It highlights how language plays a considerable role in receiving a similar factor structure as obtained for the English version in the original study. On the other hand, the English version from the present study parallels the findings for six factors as the original study (Neff at al., 2019), assimilating likewise for the Indian adolescent participants.

The reception of Hindi-based items of self-compassion also realizes a mathematically nuanced balance of two additional distinctions between self-compassion responding types (compassionate vs uncompassionate). This version (SCS_Y), is found to closely replicate the idea of 'pluralistic non-dualism' which is often principled in the Indian cultural contexts (Kaul, 2025; Dalal & Misra, 2010). Advocated in the philosophy of 'Advaitvedanta', 'Pluralistic Non-Dualism' highlights the interconnected view of reality where one can perceive the reality as undivided, single and uniform, yet dual (Giri, 2008). Over time, philosophers have prominently discussed this as an idea of consciousness that allows people to view reality in a united fashion. The bifactorial estimation of the self-compassion scale in Hindi sheds light on this indic idea. It suggests that like the factorial estimation, individuals may perceive compassionate and uncompassionate self-responding yet view self-compassion as a whole as well, which has been found by the study.

Additionally, the study also conducted demographic analyses of self-compassion on age, gender and grade. Findings across both the studies observed that self-compassion was significantly lower among older adolescents, girls and those in higher grade. The findings are in line with previous studies of SCS-Y with adolescents (Bluth et al., 2017; Chopra et al., 2025; Yarnell et al., 2015), which have found that adolescent girls generally report lower self-compassion than their male counterparts. These studies highlight that potential reasons for these findings could be their gender specific socialization in childhood (Yarnell et al., 2015), which habituates them to put others first while constant body image issues (Mahon & Hevey, 2021; Meitasya & Sugiariyanti, 2023; Turk et al., 2021) also subjects them to feeling lower self-esteem and selfworth later.

With respect to age, findings from the study are in line with the previous research (Bluth et al., 2017 (Bluth et al., , 2018;; Chopra et al., 2025; Muris et al., 2016; Tavares et al., 2026). Together, investigations of age-based differences in both English and Hindi versions observed positive associations of age with self-judgement, isolation, and over-identification. Findings highlight a trend that as adolescents grow up, they also subsequently get more critical of themselves with lower reported self-compassion. While age showed significant differences across all uncompassionate indicators, grade-based differences only found significant differences on overidentification (OI). The results support the idea of previous studies (Bluth et al., 2017 (Bluth et al., , 2018;; Chopra et al., 2025), which report that such concerns may arise because of evidenced academic pressure in older adolescence. Particularly in India, these findings make more sense given the heightened academic stress of high-stakes examinations like Board exams, which reportedly increases depression and anxiety (Pienyu et al., 2024; Trevethan et al., 2022). Studies find that this academic stress further exaggerates inaccurate academic evaluation bias (Chopra et al., 2026) academic anxiety in the last year of school due to the added pressure of competitive college entrance exams. In such contexts, rumination becomes more prominent (Yang et al., 2026), while reduced coping (Liu et al., 2025), further giving rise to greater identification with failures, feelings of worthlessness, and inaccurate self-assessments (Chopra et al., 2026).

Overall, reported demographic differences were only found significant across cohorts on negative self-compassion indicators, such as self-judgement, isolation, and over-identification.

Looking at the study comprehensively, the findings highlight a prevalence of self-compassionbased interventions for adolescents with structured modules on these factors. It observes a greater need for enabling school counsellors to look at the adolescent's inner world, which may be critical yet unnoticed. The present findings also guide future work in targeting interventions aligned with adolescents' needs for self-specific emotional regulation. This can include less selfjudgment in situations of failure that provides self-kindness, greater examples of commonality of experiences which reduce their isolation, and also skill-based coping resources for improving over-identification with failures, struggle, or difficulties, and more. Finally, the results from the present study can also be implied in curriculum enhancement at schools, where current curriculums of happiness classes and value-added classes can include self-compassion-based emotional regulation for adolescent mental health and well-being.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although the study informed several interesting findings, it also had unintended limitations. Firstly, the study used a cross-sectional design, which limits its potential for test-retest reliability. As a rescue, future studies can use longitudinal samples with multiple points of data collection to examine the temporal consistency of the above findings.

Secondly, the study begins with the idea of understanding the factorial validity of the selfcompassion scale for India since it is culturally diverse. Although factorial estimations have been calculated rigorously using both CFA and ESEM models, factors particularly affecting the cultural results on self-compassion are not explored. As a step further, future studies can undertake extensive research on the cultural facets in India that understands Indian values and beliefs which are coherent to compassionate and uncompassionate self-responding.

Thirdly, while the present study examined the psychometric validity of the scale through factorial evaluation and reliability, further validation study is needed to comprehensively evaluate the psychometric properties of the SCS-Y among Indian samples. To conduct an even more robust psychometric evaluation, future studies can undertake criterion and construct validity as well. In later studies, research can be conducted on the scale and how it differentiates from other similar and different constructs. Potentially, a robust investigation of its association with a diverse range of mental health and psychological well-being indicators would also provide additional knowledge of the same for Indian cohorts.

Fourth, although demographic analyses indicated significantly lower self-compassion among older adolescents, girls, and those in higher grades, further research is needed to advance our understanding of group differences in detail. At one side where grade 10 and 12th were found to engage significantly more in over-identification, group differences in the Hindi samples were not found. Hence, grade-based findings can be looked at with caution. Relatively, future studies can undertake an exclusive investigation of group differences along their protective and risk factors that increase self-compassion and self-criticism.

Lastly, we recruited our samples from Delhi, the national capital of India, limiting its representation from the entire country, which is culturally diverse. In response, future studies can recruit samples from across the country for generalizability, with subsequent replication studies conducted as the next steps.

Despite the limitations, the present study still meaningfully contributes to the understanding of self-compassion measurement in youth. On one hand, while it validates the English version of the SCS-Y, it uniquely contributes to its Hindi adaptation. Together, both English and Hindi language versions demonstrate acceptable validity, reliability, and confirmed suitability of the scale for adolescents. Additionally, in line with previous studies (Neff et al., 2019 (Neff et al., , 2021)), our study also corresponds to the self-compassion's theoretical assumption of its multidimensionality and its substantial balance between compassionate self-responding and uncompassionate selfresponding. In summary, we suggest the use of total scores of compassionate self-responding and uncompassionate self-responding as well as six scores when using the Hindi version (SCS-Y, Hindi), and the computation of six sub-scale scores for the assessment of self-compassion when using the English version (SCS_Y, English).

Additionally, our results also suggest further exploration among adolescents given their significantly higher uncompassionate self-responding (USR) as well as higher self-judgement, isolation and over-identification. Since all these negative indicators have been consistently correlated to depreciated mental well-being, the study recommends providing relevant selfcompassion based interventions among adolescents. Especially the ones grounded for female adolescents, older adolescents and those in higher grades since they experienced higher selfcriticism. Hopefully, this will not just make them more compassionate towards themselves, healing their inner harsh voices and inaccurate self-perceptions (Chopra et al., 2026); but also provide them a vital emotional support system that they can utilize in the face of failures, hardships and struggles from life and academics.
