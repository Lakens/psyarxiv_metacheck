Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Do people accurately judge how trustworthy others are? Trust in strangers underpins modern societies, yet people sometimes misjudge others' trustworthiness, with potentially detrimental implications for social cohesion and public well-being. In a preregistered study across 23 countries (N = 9,504), we quantify the trust gap: the discrepancy between people's belief about how many people would return a wallet lost in the street and how many people actually do return it. In line with prior research, people in Western, high-income countries underestimated others' trustworthiness. However, people in most non-Western, low-and middle-income countries overestimated trustworthiness. As anticipated, the trust gap varied with socio-economic status within countries. People of lower socio-economic status were more likely to underestimate others' trustworthiness than people of higher socio-economic status, consistent with exploitation being more costly for people with fewer resources. Finally, a randomized controlled trial showed that informing participants about actual wallet return rates reduces trust miscalibration. Information provision led those who had underestimated others' trustworthiness to increase their generalized trust, and those who had overestimated others' trustworthiness to decrease their generalized trust. These findings demonstrate the value of studying the psychology of trust beyond high-income individuals and societies, and of providing accurate information about everyday cooperation to reduce miscalibrations in how people trust one another.

Whether people trust strangers too much or too little shapes cooperation, markets and support for institutions. Research suggests that people are cynical about others' honesty, but this evidence comes mostly from laboratory games in wealthy Western countries. We compared what 9,504 people in 23 countries believe about the return of a lost wallet with how often wallets are actually returned there. On average, beliefs were only slightly too pessimistic, but this average hid opposite errors: people in high-income Western countries underestimated their compatriots' honesty, whereas people in many lower-income countries overestimated it. Within countries, poorer people and those exposed to hardship were more pessimistic than better-off people. Sharing the actual return rate recalibrated trust: a cheap, scalable policy lever.

Low social trust can reflect two situations. People may be right not to trust, for instance when cooperation is rare, and many individuals behave in untrustworthy ways (14) (15) (16). But people may also distrust others even when most individuals are in fact trustworthy. In that case, low trust reflects inaccurate beliefs about others' trustworthiness (17, 18).

A growing body of evidence suggests that this second pattern is common and that people often underestimate others' trustworthiness. Experiments show that people cheat less and cooperate more than observers anticipate (12, 19, 20). In repeated social dilemmas, players start by expecting the worst and gradually come to learn that people cooperate more than they had anticipated (21). Most recently, a meta-analysis of 11 experiments including both incentivized choice experiments and hypothetical marketplace scenarios (31 effect sizes, N = 8,127) showed that people underestimate others' honesty by an average of 14 percentage points in various laboratory settings, illustrating a pervasive tendency to overestimate dishonesty (22). This aligns with broader findings that people underestimate people's willingness to help, to tell the truth, or to keep promises (23) (24) (25) (26) (27) (28), and wrongly believe that moral behavior is declining (29). The largest test to date, published after our data collection and based on an incentivized cooperation game played by representative samples in 125 countries, likewise found that people underestimate their compatriots' cooperation (30).

This evidence is limited in several respects, however. First, most studies rely on laboratory games. While these paradigms offer valuable experimental control, their relationship with real-world cooperation is often small or heterogeneous, often not capturing how people interact in natural settings (31) (32) (33). Second, samples are drawn almost exclusively from Western and high-income populations. As a result, existing findings may reflect the psychology of these specific groups rather than a general feature of human behavior. This limits what we can infer about how trust is calibrated in other social classes and cultural settings.

Trust calibration can be understood as a signal detection problem and, as such, should obey the logic of error management (34) (35) (36) (37). When deciding whether to trust, individuals regulate their judgments in light of the relative costs of two possible errors: trusting people who in fact are not trustworthy, which results in exploitation; or not trusting people who are in fact trustworthy, which results in loss of cooperative opportunities. Both errors are costly, but the cost depends on the amount of resources people have to buffer risks. For people living in poverty, the cost of being exploited might be higher than that of missing opportunities to cooperate, which predicts greater caution to minimize losses (38) (39) (40) (41) (42) (43) (44) (45) (46).

Given that Error Management Theory predicts that decision thresholds vary with environmental risk, the fact that most research on trust calibration has focused on affluent Westerners creates a blind spot. This narrow lens ignores how scarcity alters the cost of error and it is therefore important to investigate trust miscalibration across cultures and social class.

To do so and to address the previous limitations, we asked 9,504 participants across 23 countries of 6 continents to estimate the likelihood that a stranger would return a lost wallet containing money if it was handed to them, which is an ecologically valid and widely used measure of trust (47). We compared these beliefs to the actual national return rates previously identified in a global field experiment across 40 countries (48). Participants read a short vignette describing the situation staged in that experiment: someone finds a wallet in the street and hands it to a person working in a public place nearby, who can either contact the owner and return the wallet with the cash or keep everything. They then estimated how many of 100 random people would return it. Building on the theoretical framework of Error Management Theory, we derived three predictions and eight exploratory research questions (all preregistered).

First, if the mind effectively calibrates trust based on objective cues of others' cooperation, people's expectations about wallet return rates should correlate with actual return rates across countries (Prediction 1; H1). Second, if the cost of being exploited is higher than the cost of missing cooperative opportunities, people should systematically underestimate others' trustworthiness across countries (Prediction 2; H2). Third, if the relative costliness of exploitation is even higher for people with fewer resources, people of lower socio-economic status should underestimate trustworthiness more than people of higher socioeconomic status within countries (Prediction 3; H3). Fourth, we explored how the trust gap varies across the 23 countries (Research Question 1) and whether other cues of deprivation (past financial desperation, childhood unpredictability, childhood socio-economic status, childhood underweight, and poor health now and in childhood) were associated with a more negative gap in adulthood (Research Questions 2-7). Finally, if miscalibration stems from a lack of data, providing accurate information should correct it. We therefore tested whether informing participants about actual wallet return rates would lead people who underestimate trustworthiness to increase their generalized trust and those who overestimate trustworthiness to lower their generalized trust (Research Question 8). All hypotheses, sampling decisions, research questions, data collection procedures, and statistical analyses were preregistered prior to data collection (https://osf.io/5uz8e).

Results

The trust gap varies across countries

As preregistered, we tested whether people's trust correlates with actual trustworthiness by regressing participants' average expectations of wallet return rates on the return rates observed in Cohn et al. (48), across the 23 country means, using a one-sided test. Expected wallet return rates were significantly associated with actual rates (β = 0.129, SE = 0.063, P = 0.026, R² = 0.167). The slope of the relationship (β = 0.129) was significantly lower than β = 1 (P < 0.001)-that is, it was lower than the line of perfect calibration where expected return rate would equal actual return rates. A 10-percentage-point increase in actual returns corresponded to only a 1.3-percentage-point increase in expected returns. A supplementary weighted least-squares model that accounted for differences in country sample size gave a similar estimate (β = 0.135, SE = 0.063, one-sided P = 0.022, R² = 0.180). The association also held in a spatial error model, which allows for the fact that neighbouring countries resemble one another and are therefore not fully independent observations (β = 0.149, SE = 0.059, P = 0.012; SI Appendix, section 2.2.7). Figure 1 shows the full distribution of individual expectations in each country against its actual return rate.

To measure the global gap between what people expected and the actual return rate (H2), we used a preregistered linear mixed model (LMM) of the 9,504 individual gaps, with a random intercept for each country. Across countries, expectations did not differ significantly from actual return rates (mean gap = -2.08 pp, z = -0.53, one-sided P = 0.298; 95% CI [-9.79, 5.63]; a country-level bootstrap gave -1.9 pp, SI Appendix, section 2.2.1). The preregistered secondary check with standard errors clustered by country gave a similar result (-2.21 pp, z = -0.58, one-sided P = 0.283), as did a random-effects meta-analysis of the 23 country means (-2.09 pp, z = -0.53, one-sided P = 0.298). The preregistered test therefore did not support H2, although every estimate was negative, the direction H2 predicts. However, because these tests treat the 23 countries as the units of inference and countries differ widely, an average gap would have needed to exceed about 6.5 percentage points to reach significance, and the tests had 80% power only for gaps of about 10 points or more (SI Appendix, section 2.2.1).

To estimate the average gap more precisely, we therefore fitted, as an exploratory analysis, a Beta generalized linear mixed model (Beta GLMM) of each participant's expectation, with the country's actual return rate as a covariate (SI Appendix, section 2.2.2). This model takes two features of the data into account: expectations are proportions bounded between 0 and 100%, so the possible range, spread and skew of the gap differ between countries; and much of the variation in the gap between countries comes from the actual return rates themselves, which the model accounts for instead of treating as noise. At the mean actual return rate of 45.1%, the model implies a mean expected return rate of 43.3%, a gap of -1.8 pp (95% CI [-3.9, 0.3], one-sided P = 0.046, as preregistered for H2; SI Appendix, section 2.2.2). All five estimates are therefore negative, and the more precise exploratory model detects a small average underestimation of about two percentage points.

Yet this small average hides large errors of opposite sign across countries (I² = 99.5%; Figs. 2 and 3). People in 12 countries significantly underestimated the actual return rate, most strongly in Czechia (-40.5 pp), Sweden (-33.2 pp) and Romania (-22.8 pp), whereas people in 10 countries overestimated it, most strongly in Malaysia (+24.4 pp), Kazakhstan (+24.3 pp) and Morocco (+23.9 pp); only in Turkey was the gap not significant. These country-level comparisons were preregistered as exploratory (Research Question 1), with underestimation tested one-sided as preregistered and overestimation with two-sided tests. All 22 significant gaps survived Benjamini-Hochberg correction, 20 remained significant when the sampling error of Cohn et al.'s rates was added, and the Beta GLMM classified 22 of the 23 countries in the same way (South Africa's underestimation was no longer significant; SI Appendix, section 2.2.3). Part of this variation reflects differences in expectations, which ranged from 29.6% in Peru to 52.2% in Thailand: Thailand and South Africa had almost the same actual return rate (40% and 39%) but expected 52% and 33%, respectively, and so erred in opposite directions. Most of it, however, mirrors variation in actual return rates, which varied about three times as much across countries as expectations did (SD 20.6 vs 6.5 pp) and alone account for 90% of the between-country variance in the gap (R² = 0.90, country-level regression; SI Appendix, section 3.1), consistent with the shallow slope of expectations on actual rates reported for H1.

Lastly, the direction of the error covaried with development. People in all nine Western countries (Australia, Czechia, France, Poland, Romania, Serbia, Sweden, the UK and the US) underestimated the actual return rate, whereas people in 10 of the 14 non-Western countries overestimated it. Across the 23 countries, the trust gap was associated with the Human Development Index (HDI; R² = 0.44, P < 0.001): each 0.1-point difference in HDI was associated with a gap about 12.5 pp further toward underestimation (β = -124.7, SE = 30.8, 95% CI [-188.8, -60.7], P < 0.001). The association held in a spatial error model that accounts for the similarity of neighbouring countries (β = -116.0, SE = 29.5, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, section 2.2.7).

Individuals of lower socio-economic status underestimated trustworthiness more

As predicted, within each country, higher income was associated with a more positive trust gap (Fig. 4; 23 country slopes, meta-analysed: β = 2.72 pp per SD, SE = 0.38, P < 0.001). In other words, wealthier respondents underestimated trustworthiness less, or overestimated it more, than poorer respondents. The gradient was roughly linear: adjusted for country, the mean gap rose from -8.1 pp in the lowest income category to +1.9 pp in the highest. This is not explained by greater accuracy: the income slope was positive both in countries that underestimated actual return rates (12 of 12 countries) and in countries that overestimated them (10 of 11; SI Appendix, section 3.3). This effect persists after controlling for age and gender (β = 2.75, P < 0.001). We found the same gradient using the preregistered secondary indicators (Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted across the two): higher subjective social status (β = 2.25, SE = 0.28, P < 0.001) and educational attainment (β = 0.94, SE = 0.37, P = 0.005) both predict less underestimation (or more overestimation). In a single model with all three indicators, fitted within each country and pooled across the 23 countries, income (β = 2.17, SE = 0.41, P < 0.001) and subjective status (β = 1.27, SE = 0.31, P < 0.001) each predicted the gap net of the other two, whereas education did not (β = 0.19, SE = 0.35, P = 0.58). A Beta GLMM robustness model gave the same pattern and significance (SI Appendix, section 2.3.4).

Beyond SES, environmental harshness was also associated with a more negative trust gap. We tested six predictors (Research Questions 2-7) within each country, with age and gender as covariates as preregistered, pooling the 23 country slopes, standardized and coded so that higher values always indicate worse conditions (e.g., lower childhood SES or poorer health were reverse coded). After adjusting for multiple comparisons (Benjamini-Hochberg), we found that harsher environments consistently predicted greater underestimation of trustworthiness (Fig. 5): having been under the desperation threshold in the past-i.e., unable to meet one's basic needs with no one to turn to for help (49)-predicted greater underestimation of trustworthiness (β = -2.68 per SD of this binary item, SE = 0.29, P < 0.001); greater childhood unpredictability (β = -1.46, SE = 0.38, P < 0.001), lower childhood SES (β = -2.06, SE = 0.42, P < 0.001), and being underweight in childhood (β = -2.22, SE = 1.00, P = 0.026) also predicted stronger underestimation. Finally, poorer health, both current (β = -2.49, SE = 0.29, P < 0.001) and in childhood (β = -1.79, SE = 0.42, P < 0.001), predicted a more negative gap. These results held without the covariates and in Beta GLMMs: all six slopes kept their sign and remained significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction (without covariates, from β = -1.82 for lower childhood SES to β = -2.99 for past desperation, all P ≤ 0.013; Beta GLMMs, all P ≤ 0.017; SI Appendix, section 2.3.5). Overall, miscalibration is not random noise: exposure to scarcity, instability, and poor health is systematically associated with underestimating strangers' cooperation more. These gradients were consistent across countries. The income gradient was positive in 22 of the 23 countries, with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 42%; 95% prediction interval for a new country [0.32, 5.11]), and the subjective-status gradient was positive in all 23, with no detectable heterogeneity (I² = 0%). Past desperation and poorer current health predicted more negative gaps in 22 of the 23 countries. Fewer countries reach significance on their own because each national sample has limited power: with about 300 to 400 respondents in most countries (median 324), the median chance of detecting the average income effect in a single country is 0.44. The largest difference concerned past desperation, whose link with underestimation was stronger in countries with a higher Human Development Index (meta-regression: b = -8.07, SE = 3.09, P = 0.009; P = 0.081 after Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the 18 moderator tests; SI Appendix, section 3.2). One possible reading is that where extreme deprivation is rare, having lived through it sets a person further apart from the experience of most fellow citizens, and so weighs more heavily on how they judge strangers.

Information helps people trust more accurately

As preregistered, we ran a randomized controlled trial (N = 9,504: 4,735 participants in the control group and 4,769 in the treatment group; by country in SI Appendix, Table 44) to test whether feedback on actual national wallet-return rates would shift generalized trust (Research Question 8). At the aggregate level, information provision had no detectable average effect on trust (individual-level mixed model with a random intercept for each country; Treatment main effect: β = -0.0124, P = 0.704), but the treatment interacted with the direction of the relative gap before treatment (Treatment × Relative Trust Gap: β = -0.107, P = 0.0005): the more participants overestimated trustworthiness before treatment, the more the information provision reduced their trust. Conversely, the more they underestimated trustworthiness before treatment, the more the information provision raised their trust (Fig. 6). This pattern was robust to alternative specifications, including using the raw trust gap (Treatment × Gap: β = -0.00273, P = 0.006) and a Beta GLMM accounting for data boundedness (β = -0.0505, P = 0.009; SI Appendix, section 2.4.3).

Discussion

Trust in strangers is crucial in large-scale societies. Many of our most consequential choices-entering markets with unknown partners (4, 8, 11), complying with rules (50), supporting redistribution (51, 52), and voting for norms that will bind people we will never meet (53)-depend on our beliefs about how cooperative "most people" are. Here we compared such expectations to a behavioral benchmark. In preregistered surveys across 23 countries (N = 9,504), participants estimated the share of 100 random people in their country who would return a lost wallet. We compared these estimates to observed walletreturn rates from a large field experiment by Cohn et al. (48). This design provides a direct measure of trust miscalibration: the gap between expected and actual cooperation. It also extends prior work that has relied heavily on laboratory games and self-reports among Western high-income samples (20, 31, 54, 55). Our first finding is that there is no clear average negative trust gap: averaged across the 23 countries, expectations were only about two percentage points below actual return rates, a difference the preregistered test did not detect and only the more precise exploratory model did, and this average concealed large errors of opposite sign across countries. In many Western high-income countries, respondents underestimated wallet returns, which aligns with prior claims that people underestimate others' honesty and prosociality (12, 19). However, many non-Western low-and middle-income countries showed the opposite pattern: respondents overestimated wallet returns. Thus, we observed that the direction of error depends on context. This heterogeneity matters to interpret the broader "negativity bias" literature. If most available studies draw on Western, high-income samples and do not anchor beliefs to real-world behavior, then an apparent universal tendency to underestimate others' trustworthiness may partly reflect sampling artefacts (12, 19, (22) (23) (24) (25). This includes the 125-country study (30), whose benchmark was cooperation in an incentivized game played within the same survey, whereas ours is an unobserved decision taken in everyday life. Our data suggest a more cautious conclusion: people underestimating trustworthiness is common in some ecologies, but overestimation errors appear in others, and the cross-national mean can obscure this structure. This pattern does not contradict error management, which predicts more caution where resources are scarce: expectations varied little across countries and even rose slightly with development (SI Appendix, section 3.1), so the opposite errors mainly reflect differences in actual return rates, whereas the caution predicted by error management appears within countries, among people with fewer resources. Not only does the trust gap vary by country, it also varies by social class. Within countries, lower socioeconomic status predicted more negative trust gaps: poorer people underestimated national trustworthiness more, or overestimated it less, than richer people. The same pattern appeared using subjective social status and education, and it extended to multiple preregistered indicators of deprivation, including worse health, economic deprivation, and childhood unpredictability. This convergence suggests that miscalibration is not random noise. Instead, exposure to scarcity and instability is associated with more pessimistic expectations about strangers. This result is consistent with the logic of error management. Like any signal detection problem, trust calibration trades off two costly mistakes: trusting an untrustworthy person (risking exploitation) versus distrusting a trustworthy person (forgoing the benefits of cooperation). When resources are scarce, exploitation can be particularly costly, and conservative thresholds that reduce those costs may therefore be favored (35, 36, 40, 41). Rather than implying irrationality among disadvantaged groups, trust underestimation may be an adaptive response to environments where the costs of misplaced trust are higher. An alternative possibility, however, is that lower-SES respondents may be well calibrated to cooperation in their immediate surroundings but overgeneralize those local cues to the national level. People learn from a socially limited slice of society; if cooperation is lower in one's neighborhood, workplace, or institutional interfaces, pessimistic beliefs may be locally accurate. The mistake would arise when local experience is scaled up to 100 random compatriots. This matters because national-scale trust is politically consequential. Modern governance requires citizens to form expectations about unknown others: the people who will pay taxes, comply with rules, and be affected by policies. Through elections, citizens choose norms and institutions that apply to strangers. If disadvantaged groups-whether because of asymmetric error costs or because of local-to-national overgeneralization-systematically underestimate national cooperation, they may rationally favor more coercive enforcement, lower reliance on voluntary compliance, and reduced willingness to enter cooperative exchanges. This points to an epistemic dimension of inequality: not only unequal resources, but unequal alignment between beliefs and the cooperative baseline of the wider society.

Finally, our experiment suggests that trust is responsive to accurate information about others' behaviour. When participants learned about actual wallet-return rates in their country, their trust shifted in the expected direction: underestimators trusted more, whereas overestimators trusted less. The average change was modest, but its very existence is noteworthy given how "sticky" generalized trust is typically assumed to be (56, 57). This pattern is consistent with simple updating. Generalized trust is shaped by years of accumulated experience. Although it is hard to imagine that a single statistic would entirely overwrite it, a one-shot cue was sufficient to produce measurable correction. This implies that stronger implementations (e.g., repeated exposure to transparent metrics, feedback delivered by trusted local institutions, or sustained public reporting) may yield larger and more durable recalibration. In such contexts, the priority may be less to "teach trust" than to make existing cooperation more visible: publishing clear service-quality indicators, reporting high compliance rates, or designing low-stakes entry interactions that let citizens test institutional reliability with minimal downside risk (58, 59). Several limitations should be noted. The rate of wallet return in a country captures a specific, one-shot form of honesty and does not represent all domains of cooperation. Future work should test beliefs against other objective measures of cooperative behavior. The behavioral benchmark predates our survey (48), so changes over time could contribute to measurement error. To limit this risk, we excluded the one eligible country whose HDI had changed by more than 0.05 between 2015 and 2022 (United Arab Emirates, 0.08); in the 23 retained countries it changed by -0.004 to +0.042 over the same period (SI Appendix, section 1.1.1). Furthermore, country differences in civic honesty change slowly: the Reader's Digest wallet drops of 1995-2001, made with a different protocol (8, 60), predict the rates Cohn et al. observed about two decades later in the 11 countries of our sample covered by both (r = 0.80, 95% CI [0.38, 0.94]), and repeated national measures of corruption and generalized trust taken about ten years apart correlate at 0.82 to 0.96 across our countries (SI Appendix, section 3.8).

Two further concerns bear on the respondents rather than on the benchmark. First, one could ask whether the small average gap reflects how people answer rather than what they believe: participants who answer at random, or who anchor on 50% when uncertain (61), could also produce a gap. However, this is not the case: such answers average 50%, which is 5 pp above the mean actual return rate of 45%, so they would appear as an overestimation of about +4.8 pp, not an underestimation (simulations in SI Appendix, section 2.2.2). The observed gap (-1.8 pp) lay 6.7 pp below this value (z = -5.0, P < 0.001) and averaged below 50% in 21 of the 23 countries. Second, online panels may over-represent better-off respondents in lower-income countries, and wealthier respondents underestimated trustworthiness less, or overestimated it more, than poorer ones. Such selection cannot account for the overestimation we observe: given the within-country gradient (2.6 pp per standard deviation of a composite of income, education and subjective status), even a sample drawn entirely from the most advantaged 30% of a population would raise its mean expectation by only about 6 pp, half the smallest overestimation that survives the benchmark's sampling error (12 to 24 pp), and in each of the nine countries whose overestimation remains significant once the benchmark's sampling error is included, the less advantaged half of the sample overestimated as well (SI Appendix, section 3.9).

The association between trust and deprivation is correlational and causal tests will require designs that manipulate perceived downside risk, buffers against exploitation, and information environments. In the information experiment, endline trust was measured later in the same questionnaire, so part of the shift may reflect experimenter demand or a short-lived anchoring on the figure shown rather than lasting learning, although demand effects in online experiments are typically small (62); an obfuscated follow-up would be needed to establish whether the recalibration persists (63). Most importantly, we need to measure expectations at several geographic levels (e.g. neighborhood, city, and country) and compare them with behavioral data from those same places to determine whether people are accurate about their immediate surroundings but mistaken about the nation as a whole. Such designs would directly test whether miscalibration arises from inaccurate local expectations, from overgeneralization across social distance, or from both; and would sharpen how trust in strangers can be aligned with the cooperative realities that sustain modern societies.

Materials and Methods

All hypotheses, sampling decisions, research questions, data collection procedures, and statistical analyses were preregistered. The preregistration is available at https://osf.io/5uz8e.

Sample

We restricted our sample to countries with observed wallet-return rates reported in Cohn et al. (48). All countries were ranked by their 2022 Human Development Index (HDI) and assigned to global HDI quartiles using United Nations Development Programme data. We retained eligible countries from Quartiles 1-3 and, for Quartile 4, we selected countries to maximize data depth and geographic balance (SI Appendix, section 1.1.1).

Participants were recruited through the CINT platform in 23 countries. In total, 300 participants per country were targeted, but larger numbers were recruited to ensure sufficient sample sizes after exclusions. Because the panel provider over-delivered, larger samples were obtained in France and Indonesia. The preregistered results do not depend on these larger samples (SI Appendix, section 1.1.3).

Analysis samples ranged from 254 (Czechia) to 1,573 (France; median 324; SI Appendix, Table 4). This selection covers six continents and a wide range of HDI levels, and therefore cultural and socioeconomic diversity. More information and demographics are available in SI Appendix, section 1.1.3.

Materials

We selected the lost-wallet paradigm (48) as the measure of a country's level of trustworthiness because it addresses several limitations identified in the literature (55). First, no cues are given about the person to whom the lost wallet is handed. Apart from being a fellow citizen, nothing is known about them: no face, no clothing or group markers. The decision therefore concerns an anonymous member of the public, "the generalized other", which is the object of generalized trust (64, 65). Second, unlike economic games, the scenario has high ecological validity: real people unexpectedly encounter real wallets with real money in everyday public settings, instead of making low-stakes or hypothetical choices in a laboratory task (31, 54). Third, the interaction is unilateral and one-shot: the finder decides without being observed by the owner and with no prospect of meeting again, so the outcome owes nothing to reputation or direct reciprocity. Finally, the paradigm is validated across cultures and across time. Wallets were dropped in 355 cities of 40 countries, and national return rates are stable: the Reader's Digest drops of 1995-2001 (8, 60) predict those of Cohn et al. about two decades later (r = 0.80 across the 11 countries of our sample covered by both; SI Appendix, section 3.8).

Expectations about wallet return are a measure of trust. While definitions of trust differ across fields (66-70), they share a core: trust is the expectation that another person will act well when one is at their mercy, as distinct from trustworthiness, how that person actually acts (66, 68, 71). A lost wallet puts its owner at a stranger's mercy, and expecting it not to come back has the consequences the definition implies: a tighter grip on one's belongings and less reliance on strangers. The measure is also empirically validated. The OECD Guidelines on Measuring Trust include the wallet question in their survey modules as a measure of generalized trust (47), because it is concrete and quasi-behavioural and means the same thing to respondents and researchers (72), unlike the "most people" item, whose reference group shifts across cultures (65). It has been fielded by the Gallup World Poll and Statistics Canada (2, 47, 73), and expected wallet return tracks the conventional trust item within and across countries (73, 74). Actual wallet returns, in turn, are the behaviour against which conventional trust items are validated (75).

Measuring Trustworthiness

To measure trustworthiness, we used national wallet-return rates from Cohn et al. (48), who conducted a large-scale field experiment on prosociality in 40 countries (17,303 wallets across 355 cities). In each trial, a single research assistant entered an institution-such as a bank, post office, hotel, cultural venue, or public office-and handed a wallet to an employee, explaining that they had just found it on the street and could not return it themselves. The employee thus became the sole potential returner, and their subsequent decision determined whether the wallet would be returned. Each wallet contained a grocery list, and three business cards with a fictitious name and email address. In the Money condition, which we used as our baseline, the wallet also included 13.45 US dollars in local currency, adjusted for purchasing power parity. The outcome was binary: whether or not that individual contacted the owner within 100 days. Countrylevel return rates in the Money condition provided a standardized measure of real-world trustworthiness. Computation procedures and final rates are detailed in SI Appendix, section 1.2.4.3.

Measuring Trust

We elicited expected wallet return using a vignette closely modeled on Cohn et al.'s field protocol. Participants read a short scenario describing a person who received a lost wallet containing the same items and an amount of money matched to local purchasing power and inflation-adjusted equivalence relative to the Cohn baseline. As in Cohn et al.'s protocol, the wallet had been found in the street by a passer-by who, having no time to return it, handed it to a person working in a public place nearby, and no one would learn whether this person returned it. Participants thus judged the same situation as the one in which the behavioural benchmark was measured, rather than honesty in general or a lost wallet in general. They were then asked to imagine 100 random people, each handed a lost wallet by a stranger in the same way. The question read: "Out of 100, how many do you think would keep the wallet and the money?" Participants answered by typing a number between 0 and 100 into an open numeric input box. We preferred open numerical input over sliders to ensure precision on mobile devices and to promote attentive responding. Expectation reports were not incentivized: in direct comparisons, unincentivized introspection is as accurate as incentive-compatible elicitation methods (76, 77), incentivizing accuracy in representative online surveys does not reduce bias in stated beliefs about non-political facts and mainly leads respondents to look up the answer online (63, 78), and the state-of-the-art scoring rule biases reports toward the midpoint, which would have attenuated the between-country and between-class contrasts that are our estimands (79). To control for framing, half of the participants were asked the inverse question ("Out of 100, how many do you think would return your wallet with the money still inside?"), and answers were reverse-coded; the gender of the person in the vignette (male or female) was also randomized to control for presentation effects. Averaging the two frames within each country, or adjusting for the frame and the gender of the person in the vignette, leaves the results unchanged (SI Appendix, section 1.5). As a check of convergent validity (SI Appendix, section 1.5), mean expectations across the 23 countries correlated with the share of respondents who say that most people can be trusted in the most recent wave of the World Values Survey or the European Values Study (r = 0.58, 95% CI [0.23, 0.80]) (80, 81), and within countries, expectations correlated with the standard 0-10 generalized-trust question asked earlier in the survey (pooled r = 0.24, 95% CI [0.20, 0.28]; positive in all 23 countries). We computed the trust gap for each individual by comparing their expected wallet-return rates with the country's observed rate, and then aggregated these values to estimate country-level gaps. Full vignette texts, randomization procedures, and local currency adjustments are provided in SI Appendix, sections 1.2.3 and 1.2.4.

Measuring the trust gap

We quantified the trust gap as the difference between each participant's expected wallet-return rate and the observed wallet-return rate in their country: Trust gapi,j = Rexpected(i,j) -Ractual(j) where Rexpected(i,j) is the individual's estimate of the percentage of compatriots who would return a lost wallet, and Ractual(j) is the empirically observed national return rate.

A positive value indicates overestimation (trust exceeding actual trustworthiness), while a negative value indicates underestimation (trust inferior to actual trustworthiness). Following the preregistration, we distinguish between two related measures. The raw trust gap is the signed difference in percentage points, whereas the relative trust gap is defined as (Rexpected(i,j) -Ractual(j)) / Ractual(j). The relative specification normalizes miscalibration by the country-specific return-rate baseline and was therefore used as the preregistered moderator in the RCT analyses for Research Question 8. Robustness analyses using the raw trust gap are reported in SI Appendix, section 2.4.4.

Models

We followed the preregistered analysis plan; SI Appendix, section 1.3, gives every specification. H1 was tested with an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression of the 23 country mean expectations on the actual return rates, with a second test of whether the slope was below 1, the line of perfect calibration; a weighted least squares (WLS) model weighted countries by sample size.

H2 was tested with a linear mixed model (LMM) of the individual trust gaps with a random intercept for country. It was complemented by the preregistered cluster-robust test, a country-level cluster bootstrap (200 resamples), a random-effects meta-analysis of the country means and, as an exploratory analysis, a Beta generalized linear mixed model (Beta GLMM) of expectations with the national return rate as a covariate, which models the bounded answers.

For H3 and RQ2-7, the trust gap was regressed within each country on each predictor (SES indicators standardized within country, harshness indicators across the pooled sample, childhood underweight as a 0/1 indicator; for RQ2-7, with age and gender as covariates, as preregistered), and the 23 country slopes were pooled by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) meta-analysis; this within-country design separates social standing from the actual trustworthiness of the national environment. P values for the six harshness predictors (RQ2-7) were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

For RQ8, endline trust was regressed on treatment, the pre-treatment relative gap and their interaction, controlling for baseline trust, with a random intercept for country (the preregistered specification without it gives the same interaction, -0.108, P = 0.0005).

Tests of the three preregistered hypotheses (H1-H3), including the sample-size-weighted check of H1 and the exploratory Beta GLMM estimate of the average gap (H2), are one-sided, in the preregistered direction; all other tests are two-sided. Robustness checks (alternative estimators, global standardization, Beta GLMMs, the preregistered covariates for H3, and RQ2-7 without covariates) are reported in SI Appendix, sections 2.1 to 2.5.
